This calculator vs. a flat "$1,800 installed" patio door special

"$1,800 patio door installed" is not a price for a patio door install — it is a price for one 6-foot vinyl 2-panel retrofit hang, quietly applied to every opening the truck pulls up to.

What a flat "$1,800 installed" special is

A flat special is the fastest way to quote: one headline number on a postcard or a website, read off and repeated. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match on their own opening.

The number behind it was set for one job — usually the cheapest configuration (a vinyl retrofit hang, dual-pane glass, at grade, no new-construction wrap, no transom, no sill pan) — and then reused for every patio door afterward, whether or not the next opening looks anything like that hang.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the panel configuration, frame material, glazing, whether the install is a retrofit into an existing frame or a full new-construction wrap, ground or balcony access, extras such as a screen, a transom, interior trim, a sill pan, and a threshold rebuild, and your loaded labor and overhead. It prices the set labor, the wrap, the access, and the extras separately, and returns a walk-away price for the job you describe — for this opening, not a generic one.

Prices the panels, frame, and glazing from the entered configuration, so jobs of different scope come out at different numbers instead of one.

Times the wrap, the sill pan, and the balcony access as their own extra-work hours, so that work shows up instead of being absorbed into the sash price or skipped.
Assumes dual-pane glass and a vinyl frame, so triple-pane, impact glazing, aluminum, fiberglass, or wood-clad frames get quoted the same as the cheapest option. Applies a glazing cost factor and a frame unit cost only for what was entered, so heavier frames and upgraded glass price differently.
Bakes payroll burden into a round "labor rate" nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the crew a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper flat rate on a bigger opening. Reports a walk-away price built from the entered scope, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the crew commits to it.

A flat special is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a postcard rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat "$1,800 installed" patio-door special get the price wrong?

Because the labor and material scale with the panel count, frame, glazing, install method, and access, not with the phrase "patio door" alone. A 6-foot vinyl 2-panel retrofit into an existing frame at grade is a different job from a 16-foot fiberglass 4-panel unit with impact glass, a new-construction wrap, a transom, a sill pan, and second-story balcony access. A flat special quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat special usually leave out entirely?

The frame, the glazing upgrade, the new-construction wrap, and the access. A published installed price is set on the cheapest configuration — a vinyl retrofit hang, dual-pane glass, at grade — and has nowhere to put the hours and material for a heavier frame, impact or triple-pane glass, a full-frame wrap with a new sill pan, a transom, or hoisting panels to a second-story balcony. A shop pricing off the special alone is short before the crew unloads the truck.

How much do the frame, glazing, and access actually add over the flat special?

On this calculator's own worked examples, at a $48 crew wage, 32% burden, 16% overhead, and a 32% target margin: even the cheapest job it prices — a 6-foot vinyl 2-panel retrofit at grade with a tear-out — walks away at $2,770.12, already $970.12 over the $1,800 special. A 12-foot 3-panel vinyl unit with triple-pane glass, a new-construction wrap, a screen, and a sill pan walks away at $7,975.38, over $6,175 over the special. A 16-foot 4-panel fiberglass unit with impact glass, a full new-construction wrap, a transom, a sill pan, and balcony access walks away at $23,344.28, over $21,500 over the special. The frame, the glazing, the wrap, and the access — not the word "patio door" — are what move the number.

Is a flat rate ever the right way to quote a patio door?

For the one job it was actually priced on it can land close, but on this calculator's own defaults even that cheapest job — a 6-foot vinyl 2-panel retrofit at grade — already clears $1,800 once real payroll burden, overhead, and a target margin are applied. A flat special set years ago, or set without those loaded costs, keeps quoting a number the shop's own arithmetic no longer supports, and the gap only grows on anything bigger than the base hang.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, permit fees, or what competitors charge. It takes the panel configuration, frame, glazing, install method, access, extras, and your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat "$1,800 installed" special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

This calculator vs. a flat $4-a-square-foot special

Gravel driveway shops are shopped by a published “$4 a square foot installed” special. That rate is priced for the easy job — ready grade, quarry pickup, no extras — and it cannot see excavation, haul-off, fabric, an apron, or a culvert, so a smaller job that needs all five can take more than three times the labor of a bigger job that needs none of them.

What a flat $4-a-square-foot special is

A flat per-square-foot number is the fastest thing a company can put on a postcard or a Facebook ad: one rate, times the drive's plan area, read back as a total. It is also the unit homeowners already compare between competing bids.

The rate was set for one job — commonly a ready-grade drive with no dirt work, picked up at the quarry rather than delivered, no fabric, no apron, and no culvert — and then quoted to the next caller, whether or not that driveway shares any of those.

What this calculator does instead

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the stone, drive length and width, compacted depth, compaction allowance, soil prep, delivery, fabric, apron, and culvert, plus your own wage, payroll burden, quarry and trucking rates, overhead, and target margin. It prices the trucks, the dirt work, and every extra as their own hours and cost lines — for this driveway, not a rate carried over from the last one.

Same trade, two different jobs

Using this calculator's own shipped cost inputs — $24 hourly wage, 28% burden, $55 travel and equipment, 15% overhead, 20% target margin:

A 600 sq ft drive on ready grade, in crusher-run picked up at the quarry, with no fabric, apron, or culvert — the job a $4-a-square-foot special is usually built around — takes 3.2 crew-hours and this calculator prices it at $779.40 for the job — $1.30 per square foot, a fraction of the $4 headline rate.

A 400 sq ft drive — two-thirds the area — that needs the existing surface excavated and hauled off, delivered recycled stone, a sloped site, fabric, an apron, and a culvert takes 11.2 crew-hours, costs $2,788.78 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator prices it at $3,485.97 — $8.71 per square foot. Common questions

Why does a flat $4 a square foot special get a gravel driveway bid wrong?

Because plan area only measures the pad's footprint, not what it takes to build it. A 600 sq ft ready-grade drive picked up at the quarry and spread with no extras takes about 3.2 crew-hours on this calculator's own preset. A 400 sq ft drive that needs the existing surface excavated and hauled off, delivered recycled stone, a sloped site, fabric, an apron, and a culvert takes about 11.2 crew-hours -- more than three times the labor on two-thirds the area -- because excavation, haul-off, fabric, and site extras never show up in a per-square-foot number.

What job is the $4-a-square-foot number actually built around?

One specific job, usually the easiest one a company is willing to advertise: a ready-grade drive with no dirt work, picked up at the quarry rather than delivered, no fabric, no apron, and no culvert. It is a marketing price meant to get the phone to ring, not a number derived from any particular driveway's prep, stone, or site work.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own shipped cost inputs: the 600 sq ft ready-grade pickup job takes 3.2 crew-hours and prices at $779.40 for the job -- $1.30 per square foot, a fraction of the $4 headline rate. The 400 sq ft excavate-and-haul job with delivered recycled stone, a sloped site, fabric, an apron, and a culvert takes 11.2 crew-hours, costs $2,788.78 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator prices it at $3,485.97 -- $8.71 per square foot. Quoting that job at the flat $4-a-square-foot rate reproduces $1,600.00 for the job, which is $1,188.78 below the job's own break-even cost -- before counting the $1,885.97 it falls short of this calculator's price at a 20% target margin.

Is a flat per-square-foot special ever a reasonable shorthand?

For a narrow band of jobs close to the one the special was priced on -- ready grade, quarry pickup, no fabric, no apron, no culvert -- a per-square-foot number can land close, or even leave a fat margin. The risk is quoting an excavate-and-haul job, delivered stone, a sloped site, or fabric and site extras the same way sight unseen, because the rate does not know the prep, the stone, or the site until the crew shows up, and by then the per-square-foot number has often already been quoted.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or material costs?

No. It has no data on your local labor market or your quarry's stone, fabric, or trucking rates. It takes the stone, drive size, compacted depth, soil prep, delivery, fabric, apron, and culvert you enter, plus your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into crew-hours and a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat per-square-foot special skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-square-foot special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest way to advertise that works until the driveway stops matching the one the special was priced on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. The $779.40, $1.30, $2,788.78, and $3,485.97 figures above are this calculator's own arithmetic on its own shipped defaults (open the calculator and enter the same drive to reproduce them), not a claim about any specific contractor's real costs or any specific company's advertised price.

Does the audience come before the listing?

Study · 2026-08-19 · portfolio/research/publisher-audience/README.md

Measured 2026-08-19. Primary measurements against the public npm registry, reproducible from this repo.

portfolio/research/free-listing-demand/ measured what a cold free listing gets in this portfolio's candidate categories, found demand concentrated into the top few percent, and closed on a claim it did not test:

"on these channels, publishing is not distribution ... the scarce thing to spend an approval on is a referral path, not a listing."

That is an assertion about why the top of the distribution is the top. This directory tests it on the one attribute of a referral path measurable from a public API with no credentials: did the publisher already have an audience?

Answer: mostly yes — but not exclusively, and the exception is the finding. A cold publisher's odds of landing in a category's top decile are about 4.9%, against 16.2% for a publisher who already has a package with real usage. That is a 3.3× handicap, not a closed door: 54 of the 198 top-decile listings measured here (27%) were published by someone whose entire other portfolio is under 1,000 downloads a month.

Why this and not another measurement

The prior study's conclusion has a policy consequence: if publishing genuinely cannot create demand, then the free-listing approval channel-inventory.md asks the account owner for is worth nothing, and the portfolio should spend the ask on a referral path instead. That is a real decision, and it was resting on an untested inference from a concentration curve. A concentration curve says demand is unequal; it does not say what makes the difference.

This measurement discriminates. If cold publishers are common at the top, a listing can create its own demand and the prior conclusion is too pessimistic. If they are rare, the audience precedes the listing and no amount of listing work substitutes for one.

Method

Same three categories, same queries, same relevance and artifact filters, and the same leaf definition (dependents == 0) as free-listing-demand/measure_listing_demand.py — the leaf counts came out identical (606 / 972 / 403 against the prior run's 606 / 970 / 402, the two-package drift being ordinary registry movement), so the populations are comparable. Two things are added:

  • Maintainer capture. The prior run discarded the maintainers field the search API already returns. It is what makes this study possible.
  • Publisher portfolios. For each sampled listing, every maintainer is looked up with maintainer:<username> and their whole published portfolio retrieved with download counts. A publisher's audience is the largest monthly download count among their packages other than the sampled one. 984 distinct publishers were looked up.

Two strata per category: found (the top decile of leaves by monthly downloads) and baseline (a seeded random sample of up to 60 leaves from the remaining 90%). A cold publisher is one whose largest other package is under 1,000 downloads a month.

2. Inverted, this is the number a cold entrant should plan against

The strata above are conditioned the wrong way round for decisions — they say what a found listing looks like. Inverting them against the sampling weights (the found stratum is 10% of leaves, the baseline the other 90%) gives what this portfolio actually needs:

A cold listing is not hopeless — roughly one in twenty reaches the top decile of its category — but it is materially disadvantaged, and the disadvantage is worst in exactly the category this portfolio rates "build now."

3. The handicap tracks how organised the buyer is

The gradient across the three categories is the most useful part of this result, because it is a property this portfolio can select on rather than just accept:

  • SBOM/CRA (8.5× handicap) is dominated by organisations. Four of its top five leaf listings are @cdxgen/* and @appthreat/* packages sharing one publisher's 835k-a-month audience, and the single largest (@datadog/datadog-ci-plugin-sbom, which the prior study found holds 56.5% of category demand on its own) belongs to a publisher with a 46.6M-a-month package. Only 2.5% of the category's cold listings break through.
  • Accessibility evidence (2.6×) is the friendliest to a cold entrant, and it is also the category with the most individual maintainers rather than vendor scopes (39% scoped vs 58% for SBOM, 68% for MCP).

For a portfolio that is a cold publisher by definition, category choice is worth more than listing quality. The pipeline currently ranks cra-evidence-desk "build now" and eaa-remediation-pipeline "build now"; on this evidence the accessibility candidate is roughly 3× easier to get found in than the CRA one, which cuts against the CRA candidate's already-floor-level defensibility score of 4.

4. Cold winners exist, and they look like a category, not luck

The 54 cold-publisher listings in the found stratum are not noise. Examples: jasmine-axe (71k/mo, publisher has no other package at all), n8n-mcp (590k/mo, likewise), accented, react-native-accessibility-engine, rollup-plugin-sbom, sha1-hulud-scanner. What they share is that each is a narrow tool attached to something people already use — a test-framework adapter, a bundler plugin, an integration for a named product — rather than a general-purpose entry in the category. That is a referral path in the sense the prior study meant, but a cheap one: it borrows an existing tool's audience instead of an existing company's.

This is a hypothesis generated by the data, not tested by it. It is the cheapest thing left to test, and it is testable the same way this was.

What this changes

It softens, but does not overturn, the prior study's conclusion. "Publishing is not distribution" is right in the sense that a listing alone buys a ~5% shot; it is wrong if read as "a cold listing cannot get found," which is what "the scarce thing is a referral path, not a listing" implies if applied literally. The free-listing approval is worth something — about a third of what it would be worth to a publisher with an audience.

It does not license publishing anything. The account-owner approval channel-inventory.md asks for (whether an agent session may publish under this portfolio's name at all) is still not given, and is still not on issue #139's ask table. Nothing was published, no account was registered, no credential was used.

Channel reachability: a separate, harder blocker found in passing

check_channel_reachability.py tests whether the channels channel-inventory.md recommends can be reached from an agent session at all. They largely cannot:

Three consequences, none of which were on file:

  1. "Agent-listable" and "agent-reachable" are different properties. Of the four free channels channel-inventory.md names, an agent session here can reach one and a half. WordPress.org — the channel it maps both accessibility candidates to, and the only one whose buyer is a business — cannot be read, let alone published to.
  2. WordPress.org's per-plugin active_installs is a public API. The prior study recorded "none of them publishes a comparable per-listing usage figure through an API reachable from this environment" — correct as stated, but the cause is this environment's egress policy, not the channel. Allowlisting api.wordpress.org read-only would let the business-buyer channel be measured the same way npm just was. That is a cheaper owner ask than any currently on #139, and it is the same shape as Ask 3.
  3. cra-evidence-desk's wedge is untestable from here. Its write-up rests on EUVD matching being the differentiator; euvd.enisa.europa.eu and api.osv.dev are both denied, so no session can check whether EUVD carries anything OSV does not. Combined with finding 3 above, the CRA candidate is the pipeline's hardest to validate and its hardest to get found in.

Status of these claims

Primary measurements against a live API, not secondary-source research. The raw rows are committed and the scripts re-derive the numbers. What is uncertain is interpretation, and specifically:

  • Association, not precedence. Portfolio download counts are current, not as-of the sampled listing's first publish, so a maintainer whose big package came later counts as having had an audience. This bounds the cold-publisher share from above — the direction that matters, since even this generous measure finds cold publishers in the minority at the top.
  • npm only, and npm's buyer is a developer. The category where the buyer is a business (WordPress.org) is exactly the one egress-blocked above.
  • Small strata. The SBOM found stratum is n=40 and its 8.5× handicap is the least stable number here; the pooled 3.3× rests on n=198 / n=180.
  • Keyword-defined categories. A few incidental matches survive the relevance filter — ytconv in the SBOM found stratum is not an SBOM tool, and claude-cup, the largest MCP listing, is a contest tracker (the prior study flagged it too). Both are counted as cold publishers, which if anything inflates the cold share.
  • Downloads are not users, and users are not payers. Nothing here measures willingness to pay.

Nothing here adds a candidate, changes a score, creates a ventures/ directory, or spends anything.

Does the mcp-supply-chain-watch archive accrue? A measurement of its floor

Study · 2026-08-20 · portfolio/research/mcp-archive-defensibility/README.md

Measured 2026-08-20. Primary sources, reproducible from this repo, no credentials, nothing published, no spend.

This directory does not add a candidate and does not re-screen anything — the intake gate in CLAUDE.md applies and screening is closed. It advances the probe that is already specified in portfolio/pipeline/, for the one candidate whose data sources this environment can actually reach, by measuring the dimension that both prior steps explicitly left untouched.

The question, and why it is now the whole question

Steps 1 and 2 of the mcp-supply-chain-watch experiment have been run (see ../mcp-tool-drift/). Neither kill condition fired. Step 2 ended by moving the product's centre of gravity:

Archive is the durable value and the alert is the occasional event, not the other way round.

That relocates the entire proposition onto the candidate's floor dimension. Its scorecard reads:

defensibility: 4
mitigation: Only the retained per-customer pin history accrues; the diffing
            mechanism is assumed copyable and is not the sale.

and the step-1 write-up closes: "the pinned per-customer history is still the only accruing asset."

Both are assertions, and ../../pipeline/README.md rule 2 requires a named mitigation for any dimension at 4 or below before graduation. The named mitigation is the archive. So the candidate now rests on a claim nobody has tested:

Is the pinned declaration history a scarce asset that accrues with time — or is it public data that any entrant can reconstruct in full on their first day?

Answer

Both, on disjoint halves of the market — and the portfolio can only build the half with no moat.

Three headline numbers behind that:

  1. The public half hands a cold entrant the incumbent's archive. Across 40 npm-distributed servers, public registries retain a median of 13 versions spanning 55 days per package (max 344 versions / 293 days), and only 1.7% of published versions (18 of 1,059) have ever been unpublished — the only slice a live pin captures and nobody can rebuild afterwards. On this half of the market, an archive that took an incumbent months to accrue is a download.
  2. The download takes minutes. Rebuilding complete declaration archives using this repo's own already-published extractor (measure_drift.extract_tools) ran at 0.39 s and 0.18 MB per version — 673 server-days of declaration history recovered in 25 seconds, a rate of roughly 95,000 server-days per wall hour on one machine with no credentials. Extrapolated over all 214 npm-distributed servers in the registry at the observed median depth: ≈2,800 version fetches, ≈18 minutes, ≈490 MB. The entire assumed-defensible asset, for the entire reachable market, is an afternoon coffee's worth of bandwidth.
  3. The half where the pin genuinely is the only record is the half this environment cannot touch. All 8 sampled remote endpoints returned proxy-denied at the egress proxy — the same signature #232 established is a general non-registry-egress policy, and the same wall #240 measured for runtime input data. There is no allowlist of finite length that fixes it: the endpoints are 1,453 distinct third-party hosts.

So the mitigation on file is not wrong — it is true of 84.5% of the market and false of the 14.2% the portfolio can build against. And every piece of evidence the candidate has accumulated so far (the 69% drift rate, the 152 classified changes, the 7.2% instruction-bearing share) was measured on the 14.2%, because that is the only half that is measurable from here.

What this changes

It does not kill the candidate. It relocates it. The read is:

  • The product that can be built from this environment — watch npm/PyPI-packaged MCP servers — is a convenience business, not a moat business. Customers will not rebuild 490 MB of tarballs themselves, and that is a real service; but any competitor who decides to, is at parity in eighteen minutes, and a buyer who cancels loses nothing they cannot re-fetch. Under ../../pipeline/README.md's own scoring language ("*10 means: a copy of the product does not take the customers*"), that is a defensibility of 3, not 4 — the copy takes the customers and the history.
  • The product with the moat — watch the 84.5% that lives behind remote endpoints — has a genuine time-accruing asset, because nothing else in the ecosystem is recording those declarations. It is also, as currently framed, unbuildable here.

The named mitigation this measurement produces

Rule 2 wants a named mitigation rather than a rescore, so here is one the data supports, and it is not "ask for more egress":

Ship the collector to the customer; keep the archive.

The polling that reaches a remote MCP endpoint does not have to run on the vendor's infrastructure. Shipped as something the customer runs inside their own network — an npm/PyPI package, a scheduled GitHub Action, a container in their cluster — the collector uses their egress and their approved connections, and posts back only the declaration snapshots. The vendor stores, diffs, classifies and retains.

This is not a workaround dressed up as strategy; it is better on four independent axes, three of which are on the scorecard:

The fourth row is the one that matters operationally: this shape converts the candidate from "blocked on egress the owner cannot reasonably grant" into "blocked on Ask 5 only" — the free-listing publish approval that channel-inventory.md already says costs nothing and is independent of the payout rail.

It does not remove the buyer question. Step 3 — will two of ten teams pay — is unchanged, still the deciding gate, and still not runnable by an unattended agent.

Honest limits

Every limit of ../mcp-tool-drift/ still applies (static extraction, best-effort regexes, npm-packaged servers only for the drift rates). Four more are specific to this measurement, and the first cuts hardest against the conclusion above:

  • **84.5% is a share of listings, not of deployments. The registry is weighted by who bothers to list, and hosted vendors have every incentive to — 213 of the remote entries point at a single host (server.smithery.ai). Weighted by what companies actually connect to their agents, the package-distributed share is very likely much higher than 14.2%: the widely used @modelcontextprotocol/* servers are npm packages. If enterprise deployment is mostly package-distributed, then the no-moat half is the commercially relevant half, and the finding gets worse for the candidate, not better.** Nothing here measures deployment share, and no reachable source does.
  • 1.7% unpublished is a floor on what is unrecoverable, measured on 40 packages. It is also concentrated: 2 of 40 packages account for all 18 unpublished versions, and one (@fouradata/mcp) accounts for 17. A different sample could move this number materially; it should not be quoted as a population rate.
  • The copy-cost timing is a single machine on one network, and 2 of 6 packages yielded no extractable tools at all (bundled/minified — the known static-extraction failure). Rebuilding an archive that is useful is therefore slower than rebuilding one that is complete. The 18-minute extrapolation is a lower bound on the effort and an upper bound on the moat.
  • Defensibility is not only the archive. Switching cost, alert routing, integration, and an attested approval-time snapshot are all plausible moats this measurement says nothing about. What it establishes is narrower and sufficient for rule 2: the specific mitigation currently written on the scorecard does not hold for the half of the market the portfolio can build against.

Reproducing

Standard library only, no dependencies, no credentials, read-only against public APIs. No MCP server is contacted for its tool list; the reachability control issues a plain GET and records only whether the proxy allowed it.

python3 portfolio/research/mcp-archive-defensibility/measure_archive_defensibility.py \
    --pages 45 --retention-limit 40 --copy-limit 6 --out results.json

# re-derive the headline numbers from the committed corpus, fully offline:
python3 portfolio/research/mcp-archive-defensibility/measure_archive_defensibility.py \
    --recompute-from portfolio/research/mcp-archive-defensibility/results-2026-08-20.json

Raw rows, per package and per phase: results-2026-08-20.json.

Re-run before acting on any of it. The population split is the volatile number — if the registry's package share rises materially, finding 1 gets worse for the vendor-side shape and the customer-side collector gets more attractive, not less.

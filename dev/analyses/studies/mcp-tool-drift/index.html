MCP tool-declaration drift: a measured base rate

Study · 2026-08-22 · portfolio/research/mcp-tool-drift/README.md

Measured 2026-08-19. Primary sources, reproducible from this repo.

This directory answers one question with data instead of prose:

Do third-party MCP servers actually change the tools they declare, often enough that monitoring the change is worth paying for?

Answer: yes, and not marginally. Across sampled adjacent releases of MCP servers published to npm, 69% changed their declared tool surface, 29% removed a tool outright, and at least 33% changed a tool description — the class that matters most, and the one this method under-counts.

Why this measurement and not another

The candidate pipeline proposed in PR #79 (portfolio/pipeline/) contains eight scored business candidates. Two prior firings of the screening task established that six of the eight rest on regulatory claims that cannot be verified from this environment at all — eur-lex.europa.eu, www.ftc.gov, www.w3.org, shopify.dev and six other candidate-critical domains are blocked at the egress proxy, and unlike GitHub's and WordPress's documentation, EU legislation is not generated from a public source repository, so there is no way around it.

mcp-supply-chain-watch is one of only two candidates whose thesis does not depend on regulatory timing, and the only one whose primary data sources are reachable. It is therefore the single candidate in the pipeline that can be de-risked without anyone unblocking anything. That is why it was chosen.

The candidate's own write-up specifies the test and the kill condition:

Poll a set of publicly listed MCP servers on a daily interval and measure how many change a tool description at all over two weeks. If the base rate is near zero, there is no product — that is the cheapest possible kill.

It also names the most likely ending:

Registry commoditisation. If the major MCP registries or an agent framework ship signed, versioned tool manifests, the mutable-declaration problem is fixed upstream and this candidate is obsolete rather than competed with. That is the most likely ending and it should be checked before building, not after.

Both were run. Neither killed it.

Finding 1 — the gap is not closed upstream

check_spec_integrity.py, against the spec's own normative JSON Schema:

Spec revisionFields on ToolIntegrity/version fieldIntegrity terms anywhere in schema
2025-03-26annotations, description, inputSchema, nameNONENONE
2025-06-18+ _meta, outputSchema, titleNONENONE
2025-11-25+ execution, iconsNONENONE
draft+ iconsNONENONE

No revision — including the current draft — gives a Tool a version, hash, signature, digest, or any other attribute a client could pin. Zero occurrences of signature, signing, integrity, checksum, sha256, provenance, attestation, lockfile, or immutable anywhere in any schema.

And the official registry does not fill the gap either. Across 4,000 version records covering 1,548 distinct servers:

  • inputSchema occurs 0 times. Registry records carry name, description, title, version, repository, icons, websiteUrl, packages, remotes — and no tool declarations at all.
  • 85% of records point at a remote endpoint (3,387 of 4,000). For those, the registry stores a URL. Whatever that URL serves on the day a client connects is entirely unconstrained by anything recorded at publication.
  • 31.6% of servers have published more than one version record, so servers demonstrably do change; only 85 of 4,000 records were mutated in place, which means change arrives as a new version rather than as an edit — and nothing ties a customer's approval to a particular one.

The registry indexes servers. The trust decision is made against a tool list. Those are different objects, and no artifact in the ecosystem currently connects them.

Finding 2 — the drift base rate

measure_drift.py diffs the tool declarations npm already holds across a package's published versions, so the two-week forward poll the candidate proposed is answerable today instead.

Consecutive releases — "I am pinned to version N and upgrade to N+1; how often does the tool surface move under me?" This is the number the product thesis rests on. 49 usable version-pairs across 7 servers:

What this does and does not establish

Established. Tool surfaces move, frequently, including in the backwards-incompatible direction; the protocol offers no way to pin them; the registry does not record them. The candidate's cheapest kill test does not fire, and its most-likely-ending check comes back negative. On the evidence, the technical premise holds.

Not established, and load-bearing for the business. The candidate's own write-up names buyer ambiguity as "the failure mode to test first" — security owns the risk, engineering owns the integration, neither reliably owns the budget. Nothing here tests that. A real drift rate is necessary for the product to make sense and nowhere near sufficient for it to sell. Steps 2 and 3 of the candidate's experiment — hand-labelling changes as cosmetic / schema-widening / instruction-bearing, and taking the rate to ten teams at $29/server/month — remain undone, and step 3 cannot be done by an unattended agent at all.

Also unaddressed: this measures npm packages, where a version boundary makes drift observable. The 85% of the registry that is remote endpoints is where the security argument is strongest and where drift is least observable — which is the product's reason to exist, but also means this measurement is from the easier half of the market.

Honest limits of the method

Tool declarations are extracted statically, with regexes over shipped JS/TS, because running an untrusted MCP server to ask it tools/list is precisely the thing not to do on a research box. Consequences, all of which push the reported rates down:

  • A package that adopts a bundler or minifier can stop matching entirely. That shows up as "every tool removed at once"; those pairs are flagged wipe and excluded from every rate above (3 of the 32 analysable packages in lifetime mode). They are measurement artifacts, not removals.
  • Description changes are under-counted. Only descriptions written adjacent to their tool name are recovered. The true description-drift rate is at least the reported one and likely higher — and since an instruction-bearing description change is the alert the product is actually selling, this is the gap most worth closing next.
  • Packages where nothing extracted from either version are reported no-tools-extracted and excluded rather than counted as "no change" (13 of 45 in lifetime mode).

So treat these as a floor, and as directional evidence for a build/kill decision. They are not customer-facing claims and should not appear in copy.

Reproducing

Standard library only, no dependencies, no credentials, read-only against public APIs:

python3 portfolio/research/mcp-tool-drift/check_spec_integrity.py
python3 portfolio/research/mcp-tool-drift/measure_drift.py --mode consecutive --limit 10
python3 portfolio/research/mcp-tool-drift/measure_drift.py --mode lifetime --limit 45

Re-run check_spec_integrity.py before acting on the candidate. If a future spec revision adds an integrity field to Tool, or registry records start carrying inputSchema, the gap has closed upstream and this candidate should be killed rather than built — the script prints exactly that.

Status

This is research, not a venture. No ventures/ directory and no VENTURE.yaml is created here, so the router allocates nothing to it; graduation remains a decision for the account owner, per portfolio/pipeline/README.md.

Nothing here contradicts the candidate's existing score. Its defensibility: 4 floor is untouched by this work and remains the thing most likely to decide the outcome: the mechanism is still reproducible by anyone who decides to, and the pinned per-customer history is still the only accruing asset.

Step 2: is the drift worth alerting on?

Measured 2026-08-19, same day, by a later scheduled run. Everything above is step 1 of the candidate's experiment and is unchanged. This section is step 2, which that experiment specifies and which was still undone:

"Hand-label the changes that do occur as cosmetic, schema-widening, or instruction-bearing, and check whether a model reproduces the labels."

Step 2 is the decisive one, because it tests the failure mode the candidate itself names:

"Alert fatigue. Servers under active development change constantly. If the classifier cannot keep routine churn out of the instruction-bearing bucket, the alerts get muted and the product is dead."

A 69% drift rate is a business only if a small, stable slice of it deserves a human's attention. This section measures where in that range reality sits.

Harness: classify_drift.py. Corpus: results-2026-08-19-classified.json — 152 description changes with the full before/after text of each, so every label below can be re-derived, disputed, or re-labelled offline:

python3 classify_drift.py --recompute-from results-2026-08-19-classified.json

Finding 3 — the drift is overwhelmingly cosmetic, and rules can tell

152 description changes, across 19 packages, from 270 sampled adjacent version pairs (173 of which shared at least one tool between versions, which is the denominator a description change can come from at all):

cosmetic — wording moved, meaning did not11978.3%
capability-change — the described action itself changed138.6%

The data forced it: "Get status of an uploaded media file" becoming "Get detailed information about a specific media file … including its type, dimensions, file size, upload status" is neither cosmetic nor instruction-bearing, and it has a different response (re-read the tool) than either.

Finding 4 — the risk is the opposite of the one the candidate feared

Converting the shares above into the number that actually decides the product — alerts per monitored server per month — across the 10 packages whose sampled history spans 30 days or more:

median0.00
mean0.11

A customer monitoring twenty connected servers would see roughly two alerts a month, and the median individual server would go a month producing none.

Alert fatigue does not kill this candidate. The failure mode named in the write-up as the thing most likely to end it is not present in the data at any plausible portfolio size.

The inverted risk is the one now on the table, and it is a pricing problem rather than a technical one: at $19–$49 per server per month, a customer is paying per server for something that, per server, is usually silent. The candidate's own framing — "Pin · Diff · Classify · Archive" — already contains the answer, and this measurement reorders it. Archive is the durable value and the alert is the occasional event, not the other way round. What a customer holds after a silent month is a retained, exportable record of what every connected server declared when it was approved, which is the artifact a security review actually asks for. Priced per server, silence reads as nothing delivered; priced per portfolio as a monitored-and-evidenced record, silence reads as the product working. Nothing here says which price clears — that is step 3, and step 3 still cannot be done by an unattended agent.

Finding 5 — the classification is not model work

The candidate scores AI leverage 7, justified as: "Classifying whether a tool-description change is benign or instruction-bearing is model work; the diffing underneath is not."

On this corpus that is not what happened. Deterministic regex rules over the newly added spans separate 119 cosmetic changes from 11 instruction-bearing ones with no model in the loop, no API key, and no per-change inference cost. The signals that fire are shallow and stable: cross-tool references, imperative and priority phrasing, credential vocabulary, embedded URLs, exfiltration-shaped verbs.

Two consequences, in opposite directions:

  • Against the candidate. AI leverage 7 looks overstated. If the classifier is rules, the product is a scheduled fetcher plus a diff plus a regex pass — which is cheaper to run than scored (good for gross margin, already 9) and cheaper to copy (bad for defensibility, already the floor at 4). This is evidence that the floor dimension is worse than scored, not better.
  • For the candidate. Per-change inference cost was a real objection to monitoring hundreds of servers per customer. It is now measured at zero for the common path, with a model needed only for the ~7% a rule pass promotes.

Where the first classifier was wrong

Step 2 asks whether a model reproduces the hand-labels. It was run the other way round — rules first, then hand-labelled by reading all 152 before/after pairs — because that is the direction that produces a correction rather than a score.

The first pass flagged 9 changes as instruction-bearing. Reading all 152 before/after pairs by hand disputed 4 of those 9, and all four failed the same way: signals were matched against whole sentences judged new, so rewording a sentence that already mentioned an API key re-flagged it. @bolthub/mcp's mint_scoped_token was flagged for changing "prepaid bundle" to "multi-use credential" — the word "credential" was in both versions.

The same pass also produced a false negative: social_api_revoke_key gained "Use social_api_list_keys to find the key_id", a textbook cross-tool routing instruction, and was flagged only incidentally, by the credential rule.

Three fixes, all visible in classify_drift.py:

  1. A signal fires only if the matched span itself is absent from the old description, not merely inside a sentence that changed.
  2. use <other_tool_name> to … was added to the routing signal.
  3. A capability verb preceded by an article or adjective is read as a noun — "a ranked list of results" is not a new list action.

Fix 1 is the mechanism, and its effect alone is reproducible from the committed corpus with no re-fetching. --lenient restores sentence-granularity matching while keeping the corrected patterns, so the pair below isolates that one change:

python3 classify_drift.py --recompute-from results-2026-08-19-classified.json --lenient
python3 classify_drift.py --recompute-from results-2026-08-19-classified.json
sentence granularityspan-must-be-new
instruction-bearing13 (8.6%)11 (7.2%)

The two it drops are exactly mint_scoped_token and social_api_create_key — two of the four hand-disputed flags, removed by mechanism rather than by a rule written against them.

The other two survive, and the honest reading is that they are a severity problem, not a detection one. social_api_create_webhook and social_api_exchange_oauth newly introduce the words "secret" and "token"; the words really are new, so the rule is right that something credential-shaped entered a model-visible description, and reading them shows ordinary documentation expansion. There is no text rule that separates those cases. That is the unmeasured severity axis listed under "what this does not establish", and it is the part of the classifier a model would plausibly earn its cost on.

The corrected classifier was tuned on the corpus it is scored against, so 7.2% is in-sample and will not hold unchanged on fresh data. What is not in-sample is finding 4: even at the loosest setting tried the alert volume is about half an alert per server-month, and the untuned first pass put it lower still. No tuning choice available here moves it into fatigue territory.

Finding 6 — the description under-counting is partly closed

measure_drift.py's README named this as "the gap most worth closing next": descriptions were only recovered when written immediately adjacent to their tool name. classify_drift.py adds four more declaration shapes — description-before -name, template literals, registerTool("x", { … }) with the description behind the options brace, and the positional .tool("x", "y") form.

Run head-to-head on the same twelve tarballs:

descriptions recovered
measure_drift.py187

The aggregate understates it. Two packages went from 0 recovered to 10 and 15 — entirely invisible to the first extractor, fully readable by the second. The remaining gap is minified and bundled packages, where nothing static will work.

What step 2 does and does not establish

Established. Ordinary MCP servers do emit model-directed text in tool descriptions, at a measurable and low rate; the churn around it is ~78% cosmetic; and separating the two is mechanical rather than model work. The alert-fatigue failure mode does not fire.

Not established. Every limit in the step 1 section still applies — static extraction, npm-packaged servers only, and the 85% of the registry that is remote endpoints remains the half where drift is least observable and the security argument strongest. Additionally:

  • No malicious sample. Every flagged change here is benign. Nothing measures whether the same rules catch a deliberately hostile description, and a motivated attacker writing around a published regex is the obvious next objection. Do not read finding 3 as a detection rate.
  • Severity is a second axis and is unmeasured. Of the 11 alerts, 4 are routine batching advice from a note-taking server. A customer wants those ranked below a new credential mention, and nothing here ranks them.
  • Step 3 is still undone and still not doable here. "Take the observed base rate to ten teams and ask for $29/server/month" needs contact with buyers. Finding 4 changes what to ask them — price the archive, not the alert — but it does not answer it.

Amendment, 2026-08-22: two of this classifier's signal alternatives are dead

Found by portfolio/pipeline/probes/mcp-drift-watch/, which imports classify_drift.classify unmodified and scored it against two further corpora. Recorded here rather than only there, because the defect is in this file's code and the figures above are this file's.

INSTRUCTION_SIGNALS writes each signal as one alternation wrapped in word boundaries — \b( … | important: | … | \.env\b | … )\b. A \b cannot close after a colon that is followed by a space, and cannot open before a dot that is preceded by one. So:

  • important: has never matched ordinary prose. IMPORTANT: Always report the progress percentage to the user is read as cosmetic. Two such blocks appear in the third corpus, both genuine directives to the model.
  • \.env has never matched anything, in any corpus.

Neither is a judgement call about what counts as instruction-bearing; both are patterns that cannot fire. Nothing above is withdrawn — the committed classification stands as the output of the rules as written — but read finding 3's 7.2% as a floor for those two shapes. Relabelling this corpus with the three edits that probe measured (drop secret_or_credential, widen routing to a preference toward a named tool, relax the important separator) puts it at 6.6%, and repairing the important alternative alone puts it at 7.9%.

The rules here were deliberately not changed. The probe's write-up gives the reason and the condition under which they should be: a rule for the class that carries 35 of 44 missed positives out of sample (use x_y for …, hosted users should call x_y), measured on a fourth corpus.

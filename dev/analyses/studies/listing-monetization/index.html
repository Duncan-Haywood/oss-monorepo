Winning free-registry discovery does not come with a way to get paid

Study · 2026-08-19 · portfolio/research/listing-monetization/README.md

Measured 2026-08-19 against the public npm registry. Read-only, no credentials, reproducible from this repo.

This directory measures the term every previous study in this chain had to assume. It adds no candidate and changes no score.

The gap this closes

Four measurements now describe what a cold free listing can attract:

Finding
free-listing-demand/The median leaf listing in these categories gets 68–2,399 downloads/month
#218A cold publisher's odds of a top-decile listing are 4.9%, vs 16.2% for a publisher with an existing audience
#219Central-case revenue for a cold free listing is $16–$145/month — computed as that download distribution × an assumed ~2% conversion rate, which #219 names as the weakest input in its own model
#225The concentration replicates on Packagist and RubyGems; changing registry does not improve the discovery term

Every one of those measures the entrance to the funnel — how much attention a listing can attract. None measures the exit. The ~2% conversion assumption has never been checked against the population that would have to convert, and two live account-owner asks (#139's Ask 5 and Ask 7) are justified by revenue figures that rest entirely on it.

So this study asks the question from the other end. Rather than model what a winning listing would earn, it looks at the listings that already won:

Among the listings that reached the top decile of downloads in these categories — the outcome a cold publisher has a ~4.9% chance of reaching — how many have any mechanism to receive money at all, and how many have a product mechanism rather than a donation button?

Method

The population is built by importing measure_listing_demand.py — the same CATEGORIES, queries, relevance filter, artifact exclusion, and leaf (zero-dependents) definition as the download study. The two cannot drift apart, and any correction to the population definition applies to both. Same discipline #225 used for the cross-registry replication.

Each category is split at its own top-decile download threshold. Winners are every listing at or above it. Control is a deterministic even spread across the listings below it — spread rather than top-N, so the comparison group is not just the near-winners. Each sampled package's registry metadata and published README are read and classified:

  • commercial — a declared price, paid tier, licence key, enterprise edition, pricing page, or a licence that only makes sense as one half of a dual-licence sale (AGPL/SSPL/BUSL/Elastic/SEE LICENSE IN/UNLICENSED).
  • donation — GitHub Sponsors, Open Collective, Ko-fi, Patreon, Tidelift, Liberapay, thanks.dev, Buy Me a Coffee, or any declared funding field. Money may arrive; it is a gift, not a price.
  • none — no such signal.

commercial dominates donation dominates none, so the commercial share is the share with any product-revenue route.

The two confidence intervals overlap across most of their range. The correct reading is not "winners monetise less" — the counts are 15 and 18, far too small for that — it is that there is no detectable difference at all. Reaching the top decile of downloads does not raise the probability that a listing has a way to charge. Discovery and revenue are decoupled on this channel.

Finding 2 — the monetised winners are companies that arrived with an audience

This is sharper than the percentages, and it is what actually bears on the portfolio's decision. Of the 15 commercial-route winners, roughly eleven are listings published by an organisation that already had its own distribution and its own product, with the package acting as a client or connector to it:

@alipay/mcp-server-alipay · @microsoft/github-copilot-app-modernization · @mui/mcp · slack-mcp-server · n8n-mcp · storybook-mcp-server · @aikidosec/mcp · @ironbee-ai/devtools · ato-mcp · devexpress-reporting-react · cbrowser

For these, the "free tier" or "enterprise edition" the classifier detected is the company's product. The listing is marketing for a business that already had customers; it is not itself the revenue.

Meanwhile the listings that look like what this portfolio would actually publish — an independent tool with a per-seat price and no prior audience — sit in the invisible tail, priced and unfound:

This is #218's audience handicap (4.9% cold vs 16.2% with an audience) showing up a second time, in the revenue dimension rather than the discovery dimension. Having something to sell does not get a listing found, and getting found is concentrated among publishers who did not need the channel to begin with.

Stated as the judgement it is: "published by a company with its own distribution" is a hand reading of fifteen package names, not a measured field. It is recorded here as a pattern worth checking, not as a statistic.

Finding 3 — every observed price is charged somewhere other than the registry

Of the 33 commercial-route listings across both groups:

Mechanism (mutually exclusive, most specific wins)Count
A named merchant of record (Paddle/Stripe/Gumroad/Lemon Squeezy/Polar)1
Some other off-registry pricing or checkout URL5
Prose price or tier mention with no linked checkout at all16
Licence signal only (dual-licence, EULA, SEE LICENSE IN) with no visible price11

Not one charges through the registry, because npm has no billing rail — which channel-inventory.md already asserted, and which this observes directly. Every listing that takes money does so by sending the reader somewhere else.

That has a direct consequence for the ask ordering on #139: converting attention into revenue requires a payout rail (Ask 2) regardless of which channel the attention came from. Ask 5 and Ask 7 both buy discovery, and discovery is not the step that is missing.

What this means for the open asks

Recorded as an argument, not a decision — the ranking on #139 is the account owner's.

  1. "Cheapest first" and "load-bearing" are different orderings, and #139 currently uses the first. Ask 7 (one WordPress.org egress entry) is the cheapest item on that list and buys a better measurement of discovery. Ask 5 buys permission to obtain discovery. On this evidence neither produces revenue on its own, because 92.5% of the listings that already won discovery have no price and 100% of those that do charge off-registry.
  2. This does not refute #219, and the two together are worse news than either. #219 argued Ask 2 (merchant-of-record account) is hard to justify because central-case revenue on npm/PyPI is $16–$145/month. This study finds that without Ask 2 the revenue is structurally zero, not merely small. Both hold at once, and what they jointly say is that the free-developer-registry route does not have a revenue path for this portfolio at any ask on the list — not that a different ask would unlock one.
  3. The implication is to stop buying measurements of this channel. Five studies (#166, #218, #219, #225, this one) have now measured the same funnel from both ends and converged. A sixth would be the failure mode CLAUDE.md documents, one level up from the candidate lists.
  4. The candidate scores are untouched. No dimension changes here. The distribution scores in portfolio/pipeline/ were assigned against channels including gated ones (Shopify, GitHub Marketplace paid) that this study says nothing about, and that remain the more plausible route precisely because they have billing rails attached.

Honest limits

  • A declared route is not revenue. This measures whether a listing can take money, which is an upper bound on whether it does. The true share earning anything is lower, in both groups.
  • The absence of a signal is not the absence of a business. A package may monetise through a repo, a website, or a sales conversation the README never mentions. This biases the reported commercial share down in both groups equally, which is why the winners-vs-control comparison is the load-bearing result and the absolute level is not.
  • README matching is heuristic in both directions. One false positive survived into the results and is left visible rather than hand-removed: accessibility-scanner-cli matched on https://example.com/pricing, a placeholder in an unfinished README. A badge-URL false positive (shields.io/badge/buy) was caught in the smoke run and is now filtered — see BADGE_HOSTS. Sponsor acknowledgements ("sponsored by …") are stripped before matching so that crediting someone else's product is not read as having a price.
  • funding is under-declared on npm generally, so the donation share is a floor.
  • Small counts. 15 commercial winners and 18 commercial controls. The confidence intervals are wide and are reported for exactly that reason. The claim defended here is the null one — no detectable difference — which small samples support poorly in the other direction but adequately in this one, in the sense that no large effect could hide at this n.
  • npm only, one snapshot. #225 established that the discovery term does not improve on Packagist or RubyGems; whether the monetisation term differs there is untested. Every non-developer channel in channel-inventory.md (WordPress.org, Open VSX, VS Marketplace, Chrome Web Store, addons.mozilla.org) was re-confirmed egress-blocked from this environment during this session, so the business-buyer regime could not be measured by any route.
  • Categories are keyword-defined, inherited from the download study with the same imperfection.

These are directional evidence for a build/kill decision. They are not customer-facing claims and should not appear in any copy.

Reproducing

Standard library only, no dependencies, no credentials, read-only against the public registry. Full run is roughly 25 minutes at the pacing set in the script.

python3 portfolio/research/listing-monetization/measure_listing_monetization.py \
    --out results-2026-08-19.json
python3 portfolio/research/listing-monetization/measure_listing_monetization.py \
    --categories mcp-supply-chain

Raw rows, including every classification and the evidence behind it: results-2026-08-19.json.

Resolution was near-total: 2 of 379 sampled packages failed to resolve (both in the MCP winners group), and READMEs were available for 344 of the 377 that did.

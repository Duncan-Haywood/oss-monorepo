How much to charge for a dog walk

Every answer to this question online is a range: fifteen to thirty dollars for a half-hour walk, more in cities. The range is not wrong, and it is not usable, because it hides the variable that actually decides whether your day pays — and that variable is not the walk.

A 30-minute walk is 40 minutes of your time

The walk is what the client books. The job is the walk plus getting there. At a 10-minute drive, a 30-minute visit commits 40 minutes, so a quarter of the time you are being paid for is spent in the car — before mileage, which is a separate cost on top.

That last row is the one worth sitting with. The visit earns $33.52 per hour of walking — a good-looking number — and $22.34 for two thirds of an hour of your actual life. Both are true. Only the second one fills a calendar.

The route-density trap

Now change one input and nothing else. Same walk, same dog, same rate, same margin — only the client's distance moves:

Drive each wayCommittedMileageCostPrice
5 minutes (2 miles)35 min$1.34$16.09$18.50

20 minutes (9 miles)50 min$6.03$26.78$30.80

The same 30-minute walk is a $18.50 job and a $30.80 job. One flat rate across a scattered client book means your close clients are quietly subsidising your far ones — and the far ones are why the week feels full while the month does not pay. Route density is worth more than your headline rate, and it is the one lever a marketplace listing cannot pull for you.

Three ways to handle it, in rough order of how well they hold up. A distance-based fee names the cost the client is actually creating and leaves nearby clients on a competitive rate. A service radius declines the work rather than pricing it, which is often right for a one-visit-a-week client forty minutes out. Raising the base rate for everyone to cover the worst commute is the common choice and the weakest one: it overprices the neighbours who book five times a week and refer the rest of the street.

What a target income actually costs in visits

Pricing one visit well is half the question. The other half is how many of them a target income needs. A book of four recurring clients — two at five visits a week, one at three, one at two — is 65 visits a month at an average price of $23.09:

Sixty-five more visits a month is fifteen a week — and, not coincidentally, it is the book over again. Doubling a client list is a different kind of plan from "earn another $1,500 a month", and it is the honest shape of that target: roughly three more daily clients, or two more plus a weekend block. That is a concrete thing to go and do. The revenue gap on its own is not, and it is the form the number usually arrives in.

Multi-dog households

A second dog in the same house adds handling effort and nothing else: no extra drive, no extra stop, no extra travel time. Charging a second full visit price for it is the fastest way to lose a household that would otherwise be one of your densest, most reliable clients. A per-additional-pet surcharge on top of the base visit reflects the real extra cost, which is why it is its own input rather than a multiplier.

Common questions

How much should I charge for a 30-minute dog walk?

There is no single answer, because the walk is not the whole job. Price the time you commit, which is the walk plus the drive to reach it, then add mileage, your share of monthly overhead, and a margin. At a 24 dollar an hour target rate, a 30-minute walk with a 10-minute drive and 4 miles of travel costs 19.43 dollars to deliver and prices at 22.34 dollars with a 15 percent margin. The same walk for a client five minutes away prices at 18.50 dollars, and for one twenty minutes away at 30.80 dollars.

Why does the same dog walk cost different amounts for different clients?

Because drive time is part of the job and it is not the same for every client. A 30-minute walk with a 10-minute drive is 40 minutes of committed time, so a quarter of what you are being paid for is spent in the car. Doubling the drive to 20 minutes makes it 50 minutes and adds mileage on top. Charging one flat rate across a scattered client book means the close clients subsidise the far ones, and the far ones are the reason the day feels full and the month does not pay.

Should I charge a travel fee or raise my base rate?

A distance-based fee is usually the fairer and more defensible of the two, because it names the cost the client is actually creating and leaves your close clients paying a competitive rate. Raising the base rate across the book to cover the worst commute overprices everyone near you, which is exactly the segment most likely to book several visits a week and refer neighbours. The alternative to either is a service radius: decline what falls outside it rather than pricing for it.

What is route density and why does it matter more than my rate?

Route density is how much drive time separates consecutive visits. It matters more than your headline rate because it changes how many visits fit in a day and how much of each visit you are paid for driving. Four clients within five minutes of each other can be served in a block where drive time nearly vanishes between them; four clients spread across a city cannot, whatever you charge. Tightening the geography of the book raises earnings without raising a single price, which is the one lever a marketplace listing cannot pull for you.

How many dog walking visits do I need for a target monthly income?

Divide the gap between your current route revenue and your target by your average visit price. A book of four recurring clients doing 65 visits a month at an average of 23.09 dollars brings in 1,501.15 dollars. Reaching 3,000 dollars a month means closing a gap of 1,498.85 dollars, which at that average price is 65 more visits a month, or 15 more visits a week. Seeing the visit count rather than the revenue gap is what makes the target concrete, because visits are what has to fit in a calendar.

Does an extra dog in the same household double the price?

No, and pricing it that way loses bookings. A second dog in the same house adds handling effort but no extra drive, no extra travel time, and no extra stop, so a per-additional-pet surcharge on top of the base visit reflects the real extra cost far better than a second full visit price does. The surcharge is a separate input for exactly that reason: it lets a multi-dog household stay good value to the client and still pay you for the extra work.

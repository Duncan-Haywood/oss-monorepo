This checker vs. looking up each state's statute by hand

The usual way to answer "do I need consent to record this call?" is to search "is [state] one-party or two-party consent," read whichever page ranks first, and move on. That answers the single-state question well enough most of the time. It does not answer the question that actually matters for a remote sales, support, or recruiting team: a caller in one state and a recipient in another, which needs a combining rule and a citation to check, not two separate searches and a guess.

What each one is for

A one-off search is free and needs no tool: type the state name, read the first confident-sounding answer. It works fine when both parties are obviously in the same state and the page you land on happens to be right.

This checker looks up both states at once, applies a stated, cited combining rule for the interstate case (all-party consent whenever either state is one of the fourteen that requires it), names the precedent behind that conservative reading, and generates the actual words to say on the call — all from one fixed table rather than several searches under time pressure.

Common questions

Isn't searching "is [state] one-party or two-party consent" enough?

It answers the single-state question, and plenty of free pages already answer it correctly. What it does not answer on its own is the interstate case — a caller in one state and a recipient in another — which needs a combining rule, not just two separate lookups, and which is exactly where the conservative all-party guidance and its supporting precedent matter.

What does a one-off search not give you that this tool does?

Three things bundled together: the combined requirement for a specific pair of states in one step, the exact statute citation to check against the current text, and a ready-to-read disclosure script for the call opening — rather than three separate searches and a script you write yourself under time pressure.

Why is stopping at the first search result risky here?

Because several states carry a real, disclosed ambiguity — Nevada's statute reads as one-party but its Supreme Court has applied it as all-party; Michigan's courts have gone different ways depending on who is recording. A page that states a flat answer with no caveat may simply not mention the ambiguity, and a reader has no way to tell confident from correct without checking a second source.

Does this replace reading the actual statute?

No, and it says so on every page. It points you at the exact statute citation for the fourteen all-party states so the next step is reading that specific section, not a general search that may surface an outdated summary.

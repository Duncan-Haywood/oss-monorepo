    Questions vending operators ask

    How many coils should each vending machine product get?

    Give coils where an extra coil earns the most. Each product's profit rises with its coils but with diminishing returns: the first coil catches most of the demand, later ones only cover busy weeks. The tool computes the exact best split of your coils from each product's price, cost, weekly sales and units per coil, so a fast mover gets more coils and a slow, high-margin item gets just enough.

    Why do vending machines sell out of the best sellers first?

    Coils are usually split evenly by habit, but demand is not. A product selling 70 a week on 4 coils of 10 sells out in four days, while a product selling 6 a week sits for months. Moving coils from the slow item to the fast one turns missed sales into profit with no extra visits.

    How often should I restock a vending machine?

    Enter what a restocking visit costs you (drive time, fuel, labour). The tool re-optimises the coils for restock gaps from 2 to 14 days and shows weekly profit after visit costs for each, so you can see whether visiting less often loses more in sell-outs than it saves in trips.

    What about customers who switch when their pick is sold out?

    Set the substitution share, the portion of a sold-out product's unmet demand that buys something else in stock. The recommended layout is re-measured against your current one on thousands of identical simulated restock intervals with that switching included, and the gain is shown with its standard error.

    Which products fit which coil sizes or spiral types, shelf-life rules, health and food-safety requirements, location agreements and commissions, card-reader fees, price changes, and seasonal or day-of-week swings. Weekly sales are treated as an average with random (Poisson) variation, independent between products; a product that is hidden or badly placed sells less than its number says. The optimiser ignores sell-out substitution (so it can only understate), and the gain over today is then measured with substitution included. Prices and costs are placeholders, not any business's.

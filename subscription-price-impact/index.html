    Common questions

    How much churn can a price increase actually absorb?

    On the default example — 100 subscribers going from $20 to $25 a month — revenue reaches the same $2,000 you make today at just 80 subscribers, so you can lose up to 20 of the current 100 (20% churn) before the increase has cost you anything. That 20% is not a prediction of how many people will actually leave; it is the ceiling this specific increase can absorb before it stops paying off. “Subscribers at revenue break-even” and the churn-scenario table show the same number two ways.

    Why does a price decrease show 0% maximum churn?

    Because break-even is measured in revenue, and cutting the price raises the subscriber count needed to match today's revenue above the count you already have. Drop $20 to $15 on the same 100 subscribers and break-even needs about 133 — more than you have — so the calculator reports zero headroom: any churn at all, even churn that had nothing to do with the price cut, drops you below where you started. This tool does not model new customers a lower price might attract; it only tracks retention of the base you already have.

    Is “subscribers at revenue break-even” the same as break-even on profit?

    No. That figure is revenue against revenue — it ignores costs entirely. The profit view is the contribution column in the churn-scenario table, which nets your monthly fixed costs out of revenue at each churn level. A price change can clear the revenue break-even and still fail to clear the contribution break-even if your fixed costs are large relative to the price change.

    What does “contribution” leave out?

    Only the monthly fixed costs you type in. It does not subtract payment-processing fees, support cost per seat, hosting that scales with usage, or anything else that moves with subscriber count rather than staying flat month to month. If a meaningful share of your costs are variable, contribution overstates the profit impact of both the increase and the churn.

    Why does the churn-scenario table stop at 20%?

    Those five columns (0, 5, 10, 15, 20%) are a representative spread for sanity-checking a scenario, not a forecast bounded at 20%. Churn above that is entirely possible and the table simply does not print it; use your own historical churn rate, not the table's range, to judge whether a given increase is likely to land above or below its break-even point.

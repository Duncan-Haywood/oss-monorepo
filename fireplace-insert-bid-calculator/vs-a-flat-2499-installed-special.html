This calculator vs. a flat $2,499 installed special

"$2,499 installed" is not a price for a fireplace insert — it is a price for one specific firebox, quietly applied to every firebox that calls for a quote.

What a flat installed special is

A flat special is the fastest number a hearth shop can put on a postcard or a search ad: one price, no site visit, easy for a homeowner to compare between bids. It works for the shop as a lead magnet and works for the homeowner as a round number to shop around with. Nothing about the number says which firebox it was built for.

The number was set for one job — commonly a small gas cassette dropping into an existing masonry firebox that already has a gas stub, no liner needed, on a one-story stack — and then quoted to every caller afterward, whether or not the next firebox looks anything like the one the special was priced on.

What this calculator does instead

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the fuel type, insert size, chimney scope, liner footage, stack height, and flags for a new hearth, a gas run, a dedicated circuit, a surround, and a damper, plus your shop's wage, payroll burden, and target margin. It turns that into the crew hours the job actually consumes and a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this firebox, not the firebox the special was built on.

A flat special is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one firebox it was priced on, and nothing about a postcard rate tells a shop or a homeowner when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat special skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $2,499 installed special get fireplace inserts wrong?

Because the labor and materials an insert install needs come from the fuel type, the insert size, whether a liner has to run the full chimney, the hearth, a new gas or electrical run, a surround, and stack height — not from being "an insert." A small gas cassette dropping into an existing masonry firebox with a live gas stub and a large wood-burning insert needing a full 30-foot stainless liner, a new hearth pad, a surround, and a two-story stack are both "an insert," and a flat special charges them the same number.

What job is the $2,499 number actually built around?

One specific job: a small gas cassette going into an existing masonry firebox that already has a gas stub, needing no liner, on a one-story stack. It is a marketing price meant to get the phone to ring, not a number derived from any particular customer's fuel, liner run, or stack height.

Does the $2,499 number even cover the job it was named for?

On this calculator's own small-gas-insert preset — the exact scope the special describes: existing stub, no liner, one-story stack, priced at this tool's own shop-rate defaults — the walk-away cost of the insert, tear-out, dump, permit, labor, and overhead alone comes to about $3,133.18. A $2,499 special is already about $634.18 under that job's own break-even cost, before any profit is counted, on the easiest job it names.

What happens when the real job is a large wood insert instead?

On this calculator's own large-wood-insert preset — a 30-foot stainless liner, a new hearth pad, a surround, a damper, and a two-story stack, priced at this tool's own shop-rate defaults — the walk-away cost comes to about $10,120.40. A $2,499 special on that job is about $7,621.40 under the walk-away cost, less than a quarter of what the job costs to deliver before a dollar of profit.

Is a flat installed special ever the right way to quote?

For the one job the special was actually built around — a small gas cassette, existing stub, no liner, one-story stack — a flat number can land close, though this calculator's own defaults put even that job's break-even cost above $2,499. Outside that narrow scope, the gap between the special and the real cost grows fast, and neither the postcard nor the crew knows how far outside it a given firebox is until the fuel, liner, and stack are actually measured.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, insert prices, or what competitors advertise. It takes the wage, unit cost, and overhead you enter and turns them into a walk-away price for the fuel, liner, hearth, and access you describe — the arithmetic a flat special skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat installed special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest way to advertise that works until the firebox stops matching the one the special was priced on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. The $4,607.62, $3,133.18, $14,882.94, and $10,120.40 figures above are this calculator's own arithmetic on two worked jobs built into its own preset list (open the calculator and pick the small-gas or large-wood job from Starting job to reproduce them), not a claim about any specific contractor's real costs or any specific company's advertised price.

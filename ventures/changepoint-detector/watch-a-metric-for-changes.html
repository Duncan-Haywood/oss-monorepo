Get alerted when a metric really shifts, not every time it wobbles

Daily sign-ups, orders, a sensor reading, a conversion rate. You do not want a ping for every odd day. You want to hear once, within a few days, when the level has moved for good. This watch refits your series after each new row with Bayesian online changepoint detection (Adams and MacKay, 2007; PELT, Killick et al. 2012, as an option) and posts to Slack, Discord, Teams or Mattermost once per change, after five points of evidence.

What a message looks like

orders changed from 2026-05-01: level up from 49.9 to 54.9 (4.29 noise sd), Bayesian probability 99%; 7 points since.

From the tool's own test: 120 quiet days at about 50, then a step to 55. The run on day 124 says "watching: a possible change from 2026-05-01"; the run on day 127 posts the message above; later runs stay silent about it.

So the default is the quiet one. It allows for sticky noise, where today's value leans on yesterday's, which most business metrics have. That cut false alerts on very sticky noise from 32 per 1,000 checks to 1.5. The price is real: on the labelled real series it reached 33 of the 52 changes most annotators marked, against 38 for the plain Bayesian pass. It was 29 before round 4 found why: on smooth or wandering series (a price, a population) the estimated stickiness sat at its 0.95 limit, where any real move reads as persistence, so it is now capped at 0.8 (--rho-cap; 0.95 restores the old behaviour, which is quieter on a level that wanders like a random walk). If missing a change costs you more than a false alarm, run with --detector bocpd (the previous default) or --detector pelt (all 52 marked changes reached, about six times the alerts). With --model poisson (counts) or meanvar the default is the plain Bayesian pass, since the sticky-noise term is for levels only.

The rule for picking the defaults was written down before the first run, and the labelled real series were scored once afterwards without changing anything. Script and full output: eval/watch-replay.mjs, eval/watch-replay.out.txt (round 1), eval/watch-replay-round3.out.txt (round 3, which changed the default), eval/watch-rho.mjs and eval/watch-replay-round4.out.txt (round 4, the rho cap; the only round where the real series counted in the rule, because the fault only shows there).

Two guards against flicker are on by default. A change found later but dated back inside the history the first check called quiet is held, not sent. And a new change is sent only when the next check still finds it, so a detection that appears for one check and is gone the next never reaches you; the price is one check of delay (a day, on a daily watch). On the labelled real series the second guard took 2 of 105 alerts off, and 2 of 61 on series held out from every choice, without losing a marked change (--persist 1 sends at once). It is a small gain, and the README says how small.

No setup: watch it in your browser

If you look at the metric yourself every few days, you do not need a scheduled job. On the changepoint detector page, paste your rows (or a published Google Sheet's CSV link) under Keep watching a series and press Check for new changes. It runs the same watch with the same defaults; your browser remembers which changes it has already shown you, so each one is reported once. Nothing is uploaded, and nothing messages you: you have to come back and press the button. For a message in chat without opening a page, set up the daily run below.

Set it up in your own GitHub account

  1. Make the folder (no clone of our repository needed; Node 18+ and curl): 
    curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Duncan-Haywood/oss-monorepo/main/ventures/changepoint-detector/deploy/make-watch-repo.sh | sh -s -- metric-watch
  2. Put your series in metric-watch/data/metric.csv (a date column and a value column, oldest first), or plan to use a CSV link instead.
  3. Push the folder to a new GitHub repository. In its Settings > Secrets and variables > Actions, add the variable METRIC_CSV (data/metric.csv or the link) and, for chat messages, the secret WATCH_WEBHOOK_URL. With no webhook, a new change fails the run and GitHub emails you.
  4. Run the watch-metric workflow once by hand. The first run records the changes already in your history as the baseline and does not alert on them, nor later on a change dated back inside that history; after that it runs daily.

Or anywhere with Node: node watch.mjs data.csv --state watch-state.json, from cron, a CI job or a notebook. Options for counts (--model poisson), volatility (--model meanvar), a weekly cycle (--period 7) and growth (--log) are in node watch.mjs --help.

Watch your Stripe payments, revenue and card declines

If you sell through Stripe, you do not need a CSV at all. Add a Stripe restricted key with read access to Charges (Dashboard > Developers > API keys > Create restricted key) as the secret STRIPE_API_KEY in the repository from the steps above, and leave METRIC_CSV unset. Each run fetches your complete days into data/stripe.csv: succeeded payments, their gross revenue, and failed payments, with zeros for quiet days and today left out so a half day never reads as a fall. It then watches all three, so you hear when sales, revenue or declines move to a new level and stay there: a broken checkout, a card-network problem, a price change that worked or did not.

Your key stays in your own GitHub account and calls Stripe directly; nothing passes through us. Keep the repository private: the state file records your revenue around each change. Set STRIPE_OPTIONS to --time-zone America/New_York (or your own) so days start at your midnight all year, daylight saving included, --currency usd to choose the currency summed into revenue, or --customers to add new customers per day. Locally: STRIPE_API_KEY=rk_live_... node stripe-metrics.mjs --out data/stripe.csv.

How well, measured on synthetic shops (40 payments a day, about $30 each; no real Stripe account was used): when declines jumped from 2% to 25% of attempts, all 20 shops heard it 5 days later; from 2% to 10%, 16 of 20 did, 4 to 7 days later. On 20 shops with no change, watched daily for a month, no alert was sent on any of the three columns. Real shops have a weekly cycle and promotions these synthetic ones lack, so replay your own history first (--suggest) before trusting the quiet (README, "Stripe as a source").

Watch your Shopify orders, revenue and cancellations

If you sell on Shopify, you do not need a CSV either. In your Shopify admin, open Settings > Apps and sales channels > Develop apps, create an app, give it the Admin API scope read_orders (and read_all_orders if you want more than the last 60 days of history), install it and copy its Admin API access token. Add the token as the secret SHOPIFY_ACCESS_TOKEN and your store name (my-store or my-store.myshopify.com) as the variable SHOPIFY_SHOP in the repository from the steps above, and leave METRIC_CSV unset. Each run fetches your complete days into data/shopify.csv: orders placed and not cancelled, their revenue in your store's currency, and cancelled orders, with zeros for quiet days and test orders left out. It then watches all three, so you hear when orders or revenue fall to a new level and stay there: a broken theme or checkout app, a lost ad account, a price change.

The token stays in your own GitHub account and goes only to your store's myshopify.com address; nothing passes through us. Keep the repository private. Days start at midnight in the store's own time zone (Settings > General in your Shopify admin), as in Shopify's own reports; SHOPIFY_OPTIONS takes --time-zone to choose another, or --customers to add new customers per day. Locally: SHOPIFY_SHOP=my-store SHOPIFY_ACCESS_TOKEN=shpat_... node shopify-metrics.mjs --out data/shopify.csv. Tested against a stand-in for Shopify's API, not yet a live store.

If your site lives on Google search traffic, Search Console works the same way. In a Google Cloud project, enable the Search Console API, create a service account and download its JSON key; in Search Console open Settings > Users and permissions and add the service account's email as a Restricted user. Add the whole key file as the secret GSC_SERVICE_ACCOUNT_JSON and your property as the variable GSC_SITE (sc-domain:example.com, or the URL prefix with its trailing slash). Each morning the workflow fetches your finished days (clicks, impressions, CTR, position) and watches clicks and impressions. In simulation it heard a 40% fall in clicks about four days after it began, with almost no false alerts, but missed most 20% falls. To hear those, set the variable WATCH_OPTIONS to --columns clicks:poisson,impressions:poisson --detector pelt --overdispersion nb --trend gated (the middle setting for counts; --trend gated lets a steadily growing site grow without alerts, and adding --wander gated keeps most of a level that drifts with seasons and news from alerting; it decides that from the days before a change, so a real fall on a steady site is heard as soon as without it): on fresh simulated sites it heard 115 of 120 20% falls, a median of 8 days after they began, with under one false alert per 1,000 days, against 38 of 120 for the default; it heard the 40% falls just as fast. A 10% slide is still slow to hear (about a month), so read a month of rows with the CLI too. No API set-up? Export the Performance report and run node search-console-metrics.mjs --from-export Dates.csv --out gsc.csv. To check whether a fall began inside a Google update or your own release, list those dates in a file and add --events updates.csv to node cli.mjs: it says which changes fall in a window and how often a change that wide would by chance. Tested against a stand-in for Google's API, not yet a live property.

Google Analytics 4 works the same way, and catches the quiet failures: a release that drops the tag from some pages, or a sign-up form that stops sending its event. In a Google Cloud project, enable the Google Analytics Data API and create a service account with a JSON key; in Google Analytics open Admin > Property access management and add the service account's email as a Viewer. Add the whole key file as the secret GA4_SERVICE_ACCOUNT_JSON and the numeric property ID (Admin > Property details, not the G- measurement ID) as the variable GA4_PROPERTY. Each morning the workflow fetches your days in the property's own time zone (sessions, active users, page views, key events) and watches sessions, active users and key events. Name other metrics, such as revenue, with GA4_OPTIONS set to --metrics sessions,keyEvents,totalRevenue. No API set-up? Download a report with Date as its dimension as CSV and run node ga4-metrics.mjs --from-export report.csv --out ga4.csv. Tested against a stand-in for Google's API, not yet a live property.

Plausible Analytics works too, with no Google Cloud project: in Plausible open Account settings > API keys, make a Stats API key, and add it as the secret PLAUSIBLE_API_KEY and your site's domain as the variable PLAUSIBLE_SITE. To hear when sign-ups or purchases stop arriving, set the variable PLAUSIBLE_OPTIONS to --goal Signup (repeat --goal for up to five goals, named as in Site settings > Goals); each goal becomes its own column of daily conversions. A self-hosted Plausible? Add its https address as the variable PLAUSIBLE_URL. Each morning the workflow fetches your days in the site's own time zone (visitors, visits, page views and each goal) and watches visitors, page views and every goal. Plausible counts live, so an empty day is written as a real zero, which is exactly what a tag that fell off the site looks like. On 40 simulated sites (about 400 visitors a weekday), a tag lost from 40% of pages was heard on all 40 in visitors, and a form that lost 65% of its sign-ups (8 a day to 3) on 38; on 40 sites with no fault, the goal column sent a false alert on 2 and visitors on none (README, "Plausible Analytics as a source"). Locally: PLAUSIBLE_API_KEY=... node plausible-metrics.mjs --site example.com --goal Signup --out data/plausible.csv. Tested against a stand-in for Plausible's API, not yet a live site.

PostHog works the same way, for products that track events rather than pages: in PostHog open Settings > Personal API keys, create a key with the Query: Read scope, and add it as the secret POSTHOG_API_KEY and your project's ID (Settings > Project) as the variable POSTHOG_PROJECT. Name the events to watch in the variable POSTHOG_OPTIONS, for example --event '$pageview' --event signed_up --event "Checkout Started" (up to eight; page views alone if unset); on PostHog EU Cloud set POSTHOG_HOST to eu. Each morning the workflow counts each event per day in the project's time zone, plus the people who did any of them, and watches every column. On 40 simulated products (about 300 people a weekday), a release that stopped sending 65% of sign-ups was heard on 39, and page views falling to 60% on 36 to 40 depending on how quiet the weekends are; on 40 products with no fault, page views and people sent no false alert and sign-ups one or two (README, "PostHog as a source"). Locally: POSTHOG_API_KEY=phx_... node posthog-metrics.mjs --project 12345 --event signed_up --out data/posthog.csv. Tested against a stand-in for PostHog's query API, not yet a live project.

How sensitive. Since 2026-11-20 a count column (orders, cancellations) is watched with a cost for days that come in bunches and a lower alert bar, chosen on 2,200 simulated stores and then checked once on 2,200 fresh ones (pre-registered, README "A count default for clumped orders"). Against the older setting it sent 1.03 false alerts per 1,000 days instead of 3.26, and heard 1,056 of 1,200 drops and rises instead of 701, about 4 days after they began. A 30% drop in orders (30 to 21 a day) was heard in 173 of 200 stores instead of 70, and a 40% drop in very bunched orders in 145 instead of 129. The price: on counts that do not bunch it sends about one false alert in two to three years of daily checks where the older setting sent almost none. --detector bocpd brings the older setting back. --detector auto now picks between this setting and the eager one for a count column and keeps the bunching cost when it stays quiet; before, it stored the older setting (pre-registered, README "The How eager hint for counts": 2.04 false alerts per 1,000 days instead of 4.83 on 2,200 fresh simulated stores, 1,085 of 1,200 changes heard instead of 1,047). Never letting it pick sent 0.54 and heard 1,057, so on orders the plain default is a fine place to start. On the one real shop we have, it sent 2 alerts in 300 days instead of 37, but it missed the start of the Christmas build-up, which the older setting caught: it is slower to call a gradual climb. Simulated stores, not real ones: no deployer's orders have been scored yet.

Several metrics from one sheet

A season building is the change a daily watch is worst at: sales climbing a little each week for two months, never one day that stands out. Set the variable WATCH_WEEKLY to on and each run also watches whole Monday-to-Sunday weekly totals, with its own record, and its messages say "(weekly total)". On 150 simulated shops that climbed to 1.9 times their level over four to eight weeks, the daily watch alone heard 92 within eight weeks of the climb's start and the two watches together 131, for about half a false alert a year more. It is late by design: it hears a climb weeks in, not days, so it sits beside the daily watch rather than replacing it. With Stripe or Shopify it watches revenue and payments or orders, with Search Console clicks and impressions, with Google Analytics sessions and key events, with Plausible visitors, with PostHog the people doing your events.

Most teams track more than one number: orders, revenue, sign-ups, tickets. If they sit as columns of one CSV, one repository watches them all. Add --columns all to WATCH_OPTIONS, or name them, each with its own model if it needs one: --columns orders:poisson,revenue,signups:poisson. Each column keeps its own record of what was already reported, and a day's new changes arrive as one message that names the columns that moved. A column can also name its own detector after its model: --columns orders:poisson:bocpd-window:nb,revenue watches daily orders with the windowed Bayesian pass and the cost for bunched counts (the trade is in the paragraph above), while revenue keeps its default.

Watching five numbers instead of one means five chances a day for noise to look like a change. We tested whether to split the alert budget across the columns, and the test said no: on five columns of pure noise the default settings sent 1.3 alerts per 100 checks of the whole watch without any split, and on five real road-casualty columns the split sent more alerts, not fewer (README, "Several metrics in one watch"). So --detector auto --alerts-per 1/month picks each column's setting on its own history, as for a single metric.

Each column you add is one more chance for noise, and the default budget is per column, not for the whole watch. On a real normal run of an eight-sensor water-pump testbed (the Skoltech Anomaly Benchmark, 2.6 hours in 5-second steps), the quiet default on all eight columns sent 8.5 alert lines per 100 checks, over four times the budget, and every one of them was false. On the same testbed's 33 fault runs it caught all 33 faults, mostly through the flow-rate column. So replay your history first (--suggest, or the replay on this page), see which columns are loud on an ordinary stretch, and watch the ones that matter (README, "Round 12"). Asking for two columns to agree before an alert was tested on the same testbed and does not ship: sensors that move together, like the two accelerometers or the two temperatures, agree on noise too, so the normal run still sent 2.7 messages per 100 checks, and 9 of the 33 faults that one column alone had seen were missed (README, "Round 13"). A smallest-move floor works better: --min-shift temperature=0.5 drops any move smaller than that in that column. Read off the normal run for every column at once, the floors cut false alert lines on the fault runs' normal stretches from 6.3 to 2.3 per 100 checks, and the alerts left sat at a fault mark 47% of the time against 37%; but they lost 5 of the 33 faults, because the flow-rate column that sees the faults was floored as hard as the rest. So set a floor by hand on the loud columns that do not matter and leave the one that sees the problem alone (README, "Round 14"). A single floor for every column, measured against how much each column wandered in the five minutes before the move, did better on false alerts (1.4 per 100 checks, and 59% of the alerts left sat at a fault mark) but lost 6 of the 33 faults, so it is not offered either (README, "Round 15"). Giving the loudest column its own floor and the rest a shared, lower one caught 30 of the 33 faults but let 2.5 false alert lines per 100 checks through, most of them from a slowly drifting motor temperature whose ordinary day did not look like the fault days (README, "Round 16"). You can set that kind of floor yourself: --min-spread 2.5 drops any move smaller than 2.5 times how much the column wiggled in the 60 points before it, and --min-spread temperature=8 sets it for one column. Re-reading the floor from each column's own last five minutes, alerting only on a move bigger than any it made there, caught 32 of the 33 faults but let 4.7 false alert lines per 100 checks through: the temperature's moves on the fault days were bigger than its recent wander, not drift a rolling window explains (README, "Round 17"). On a fresh test, 28 servers with about 30 metrics each (the Server Machine Dataset, hourly), the quiet default on every metric sent 76 false alert lines per 100 checks on normal days, and the three automatic floors that came closest on the pump all lost most of the labelled incidents: the best kept 106 of the 290 that no floor caught (README, "Round 18"). Finer data did not change that: on three of those servers in 10-minute steps the floors still missed most incidents under an hour, though every setting reported the incidents it did catch about three hours sooner (README, "Round 19"). So this watch is for level changes in the few metrics you pick, not for anomaly detection across everything on a machine.

What you would use otherwise

  • Stripe analytics apps (Baremetrics, ChartMogul, ProfitWell and the like). Dashboards of subscription metrics, mostly paid, and they hold your data. This sends one message when a daily number moves to a new level, runs in your account and costs nothing.
  • Shopify reports and analytics apps (Triple Whale, Lifetimely, Better Reports and the like). Dashboards and scheduled reports, the useful ones on paid plans; someone still has to read them to notice that a drop has lasted. This sends one message when it has.
  • A fixed threshold. Simple, but you have to know the level in advance, and a slow series crosses it on noise.
  • Anomaly detection in a monitoring or BI product. Good at single odd points and usually part of a paid plan. A lasting step in level is a different question, and it is the one this answers.
  • R's changepoint or Python's ruptures in a scheduled script. More complete methods, but you write the scheduling, the "already told you" memory and the message yourself. This is that glue, done and tested.

Limits

  • A series whose level wanders all the time (a bank balance, an exchange rate, a price) will keep alerting: on TCPD's bank series the default sent 42 alerts in 465 checks, none at a marked change. Watch a quantity that holds a level between changes, or its daily change instead of its level.
  • Five points of evidence means a change is reported no sooner than five rows after it. Use --confirm 3 to hear sooner at the cost of more false alarms.
  • It reports when the data changed, not why. Whether your release, a holiday or an outage caused it needs what you know about that date.

Questions

How is this different from a threshold or anomaly alert?

A threshold or anomaly alert fires on single odd points. This watch asks whether the level of the series has changed for good: it refits the series each day with Bayesian online changepoint detection (Adams and MacKay 2007) and reports a change once five new points sit on its far side, then never again for that change.

Where does it run, and who sees my data?

In a GitHub repository you own, on GitHub Actions, or on any machine with Node 18 or later. Your data and your webhook URL stay in your account; nothing is sent to the people who made the tool.

How many false alarms should I expect?

Measured by replaying series one point at a time: on 4,000 daily checks of independent noise with no change, 2; on sticky (autocorrelated) noise, about 1 per 1,000 checks. On 31 real labelled series from the Turing Change Point Dataset it raised 18 alerts per 1,000 checks, 41% of them at a change a human annotator marked, and reached 33 of the 52 changes most annotators marked; the plain Bayesian pass reached 38 at 30 alerts per 1,000 checks.

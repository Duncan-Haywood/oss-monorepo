This calculator vs. a flat per-walk rate

Names no specific product or company. This compares a free calculator against the generic method every independent walker already has: one flat price — commonly $20 a walk — quoted the same regardless of where the stop is.

First — the flat rate is right, and you should have one

A website, a flyer, or a quick text back to a lead needs a number before you know the address. "$20 a walk" is a perfectly fine headline price, and no calculator replaces having one. What follows is not an argument that the flat rate is wrong. It is what that same number stops showing you the moment a stop is real and you know how far it is from the last one.

The two places the flat rate goes quiet

1. Drive time and mileage never separate from the walk

A flat rate answers "what do I charge for a walk," not "what does it cost me to reach this stop." Both live inside the same $20 either way. The calculator splits them out — $2.68 of mileage on the nearby stop, $8.04 on the farther one — so the drive, the overhead, and the labor are three visible numbers instead of one flat guess that happened to work out.

2. The same price can turn from a thin margin into a loss

This is the one that costs real money over a full route. The flat rate charges $20 whether the stop is five minutes off your last one or twenty-five, but the break-even cost is not the same, so a $20 walk that barely clears cost nearby (by 57 cents) becomes a $10.79 loss once the drive grows — with nothing on the price sheet to say so. A walker filling a day off the flat rate alone can run "the same $20 walk" all afternoon and end the day having lost money on the stops that happened to be spread out.

When the flat rate is still the better tool

For advertising, it plainly is. A website or a flyer cannot show a live calculator, and a new lead calling for a price wants one number, not a form. The sensible arrangement is both: quote the flat rate to get the booking, then run this calculator once you know which stops are on today's route, to check whether that day's version of "the $20 walk" still covers the drive — and, on a stop far enough out, whether it is worth a distance surcharge or a decline instead.

Every figure on this page is this calculator's own worked example, computed from its own default inputs, not a claim about any real walker's business or an industry benchmark. Your rates, mileage, and overhead will produce different numbers — the point is which numbers to produce, not what they should come out to.

Common questions

Is a flat per-walk rate wrong?

No, and it should not go away. A website, a flyer, or a quick text back to a lead needs one number, and "$20 a walk" is a fine headline price. The point of this page is not that the flat rate is wrong — it is that the flat rate is the same number whether the stop is five minutes from your last one or twenty-five, and only one of those two stops actually covers what the visit costs.

How much does drive time actually cost on a route stop?

Two things a flat rate shows neither of: the mileage cost of reaching the stop, and the clock minutes it occupies that could otherwise go to another visit. On this calculator's own defaults, a nearby stop (10 drive minutes, 4 round-trip miles) costs $2.68 in mileage alone on top of 40 total billed minutes. Move the same $20 walk to a stop with 25 drive minutes and 12 round-trip miles and the mileage cost alone triples to $8.04, before the extra 15 minutes of driving is even counted.

Why does the same $20 walk lose money on some stops and not others?

Because the flat price is fixed but the drive behind it is not. At the nearby stop, this calculator's own break-even cost (labor at $24/hr, mileage, and allocated overhead) is $19.43 — so the flat $20 still clears it, by 57 cents, well under the 15% target margin the calculator is priced to. At the farther stop the same $20 walk faces a break-even cost of $30.79 — the flat rate is now $10.79 short of covering the visit at all, a loss on a route that looked identical from the price sheet.

Does the calculator replace the flat rate?

No, and it is not trying to. The flat rate is what you advertise before you know the route. This calculator is what you run once you know which stops are actually on today's route, to check whether that day's version of "the $20 walk" still covers the drive — and, on a stop far enough out, whether it needs a distance surcharge or a decline instead.

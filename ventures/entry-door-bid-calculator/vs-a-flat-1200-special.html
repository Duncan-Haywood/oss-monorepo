This calculator vs. a flat $1,200 special

Names no specific product or company. This compares a free calculator against the generic pricing habit every entry-door shop competes with: one flat number — commonly a "$1,200 installed" special — advertised the same regardless of configuration, material, rot, or extras.

First — the flat special is right, and you should have one

A brochure or a yard sign needs a number before anyone has seen the opening. "$1,200 installed" is a perfectly fine headline price, and no calculator replaces having one. What follows is not an argument that the flat number is wrong. It is what that same number stops showing you the moment an opening is real and you know its configuration, material, rot, and extras.

The two places the flat special goes quiet

1. Sidelights, rot, and extras never separate from the door

A flat special answers "what do I charge for a door," not "how much rot, glass, and trim this opening needs." Both live inside the same $1,200 either way. The calculator splits them out — no extras on the brochure opening, $2,992.00 of material on the harder job — so sidelights, rot repair, brickmold, and a storm door are visible numbers instead of one flat guess that happened to work out on a good day.

2. The same advertised price does not scale with the opening

This is the one that costs real money. The flat special charges $1,200 whether the opening is the brochure door or the hardest job in the catalogue, but the cost behind it is not the same — on this calculator's own defaults, the brochure opening clears the flat price by $240.45, and the harder job runs $3,742.35 over it. A shop quoting "the $1,200 special" on every call can sell every job on the price sheet and still lose money on the ones that turn out to need sidelights and rot repair, with nothing on the ad to say so.

When the flat special is still the better tool

For advertising, it plainly is. A yard sign or a brochure cannot show a live calculator, and a homeowner calling for a price wants one number, not a form. The sensible arrangement is both: run the flat special to get the call, then run this calculator once you know the configuration, material, rot, squareness, and extras, to check whether that job's version of "the $1,200 door" still covers the opening — and, on a job far enough from the brochure opening, whether it needs a real bid instead.

Every figure on this page is this calculator's own worked example, computed from its own built-in starting jobs and shipped default shop rates, not a claim about any real shop's business or an industry benchmark. Your rates, wages, and overhead will produce different numbers — the point is which numbers to produce, not what they should come out to.

Common questions

Is a flat $1,200 special wrong?

No, and it should not go away. A brochure or a yard sign needs one number, and "$1,200 installed" is a fine headline price. The point of this page is not that the flat number is wrong — it is that the flat number is the same whether the opening is a square 36-inch steel prehung or a fiberglass unit with two sidelights, a transom, eight feet of jamb rot, brickmold, a storm door, and a second-story porch roof, and only a full bid separates the two.

Does the $1,200 special even cover the door it was built for?

On this calculator's own shipped defaults, yes, comfortably. The cheapest preset — a 36-inch steel prehung in a square, rot-free one-story opening — comes out to $959.55 in total cost before any profit is added, so the flat $1,200 special clears it by $240.45, just $211.11 short of this calculator's own $1,411.11 target-margin price.

How much worse does it get on a harder job?

A fiberglass unit with two sidelights and a transom, eight feet of jamb rot, brickmold, a storm door, a smart-lock prep, paint, and a second-story porch roof costs $4,942.35 on this calculator's own defaults — $3,742.35 more than the flat $1,200 special, before a dollar of profit. Sidelights, rot repair, brickmold, and a storm door do not live inside a per-door special.

Does the calculator replace the flat special?

No, and it is not trying to. The flat number is what you advertise to get the call. This calculator is what you run once you know the configuration, material, rot, squareness, and extras, to check whether that job's version of "the $1,200 door" still covers the opening — and, on a job far enough from the brochure opening, whether it needs a real bid instead.

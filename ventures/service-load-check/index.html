Can my existing electrical service take an EV charger, heat pump or induction range?

A panel or service upgrade often costs thousands of dollars and waits on the utility. The National Electrical Code gives two ways to show the existing service is big enough: 220.83, a calculation from what is installed, and 220.87, the highest 15-minute demand your meter actually recorded in a year, plus 25%. This tool does both, from your appliance list and from the interval data your utility lets you download, and tells you how much new load fits.

How the two methods work

220.83: calculated from what is installed

General lighting and receptacles at 3 VA per square foot, 1,500 VA for each 20 A small-appliance and laundry circuit, and the nameplate of every appliance that is fastened in place or on its own circuit (range, oven, cooktop, dryer, water heater, dishwasher, EV charger, well pump). The first 8,000 VA counts at 100% and the rest at 40%. That is 220.83(A), used when no air conditioning or electric space heat is being added; existing air conditioning sits in the list like any appliance. When air conditioning, a heat pump or electric heat is being added, 220.83(B) applies: the larger of the total cooling or the total heating load counts at 100%, and everything else at 8,000 VA plus 40%.

220.87: measured from your meter

The existing load may instead be the actual maximum demand: the highest average kilowatts held over a 15-minute interval in a year of data. That times 125%, plus the new load at its nameplate, must not exceed the service rating. A smart meter's 15-minute readings give it directly. With 5-minute data the tool adds readings into clock-aligned 15-minute blocks. With hourly data it shows the hourly peak as a screen only: an hour's average is lower than the busiest quarter-hour inside it. Less than a year is allowed only as a recording of at least 30 days on the highest-loaded leg, with the larger of heating or cooling added if that season is missing.

220.82: sizing the whole service

When the question is what size a new or replacement service should be, 220.82 is the usual calculation for a single dwelling on a 100 A or larger 3-wire service. It counts everything at once: general loads (3 VA per square foot, 1,500 VA per small-appliance and laundry circuit, the nameplate of every fixed appliance and motor) take the first 10,000 VA at 100% and the rest at 40%, and heating and cooling add the largest of six selections: cooling at 100%; a heat pump alone at 100%; a heat pump compressor at 100% plus backup strips at 65% (the compressor drops out if it is locked out while the strips run); space heat at 65% with fewer than four separately controlled units, or 40% with four or more; thermal storage heat at 100%. The guide what size electrical service a house needs walks through an all-electric example.

Which one to use

Either. The code permits both; a home that fails one may pass the other. Homes with gas heat and modest use often show far more room by 220.87 than by 220.83, because 220.83 assumes the range, dryer, water heater and air conditioner can all run near the same time. All-electric homes with resistance heat can come out the other way. Some inspectors ask for the utility's data printout with the permit.

Questions people ask

Do I need a panel upgrade to add an EV charger?

Not always. If either the 220.83 total or the 220.87 total with the charger fits under the service rating, the code permits the charger on the existing service, subject to your inspector. Where neither fits, a lower charger setting, a load management device (NEC 625.42 and 220.70 in the 2023 edition) that holds the charger back when the house is busy, or a shared-circuit switch is often cheaper than a service upgrade. The result shows the largest charger setting that fits each way.

What is NEC 220.87?

It lets the existing load be the actual maximum demand, the highest 15-minute average kW over a year, times 125%. Smart-meter interval data from the utility is the usual source. Hourly data understates the 15-minute peak and is not enough on its own.

What if I have solar?

A meter behind solar panels records the net of what the house uses and what the panels make, which hides demand while the sun is up. Use a consumption export if your system's monitor offers one, or the 220.83 calculation. The 2023 NEC does not let the 30-day recording option be used where solar or peak shaving is present.

Where do I get my interval data?

Most US utilities with smart meters have a Green Button Download My Data button in the online account. Choose the longest range, ideally 12 or 13 months, and 15-minute readings if offered. This tool reads the CSV or the XML.

Not decided

Whether your inspector accepts either method, which code edition and local amendments apply, the condition of the panel, service conductors and grounding, available breaker spaces, conductor and breaker sizing for the new circuit, whether the utility's transformer and service drop are adequate, and whether a given appliance's nameplate figures are right. A licensed electrician checks those on site.

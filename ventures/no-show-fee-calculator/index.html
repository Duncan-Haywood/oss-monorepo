Common questions

How much should I charge for a no-show or late cancellation?

There's no single right number, but a common starting point is charging close to the full service price for a true no-show (the slot usually can't be rebooked) and a partial fee for a late cancellation with some notice. This calculator turns your own price, notice window, and fee percentage into a specific number.

Is a cancellation or no-show fee legal?

Charging a disclosed cancellation fee is common practice across many service industries and is generally enforceable when clients agree to it in advance, but rules vary by location, payment processor, and industry — some regions treat fees for medical or dental appointments differently, for example. This is not legal advice; check the requirements for your business type and location.

How much notice should I require?

24 hours is the most common default — usually enough time to fill the slot from a waitlist. Businesses with longer or harder-to-fill sessions often use 48 hours instead. The table above compares same-day, 24-hour, and 48-hour windows using your own fill-rate assumption so you can see how much empty-slot cash a longer window actually recovers.

Should I overbook or charge a no-show fee?

They recover different money. A fee pays you when the chair stays empty and you collect. Overbooking tries to fill that chair in advance and risks a double-booked hour when everyone shows. The table above compares a fee-only book to overbooking 50%, 100%, and 150% of your expected no-shows, plus matching the rate and dropping the fee. It does not tell you which policy to publish.

Should I take a deposit instead of charging a no-show fee?

A deposit is collected when the client books, so a late cancel does not depend on a second collection attempt. A no-show fee is invoiced after the missed slot, so it only comes in at the rate you actually enforce. Compare the two on your own numbers above — this tool does not decide which is lawful or appropriate for your business.

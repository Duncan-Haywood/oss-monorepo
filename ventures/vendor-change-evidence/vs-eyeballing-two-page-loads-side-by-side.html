This tool vs. eyeballing two page loads side by side

Ten words added to a page of hundreds reads naturally either way. A glance cannot tell that anything moved; a byte-for-byte diff cannot miss it.

What eyeballing two page loads tells you

Opening a vendor's privacy or subprocessor page again and comparing it to what you remember, or to a saved copy, is the cheapest, most common way anyone re-reviews a vendor page, and it is not wrong to do it: a whole new section, a moved heading, or an obviously rewritten paragraph is exactly the kind of change a person scanning for something different will catch.

What a glance is unreliable for is the change that matters most in practice: a clause added or removed inside a paragraph that still reads naturally, in a page of hundreds of words where nothing about the page's shape signals that anything moved. Nobody scanning a long policy page is running a word-by-word count against last month's version in their head.

A real pair, from this tool's own shipped example

This is not a hypothetical. It is the before/after snapshot pair shipped with this tool's own examples, reproduced exactly: 

before.json (captured 2026-08-23T09:00:00Z):
  "We process account data to provide the service."

after.json  (captured 2026-08-24T09:00:00Z):
  "We process account and usage data to provide the service."

A day apart, the same URL, the same fetch status. On a real vendor page this sentence would sit inside a much longer document, and "account data" quietly becoming "account and usage data" changes what the vendor says it collects without changing the shape of the page at all -- no new heading, no bold notice, nothing to catch the eye of someone comparing the two by scrolling and scanning.

Running this tool against the same pair instead: 

{
  "status": "changed",
  "changes": [
    { "kind": "content_changed", "before_length": 47, "after_length": 57 }
  ],
  "review_required": true,
  "evidence_boundary": "Reports observations; the customer decides
    contractual or security significance."
}

The tool does not know or care whether ten extra characters matter -- that judgment is explicitly left to you, per its own evidence_boundary. What it does not do is fail to notice the ten characters, because it never reads for meaning; it compares every character in before.text against every character in after.text.

Common questions

Isn't glancing at a vendor's page again good enough?

It catches a rewritten page or an obviously new section, which is real and worth doing. It is unreliable for the change that actually matters most: a few words added or removed inside a page of hundreds, in a paragraph you are not specifically looking at, worded so it reads naturally either way. A privacy page that grows from 'We process account data to provide the service' to 'We process account and usage data to provide the service' looks unchanged to someone skimming for a new heading or a bold warning, because nothing about the page's shape signals that ten words moved.

What does eyeballing two page loads actually miss, concretely?

On this tool's own shipped example, a vendor's privacy snapshot goes from 47 characters to 57 -- 'We process account data to provide the service.' becomes 'We process account and usage data to provide the service.' -- between two captures a day apart. A person opening the page twice and comparing them by eye, especially on a long real policy page rather than this one-sentence example, has to notice one clause changed inside surrounding text that reads naturally either way. Running this tool against the two snapshots reports status changed, review_required true, with the before and after lengths right there -- it does not need to notice, because it compares every character.

Can eyeballing a page ever tell you something this tool can't?

Yes -- a human reading the page can judge whether a change matters, whether new wording is friendlier or more permissive, and whether a whole new section changes what the vendor is promising. This tool deliberately does not make that judgment; its own evidence_boundary says it reports observations and the customer decides contractual or security significance. What it replaces is not judgment but noticing -- the step where a change has to be seen before anyone can judge it.

What happens when the page fails to load instead of actually being unchanged?

A person who tries to reload a vendor's page and gets a timeout, a login wall, or a CDN error page usually either notices and tries again, or -- on a routine check done quickly -- assumes the earlier capture is still good and moves on, which silently reports 'no change' when the truth is 'not checked.' This tool's compare() function checks fetch_status on both snapshots first: if either one is not ok, the result is inconclusive, not unchanged, so a failed capture can never be mistaken for a stable page.

Does this tool fetch the vendor's page for me?

No. It compares two snapshots -- url, captured_at, fetch_status, and text -- that you already have, however you captured them: a saved page, a scheduled fetch, or a copy-paste. It runs entirely in your browser and sends nothing anywhere.

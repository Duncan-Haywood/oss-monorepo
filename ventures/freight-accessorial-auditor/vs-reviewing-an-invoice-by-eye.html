Freight Accessorial Invoice Auditor vs. reviewing an invoice by eye

Reading a carrier invoice yourself catches the one line that looks obviously high. It reliably misses a second identical charge billed at an ordinary-looking price, the free-time-and-cap math behind a per-unit rate, a fuel surcharge never turned into a percent of linehaul, and whether an overage actually has evidence attached — which is what this free, no-upload auditor computes exactly, on every line.

Where reading it yourself stops being enough

Every AP clerk, broker, and small shipper who pays freight bills already does this: open the invoice, scan the lines, and flag whatever looks expensive. For the one line that is genuinely several times what a normal stop costs, that scan works — it is designed to catch exactly that. The problem is everything else on a real invoice does not look wrong on its own; it only becomes wrong when compared against something else, and a skim does not make that comparison.

Common questions

Can't I just review the invoice myself and catch the overcharges?

For one line that is obviously high -- a detention charge several times what a normal stop costs -- yes, that is often visible on a single read. What a read misses is a line repeated twice at an ordinary-looking price, a per-unit rate with a free allowance and a cap that needs a two-step calculation, a fuel surcharge whose percent of linehaul was never actually computed, and whether an overage has supporting evidence attached once the invoice runs past a handful of lines.

Why is a duplicate line harder to catch than one that looks expensive?

An expensive-looking line stands out on its own -- it triggers a "that seems like a lot" reaction independent of anything else on the invoice. A duplicate does the opposite: the second liftgate charge at the same ordinary price looks completely fine by itself, so reading it a second time raises nothing. Catching it means noticing that two separate lines share the same invoice, reference, code, and amount -- a comparison across rows, not a judgment about any one row -- which is exactly what a skim does not do.

What does the detention or fuel math actually require?

A per-unit accessorial like detention, storage, or reweigh is rarely a single number: it is a rate per hour or per hundredweight, usually past a free allowance and often under a cap. Getting the expected amount right means subtracting the free time first, multiplying only the remainder by the rate, then checking the cap -- three steps, and the middle one is easy to skip when a dollar figure already looks plausible. A contracted fuel surcharge is harder still to eyeball: the dollar amount alone says nothing until it is divided by the linehaul charge and compared against the contracted percent, which is a division most reviews never actually do.

If I already track evidence for each disputed line, doesn't that cover it?

Tracking whether you hold a scale ticket, BOL, or rate confirmation for each line is exactly the bookkeeping a real review needs -- but holding that alongside which lines are actually over the rate card, across an invoice with more than a handful of rows, is where a manual review loses the thread. An overage with supporting evidence attached is a settled line, not a dispute; an overage with no evidence, or evidence that contradicts the billed condition, is the one worth raising. Keeping that pairing straight by eye across a whole invoice is the same cross-row bookkeeping the duplicate check needs, one column over.

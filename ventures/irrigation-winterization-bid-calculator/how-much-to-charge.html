How much should I charge for a sprinkler blowout?

Enough to cover the minutes and the miles on that system, not enough to match the cheapest postcard on the block. A ten-zone house with two drip laterals and a backflow to drain is not the same stop as a four-zone front yard, and a flat “$75 blowout” cannot see the difference.

Price the head type, not the zone count

Start with how many spray, rotor, and drip zones you will actually blow. Spray is often around four minutes a zone. Rotors take a little longer. Drip laterals need a longer, lower-pressure purge — often closer to eight minutes — because you cannot just slam 80 PSI through emitters and walk away. Ten zones that are eight spray and two drip are not the same labor as ten spray. A per-zone special that treats them as equal is unpaid drip time.

The backflow is not “included”

A backflow preventer is a drain-and-insulate, not a bonus line on the blowout. Fifteen minutes to isolate, drain, and wrap one device is a quarter hour on the clock. Two devices on a commercial split is half an hour. If that work is “included” in a $75 neighborhood special on a typical house, it is unpaid. That special is often below the walk-away once loaded labor, compressor fuel, and a ten-mile round trip are counted.

The drive is a second job

Miles are a round trip. Ten miles at 35 mph is not “in the neighborhood”; it is more than half an hour on the clock before you hook the compressor. A trip / truck fee exists because the first hour of a two-zone bed still required the trailer, the insurance, and the setup. If on-site time would land under your minimum, charge the minimum.

Then load the wage and keep a margin

A tech paid $26 often costs more than $33 once employer taxes, workers’ compensation (this is outdoor compressed-air work), liability, and paid time off are counted. A freeze-warning or after-hours call premiums that labor, not the compressor. Overhead sits on top of direct cost. Margin is a share of the price the customer sees, not a markup you add last and forget. The walk-away number is total cost: below it the stop makes nothing, even before you pay yourself.

This is not a winterization specification

This is not a manufacturer blowout procedure, not a freeze-protection spec, and not a finding that a system is winterized. Starting minutes-per-zone are ballpark numbers so a contractor can see the shape of a bid. Replace them with times from your own jobs, confirm the backflow drain method on site, and have a qualified professional review anything that has to be signed or insured.

Common questions

How much should I charge for a sprinkler blowout?

Charge the hours the stop will actually take — spray, rotor, and drip zones at their own minutes, plus backflow drain-and-insulate time, compressor setup, travel, and a trip fee — then divide cost by one minus your target margin. A flat $75 or $8-per-zone price hides the difference between a four-zone front yard and a ten-zone house with drip.

Why do two houses with the same zone count bid so differently?

Head type, backflow, and the drive. Drip laterals need a longer, lower-pressure purge than spray. A backflow preventer is a drain-and-insulate, not “included.” Ten miles one way is almost half an hour on the clock before you hook the compressor. A neighborhood special that ignores those lines is how a tech works a morning for a one-stop price.

Is this a winterization specification or a backflow procedure?

No. Starting minutes-per-zone are editable ballpark numbers, not a manufacturer procedure, a freeze-protection spec, or a finding that any system is winterized. Confirm drain method, local freeze rules, and condition on site.

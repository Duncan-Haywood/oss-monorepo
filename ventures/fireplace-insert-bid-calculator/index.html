      Common questions

      Why can a competitor advertise “$2,499 installed”?

      Usually because the special is written around one specific job, and that job is a preset here: a small gas insert into an existing masonry firebox with a live gas stub already there, no liner needed, on a one-story stack. It really does price low. What the flat number cannot see is fuel type, liner footage, hearth work, a new gas run, a dedicated circuit, a surround, and stack height. Price your actual fireplace beside the special and the gap becomes a list of line items instead of an argument about who is honest.

      Why does a wood insert cost more to install than a gas insert of the same size?

      Mostly because of what has to happen above the firebox. A gas insert with an existing stub can skip the liner entirely. A wood-burning insert almost always needs a full stainless liner run to the top of the chimney for safe draft, priced by the linear foot — 30 feet on a tall stack is a very different job from 16 feet on a one-story house, even with the same size insert sitting in the firebox.

      Why does stack height change the price when the insert itself is identical?

      Because the liner run and the roof access both scale with the chimney, not the insert. A one-story stack with ground roof access is the cheap-special assumption. A two-story or unusually tall stack needs harder roof access and more liner footage to reach the top, and that access and material cost is separate from whatever insert gets set in the firebox.

      Is this the same as a chimney sweep or a full chimney rebuild?

      No. This prices installing the insert itself, the liner, the hearth, and the extras that go with it. It is distinct from a CSIA chimney sweep (cleaning the flue and clearing creosote, priced by flue height and condition) and from a full masonry chimney rebuild sold as its own job. A chimney that needs rebuilding before an insert can safely go in needs that work priced separately.

      What’s the difference between the bid price and the walk-away price?

      The bid price already includes your target margin on top of labor, materials, and overhead. The walk-away price is the total cost underneath it — the insert, the liner, the hearth, the gas or electrical work, and the surround — the point below which the job stops being profitable at all. Typing a price you are considering shows you the margin or the loss before you send it.

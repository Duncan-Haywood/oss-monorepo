Babysitting taxes, explained

Yes, babysitting money is taxable in the US: cash, Venmo and checks alike, whether or not anyone sends you a form. Here is what a babysitter who runs their own small business owes, how much to set aside from each sit, and when to pay. The figures are for 2026.

1. The $400 line

If your babysitting profit (what families paid you, minus business expenses) is $400 or more for the year, you file a tax return with Schedule C (profit or loss from business) and Schedule SE (self-employment tax), even if you owe no income tax. Under $400, there is no self-employment tax, and if it is your only income you may not need to file at all. Teenagers follow the same rules.

2. Self-employment tax: 15.3%

Self-employment tax is Social Security (12.4%) and Medicare (2.9%), the share an employer would normally pay plus your own. It applies to 92.35% of your profit. On $10,000 of profit that is about $1,413. Half of it is deducted when working out your income tax. The Social Security part stops at the 2026 wage base of $184,500.

5. What you can deduct

  • Driving between sits and for business errands: 72.5¢ a mile in 2026 under IRS Notice 2026-10, with a reported mid-year raise to 76¢ from July 1 that is worth confirming on irs.gov. Your drive from home to your first sit and back usually counts only if your home is your main place of business. Log the miles as you go.
  • CPR and First Aid classes, background checks, a liability insurance policy.
  • Supplies you buy for sits: crafts, books, games, snacks you provide.
  • Advertising: your website, flyers, business cards, a listing fee.
  • The business share of your phone.

6. Cash, Venmo and the 1099-K

Payment apps send a Form 1099-K only above $20,000 and 200 payments for goods or services. Most babysitters never get one. The money is income anyway, and so is cash. Keep your own record of every payment; that record is what your Schedule C is built from.

7. Self-employed, or a family's household employee?

A babysitter who sets their own rates and schedule and sits for many families is usually self-employed. If one family controls how and when you work in their home, as with a regular nanny, the IRS can treat you as their household employee (Publication 926). Then, once a family pays you $3,000 or more in 2026, they owe Social Security and Medicare taxes on your wages and give you a W-2. Workers under 18 for whom household work is not their main job are exempt from those taxes. If you sit for one family most of the week, talk it through with them.

8. What families need from you

Parents claiming the child and dependent care credit (Form 2441) or a dependent-care FSA need your name, address, taxpayer ID and how much they paid. A free EIN from the IRS lets you give them an ID without your Social Security number, and Form W-10 is the IRS's form for providing it. The app prints a year-end statement for each family.

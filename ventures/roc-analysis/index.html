Method and citation

The curve and the AUC. Each distinct score value is a cut-off ("a case when the score is at least t"); the curve joins the sensitivity and false positive rate at each. The AUC is the probability that a random case scores higher than a random non-case, ties counted one half, which equals the area under that polyline (Hanley & McNeil 1982).

DeLong's interval and paired test. Every case gets a placement value, the share of non-cases it outscores, and every non-case the share of cases that outscore it. The AUC's variance is the variance of the case placements over the number of cases plus that of the non-case placements over the number of non-cases. For several scores on the same rows the same placements give a covariance matrix, and the difference of two AUCs is tested with its own standard error, which accounts for the correlation (DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson 1988); with three or more scores a chi-square test asks whether all are equal. Placements are computed from midranks (Sun & Xu 2014), so 200,000 rows take a fraction of a second.

Cut-offs and accuracy. Youden's (1950) J = sensitivity + specificity − 1; the closest point to the top-left corner; the most specific cut-off with sensitivity at or above the target, and the most sensitive with specificity at or above it. At a cut-off: sensitivity, specificity and predictive values with Wilson (1927) intervals (predictive values at a prevalence you give are by Bayes' rule, without an interval), and likelihood ratios with intervals on the log scale (Simel, Samsa & Matchar 1991).

Partial AUC and decision curves. The area over false positive rates 0 to e, standardised to a 0.5 (useless) to 1 (perfect) scale by McClish (1989), with a stratified bootstrap interval. For a score that is a predicted probability, net benefit TP/N − FP/N × pt/(1 − pt) against treating everyone and no one (Vickers & Elkin 2006).

Verified by tests in the source against an independent computation (eval/reference.py): on the example, a tied rating scale and a simulated three-score case, the AUCs match scipy 1.18.1's Mann-Whitney U to 10⁻¹²; the DeLong covariance matrices match a direct O(mn) computation from the definition with numpy 2.5.3 to 10⁻¹⁴; the paired z, p-values and chi-square match scipy; the Wilson intervals match scipy.stats.binomtest; and on Hanley & McNeil's (1982) 109 rated CT images the AUC is 0.8932, the 0.893 the paper reports.

Limits

  • Discrimination is not calibration. The AUC only says how well a score ranks. A model can rank perfectly and still say 90% when the truth is 40%; check its probabilities with the probability calibration check.
  • Paired scores only. DeLong's test here compares scores measured on the same rows. Two AUCs from different samples need an unpaired comparison, which this page does not do.
  • Small samples and AUCs near 1. The interval for one AUC is AUC ± z × SE, clipped to [0, 1]; with few cases and an AUC near 1 it can be too narrow. The page warns below 30 cases or non-cases.
  • The cut-off is fitted to these data. Youden's cut-off and its sensitivity and specificity are optimistic on the sample that chose them; confirm a cut-off on new data.
  • Spectrum and verification bias. Accuracy measured on clear-cut cases and healthy volunteers overstates accuracy in practice, and so does checking only the people who tested positive. The numbers are only as good as the sample.
  • Decision curves need the real prevalence. Net benefit uses the sample's prevalence, so a case-control sample, where the share of cases was set by design, gives a misleading curve.
  • Not modelled: covariate-adjusted ROC, ROC for censored survival times, multi-class ROC, and smoothed (binormal or kernel) curves.

Questions

What does the AUC mean?

The area under the ROC curve is the chance that a randomly chosen case scores higher than a randomly chosen non-case, with ties counting half. 0.5 is a coin toss and 1 is perfect separation. It says nothing about calibration or about where to put the cut-off.

How do I compare two AUCs measured on the same patients?

Use DeLong's paired test. Two scores measured on the same people are correlated, so comparing their two confidence intervals, or using a test for independent samples, is too conservative. DeLong's method estimates the covariance of the two AUCs from the same data and tests the difference with it.

Why can two scores with overlapping intervals still differ?

Because the paired test uses the correlation between them. When two scores rank the same people in nearly the same order, the difference between their AUCs varies much less than either AUC, so a small difference can be significant even when the separate intervals overlap.

Which cut-off should I use?

It depends on what a missed case and a false alarm cost. Youden's J weighs sensitivity and specificity equally; a screening test usually wants a target sensitivity, a confirmatory test a target specificity. For a predicted probability, the decision curve shows which threshold gives the most net benefit for a given trade-off.

What is the partial AUC for?

Often only part of the curve matters, for example specificity above 90% because false positives are expensive. The partial AUC measures the area over that part only. McClish's standardisation puts it back on a 0.5 (useless) to 1 (perfect) scale, so two scores can be compared where it counts.

What is a decision curve?

For a model that predicts a probability, the net benefit at a threshold probability pt is true positives per person minus false positives per person weighted by pt/(1 - pt), the odds at which a person would accept treatment. The model is worth using where its curve is above both treating everyone and treating no one (Vickers and Elkin 2006).

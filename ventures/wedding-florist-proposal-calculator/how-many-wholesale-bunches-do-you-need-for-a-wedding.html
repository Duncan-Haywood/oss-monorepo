How many wholesale bunches do you need for a wedding?

A recipe is written in stems: 85 garden roses, 60 spray roses, 40 ranunculus. A wholesale invoice is written in bunches, and a bunch is a fixed count you buy whole or not at all. Getting from one to the other means rounding, and rounding the wrong way — or at the wrong point in the calculation — either under-orders the job or quietly pads the quote. Here is the arithmetic, worked on a real five-variety recipe.

Round the shrink allowance in first, then round the bunches up

Two roundings happen, in this order and not the other way round. First, add a shrink allowance — breakage, blown blooms, processing loss — to the stem count the recipe calls for, and round that up to a whole stem. Second, divide by the bunch size and round up to a whole bunch. Doing the bunch rounding first and adding shrink to the result buys a fraction of a bunch, and no wholesaler sells one.

Worked on 85 stems of a ten-stem variety with an 8% shrink allowance: 85 × 1.08 = 91.8, rounded up to 92 stems needed. 92 ÷ 10 = 9.2, rounded up to 10 bunches. The invoice is for 100 stems against a recipe that called for 85 — not because the shrink allowance is generous, but because the tenth bunch was always going to be bought in full once the count passed 90.

A recipe that lands just past a boundary on every line rounds up every time; one that lands just under rounds up on none of them. Both are normal, and neither is a sign anything was priced wrong.

Where the leftover stems actually go

Fifty stems left over on a 240-stem recipe is not fifty stems of waste — it is stems that were always going to be part of the bunches the wholesaler sells, whether the base recipe used them or not. The ordinary destinations, roughly in the order studios reach for them:

  • Boutonnieres and corsages. These use a handful of stems each and are easy to forget to budget for separately — the leftover from the main recipe often covers them without a dedicated line.
  • Extra bud vases for a welcome table, escort-card table, or restroom counter — small, cheap arrangements that use up odd numbers of stems well.
  • Topping up an arrangement that looks thinner after transport than it did in the shop, which is common enough that having spare stems on hand is worth more than the stems themselves.
  • A take-home bouquet for the couple, or a thank-you arrangement, if none of the above use up what is left.

Whatever is left after that is a reasonable thing to compost or donate. None of this needs to be quoted to the client in advance — the bunch rounding already priced the stems, so what becomes of them afterward doesn't change what was charged.

Common questions

Why can't I just order the exact number of stems the recipe calls for?

Because a wholesaler doesn't sell single stems of most varieties — they sell bunches, and a bunch is a fixed count you either buy whole or don't buy at all. A recipe calling for 85 garden roses from a variety that ships ten to a bunch means the invoice is for 90 or 100, never 85. There is no line item for five-ninths of a bunch.

How do you work out how many bunches to order for one line of a recipe?

Add the shrink allowance to the stem count first, then divide by the bunch size and round up. For 85 stems with an 8% shrink allowance, that's 85 x 1.08 = 91.8, rounded up to 92 stems needed, divided by a bunch size of 10 and rounded up again to 10 bunches — 100 stems purchased. Rounding at the end instead of the start of that sequence gives a different, wrong, answer on some lines.

What should I do with the leftover stems?

They are not waste unless you treat them that way. The usual destinations are boutonnieres and corsages (which use a handful of stems each and are easy to under-budget for separately), extra bud vases for a welcome or gift table, topping up an arrangement that looks thin after transport, or a take-home bouquet for the couple. Whatever isn't used is a reasonable thing to compost or donate rather than something to feel you must price a use for in advance.

Does raising the shrink allowance always add another bunch?

No — only when it pushes the stem count across a bunch-size boundary. On a line already sitting well under a full bunch's worth of headroom, a one- or two-point change in the shrink percentage does nothing to the bunch count, because the rounded-up total doesn't change. On a line sitting just under a boundary, the same change adds a whole bunch. That is why the effect of shrink is a per-line question, not a flat percentage of the total.

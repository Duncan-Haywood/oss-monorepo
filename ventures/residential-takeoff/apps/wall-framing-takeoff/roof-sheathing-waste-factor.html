Roof sheathing waste factor: gable vs hip, measured on sixteen roofs

Estimators add 10% to a gable roof's sheathing and 15% to a hip roof's. This page lays out the 4 x 8 ft sheets on sixteen rectangular roofs, course by course from the eave, and counts how many more sheets each one takes than its area: four footprints, four pitches, three ways of cutting. The figures come from roof-waste-bench.mjs, run on the same roof engine as Residential Takeoff (measured 2026-11-28).

Every roof has 12 in eave and rake overhangs and rafters at 16 in on center; sheets run with their long edge across the rafters, end joints land on rafters, and alternate courses are staggered. "Gable" is two planes. "Hip, cut course by course" cuts each course of the four hip planes to its bottom length, diagonally at both ends, and throws the triangles away. "Hip, offcuts reused" is the same roof with each triangle cut off one course's hip end turned end for end to start another course's hip end, kept only where it saves a sheet; this is what the takeoff does by default.

What the sixteen roofs show

  • A hip roof has no more area than a gable roof. On the same footprint, pitch and overhangs, both roofs cover the footprint times the slope factor, so the extra sheets a hip roof is said to need are all cutting waste.
  • That waste is the hip triangles. Cut course by course, the hip roofs wasted 13.0 to 32.7%. Starting other courses with those triangles saved 4 to 10 sheets a roof and brought them to 4.4 to 16.2%, within 1 fewer to 3 more sheets of the gable roof on every footprint, and no more than the gable on 10 of the 16.
  • The gable roofs ranged from 3.4% to 16.6%, so a flat 10% ran short on 3 of the 16 and was more than twice the need on 5.
  • The top course decides most of it. The slope from eave to ridge is rarely a multiple of 4 ft, so the last course is a strip ripped from whole sheets. On the 28 x 40 ft roof at 8:12 the slope is 18 ft 0-5/16 in: four full courses and a strip 24-5/16 in wide, just too wide to get two from a sheet, so each strip costs a whole sheet (16.2% waste). At 32 x 48 ft and 8:12 the strip is 5-3/16 in and nine come from one sheet (3.4%). A shorter overhang or a different pitch moves that strip, so the waste factor for one roof is not the waste factor for the next.

What this does not show

Rectangles only: no valleys, dormers, wings, skylights, chimneys or vents, which all add cuts. Four by eight sheets on rafters at 16 in, not trusses at 24 in or 4 x 12 sheets. Damaged sheets, mistakes and the openings cut out later are a separate allowance this cannot measure. Reusing a hip triangle puts an end joint off a rafter, which needs blocking; the takeoff lists those joints. Shingles, underlayment and ridge cap are not measured here: the takeoff orders them from the area with the waste factor you type (10% gable, 15% hip by default). Nothing here checks the sheathing grade, thickness, span rating or nailing against a code.

Common questions

What waste factor should I add to a roof sheathing order?

On sixteen rectangular roofs laid out sheet by sheet, a gable roof needed 3.4 to 16.6% more sheets than its area, and a hip roof 4.4 to 16.2% when the triangles cut off at the hips were used to start other courses, but 13.0 to 32.7% when every course was cut on its own and the triangles thrown away. Most of the spread comes from the top course: its width is whatever is left of the slope after the full 4 ft courses, and one just over 24 in wide gets only one strip per sheet. Damaged sheets and the openings cut out later are a separate allowance.

Does a hip roof need more sheathing than a gable roof?

Not more area: on the same footprint, pitch and overhangs the two roofs have the same area, because both are the footprint times the slope factor. A hip roof needs more sheets only if the diagonal offcuts at the hips are thrown away. Reusing them, the hip roof came within 1 fewer to 3 more sheets of the gable roof on all sixteen roofs, and needed no more on 10 of them.

Is 10% waste for a gable roof and 15% for a hip roof right for sheathing?

For a gable roof 10% is near the middle, but it ran short on 3 of the 16 roofs here and was more than twice the need on 5 (under 5%). For a hip roof, 15% is too little if the courses are cut one by one (13 of the 16 needed more) and about twice the typical need once the offcuts are reused (1 of the 16 needed more). Laying the courses out answers it for your roof.

Can I use these figures for my roof?

Not as a number to order by. They are rectangles with no valleys, dormers, skylights or chimneys, 12 in overhangs and 4 x 8 ft sheets with end joints on rafters at 16 in; real roofs with more planes waste more. They show where the waste comes from; your own roof in the takeoff gives your own count.

Why was my listing photo rejected?

A photo bounced back the afternoon a listing was supposed to go live is an expensive kind of small problem: the shoot is over, the seller is waiting, and the message usually says what failed without saying which file. Most of the reasons are mechanical — readable from the file before anyone looks at the picture — which means they are also catchable before you deliver.

Why these particular things

The listing does not stop at the MLS. The same images syndicate out to portals, each of which resizes and re-crops them into its own frame. A file that is just large enough for the board is often not large enough after that, and an unusual crop gets cut again by something with no opinion about which half of the kitchen matters.

Branding on a photo is a policy question, not a taste one. Boards differ, but the common position is that listing images carry the property and nothing else, because the display surface belongs to whoever is showing it. A logo in the corner is the sort of thing that gets a whole set kicked back rather than one file.

Staging disclosure is where the real exposure sits. A too-small photo costs an hour. A digitally altered room presented as the room can be a misrepresentation problem, which is why disclosure requirements are widespread and why a staged file delivered on its own is worth flagging even when nobody has rejected it yet.

What a preflight can and cannot tell you

The checker on this site reads file headers, pixel dimensions and filenames. From those it can decide resolution, format, byte size, crop shape, branding tokens in a name, and whether a staged photo is paired. It produces a dated record of what it checked, which is useful to keep with the delivery.

It cannot see a watermark burned into the pixels, contact details printed inside the image, or anything your board requires that is not visible in a file header — and it names those exclusions in its own output rather than leaving you to assume they were covered. It is a floor, not your board's rulebook. The rules that apply to you are the ones in your board's current photo standards; this catches the mechanical failures early so that the only thing left to review is the photography.

Common questions

Why was my listing photo rejected?

Most rejections come from something readable in the file rather than from the picture itself: the image is too small on its long edge, the file is in a format the system does not take, it is too large to upload, the crop is not a standard listing shape, or the filename or the image carries brokerage branding, a watermark or a headshot. Virtual staging that is not disclosed is the other common one. Every item on that list can be checked before delivery, which is what the free preflight on this site does.

What is the minimum size for an MLS listing photo?

Boards set their own numbers, so the only correct answer for you is the one in your own board's current photo standards. The floor this tool checks is 1024 pixels on the long edge, which is deliberately conservative: an image under it is too small for essentially any board and for the syndication portals a listing feeds into. Clearing that floor is not the same as meeting your board's requirement, which may well be higher.

Can I put my logo or my brokerage name on a listing photo?

Usually not on the photos themselves. Rules vary by board, but the common position is that listing images show the property and not the agent, because the same images syndicate out to portals that display their own branding. This checker flags a filename containing logo, watermark, branded, brokerage or headshot as a warning, since those names are the usual sign that a branded asset has been dropped into a delivery folder by mistake. It reads names and headers only, so it cannot see a watermark burned into the pixels.

Do I have to label a virtually staged photo?

Assume yes and check your board's wording. Disclosure of digitally altered or virtually staged images is a widespread requirement, and the usual practice is both a label on the image and an unaltered version of the same room available alongside it. This tool looks for staged, virtually-staged or virtual-staged in filenames and checks that a staged photo is paired rather than delivered on its own; it cannot tell you what your board's disclosure text has to say.

Why does a photo get flagged for its aspect ratio?

Because an unusual crop is nearly always accidental, and a listing system that fits every image into one frame will crop it again to fit. The checker accepts the standard listing shapes in either orientation, 4:3, 3:2, 16:9, 5:4 and square, within a small tolerance. Something outside those is typically a screenshot, a phone panorama, or a photo trimmed by hand, and it is worth looking at before it becomes the shot the portal decides to cut in half.

Does this replace my MLS board's photo rules?

No, and it is not trying to. It is a dated floor spec covering what can be decided from a file header and a filename, so it catches the mechanical rejections early and says plainly what it cannot see: a watermark burned into the image, contact details inside the picture, and every board-specific rule. Read it as a preflight that saves you a round trip, not as a compliance sign-off.

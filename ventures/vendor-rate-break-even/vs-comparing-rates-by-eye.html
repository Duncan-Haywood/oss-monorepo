This calculator vs. comparing the quoted rates by eye

Two rate cards side by side make the lower per-job number look like the obvious pick. It isn't, once a monthly minimum is attached to one of them — the pick depends on how many jobs you actually run.

What comparing rates by eye does

The fast way to size up a vendor switch is to put the two per-job numbers next to each other: your current cost, and the new rate on the quote or rate card in front of you. Whichever number is lower reads as the better deal, and the gap between them reads as the savings.

That works as long as both numbers are the whole cost. It stops working the moment one of them carries a fixed monthly cost the other doesn't -- a minimum order fee, a subscription, a lease payment -- because a fixed cost spread over your actual job volume changes the real per-job number, and by a different amount depending on how many jobs you run.

What this calculator does instead

It takes your current jobs per month, your current fixed and variable cost, and up to three alternatives as a fixed monthly cost plus a variable cost per job -- the same numbers already on the rate card, just entered in full instead of read off the headline. It computes total monthly cost and true cost per job for each option at your own volume, and the exact job count where a new fixed fee stops costing more than its per-job saving returns.

Same two rate cards, opposite answer

Take this calculator's own default comparison: a current cost of $120 per job with no fixed fee, against a bulk supplier quoting $90 per job plus a $100 monthly minimum. Read as two rate cards, $90 beats $120 by $30 a job, every time, at any volume.

Run the numbers at 3 jobs a month: the current vendor costs $360, and the bulk supplier costs $370 — ten dollars more, not thirty dollars less, because the $100 minimum outweighs three jobs' worth of $30 savings. At 4 jobs a month, the calculator's own break-even point, the bulk supplier is already $20 cheaper ($460 vs. $480), and at 20 jobs a month the same bulk supplier saves $500 a month ($1,900 vs. $2,400). The rate cards never changed. Only the volume did.

Common questions

What's wrong with just comparing the two rates side by side?

A per-job rate alone leaves out any fixed monthly cost attached to it -- a minimum order fee, a subscription, a lease payment. Two rate cards showing $90/job and $120/job look like a clear $30/job win for the first, but if the $90 rate carries a $100 monthly minimum and the $120 one does not, the comparison that matters is total monthly cost at your actual volume, not the per-job number alone.

How wrong can eyeballing the rate get, in practice?

Using this calculator's own defaults -- a current cost of $120/job with no fixed fee, against a bulk supplier quoting $90/job plus a $100 monthly minimum -- the crossover is 4 jobs a month. Below that, the $100 minimum costs more than the $30/job saving returns: at 3 jobs a month the current vendor costs $360 and the bulk supplier costs $370, ten dollars more overall, despite its rate looking $30/job cheaper on the card. At 20 jobs a month the same bulk supplier saves $500. Same two rate cards, opposite answer, depending only on volume.

Does a higher quoted rate ever still win?

Only through a lower fixed cost, and only up to a volume where that saving runs out -- this calculator reports that case as a job count the deal stays cheaper up to, the mirror image of a break-even count. If the new per-job rate is equal to or higher than your current one with no offsetting fixed-cost saving, no volume makes it worth it, and eyeballing the rate alone cannot tell you which case you are in.

Do I need my accountant's numbers to use this?

No. Four numbers you already have: your jobs per month, your current fixed and variable cost, and the new rate's fixed and variable cost from the quote in front of you. The calculator does the break-even arithmetic; nothing is uploaded, and a share link carries only the numbers you entered.

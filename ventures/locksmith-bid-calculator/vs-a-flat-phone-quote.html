This calculator vs. quoting a flat price on the phone

A round number given in the moment — "call it $150" — is arithmetic anyone can do in their head. Keeping labor, travel, parts, the after-hours premium, and a minimum service charge separate, and doing it the same way for every call, is where a phone quote slips.

What each approach actually does

A flat phone quote blends everything into one number as you say it: labor, travel, parts, overhead, and whether it's an after-hours call all get weighed at once and rounded to something that sounds fair. Done by someone experienced, it's often close. What it has no built-in way to do is apply an after-hours premium to billed time only rather than to parts (which cost the same at any hour), or check the number against a stated minimum before it's said out loud — both of which take a deliberate second step that a quote given in the middle of a phone call skips by default.

This calculator keeps labor minutes, travel minutes, an hourly rate, an after-hours premium applied to billed time only, a trip fee, parts cost and markup, a callback reserve, overhead, and target margin as separate fields, then compares the result against a minimum service charge and uses the higher of the two. A standard 30-minute rekey with 20 minutes of travel and $40 of parts, run through the default rate and margin, prices at $283.62 — a figure built from eight separate numbers rather than rounded from one impression. A five-minute lockout with five minutes of drive time and no parts calculates to about $85.43 before the floor is applied, which is close enough to a round "$80, cash" guess that the $89 minimum — not the arithmetic — is what keeps that trip from running at a loss.

Common questions

Can't I just quote a flat price over the phone?

You can, and most calls do get priced that way — but a round number given in the moment blends labor, travel, parts, the after-hours premium, and overhead into one guess with nothing to check it against. It works until it doesn't: a familiar-sounding job that's actually longer, farther, or after-hours prices the same as an easier one that happens to sound alike on the phone, and neither the customer nor the caller has any way to tell until the job is already booked.

What does a phone quote usually get wrong?

Two things most often. First, the after-hours premium gets folded into the whole number in your head instead of applied to billed time only, so it's easy to either forget it on a rushed 2am call or accidentally apply it to parts, which cost the same at any hour. Second, a short job — a single cut key, a car door unlock with nothing replaced — has no floor in a mental quote, so "call it $60" can price below what the trip itself costs once fuel, dispatch time, and overhead are counted, a mistake that only shows up at the end of the month rather than on the call.

Why does the minimum service charge matter more than it seems?

Because it is the only thing standing between a fast, easy call and a loss. A five-minute lockout with five minutes of drive time and no parts calculates to about $85 before any floor is applied — close enough to a round "$80, cash" phone quote that the gap looks trivial, until a stated minimum service charge, checked every time, is what actually keeps that trip from paying less than it cost to make.

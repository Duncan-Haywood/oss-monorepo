This calculator vs. a flat price per square foot

“$X a square foot” is not a price for a hardwood refinish — it is a price for one specific method, floor plan, and finish, quietly applied to every room that calls in.

What a flat price per square foot is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote a refinishing job: multiply the field footage by a number and read off a price. It is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's flyer or a search-result special will call and ask you to match, whatever their floor actually needs.

The number behind it was set for one job — a living room of same-color screen-and-recoat, no stairs, no closets, no stain change — and then reused for every refinish afterward, whether or not the next job looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the field square footage, stair treads, closets, the refinishing method (screen-and-recoat or full sand), whether the job is staining or a color change, and your own labor hours and material costs. It prices the treads, the closets, and the stain separately by kind, then returns the walk-away price and a bid price above your target margin — for this floor, not a generic one.

What a flat price per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sq ft whether the room has zero stair treads or fourteen, even though every tread adds its own hours plus tread-and-riser finish area the field footage never counts. Prices stair treads separately as their own hours and their own finish area, so a stair-heavy job costs more than a flat field of the same size.
Has no line for a stain color change or the extra coat it needs, regardless of whether the job is poly-only or a full color change. Prices stain and extra-coat hours and gallons separately, following the finish area including treads, so a color change costs more than a poly-only recoat.
Charges the same rate for a full sand as for a screen-and-recoat, even though a full sand runs several times the hours per 100 sq ft. Prices each method's own hours-per-100 figure, so a full sand costs more than a screen-and-recoat of the same field.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against a flat per-square-foot rate, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

A flat per-square-foot rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one floor it was built for, and nothing about a flat number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per square foot get a hardwood refinishing bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is stair treads, closets, method (screen-and-recoat versus full sand), a stain color change, and extra coats — not the field square footage a homeowner measures. A 250 sq ft hallway with fourteen stair treads and a stain change is doing far more work than a 250 sq ft living room of same-color screen-and-recoat, none of which shows up in the room's own square footage.

What does a flat price per square foot usually leave out entirely?

Stair treads and their extra tread-and-riser finish area, closets, whether the job is a full sand or a screen-and-recoat, a stain color change, and any extra coat that change requires. A hallway with fourteen treads is doing several times the labor of the same field square footage with no stairs at all, and a per-square-foot number has no line for any of it.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped presets, at its own shipped shop rates: a 400 sq ft living room of screen-and-recoat prices at $2,036.11, or $5.09 a field square foot. Applying that same $5.09 rate to a 250 sq ft hallway with fourteen stair treads and a stain color change — 306 sq ft of actual finish area once the treads are counted — quotes $1,272.57, which is $1,427.37 below that job's own $2,699.94 break-even cost, before a dollar of profit, and $2,327.35 below the calculator's own $3,599.92 bid price.

Is a flat price per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same method, no stairs, no closets, no stain change — a per-square-foot rate can land close, and on this calculator's own screen-and-recoat living-room preset it is exactly the rate that job supports, because it was set from that job. The risk is applying the same rate outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which floor it was built on and neither does the estimator until stairs, closets, method, and stain are actually spelled out.

Does this calculator know what materials or labor cost in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates. It takes the field square footage, stair treads, closets, method, stain, and coats, plus the labor hours, finish and stain costs, and wage you enter, and turns that into a price for this job — the arithmetic a flat per-square-foot rate skips, not a market survey.

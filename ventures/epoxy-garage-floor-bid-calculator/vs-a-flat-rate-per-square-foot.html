This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

"$4 a square foot" is not a price for a garage floor — it is a price for one specific coating system and one specific prep, quietly applied to every slab that walks in the door.

What a flat per-foot rate is

A flat rate is the fastest way to quote: multiply the floor's square footage by a number and read off a price. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and shops use it because it needs no takeoff. It is also the rate a customer holding a flyer or a competitor's website will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one coating system, degrease prep only, no cracks, no moisture barrier — and then reused for every garage afterward, whether or not the next slab looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the garage's square footage, the coating system (solid-color epoxy, full-broadcast flake, metallic, or 1-day polyaspartic), the prep method (degrease, light grind, full diamond grind, or shot blast), any cracks, pits, or a moisture barrier, and your loaded labor and overhead. It converts prep and coating into hours by rate, adds flake pounds by billable area and waste, and returns a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this slab, not a generic one.

What a flat per-foot rate doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sq ft whether the job is a degrease-only solid-epoxy coat or a full-diamond-grind full-broadcast flake system — a several-times difference in prep and material for the same floor area. Prices each coating system's own kit cost, coat count, and coat-hour rate, and each prep method's own hours per square foot, so the number tracks the job actually described, not the floor plan alone.
Prices "the garage" as one coated area, with no separate line for cracks, pits, or a moisture barrier. Times cracks at hours and fill per lineal foot, pits at fill per square foot, and a moisture barrier at the full slab area once, so that scope shows up instead of being absorbed into the coated area or skipped.
Assumes degrease-only prep is enough, so a slab needing a full diamond grind gets quoted the same as one that does not. Adds prep hours only for the method actually selected, so a degrease-only job and a full-grind job price differently — about $1,743 walk-away versus about $2,073 once a full diamond grind is added on the same 400 sq ft two-car, while a flat $4-a-square-foot quote on that same slab comes to $1,600.
Guesses flake as "a couple of bags" rather than pricing the billable area. Converts billable square feet (coated area plus waste) into flake pounds from the system's own lb-per-square-foot figure, so the kit invoice the flake bill matches is the one the calculator priced.
Gives the crew a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price, the effective hourly rate, and the price per square foot, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the crew commits to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a flyer rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get an epoxy garage floor wrong?

Because prep hours and coating system drive the cost, not the floor area alone. A degrease-only solid-epoxy job and a full-diamond-grind flake job on the same 400 square foot two-car differ by hours of grinding and a heavier kit before a single coat goes down. A flat rate charges both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat rate usually leave out entirely?

The grind, the cracks, and which coating system was actually quoted. Solid-color epoxy, full-broadcast flake, metallic, and 1-day polyaspartic each carry a different kit cost per square foot and a different coat-hour rate, and a full diamond grind to profile is often as many hours as the coats themselves. A rate built around "square feet of garage" has nowhere to put any of that.

What happens when the flyer price assumes degrease-only but the slab needs a real grind?

A $4-a-square-foot flyer on a 400 square foot two-car comes to $1,600. This calculator's walk-away price for that same slab with degrease-only prep and a solid-color epoxy kit is about $1,743 once loaded labor, kit cost, and overhead are counted — already short of the flyer rate — and about $2,073 once a full diamond grind to profile is added, which is the prep the trade recommends before a durable coat. The flyer rate is already tight on the lighter job and underwater on the one the slab actually needs.

Is a flat rate ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same coating system, same prep, no cracks or pits, no moisture barrier — a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the postcard rate does not know which band a given garage is in and neither does the crew until the slab is profiled.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, kit prices, or what competitors charge. It takes the wage, kit cost, and overhead you enter and turns them into a walk-away price for the system, prep, and scope you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per square foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. The $1,743, $2,073, and $1,600 figures above are this calculator's own arithmetic on the example job described in its README, not a claim about any specific contractor's real costs.

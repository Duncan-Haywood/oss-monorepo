This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

A $-per-square-foot rate set on a small first-story patch is not a price for stucco repair in general — it is a price for one system, one story count, and one set of extras, quietly charged again for a job that carries more of all three.

What a flat rate per square foot is

It is the fastest way to quote a stucco or EIFS repair: multiply the repair area — hollow plus tear-off square footage, the number a homeowner can read off an estimate sheet — by a published rate. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a postcard special or a cheaper competitor's quote will ask you to match.

It is wrong because the repair area alone says nothing about the system, the story count, or the extras. A rate set on a first-story one-coat patch gets reused for a second-story EIFS tear-off with a moisture investigation and several window flashings, because both jobs happen to cover a similar number of square feet.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the crack feet, hollow area, tear-off square footage, wall system, stories, window and door flashings, color match, and moisture investigation, plus your crew's rates. It turns that into the walk-away price, the price per square foot of repair, and where the bid's hours are actually going.

Prices the job by repair area alone, whichever wall system, story count, or extras are actually involved. Prices hard-coat, one-coat, and EIFS systems from their own mix and mesh costs, and scaffolding from the actual story count.
Uses the same $/sq ft whether it is a ground-floor patch or a second-story job needing a scaffold lift. Adds scaffold hours and rental, plus extra hours for a third-story gable, by story count.
Has no line for a moisture investigation, so a clean patch and a job with suspected water intrusion are quoted the same. Prices moisture investigation and window/door flashings as their own lines, each visible instead of buried in a bigger, wrong number.
Folds color match into the same flat rate, or leaves it out. Prices integral or finish color match as its own line, only when the job actually needs it.
Gives the crew a rate to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price, the price per square foot of repair, and the walk-away price, so a shopped rate can be tested against the real job before it goes out.

One rate, two different repairs

Using this calculator's own job presets. An 80-square-foot first-story one-coat delamination repair, 30 feet of cracks, color match, and one window flashing prices at $3,049.99, which works out to $38.12 per square foot of repair.

A two-story EIFS tear-off — 180 square feet torn back to the substrate, 40 more square feet of hollow, a moisture investigation, four window flashings, and color match — is 220 square feet of repair, not far off the smaller job's 80 in relative terms once the tear-off is counted the same way. But it carries a pricier EIFS system, second-story scaffolding, a moisture investigation, and three more flashings than the job the $38.12 rate was set on. Matching that rate to this job's 220 square feet gives a quote of $8,387.47.

This calculator prices the same EIFS job at $11,941.26 — a real rate of $54.28 per square foot, 1.4 times the smaller job's rate. The flat square-foot quote covers about 70.2% of the real cost, a shortfall of $3,553.79. None of that gap shows up in the repair-area number alone — it comes entirely from the system, the access, and the extras a square-foot rate has no line for.

Nobody is being careless by reaching for a $-per-square-foot rate first — it is the number that is easiest to compare between bids. The gap is everything a repair-area number cannot see: the wall system, the story access, the moisture work, and the flashings, which is exactly what this calculator prices and a rate copied from the last job cannot.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get stucco and EIFS repair wrong?

Because the repair square footage alone does not say whether it is a first-story patch with a hand-mixed hard-coat lamina or a second-story EIFS tear-off that needs scaffolding, a full mesh reset, a moisture investigation, and multiple window flashings. A rate quoted from the smaller job carries none of that: no access hours, no moisture kit, no extra flashing labor, and a lighter mesh and mix cost than an EIFS system actually needs.

How big is the gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own job presets: an 80-square-foot first-story one-coat delamination repair with 30 feet of cracks and one window flashing prices at $3,049.99, which works out to $38.12 per square foot of hollow-plus-tear-off. Applying that same $38.12 rate to a 220-square-foot two-story EIFS repair -- 180 square feet of tear-off, 40 square feet of hollow, a moisture investigation, and four window flashings -- gives a quote of $8,387.47. This calculator prices that job at $11,941.26: the flat rate covers about 70.2% of the real cost, a $3,553.79 shortfall.

What does pricing by a flat square-foot rate leave out entirely?

Everything the repair-area number alone cannot see: whether it is a hard-coat patch or an EIFS system with a higher mix and mesh cost, whether the job needs scaffolding for a second story, a moisture investigation, and how many window or door flashings have to be reset. A square-foot rate has no line for any of them, so a job that looks like a similar repair area to the one the rate was set on is priced the same as one carrying several extras it does not have.

Is pricing by a flat square-foot rate ever close enough?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same system, same story count, little or no moisture or flashing work -- a flat rate set for one job can land close on the next. The risk is applying a rate learned on a small first-story patch to a job that looks like a similar repair area but carries a second story, an EIFS system, moisture work, or extra flashings, because the square-foot number alone cannot tell you when that has happened.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, material costs, or what competitors charge. It takes your crack feet, hollow area, tear-off, stories, flashings, color match, and moisture work, and your own crew rates, and turns them into a price built from the actual job -- the arithmetic a flat per-square-foot rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per square foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the next job stops matching the one the rate was set on.

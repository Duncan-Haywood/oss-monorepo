This checker vs. looking up the 1099-K threshold yourself

Figuring out whether a marketplace or payment app is likely to send you a Form 1099-K is arithmetic anyone can do — if they have the right figure. The problem is that the right figure depends on the tax year, sometimes on your state, and always on treating each platform separately, and a quick manual check often misses one of those without any sign that something was skipped.

Common questions

What's the most common mistake when looking up the 1099-K threshold yourself?

Using last year's figure. The federal threshold has changed four times since 2022: $20,000/200 transactions through 2023, $5,000 with no transaction minimum for 2024 only, then back to $20,000/200 from 2025 on. A search result, a news article, or even an old bookmark can easily be reporting a figure that applied to a different tax year than the one you're checking.

Why does it matter whether the threshold is "at least" or "exceeds"?

Because they produce opposite answers at the boundary. The IRS's own wording is "exceeds $20,000" and "exceeds 200 transactions" — strictly greater than, not "at least." A seller paid exactly $20,000 across exactly 200 transactions has not crossed the federal threshold under either condition. A quick mental check that rounds "exceeds" up to "at least" gets this specific case wrong.

Do I need to add up what every platform paid me before checking the threshold?

No — and this is the mistake most likely to produce a wrong answer for someone selling on several platforms. The threshold applies separately to each Third Party Settlement Organization (TPSO). $15,000 from eBay and $15,000 from Etsy in the same year does not combine into $30,000 for either platform's own reporting decision; each platform checks only what it itself paid you.

How would I know if my state has a stricter threshold than the federal one?

Only four states currently do: Maryland, Massachusetts, Vermont, and Virginia, each at $600 with no transaction-count minimum, independent of whichever federal figure applies that year. That is not the kind of fact most people carry into a manual check, and a state's own department-of-revenue page is easy to overlook if you're only checking the federal rule.

If I check by hand and land on the wrong side of the threshold, what actually happens?

Nothing about the actual law changes either way — a platform still applies its own reporting obligation regardless of what you calculated. What changes is whether you're caught off guard: expecting no form and getting one in January, or assuming a form means taxable income was newly created when it was always reportable. Getting the estimate right ahead of time is about avoiding the surprise, not the obligation.

How many appointments should you book per slot?

Paste or upload your booking history: for each slot, how many were booked and how many showed up. The tool measures how your no-shows cluster (a bad-weather day empties several chairs at once), works out the chance of every outcome for each booking level, and finds the number that earns the most after the cost of someone being turned away. It gives a bootstrap range on the answer and back-tests it on your most recent slots. If you have too little data, or your no-show rate has recently moved, it says so instead of guessing.

How it is calculated

Model: in a slot with n people booked, the number who show is beta-binomial: a binomial whose show-up probability itself varies from slot to slot. That gives two fitted numbers, your average no-show rate and a clustering strength (intraclass correlation) that says how much no-shows travel together; it is fitted by maximum likelihood on each slot's (booked, showed) counts. Decision: expected profit for booking n into s seats = earnings × expected people served − overflow cost × expected people beyond capacity, searched from s up to s + the extra bookings you allow, optionally keeping the chance of turning anyone away under your limit. Ranges: 200 seeded bootstrap resamples of your slots refit the model; each resample's choice is scored on the main fit, so the 80% range of the gain and the share of resamples that pick each level are reproducible. Back-test: the first 75% of slots fit the model; the last 25% are scored on log-likelihood against a plain binomial, and the observed no-shows are compared with the predicted count.

Not modelled: who cancels, how much notice they gave, patient or client type, rebooking of turned-away people, how waiting affects loyalty, or whether there is enough demand to fill the extra booking. It assumes you can always find someone to book into the extra seat. Unverified: checked against simulated bookings with known patterns in unit tests, not against a published dataset or a commercial scheduling product.

Common questions

What is an overbooking policy?

It is a rule for how many appointments you accept for a slot that has fewer seats than that. If 15% of people do not show, booking four into three seats gives you a good chance of a full schedule. The cost is the nights it goes the other way and everyone shows, so the question is how many to book, not whether to.

How do I work out how many extra appointments to book?

Count what one extra booking is worth and what it can cost. It is worth your margin when it fills a seat someone else left empty; it costs your overflow penalty when everyone shows. This tool turns your past show-up counts into the chance of each outcome and picks the number of bookings with the highest expected profit.

Why does it look at each slot rather than my overall no-show rate?

Because no-shows clump. A storm, a school holiday or a flu week empties several chairs at once, so an overall 15% average hides that some sessions lose 50% and most lose almost nothing. The tool fits a beta-binomial model that measures this clumping; ignoring it makes overbooking look safer than it is.

What data do I need?

Either one row per appointment (slot, status: showed or no-show) or one row per slot (slot, booked, showed). Take it from your booking system export. You need at least 40 appointments, 5 no-shows and 15 slots. Leave out appointments that were cancelled early enough to be refilled; label late cancellations as late cancel.

What do margin and overflow cost mean?

Margin is what one completed appointment earns you after direct costs. Overflow cost is what it costs you when someone extra turns up and cannot be seen on time: a refund or credit, overtime, a lost regular, or a bad review. A careful estimate matters more than a precise one; try both a low and a high figure.

Will this work for my business?

It suits anything booked in slots with a fixed number of seats that can absorb a waiting customer: clinics, salons, tutors, studios, repair appointments. It is a poor fit where an overflow is dangerous or illegal, such as emergency medicine, or where you cannot apologise and rebook someone. Bookings also need enough demand to fill the extra seat.

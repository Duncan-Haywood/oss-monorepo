A lien-auction unit is sold sight-mostly-unseen, and every forum repeats the same rule: don't bid more than 20-30% of what it's worth. That rule skips the buyer's premium charged on top of your bid, prices "worth" at retail instead of realistic resale recovery, and has no room at all for dump, labor, transport and holding cost. Common questions

Is the winning bid what I actually pay?

No. Lien-auction platforms and most in-person auctioneers charge a buyer's premium on top of your hammer bid, commonly 10-15%. If you bid $400 with a 12% premium, your actual purchase price is $448, not $400. This calculator applies the premium to every figure it reports, so the ceiling it gives you is a hammer bid, and the total-outlay figure next to your planned bid already includes the premium.

Why isn't the max bid just a percentage of what the contents are worth?

Because a flat percentage skips two real cuts and ignores a real cost stack. What the contents would fetch new or with time to negotiate is not what a stranger's used goods net on a resale deadline — that is the recovery rate. What they do net is then cut again by resale channel and payment fees. And the cash cost of dump runs, a truck, cleanout labor and holding inventory while you sell it does not scale with the unit's value at all, so a percentage rule has no room for it — which is exactly backwards on a small or hard-to-sell unit, the outcome you cannot rule out sight-unseen.

What is the difference between the break-even bid and the max bid at my target margin?

The break-even bid is the hammer price at which this deal nets exactly $0 profit — the absolute ceiling past which every extra dollar is a loss. The max bid at your target margin is lower: it is the bid that leaves your required profit margin on the table, not just avoids a loss. Bidding between the two can still be a real deal, just a thinner one than your target; bidding past the break-even bid is a loss no matter how the resale goes.

What happens if I don't clean out the unit in time?

Facilities set a removal window — commonly 24 to 72 hours — and most require a refundable cleanout deposit that you forfeit if you miss it or leave the unit in bad condition, sometimes alongside being barred from future auctions there. This calculator divides your total cleanout labor hours by your crew size to get the wall-clock hours the job actually takes, and flags it when that exceeds the facility's window, so the deposit risk is a number you see before you bid, not a surprise the next morning.

This calculator vs. bidding a flat percentage of value

Every storage-auction forum repeats some version of the same rule: don't bid more than 20-30% of what the unit is worth. That rule prices "worth" at retail instead of realistic resale recovery, skips the buyer's premium charged on top of your bid, and has no room at all for the cash cost of cleanout. This page shows exactly where it goes wrong, with the calculator's own worked example.

Where the percentage rule breaks down

A flat percentage-of-value rule is popular because it needs almost nothing to apply: eyeball the contents, multiply by a number you already remember, bid. That is also exactly why it misses three real costs that do not scale the same way the eyeballed value does.

The winning bid is not the purchase price. Lien-auction platforms and most in-person auctioneers charge a buyer's premium on top of the hammer price, commonly 10-15%. A bidder applying a percentage rule to their walk-away number is quietly bidding past it every time the premium is not already backed out.

"What it's worth" and "what it nets you" are different numbers, twice over. A percentage rule is usually applied to an eyeballed retail value. Liquidating a stranger's unit on a resale deadline realizes a fraction of that as used goods with no provenance, and whatever it does fetch is cut again by the resale channel's own fees.

Cleanout has its own cash cost, and it does not scale with the contents' value. Dump loads, a truck, labor hours, and months of holding cost to resell through are roughly the same size whether the unit holds antiques or a sofa nobody wants -- and a percentage-of-value rule has no room for a fixed cost stack at all, which is exactly backwards on a small or hard-to-sell unit, the outcome you cannot rule out sight-unseen.

Common questions

What does the flat percentage-of-value rule actually get wrong?

Three things. The winning bid is not the purchase price — lien-auction platforms and most in-person auctioneers charge a buyer's premium on top of the hammer bid, commonly 10-15%, and a percentage rule applied to the hammer number quietly bids past it every time. Retail value and realistic net resale value are different numbers, twice over: a flat percentage is usually applied to an eyeballed retail value, but liquidating a stranger's unit on a deadline realizes only a fraction of that as used goods with no provenance, and whatever it does fetch is cut again by the resale channel's own fees. And cleanout has its own cash cost — dump loads, a truck, labor hours, and holding cost — that does not scale with the contents' value at all, which a percentage-of-value rule has no room for.

How far off can a flat 20%-of-value bid actually be?

On this calculator's own default worked example — a $4,000 estimated retail value, 35% realistic recovery, 15% blended resale fees, and a $650 cleanout cost stack — the flat 20%-of-value rule says bid up to $800. The real math supports a $57.14 hammer bid at a 40% target margin, and the unit's own break-even bid (0% margin, the absolute ceiling) is $482.14. An $800 bid is not just thinner than target, it is a guaranteed loss before cleanout even starts.

Does a percentage rule ever get close to the right number?

It can, on a unit whose recovery rate and cleanout cost happen to sit near whatever assumptions produced the rule of thumb in the first place — but nothing about the rule tells you when that is true, because it has no recovery rate, no resale fee, and no cost stack built into it at all. This calculator does not replace the percentage rule with a different flat number; it prices your break-even and target-margin bid from the actual inputs for the unit in front of you, and reports the naive rule's own bid alongside them so you can see directly whether it over- or under-shoots.

Is this page telling me never to use a rule of thumb?

No — a rule of thumb is a reasonable starting guess when you have thirty seconds at a lockout and no calculator. The point is narrower: know which number you're using. A percentage rule is a guess at what the real math would say; this calculator is the real math, run on the estimate you can make from the doorway. If the two agree, the rule happened to be right this time. If they don't, this page's break-even and target-margin figures are the ones your money is actually exposed to.

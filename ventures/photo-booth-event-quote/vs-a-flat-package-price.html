This calculator vs. a flat "3-hour, $500" photo booth package

"3-hour, $500" is not a price for a photo booth booking — it is a price for one nearby, modestly-printed, single-attendant event, quietly applied to every booking the calendar takes.

What a flat "3-hour, $500" package is

A flat package is the fastest way to sell a booking: one headline number on a website or an Instagram post, read off and repeated. Couples and event planners shop it because it is easy to compare between vendors, and it is the number a client holding a competitor's price sheet will ask you to match on their own event.

The number behind it was set for one booking — usually the cheapest configuration this calculator prices (a short drive, one attendant, a modest print estimate) — and then reused for every 3-hour booking afterward, whether or not the next event looks anything like that one.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the booked hours, setup and teardown minutes, round-trip drive time and mileage, attendant count and pay, expected prints and cost per print, props and equipment cost, and your target margin. It prices the labor, the travel, and the print-volume risk separately, and returns a walk-away price for the booking you describe — for this event, not a generic one.

What a flat package doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $500 whether the venue is a 10-minute drive or an hour away, and whether one attendant staffs it or two — several times the labor and mileage for the same words "3-hour package". Prices round-trip drive time and mileage from the entered numbers, and multiplies labor by attendant count, so bookings of different scope come out at different numbers instead of one.
Prices "unlimited prints" as a fixed line item, with no separate field for how busy the event turns out to be — a seated dinner and an open bar quote the same. Times an entered print estimate by cost per print, and reports what share of the price that line already represents, so a print-heavy event shows up instead of being absorbed into the package.
Prices only the hours a guest sees the booth running, with no line for the setup before doors open or the teardown after they close. Adds setup and teardown minutes to the booked hours before pricing labor, so the attendant's full committed time is charged, not just the visible hours.
Applies a weekend or holiday premium inconsistently, if at all, since there is no separate line to apply it to. Applies the entered weekend/holiday premium to labor and mileage cost specifically, so a peak date is priced differently from a weeknight.
Gives the operator a price to sell, not a number to check a quote against when a client asks to match a cheaper flat rate on a farther, busier event. Reports a walk-away price built from the entered scope, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the booking is confirmed.

A flat package is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one booking it was built for, and nothing about a posted price tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat package skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat "3-hour, $500" photo booth package get the price wrong?

Because the real cost of a booking scales with setup and teardown time, travel distance, attendant count, and how many prints the event actually produces, not with the phrase "3-hour package" alone. A quiet Tuesday corporate dinner close to base is a different booking from a Saturday open-bar wedding an hour's drive away with a second attendant, even though both are sold as the same 3-hour package. A flat price quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat package price usually leave out entirely?

Setup and teardown time, the drive to the venue, and print-volume risk. A published package price is set on the cheapest configuration -- an hour of setup, 45 minutes of teardown, a short drive, one attendant, and a modest print estimate -- and has nowhere to put the extra hours and paper and ink cost of a farther venue, a second attendant, or an open bar that prints far more strips than a seated dinner. An operator pricing off the package alone finds out about the gap only after the event.

How much do travel, a second attendant, and print volume actually add over the flat package?

On this calculator's own worked examples, at the form's own default rates ($25/hr attendant pay, $0.35/print, 10% overhead, 35% target margin): the booking a $500 package is actually sized for -- a 24-mile round trip, one attendant, 150 prints -- walks away at $468.68, comfortably inside the package price. A busier open-bar event at the same 3 booked hours but a 40-mile round trip and 400 prints walks away at $640.38, $140.38 over the package. A Saturday wedding with a second attendant, a 60-mile round trip, and 500 prints walks away at $1,032.50, $532.50 over the package. Travel, attendant count, and print volume -- not the phrase "3-hour package" -- are what move the number.

Is a flat package price ever the right way to quote a booking?

For the one booking it was actually priced on, yes -- this calculator's own default job, a nearby venue with one attendant and a modest print count, walks away under the $500 package price. The package breaks down once a booking moves away from that base case: farther travel, a second attendant, or a busier open bar all add real committed time and print cost that a flat package was never sized to cover.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the booked hours, setup/teardown time, travel, attendant count and pay, expected prints, and your target margin, and turns them into a walk-away price for the booking you describe -- the arithmetic a flat package skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat "3-hour, $500" package is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works for the booking it was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

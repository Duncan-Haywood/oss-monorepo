Deposit or no-show fee — which should I charge?

Both exist to protect a slot on your calendar, but they act at different moments. A deposit is collected when the appointment is booked, before you know how it will go. A no-show fee is only charged afterward, and only if the appointment is missed. Confusing the two leads to the wrong policy — and sometimes to advertising a "no-show fee" that customers never actually see, because nothing was collected up front to enforce it against.

What the numbers look like

On this calculator's defaults — a $150 service, a 25% deposit — the deposit is $37.50, applied to the price, leaving a $112.50 balance due at the appointment. That $37.50 is in hand whether or not the customer eventually shows. At 12 bookings a week and an assumed 93% booking rate once the deposit is required, that is 11.16 bookings a week and $21,762 a year in deposits collected.

That $21,762 is not extra money, and it is the number most easily misread on this page. A deposit is applied to the service total rather than added to it, so the deposits collected in a year are revenue that would have been billed anyway, taken earlier. The question of whether a deposit leaves you better off is a different sum, and the calculator now does it: on the same defaults, plus a 15% no-show rate today and 5% once a deposit is required, a year comes to $79,560 with no deposit against $83,783.70 with one — $4,223.70 better, with break-even at an 88.3% booking rate. Keep more than 88.3% of today's bookings and the deposit is ahead; keep less and it is behind.

A no-show fee has no equivalent up-front number, because nothing is collected until a slot is actually missed. Its size is usually set as a share of the service price — the sibling calculator defaults to 100% of a $75 service, so $75 — but whether it is ever paid depends on being able to charge a card on file after the fact, and on local rules about that.

The two tools answer the two halves and neither repeats the other. This calculator asks whether requiring a deposit is worth what it costs you in bookings. The No-Show & Cancellation Fee Calculator takes the no-shows as given and asks how much of that lost revenue each policy claws back — a fee charged afterward, a forfeited deposit, or a deposit plus the remainder — at an enforcement rate you set for how often an after-the-fact charge actually gets paid. Common questions

Should I charge a deposit or a no-show fee?

They answer different questions. A deposit is collected at booking, before you know whether the customer will show — on this calculator's defaults, $150 at 25% is a $37.50 deposit applied to the price, leaving a $112.50 balance due at the appointment. A no-show fee is only charged after the appointment is missed, and only exists as a policy you enforce afterward. A deposit secures cash and commitment up front; a no-show fee compensates you after the fact for a slot that already went empty.

Can I use both a deposit and a no-show fee?

Yes, and many service businesses do: the deposit is applied to the final price if the customer shows, and forfeited (acting as the no-show fee) if they cancel late or do not show. That is what this calculator's policy draft describes under its cancellation-notice window. A separate, larger no-show fee on top of a forfeited deposit is unusual and should be disclosed clearly before booking.

Which one actually gets paid?

A deposit is easier to collect, because it is taken while the customer is still booking and motivated to secure the slot. A no-show fee is charged after the fact, often against a saved card, and is harder to collect and more likely to be disputed. If cash-in-hand before the appointment matters more than punishing a no-show after it, a deposit is the more reliable mechanism.

Does a deposit stop no-shows?

It changes who books, not just who shows. On this calculator's defaults, requiring a 25% deposit is modeled as keeping 93% of the 12 weekly bookings you'd otherwise get — 11.16 a week — and collecting $21,762 a year in deposits at that rate. Those deposits are not profit: they are revenue you would have billed anyway, collected earlier. What the deposit is actually worth is the difference it makes to the year, which on those defaults plus a 15%-to-5% no-show improvement is $4,223.70, with break-even at an 88.3% booking rate. Some prospective customers who would have booked without a deposit will not book with one; the calculator asks you to estimate that trade-off rather than assuming it away.

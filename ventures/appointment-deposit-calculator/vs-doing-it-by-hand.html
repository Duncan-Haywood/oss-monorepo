This calculator vs. doing it by hand or in a spreadsheet

Deposit and balance are one line of arithmetic—price times percentage, and whatever's left. Anyone can do that by hand or in a spreadsheet. The part that's easy to get wrong, or to skip entirely, is whether requiring a deposit actually leaves you with more money once you account for the customers a deposit turns away and the no-shows it prevents. That's the step this calculator was built for.

What each approach actually does

By hand or in a spreadsheet, you can compute the deposit amount, the balance due, and even a rough sense of how much cash a year of deposits secures. All of that is straightforward multiplication and subtraction, and a correctly built spreadsheet gets it exactly right. What a spreadsheet does not give you, unless you build it in yourself, is a prompt to check whether the deposit is actually worth charging — because “cash secured” on its own looks like a gain even though it is mostly revenue you would have billed anyway, just collected earlier.

This calculator takes the same inputs — service price, deposit percentage, bookings per week, an expected booking rate once a deposit is required, and no-show rates with and without one — and adds the comparison a spreadsheet does not set up on its own: yearly revenue with the deposit against yearly revenue without it, the difference, and the break-even booking rate at which the policy stops paying for itself. It then turns the same inputs into a policy paragraph you can paste into whichever booking system you already use.

Common questions

Can't I just work out the deposit and balance by hand?

Yes — deposit is service price times deposit percentage, and balance is whatever is left. That part is one line of arithmetic and a spreadsheet does it exactly as well as this calculator does. Where a hand calculation or a quick spreadsheet formula stops short is the harder question underneath it: given your own no-show rates and how a deposit changes how many people actually book, does requiring one leave you with more money at the end of the year, or less. That comparison has more moving parts than deposit-times-percentage, and it is the part this calculator exists for.

What does a spreadsheet not check that this calculator does?

Whether the deposit pays for itself. A spreadsheet will happily multiply your weekly bookings by the deposit and call the result "revenue secured" — but that figure is money you would have billed anyway, collected earlier, not new income, and reading it as a gain is the mistake this calculator's own FAQ warns against. The real trade has a cost side too: some prospective customers do not book at all once a deposit is required. A spreadsheet built from scratch has no built-in prompt to model that cost against the benefit of fewer no-shows; this calculator puts both sides of the trade next to each other and shows the break-even booking rate — the share of today's bookings you would need to keep for the policy to be worth it at all.

Is the break-even booking rate something I could work out myself?

With the right formula, yes — it is the booking rate at which yearly revenue with the deposit equals yearly revenue without one, given both no-show rates and the deposit amount. Setting that formula up correctly, and re-deriving it correctly every time a price or a no-show estimate changes, is exactly the kind of one-time-easy, easy-to-get-subtly-wrong-later math this calculator exists to remove. A copied cell reference or a forgotten weighting is not obvious in the output the way a typo in a deposit percentage is.

Does a spreadsheet draft the policy wording too?

Not unless you write it yourself. Once the numbers are decided, the policy still has to say, in plain language, what the deposit is, what the balance is, and what happens if the customer cancels with or without notice — and it has to do that in a way clients actually read. This calculator turns your inputs straight into a policy paragraph you can paste into whichever booking system you already use; a spreadsheet, however good its formulas, still leaves that paragraph for you to draft separately.

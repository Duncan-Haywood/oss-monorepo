How it is calculated

Drop = 2 x one-way length x current x resistance per length for DC and single-phase AC, and 1.732 x length x current x resistance per length for three-phase (line to line). On AC the resistance is replaced by R x power factor + X x sin(angle) when you enter a reactance. Parallel sets divide both by the number of sets. Watts are turned into amps with the voltage (and the power factor and 1.732 on AC).

Resistance is computed, not copied from a table: the AWG area from the standard geometric series (0.46 in for 4/0 down to 0.005 in for 36 AWG), kcmil at 0.5067 mm² each, metric sizes at their nominal area; copper at 1.7241 x 10⁻⁸ ohm m and aluminium at 2.8264 x 10⁻⁸ ohm m at 20 C; times 1 + coefficient x (temperature - 20), with 0.00393 for copper and 0.00403 for aluminium; times 1.02 for stranding. The longest run and most current are the lengths and currents at which each size reaches your target. Wire loss is 2 (or 3) x current² x resistance x length.

Not modelled: ampacity and its corrections, skin and proximity effect in large AC conductors, reactance unless you enter it, unbalanced or harmonic loads, motor starting dips, and the drop across breakers, fuses and terminals. Published resistance tables (and the maximum resistances of metric conductors) can be a few percent higher than the computed values for some sizes; type your table's figure for the size you check. Unverified: checked by hand arithmetic and unit tests only, not against a manufacturer's tool or a code table.

Common questions

How do I calculate voltage drop?

For DC and single-phase AC, voltage drop = 2 x one-way length x current x the conductor's resistance per unit length; the 2 counts the out and return conductors. For three-phase, use 1.732 instead of 2 and the drop is line to line. Divide by the source voltage for the percentage.

What voltage drop is acceptable?

A common design target is 3 percent on a branch circuit and 5 percent from the service to the farthest outlet, the figures in the informational notes of the US National Electrical Code. Low-voltage DC systems on boats and vehicles often use 3 percent for loads that matter and 10 percent for lighting and the like. Your code, the equipment maker and the job decide.

What size wire do I need for 30 amps at 12 volts over 15 feet?

With stranded copper at 75 C and a 3 percent target, 4 AWG: it drops 0.28 V, 2.31 percent. 6 AWG drops 3.68 percent and 8 AWG 5.84 percent. The same 360 W at 120 V needs only 18 AWG for voltage drop, though building wiring has its own minimum sizes.

Does this tool check ampacity?

No. Voltage drop and ampacity are separate checks and the wire must pass both. Ampacity comes from the table your code uses, with its temperature and bundling corrections. Enter the smallest size that table allows and the tool will not recommend anything smaller.

Where do the resistance values come from?

They are computed, not copied: the conductor area from the AWG series, kcmil or the metric size, the resistivity of annealed copper or aluminium at 20 C, the temperature coefficient for the conductor temperature you choose, and about 2 percent extra for stranding. Published tables can be a few percent higher for some sizes, so you can type a resistance from your own table for the size you choose.

Why is the run length one way?

Because that is what you measure on the drawing or with a tape: source to load. The formula doubles it for the return conductor on DC and single-phase circuits.

Which conductor temperature should I use?

The temperature the conductor runs at under load. 75 C is a common choice for building wire sized at its 75 C rating; a lightly loaded conductor runs cooler and has less resistance. 20 C gives the lowest, optimistic drop.

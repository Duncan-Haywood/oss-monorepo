What size wire for 12 volts?

A 30 amp load, a fridge compressor, an inverter or a winch, sits 15 feet from a 12 V battery. The wire that would be generous on a 120 V circuit is far too thin here. The reason is one line of arithmetic.

The formula

On a DC circuit the current goes out on one conductor and back on the other, so the wire length is twice the run. The voltage lost in the wire is:

drop = 2 x one-way length x current x resistance per length

Stranded copper at 75 C has, by the method the calculator uses (area from the AWG series, copper's resistivity, its temperature coefficient and 2 % for stranding), about 1.239 ohm per 1000 ft at 10 AWG and 0.308 ohm per 1000 ft at 4 AWG.

The numbers

10 AWG: 2 x 15 ft x 30 A x 0.001239 ohm/ft = 1.12 V. On 12 V that is 9.29 %: the load sees 10.88 V, and 33.5 W turns into heat in the wire.

With a 3 % target, 4 AWG is the smallest size that works. 6 AWG misses by a little; whether 3.68 % is acceptable depends on the load and the standard you follow (lighting is often allowed more than a motor or electronics).

Why 12 V is so demanding

The same 360 W at 120 V draws 3 A, a tenth of the current. The drop in volts is a tenth too, and as a share of a voltage ten times higher it is a hundredth. Run the same 15 ft at 120 V and even 18 AWG drops only 0.59 %. At 24 V the 360 W draws 15 A and 10 AWG is enough; at 48 V, 7.5 A, and 16 AWG passes the drop test. Doubling the system voltage quarters the copper needed for the same percentage drop.

Voltage drop is only half the answer

The conductor must also carry the current without overheating, which is the ampacity table's job (with its corrections for temperature, bundling and insulation). The size you install is the larger of the two. The calculator takes the smallest size your ampacity table allows and never recommends below it. It also does not include the drop in fuses, breakers, switches and crimped terminals, which on a 12 V system can be a noticeable share of the total.

Questions

What size wire do I need for 30 amps at 12 volts?

Over a 15 ft one-way run with stranded copper at 75 C and a 3 percent target, 4 AWG: it drops 0.28 V, 2.31 percent. Longer runs need larger wire; the drop grows in proportion to the length.

Why does 12 V need so much thicker wire than 120 V?

For the same power, 12 V draws ten times the current of 120 V, so the voltage lost in the wire is ten times larger, and as a share of a supply ten times smaller it is a hundred times larger.

Is voltage drop the only thing that sets the wire size?

No. The wire must also carry the current without overheating, which comes from an ampacity table and its corrections. Install the larger of the two sizes.

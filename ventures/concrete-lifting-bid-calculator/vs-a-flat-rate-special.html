This calculator vs. a flat “$X/sq ft” or “$X/hole” special

Shops get quoted a published $4.50/sq ft or $75/hole special. That rate cannot see the void — an 80 sq ft sidewalk with a one-inch void and the truck at the curb is not the same job as a 400 sq ft backyard driveway with a four-inch void, polyurethane, a hose run, six extra holes, and joint seal.

What a flat rate special is

A per-square-foot or per-hole special is the fastest number to publish or quote over the phone: multiply the footprint, or count the holes, and read back a total. It is what a homeowner finds on a postcard or a competitor's website and asks you to match.

The rate behind it was set from one job — a given void depth, a given method, a given access path, no extra holes, no sealing — and then reused for the next slab that calls, whether or not that slab shares any of those.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the slab area, the void depth, the method — mudjacking or polyurethane — the access path — street-side, backyard, or interior — extra holes past the included set, and whether joint seal is included, plus your own hourly wage, payroll burden, travel and equipment cost, overhead, and target margin. It turns the void into cubic feet the way pump time and fill material actually scale, prices method and access as their own hours, and returns the price, the price per square foot, the effective hourly rate, and the walk-away price — for this lift, not a rate carried over from the last one.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate special get a lifting job wrong?

Because a lift is priced by the void it fills, not by the slab's square footage or its hole count alone. Void cubic feet equal slab square feet times void depth in inches, divided by 12. An 80-square-foot sidewalk settled one inch has about 6.67 cubic feet of void. A 400-square-foot driveway settled four inches has about 133.33 cubic feet -- 20 times as much fill for five times the square footage. A rate learned from the sidewalk and reused on the driveway prices the void as if it barely changed, because square footage and hole count cannot tell a one-inch settle from a four-inch one.

What else does a flat special leave out?

Method, access, and the extras past the coupon. Polyurethane foam costs several times as much per cubic foot as mudjacking slurry, but sets faster and needs less pump time. A backyard hose-and-pump run or an occupied interior floor adds its own access hours that a street-side truck job never has. Extra holes past the included set and joint seal or crack fill after lifting are their own labor and material lines, not something a flat per-square-foot or per-hole number has anywhere to put.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped starting inputs -- a $42 hourly wage, 32% payroll burden, 16% overhead, and a 30% target margin: an 80 square foot sidewalk with a 1-inch void, mudjacked, truck at the curb, walks away at $628.44 and prices at $897.77 -- $11.22 per square foot. A 400 square foot backyard driveway with a 4-inch void, polyjacked, six extra holes, and joint seal walks away at $9,493.96 and prices at $13,562.80. Quoting the driveway at the sidewalk's learned rate of $11.22 per square foot comes to $4,488.87 -- $5,005.09 below the driveway's own walk-away cost, and $9,073.93 short of what this calculator says the job should charge at a 30% target margin.

Is a flat special ever a reasonable way to quote a lift?

For a job close to the one the special was set on -- a small slab, a shallow void, street-side access, no extra holes, no sealing -- a flat rate can land close, and this calculator's own sidewalk example prices near typical special rates. The risk is applying that same rate to a deeper void, foam instead of slurry, backyard or interior access, or extra holes and joint seal stacked on top, because a flat per-square-foot or per-hole number has no way to see any of those until the crew is already on site.

Does this calculator know my local material or labor rates?

No. It has no data on your local slurry or foam supplier's price or your crew's wage. It takes the slab area, void depth, method, access path, extra holes, and joint seal you enter, plus your own hourly wage, payroll burden, travel and equipment cost, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into void cubic feet, crew hours, and a price for this lift -- the arithmetic a flat special skips, not a market survey.

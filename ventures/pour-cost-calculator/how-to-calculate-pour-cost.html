How to calculate pour cost

Pour cost — also called liquor cost percentage — is ingredient cost divided by menu price, as a percentage. It answers one question: of every dollar a drink sells for, how much of it went to the bottle it came from? The rest of this page works through the formula on a real recipe, not a rounded example.

The formula

pour cost % = (ingredient cost of the drink ÷ menu price) × 100

Ingredient cost is the sum of every liquid poured into the glass. Each ingredient's cost is:

(bottle cost ÷ bottle size in ounces) × ounces used

Worked example

A margarita-style drink: 1.5oz of a $24 tequila (a 1L, 33.8oz bottle), 0.75oz of a $20 orange liqueur (a 750ml, 25.4oz bottle), and 1oz of lime juice from a $6, 32oz bottle — sold for $12.

IngredientCost / ozAmount usedCost
Tequila ($24 / 33.8oz)$0.711.5oz$1.07
Orange liqueur ($20 / 25.4oz)$0.790.75oz$0.59
Lime juice ($6 / 32oz)$0.191oz$0.19

Total ingredient cost: $1.84. At a $12 menu price, pour cost is $1.84 ÷ $12 = 15.4% — 4.6 points below a common 20% target, and not a problem: a drink running below target has margin room, not a mistake to fix. Priced to land exactly on a 20% target, this exact recipe would sell for $9.22 — so at $12 it is currently priced $2.78 above what the target alone requires. That number is a margin comparison, not a suggested price change; the market and your own menu decide what to actually charge.

What a small over-pour does to that number

Free-pouring the tequila a quarter-ounce heavy — 1.75oz instead of 1.5oz — adds about $0.18 to this one drink and moves its pour cost from 15.4% to 16.8%. That looks small at the register. At 200 of these drinks a week, it is $35.50 a week, or about $1,846 a year — liquor that left the bottle and never showed up as a cost or a margin anywhere the menu price could see it.

Common questions

What is the pour cost formula?

Pour cost percentage = ingredient cost of the drink ÷ menu price, times 100. The ingredient cost is the sum of every liquid that goes into the glass, each priced as (bottle cost ÷ bottle size in ounces) × ounces used. Nothing about labor, garnish, or overhead belongs in it — it is a liquor cost figure, not a full drink-cost figure.

How do I find the cost per ounce of a bottle?

Divide what the bottle actually cost (from the invoice, not a rounded guess) by its size in ounces. A 1L bottle is 33.8oz; a 750ml bottle is 25.4oz; a 1.75L handle is 59.2oz. A $24 bottle at 33.8oz costs about $0.71 an ounce, and that per-ounce figure is what every pour size gets multiplied by.

Is 20% pour cost good?

20% is a common target for spirits, but it is not a rule — it is a starting point you check your own recipes against. A drink running well under your target has room; a drink running well over it is being sold near or below what it costs once everything else the bar pays for is considered.

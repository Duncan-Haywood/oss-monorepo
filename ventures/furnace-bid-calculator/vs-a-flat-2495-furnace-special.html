This calculator vs. a flat $2,495 furnace special

“$2,495 installed” is not a price for a furnace changeout — it is a price for one specific 60k basement swap, quietly applied to every furnace call that comes in.

What a flat $2,495 furnace special is

A flat installed price is the fastest number a shop can put on a postcard, a search ad, or a truck wrap: one price, no takeoff, easy for a homeowner to compare between bids. It works as a lead magnet, and it works for the homeowner as a round number to shop other companies against. Nothing about the number says which furnace, which location, or which extras it was built for.

The number was set for one job — commonly a smaller 60,000 BTU 80% AFUE furnace, swapped in an accessible basement with no more than the first 15 ft of duct, no matching coil, no gas upsize, no haul-away, and no new thermostat — and then quoted to every caller afterward, whether or not the next furnace looks anything like the one the special was priced on.

What this calculator does instead

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter capacity, equipment type (80% AFUE, 96% condensing, or heat pump), and location (basement, closet, or attic), with duct footage past the included run, a matching coil, a gas upsize, haul-away of the old unit, and a thermostat as their own line items, plus your crew's loaded wage, travel and equipment, overhead, and target margin. It turns that into the price, the effective hourly rate, and a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this furnace, not the furnace the special was built on.

Common questions

Why does a flat $2,495 furnace special get a changeout wrong?

Because the cost driver is capacity, location, and extras, not "a furnace" alone. A 60,000 BTU 80% AFUE basement swap with its first 15 ft of duct included needs a fraction of the labor and material that a 100,000 BTU 96% condensing changeout in an attic needs, with a matching coil, a gas upsize, 40 ft of duct, haul-away of the old unit, and a new thermostat -- none of which scale with a flat installed price. A $2,495 special charges both the same.

What job is the $2,495 number actually built around?

One specific job: a smaller 60,000 BTU 80% AFUE furnace, swapped in an accessible basement or open utility space, with no more than the first 15 ft of duct, no matching coil, no gas upsize, no haul-away, and no new thermostat. It is a marketing price meant to get the phone to ring, not a number derived from any particular home's capacity, location, or extras.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped presets, at its own shipped shop rates ($52/hr crew wage, 34% burden, $155 travel and equipment, 18% overhead, a 35% target margin): even the 60k basement swap the $2,495 number is named for has a walk-away cost of $2,608.58 and a bid price of $4,013.20 -- the flat special already sits $113.58 below that job's own break-even cost, before a dollar of profit, and $1,518.20 below the calculator's own bid price. Applied to a 100k 96% condensing changeout in an attic with a matching coil, a gas upsize, 40 ft of duct, haul-away, and a thermostat, the same $2,495 special is $4,616.30 below that job's own $7,111.30 walk-away cost, and $8,445.47 below the calculator's own bid price.

Is a flat furnace special ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of jobs close to the one the special was built around -- similar capacity, basement or open-utility access, no coil, no gas upsize, no haul-away -- a flat number can land close, though on this calculator's own worked example even that job already runs slightly under cost. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the postcard price does not know which band a given job is in and neither does the crew until capacity, location, and extras are actually spelled out.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor or equipment rates. It takes the capacity, equipment type, location, duct footage, coil, gas upsize, haul-away, thermostat, and your own crew wage, burden, and overhead, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat special skips, not a market survey.

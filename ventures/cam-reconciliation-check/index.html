    Common questions

    How is the tenant's area share calculated?

    Tenant square footage divided by the building's total rentable area. On the defaults — a 1,000 sq ft suite in a 10,000 sq ft building — that is 10.00%. This is the pro-rata share most CAM clauses use, but some leases fix a share by exhibit instead of by current measured area; check the lease's own definition before treating the ratio as automatic.

    What does “modeled tenant share” mean, and how is it different from the billed amount?

    Modeled tenant share is annual CAM expenses multiplied by the area share — on the defaults, $120,000 × 10.00% = $12,000. The billed amount is whatever the landlord's statement actually charged, net of credits: $10,000 − $500 = $9,500 net billed. The variance ($12,000 − $9,500 = $2,500) is the arithmetic gap between what the area ratio alone would produce and what the statement actually asked for — it is a starting point for review, not proof of an error.

    What does a positive variance mean, and what does a negative one mean?

    A positive variance (modeled share above net billed, as in the $2,500 default) means the area-ratio math produces a bigger number than what was billed — worth checking that exclusions, caps, or a different pro-rata method in the lease account for the gap before assuming an undercharge. A negative variance means net billed is above the modeled share, which is the direction worth checking for an overcharge, an estimate true-up, or a category that should not have been included at all.

    What is a CAM gross-up, and when should I use it?

    A gross-up clause lets a landlord adjust the occupancy-sensitive share of CAM expenses (utilities, janitorial — the costs that track how many suites are occupied, not fixed costs like a management fee) up to what they would have cost at a stated target occupancy, usually 95%, when the building actually ran below that. Leave the gross-up fields blank unless your lease has this clause; entering the variable expense share and the actual and target occupancy percentages adds the adjustment to the modeled expenses before the area share is applied, and the worksheet shows the dollar amount added so it can be checked against the lease's own clause.

    What should I actually check before disputing a CAM bill?

    Three things in order: that the expense total and the statement cover the same lease year, that rentable and tenant area match the lease's own definitions (not just the current rent roll), and that every category on the statement is one the lease actually allows to be passed through. This tool only checks the arithmetic between those inputs — it does not read the lease and cannot tell you whether a category belongs on the bill.

    Does this replace a lawyer, accountant, or property manager?

    No. It is a private arithmetic worksheet for spotting a variance worth asking about. Whether that variance is allowed, owed, or worth disputing depends on the lease's own reconciliation clause, which only a qualified reviewer of the actual document can answer.

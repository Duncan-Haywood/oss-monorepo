This calculator vs. a flat price-per-foot quote

"$35 a foot installed" is not a price for a fence — it is a price for one flat run with no gates, quietly applied to a corner-heavy yard with tear-out and hard digging.

What a flat price-per-foot quote is

A flat per-foot number is the fastest way to bid over the phone or match a homeowner's forum post: one figure, easy to multiply by any run length, and the number a caller holding a competitor's estimate will ask you to match on their own yard.

The number behind it was set for one job — usually a flat, easy run with no corners, no gates, normal soil, and nothing to tear out — and then reused for every fence afterward, whether or not the next yard has terminals at every corner, a walk gate and a drive gate, an old fence to pull, or clay that slows every post hole.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter each run's length, post spacing, corners, and gates, the existing fence to tear out, the digging condition and slope, and your own material and labor rates. It prices posts, terminals, gates, tear-out, and the extra hours harder digging adds separately, and returns a walk-away price and price per foot below which the job loses money — for this yard, not a generic one.

What a flat price-per-foot quote doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate whether the run is a flat chain-link stretch with no gates or a corner-heavy privacy fence — counting terminal posts the same as cheaper line posts either way. Counts posts from run length and spacing, then upgrades ends, corners, and gate posts to heavier terminals, so a corner-heavy yard prices differently from a flat run.
Has no separate line for a walk gate or a drive gate, so hardware and hang time are absorbed into the same per-foot number as plain infill. Subtracts gate openings from infill and prices gate material and hang hours per gate, scaled to walk or drive width.
Prices "a fence" the same whether or not an old one has to come out first. Adds tear-out hours and haul cost per foot only when there is an existing fence to pull.
Has no line for clay, rock, or a sloped yard, so a hard dig gets the same price as a flat, easy one. Multiplies labor hours by a dig-condition and slope multiplier entered for the actual site.
Gives the homeowner a price to shop, not a number to check a quote against when their yard turns out to have three corners and 40 feet of rotten fence to remove after the flat rate was already promised. Reports the walk-away price and price per foot for the job as entered, so a shopped rate can be scored against real cost before it is confirmed.

A flat price-per-foot quote is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied to a run, corner count, gate set, or digging condition it was not set on, and "a fence" alone never tells you that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat price-per-foot fence quote get the price wrong?

Because fence cost scales with posts, terminals, gates, tear-out, and digging conditions — not with linear feet alone. A flat run of chain-link with no gates and easy digging is a different job from the same footage in 6-foot wood privacy with two corners, a walk gate, a drive gate, tear-out of the old fence, and heavy clay. A flat per-foot price quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat price-per-foot quote usually leave out entirely?

Terminal posts at corners and gates, gate material and hang time, tear-out of an existing fence, and the extra labor hours that clay, rock, or a slope add to every hole. A published per-foot number is set on one configuration — usually a flat, easy run with no gates and normal soil — and has no separate line for any of those.

How much do corners, gates, and tear-out actually add over a flat rate?

On this calculator's own worked examples, at a $25 hourly wage, 28% burden, 15% overhead, and a 25% target margin: 140 feet of chain-link with no corners, no gates, and no tear-out walks away at $2,532.76, comfortably under a $35/ft, $4,900.00 quote. This calculator's own shipped default job — 140 feet of 6-foot wood privacy with two corners, a walk gate, a drive gate, and 40 feet of tear-out in heavy clay — walks away at $6,033.93, $1,133.93 short of that same $35/ft number. A 220-foot vinyl-privacy run with three corners, two gates, 60 feet of tear-out, rocky digging, and a steep slope walks away at $16,261.98, $8,561.98 short of a $35/ft, $7,700.00 quote.

Is a flat price-per-foot quote ever the right way to bid a fence?

For something close to the easy run it was probably priced on — flat ground, no corners, no gates, nothing to tear out — a flat rate can land close, and on this calculator's own numbers that easy job clears a $35/ft quote with over $2,100 to spare. The risk is corners, gates, tear-out, and hard digging stacking on top of each other, because a flat per-foot price has no way to see any of them until the crew is already on site.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local material costs or what competitors charge. It takes the runs, posts, gates, tear-out, and digging conditions you enter, plus your own wage, burden, and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat per-foot number skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat price-per-foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the run, gates, and ground it was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

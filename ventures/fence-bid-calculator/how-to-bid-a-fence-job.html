How to bid a fence job without a flat price per foot

Homeowners google “fence installation cost per foot” and call you with a number they found on a forum. If you match it, you have quoted their yard as if it were a roll of fencing. It is not. After using the fence bid calculator, you can send a price that already counted the posts, the gates, and the tear-out — work that used to hide inside a scratch-pad per-foot guess.

Count the posts first

A run of L feet on spacing S needs a post at each end plus one every S feet: ceil(L ÷ S) + 1. A 140-foot backyard on 8-foot centers is 19 posts, not 140 ÷ 8 = 17.5 rounded however feels lucky. Each corner adds a terminal that is not already on that straight spacing.

Ends, corners, and gate posts are terminals — heavier stock, more concrete, more time. Line posts are cheaper. If you price every post as a line post, the hardware invoice is already over the bid.

Gates are not fence

A 4-foot walk gate and a 12-foot drive gate remove 16 feet of infill and add two kits plus hang time. Treating those 16 feet as privacy panels understates material and understates labor. The calculator subtracts the openings from infill and prices the kits and hours separately.

Tear-out and dirt are hours, not footage

Pulling 40 feet of rotten stockade does not add 40 feet you can bill at the new-fence rate. It adds haul, dump, and crew time. Clay and rock do not change the panel price; they change how long each hole takes. Multiply labor hours by the site, not the material.

Build the number the same way every time

  1. Split the job into runs (front vs back, wood vs chain-link).
  2. Count posts from spacing, then upgrade terminals for ends, corners, and gates.
  3. Price infill, posts, gates, concrete, and tear-out from your last invoices.
  4. Load the wage with payroll burden. Add permits, dump, and travel.
  5. Recover overhead as a percent of that direct cost. Set margin as a share of the price, not a markup on cost.
  6. Read the walk-away number — total cost — before you answer “can you do it for $35 a foot?”

A number that looks cheap is usually underwater

On a typical 140-foot, 6-foot wood privacy run with two corners, one walk gate, one drive gate, 40 feet of tear-out, and heavy clay, a $35/ft Facebook quote is below cost once posts and gates are counted. The installed price per foot on that job is a result, not an input. Quote the job, then glance at the per-foot figure to see whether you are in the range the homeowner has heard — and whether matching it would lose money.

Common questions

How do you calculate how many fence posts you need?

Posts along a run equal ceil(linear feet ÷ post spacing) + 1, plus one extra terminal for each corner. A 140-foot run on 8-foot centers is 19 posts; two corners make 21. Ends, corners, and gate posts are terminals and cost more than line posts.

Why is a flat fence price per foot often wrong?

A flat installed price assumes every foot is the same. Gates replace infill but add hardware and hang time. Corners and ends need heavier terminals and more concrete. Tear-out and rocky digging add hours with no extra footage to bill. Two 140-foot jobs can differ by thousands of dollars.

What should a fence bid include besides material?

Loaded labor (wage plus payroll burden), concrete, gate hardware, tear-out and haul, permits and travel, overhead, and a target margin as a share of the price — not a markup on cost. The walk-away number is total cost, below which the job makes nothing.

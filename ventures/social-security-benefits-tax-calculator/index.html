Common questions

    
How is Social Security taxed?

    
Up to 50% or 85% of your benefits can be included in taxable income, depending on your provisional income: your other income (including tax-exempt interest) plus half of your benefits. At or under $25,000 single or $32,000 married filing jointly none is taxable. Between the two bases up to 50% is. Above $34,000 single or $44,000 joint up to 85% is. It is a share of benefits that gets taxed, not a tax rate.

    
What is the tax torpedo?

    
In the zone where taxable benefits are rising, each extra dollar of income (an IRA withdrawal, a Roth conversion, a capital gain) also makes up to 85 cents more of your benefits taxable, so it is taxed as up to $1.85. At a 22% bracket that is an effective 40.7% on the next dollar. Above the 85% ceiling the effect stops.

    
Do the $25,000 and $32,000 bases rise with inflation?

    
No. They were set in 1984 and 1993 and are not indexed, so more retirees cross them every year. This calculator lets you edit both bases in case the law changes.

    
What counts as other income?

    
Adjusted gross income without the benefits (wages, pensions, IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, interest, dividends, capital gains, rental and self-employment income) plus tax-exempt interest. Roth IRA withdrawals that are qualified are not income.

    
Does this include state tax?

    
No. It is the federal worksheet only. Most states do not tax Social Security, but a few do under their own rules.

    
Does this tell me what to do?

    
No. It is a worksheet that does arithmetic on the figures you enter. Check IRS Publication 915 or a tax professional before changing a withdrawal plan.

    
Is my data uploaded anywhere?

    
No. Everything is worked out in your browser and nothing is sent; a test in the code asserts there are no network calls.

This calculator vs. the "$10 per 1,000 followers" rule

The flat rule multiplies your whole follower count by a constant and calls that the price. This calculator multiplies only the followers who actually engage, using the same kind of creator-economy benchmark ranges — and for most real accounts, those two numbers are not close.

What each one prices

The "$10 per 1,000 followers" rule is a mental-math shortcut, not a calculation: take your total follower count, divide by 1,000, multiply by roughly $10 (sources vary from $5 to $25), and quote that. It needs one number, gives the same answer for a Story as for a dedicated video, and cannot tell a 100,000-follower account nobody interacts with from a 100,000-follower account whose audience shows up every time — because it never asks.

This calculator multiplies the same kind of CPM benchmark ranges against your engaged followers — your follower count times your own engagement rate — and varies the range by deliverable and platform. A brand is paying for people who see and react to the post, not for a number on a profile, so the two only produce the same price when your engagement rate happens to be unrealistically high.

Common questions

What is the "$10 per 1,000 followers" rule?

A pricing shortcut floated across influencer-marketing blogs and rate-card templates: multiply your total follower count by roughly $10 per 1,000 to get a sponsored-post price, sometimes with a range like $5-$25 depending on the source. It needs only one number, follower count, and gives the same answer to every account that size regardless of platform, deliverable, or how many of those followers ever open the app.

Why does a flat per-follower rule overstate the price so often?

Because it prices your entire follower count as if all of it were engaged, and for almost every real account only a small fraction is. This calculator applies its CPM benchmark ranges to engaged followers — count multiplied by engagement rate — not raw follower count, so the two only agree at an engagement rate high enough that essentially nobody hits it in practice.

Does a higher engagement rate close the gap?

It shrinks the gap but in this calculator's benchmark ranges it does not close it. A well above-average, highly engaged small account still prices lower under engaged-follower CPM than under the flat $10-per-1,000 rule — the flat rule overstates less for a highly engaged audience, but it is not built to ever land below what the engagement-adjusted math supports.

Does the flat rule change by deliverable, the way this calculator does?

No — a flat per-follower rule gives the same number whether you are pricing a 24-hour Story or a dedicated long-form video, because it has no input for deliverable type at all. This calculator's benchmark ranges price a Story lower than a feed post and a dedicated video higher than either, because a brand is buying a different amount of attention in each case.

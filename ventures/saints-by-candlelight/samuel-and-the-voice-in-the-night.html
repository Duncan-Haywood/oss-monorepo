Samuel and the Voice in the Night

Full script

It is very late, and very dark, and a boy of about twelve is lying on a mat in a stone room, trying to sleep.

He is not very good at it tonight. He has been told, quite firmly, to go to sleep, and he is doing his best, but he has a feeling that someone has just said his name.

"Samuel."

He sits up. He listens. Somewhere down the passage, an old man is snoring like a very large, very tired bellows. The lamp by the sacred chest is burning low. Nothing else moves.

So the boy gets up, pads down the passage in the dark, and shakes the old man by the shoulder.

"Here I am," he says. "You called me."

And the old man, who was in the middle of a dream about a really excellent lamb, says what any of us would say.

"I did not call you. Go back to bed."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the first book of Samuel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and we will tell it straight, as it is written. A few of the small conversations and a good deal of the weather are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will tell you at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

First, a word about the book we are in.

The first book of Samuel is part of the long history of Israel, and it sits at a very particular moment. Before it, Israel is a loose family of tribes in the hill country, with no king, and with judges, who were something between a war leader and a village magistrate and a person people went to when they could not settle an argument. After it comes the first king, and then the second, and then David, and all the music and the trouble that follows.

So this book is a hinge. It is the place where one age turns into the next. And the person who stands on the hinge, and who anoints both of the first two kings, is a prophet named Samuel.

One small note on names. If you grew up with the Greek Bible, as the Orthodox Church did, this book is called the first book of Kings, and the books we call Samuel and Kings are numbered as one long set. Same story, different shelf label. It is a bit like two libraries filing the same novel under different numbers. The book is the same book, and the boy in it is the same boy.

But it does not begin with the boy. It begins, as most good stories do, with his mother.

Her name was Hannah, and the book says she was one of the two wives of a man named Elkanah, who lived in the hill country of Ephraim and who went up every year to the sanctuary at a town called Shiloh to worship and to offer sacrifice.

Now, the Scripture is quite honest about this household, and so should we be. It was not an easy one. Elkanah had two wives, which was allowed in that age and which, as a quick glance at the book of Genesis will tell you, never once made anybody happy. The other wife, Peninnah, had children. Hannah had none. And the book says, in a sentence that is almost unbearably plain, that Peninnah used to provoke her bitterly, year after year, whenever they went up to the house of the Lord.

You can picture it. A long road, a crowd of families walking to a festival, children running ahead, and in the middle of all the happy noise, one woman who has learned to keep her face still.

Elkanah loved her. The book says so. When he gave out the portions of the sacrificial meal he gave Hannah a double portion, because he loved her, though the Lord had closed her womb. And he said, in what is one of the most recognisable husband sentences in all of Scripture, "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? Why is your heart sad? Am I not more to you than ten sons?"

I would like to say something kind about Elkanah, because he is trying. He really is. He is a good man who has run out of things to say, and who has decided that a double portion of meat and a heartfelt question is the best he can do. He cannot fix it. He can only sit next to it. And, to be fair to him, that is sometimes the whole of what love can do.

But Hannah could not eat. She got up, after the meal at Shiloh, and she went to the sanctuary.

Here is the scene, and it is the heart of the first chapter.

At the doorpost of the sanctuary sat an old priest named Eli. Eli was the priest at Shiloh, the one the whole region went to, and he sat on his seat by the door like a man who has seen everything pass through that door at least twice.

And in came a woman, in deep bitterness of soul, and she prayed to the Lord and wept. And she made a vow. She said, and this is nearly her exact wording, "O Lord of hosts, if you will look on the affliction of your servant, and remember me, and not forget your servant, but will give your servant a son, then I will give him to the Lord all the days of his life."

She prayed for a long time. And she prayed without making a sound. The book tells us that her lips moved, but her voice could not be heard.

Now. Eli was watching her mouth, and it is here that we meet the old priest at his least impressive, and also, I think, at his most human. He looked at a woman whose lips were moving and who was swaying a little and who was clearly in some kind of distress, and he drew the only conclusion available to a tired man who had seen a lot of festivals.

"How long will you go on being drunk?" he said. "Put away your wine."

I want you to feel how much that must have hurt. She has come to the one place in the world where she is trying to be as honest with God as a person can be, and the priest, of all people, has just told her she is tipsy.

And Hannah, bless her, does not shout. She does not storm off. She says, very steadily, "No, my lord. I am a woman sorely troubled in spirit. I have drunk neither wine nor strong drink. I have been pouring out my soul before the Lord. Do not take your servant for a worthless woman. I have been speaking out of my great anxiety and vexation."

And Eli, to his great credit, hears her. He stops. He looks at her properly. And he says, "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition which you have made to him."

I like that very much. It is a small moment, and it shows us something about Eli that we will need to remember. He can be wrong. He can be quick and tired and unfair. And he can also stop, and listen, and bless. Hold on to that. We shall need it later.

The book says that the woman went her way, and ate, and her face was no longer sad.

Notice what had changed. The book does not say that she had been given a child. She hadn't yet. All that had happened was that she had said it all to God out loud, or silently, and been blessed by a priest who had got it wrong and then got it right. And her face was no longer sad.

In due time, the book says, Hannah conceived and bore a son, and she called his name Samuel, saying, "I have asked him of the Lord."

Now, there is a little scholarly wrinkle in the name, and I will mention it once and then leave it alone. The Hebrew name, Shemuel, sounds like the words for "heard of God", and also sounds a bit like the word for "asked", and the book plays on both. A name that says, in one breath, that she asked and that God heard. If you wanted to name a child so that every time you called him in for dinner you reminded yourself of a prayer, that would do nicely.

And here is the part of the story that I find the hardest and the most beautiful.

She had made a vow. She had promised that if God gave her a son, she would give him back. And when the boy was weaned, which in that age could be at three years old or so, she kept it.

She did not argue with herself. She did not find a clever reason why the vow had really meant something smaller. She took the boy up to Shiloh, with a three-year-old bull, a measure of flour and a skin of wine, and she found the old priest, and she said something I would like you to hear very slowly.

"Oh, my lord! As you live, my lord, I am the woman who was standing here in your presence, praying to the Lord. For this child I prayed, and the Lord has granted me my petition which I made to him. Therefore I have lent him to the Lord. As long as he lives, he is lent to the Lord."

She was the woman who had been standing there. Eli had forgotten her face, as people forget the faces of those they have misjudged. She had not forgotten his.

And then the book says, so quietly that it is easy to read past it: "And they worshiped the Lord there."

I will not pretend that I know what that was like. I only know that if a mother brings her small child to a stranger's house and leaves him there, and if she really means it as a gift and not a loss, then it takes a very large heart indeed.

I will tell you what she does next, because it is the thing I love most about her. She sings. The second chapter of the book is not a lament. It is a song, the Song of Hannah, and it begins, "My heart exults in the Lord; my strength is exalted in my God." It goes on to praise the God who lifts up the poor from the dust, who raises the needy from the ash heap, who makes the barren woman bear seven children, and who brings down the mighty from their thrones.

If that sounds familiar, it should. Hundreds of years later, another young woman, at the beginning of another story, opens her mouth and sings a song that is full of the same words, about the hungry filled with good things and the lowly lifted up. The Gospel of Luke gives us the Magnificat, and a reader who knows Hannah's song hears her in it at once. Both traditions have noticed this for as long as there have been traditions. A song first sung by a woman who gave her child to the Lord, echoed by the woman who would give hers.

I think it is a rather lovely thing to know, as you lie in the dark.

So Samuel stays at Shiloh.

And the book tells us in a few warm sentences how he grew up. He ministered before the Lord, a boy wearing a linen garment, a little robe of the sort a young attendant would wear. And every year, Hannah made him a little robe and took it up to him when she came with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice.

I want to dwell on this robe, because the book dwells on it, in the middle of a chapter full of large matters. A little robe, made every year, a size bigger each time.

You can see her sitting at home through the winter, with the wool in her lap, measuring him in her mind. He was about this high last year. He must have grown. Better make it a bit longer in the arm. And then the long road up to Shiloh, and the robe held up in front of him, and the boy who has grown another hand's breadth and who is trying to look dignified in front of the priests while his mother fusses with the sleeves.

And Eli blesses Elkanah and Hannah, and says, "May the Lord give you children by this woman for the loan which she lent to the Lord." And the book says that she conceived and bore three more sons and two daughters, and that meanwhile the boy Samuel grew in the presence of the Lord.

I have always found that a gentle detail. She gave one, and she was given five more. The book does not say that this was a bargain. It just says that this is what happened. But I think a listener is allowed to smile.

Now, not everything at Shiloh was well, and the book is honest about that too.

Eli was old. His sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests as well, and the book is blunt about them. It says they were worthless men, who did not know the Lord. They took more than their share of the sacrificial meat by sending a servant with a three-pronged fork to stab into the pot and keep whatever it brought up. They demanded their portion raw, before the fat was offered. In short, they were using the holy place as a kind of private buffet.

And Eli, who was old and who had heard it all, said a few words to them. He said, "Why do you do such things? I hear of your evil dealings from all the people. No, my sons, it is no good report that I hear." And he did nothing else.

Remember that. A mild word, said once. He said it with sorrow, and then he let it lie.

I do not think Eli was a wicked man. I think he was a tired and loving father who could not bring himself to do the hard thing, and who hoped that if he said nothing very loud, it would somehow right itself. Most of us have met that hope. Most of us have been it.

And the book gives us one of its quietest and strangest sentences, in the middle of this tangled house. It says: "The word of the Lord was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision."

Rare. Think about what it is to live in a place like that. The holy house is still standing. The lamp is still lit. The priests are still in their robes. But they have stopped being able to hear anything.

And in the middle of that silence, a boy is growing, a size bigger every year, sleeping in the house of the Lord.

So here we are at the night in the title. And I want to set it up carefully, because the book does.

The book says that Eli, whose eyesight had begun to grow dim, so that he could not see well, was lying down in his own place. And the lamp of God had not yet gone out. And Samuel was lying down within the temple of the Lord, where the ark of God was.

Let me give you the picture, a room at a time.

There is an old man in his bed, in a room off to one side. His eyes are failing, and he has the look of someone who has stopped expecting surprises. In the main room there is a lamp, a good oil lamp that is meant to burn through the night, and it is down to its last few hours. The book mentions it for a reason: the lamp had not yet gone out. It is almost the end of the night, and the lamp is still burning, and it is almost, but not quite, morning.

And there is a boy on a mat near the sacred chest, the ark of God. He is about twelve. He is wearing his linen garment, or maybe he has taken it off for sleep. He is a very ordinary boy, who has been told to go to bed and is lying there with his eyes wide open, looking at the ceiling, thinking about whatever twelve-year-olds think about in the dark.

And then the Lord called, "Samuel! Samuel!"

And he said, "Here I am!" And he ran to Eli, and said, "Here I am, for you called me."

But Eli said, "I did not call; lie down again."

And he went and lay down.

Now, I want you to notice what Samuel does, because it is a thing I admire. He does not say, "I must have been dreaming." He does not roll over and pull the blanket up. He hears his name, and he gets up, and he runs. The book says he ran. In the middle of the night, to an old man whom he trusts, to be of use.

He is, whatever else you can say about him, a very good boy.

And the book tells us something curious, in a small explanatory aside. It says: "Now Samuel did not yet know the Lord, and the word of the Lord had not yet been revealed to him."

That is not an accusation. He is a boy of twelve who has grown up in the sanctuary, who knows all the words and all the rituals and the songs, and who has never yet been spoken to. He knows about God the way you know about the sea when you have grown up a long way from it. He has been taught it all, with great love. He has not yet heard it.

So the Lord called again, "Samuel!" And Samuel arose and went to Eli, and said, "Here I am, for you called me."

I imagine that second visit with a little more yawning. The passage is a long one, and the stone is cold underfoot, and the old man has just fallen asleep again.

"My son, I did not call; lie down again."

And again, a third time, "Samuel!" And again he gets up and he goes.

Nobody in this story is being foolish. I would like to say that clearly. Samuel is not being slow. He is being obedient, and respectful, and he does exactly what a well-brought-up boy would do on hearing his name in the night in a house with exactly one other person in it. And Eli is not being unkind. He is half asleep and he is quite sure he did not call.

It is only the third time that the old man stops.

The book says: "Then Eli perceived that the Lord was calling the boy."

I find this one of the finest moments in the whole of Scripture, and I want to say why.

Remember Eli. He is the old priest whose sons are a scandal. He is the man who spoke a mild word and then did nothing. He has been, for some time now, in the position of a man who has lost the thread. And on the third time, in the dark, with a sleepy child at his bed, something clears. He perceives. He understands, and it is not the boy's voice he is hearing but the quiet around it.

And what he does with that understanding is exactly right. He does not take it for himself. He does not say, "Ah, let me go and see what God has to say." He turns, and he gives the boy what a priest is for. He teaches him how to listen.

"Go, lie down," he says, "and if he calls you, you shall say, 'Speak, Lord, for your servant hears.'"

That is the sentence. If you take only one thing from this whole long evening, take that. It is simple enough for a child to say, and deep enough that people have been saying it for three thousand years. Speak, Lord, for your servant hears.

It does not say, "Speak, Lord, and I will tell you what I think about it." It does not say, "Speak, Lord, as long as it is good news." It says, I am listening. Say it.

And so Samuel went and lay down in his place.

I would love to know what he thought about, in those last minutes of waiting. Whether he was frightened. Whether he practised the words in his head. Whether he lay very still, the way you do when you are trying not to scare away a bird.

And the Lord came and stood, the book says, and called as at other times, "Samuel! Samuel!"

Notice the words: the Lord came and stood. Not a voice from nowhere. A presence. Something stood in that room, near a boy on a mat.

And Samuel said, "Speak, for your servant hears."

You will have noticed that he left off one word. Eli taught him to say, "Speak, Lord." The book gives us only, "Speak, for your servant hears." I have read a number of explanations, and some say it is just the shortened form of a boy in a hurry, and some say it is a mark of how quietly it was spoken. I do not know. I like to think that his heart was going so fast that he forgot the first word and was too honest to make it up.

It does not matter. He said the part that counts.

And now we come to the thing the story has to tell us next, and I want to be gentle about it, because the Lord does not give the boy a pleasant message.

The Lord said to Samuel, "Behold, I am about to do a thing in Israel at which the two ears of every one who hears it will tingle. On that day I will fulfil against Eli all that I have spoken concerning his house, from beginning to end. For I have told him that I am about to punish his house for ever, for the iniquity which he knew, because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them."

I will not dress that up. It is hard. The first word God ever speaks to this child is a word about the old man sleeping down the passage, the one who has just taught him how to listen.

And here is what I would ask you to notice, because it matters for how we hear it. The charge is not that Eli was cruel. It is not that he was greedy. It is that he knew, and he did not restrain them. A father who loved his sons too softly, who said a mild word and then let it lie, and who let the house of God become the place where the poor were cheated of the sacrifices they had brought in good faith. The book is not telling us that God is unfair. It is telling us that the silence of a good man can do real harm, and that a holy house cannot be run on mild words alone.

We will not say more about what happens to Eli's house. It is a grave matter, and the book tells it in its proper place, and it is not a bedtime matter. What matters tonight is a single boy in the dark with something heavy in his hands.

Samuel lay there until morning.

Think about that. He has had the most extraordinary experience of his life. He has been addressed, by name, by God. And the first thing he has been told is something he cannot bear to repeat. He has no one to ask. He cannot go back to the old man in the middle of the night and say, "Good news, I heard it, and it is about you." So he lies there, with his eyes open, until the grey comes up in the window and the lamp finally goes out.

Then he got up, and opened the doors of the house of the Lord. That was his work. Every morning, the boy opened the doors. Even that morning, the book says. He did the ordinary task. He opened the doors.

And, the book says, "Samuel was afraid to tell the vision to Eli."

I love how honest that is. The great prophet of Israel, who will go on to anoint kings, begins with a boy who is afraid to speak to his old teacher at breakfast.

But Eli called him. "Samuel, my son."

And he said, "Here I am."

"What was it that he told you? Do not hide it from me. May God do so to you and more also, if you hide anything from me of all that he told you."

I want to say something now about Eli, and then I will leave him.

He knew. Of course he knew. It was not hard to guess. He had seen a boy come to his bed three times in the night, and he had sent him back with a prayer, and now here was the child in the morning with a face like a shut door. An old man does not need much to work out what has been said.

And he does not make it easier for the boy. He does not say, "Never mind, child, I can guess." He says, tell me. All of it. Hide nothing.

So Samuel told him everything, and hid nothing from him.

And Eli said, "It is the Lord. Let him do what seems good to him."

That is all. It is not an angry sentence. It is not even a very long one. It is the sentence of a man who has just been told the worst, by a child, at breakfast, and who answers with something that is, I think, the best thing he ever said. It is the same prayer he taught the boy to pray in the night, said back to him in a different voice. It is the Lord. Let him do what seems good.

Whatever else you say about Eli, he did not shoot the messenger.

The last paragraph of the third chapter is a summary, and it is one of my favourites in the whole book.

It says: "And Samuel grew, and the Lord was with him and let none of his words fall to the ground."

None of his words fall to the ground. What a thing to be said about a person. Not that he was brilliant, or powerful, or even that he was always right in the way we count rightness. Only that when he spoke, what he said had weight, as if every word he picked up was something that had been put into his hand first, and so it did not slip.

And all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel was established as a prophet of the Lord. Dan was at the far north and Beersheba at the far south, so this is the book's way of saying everywhere, from the top of the map to the bottom, the way we might say from coast to coast.

And the chapter ends with a sentence that I think is the real point of the whole night: "And the Lord appeared again at Shiloh, for the Lord revealed himself to Samuel at Shiloh by the word of the Lord."

The word that had been rare was not rare any more. It had come back, and it had come back through a twelve-year-old who got up three times in the dark, and an old man who finally worked out what was going on.

Let me bring us down a little now, and take a last look around.

There are a good many things in this story that are for grown-ups, and I will not pretend there aren't. There is a long account of what happens to Eli's sons, and to the sanctuary, and to the sacred chest, and it does not belong in tonight's hour. There is a prophet who grows older and who has to learn that the people want a king, and there is a king who begins well and does not finish well, and there is a boy shepherd with a sling. That is for other nights.

Tonight we only need to stay in the room where it begins.

Both traditions have loved this story. Her song is one of the biblical odes the Orthodox Church sings in its services. The prophet Samuel is remembered on the twentieth of August. And the little sentence, Speak, Lord, for your servant hears, has been prayed at the beginning of an awful lot of mornings, by monks, by mothers, by schoolchildren, and by people who did not know what else to say.

I notice that nothing in the story needs anyone to be clever. Hannah is not clever. Eli is not clever, though he is quick in the end. Samuel is a boy who gets up when he hears his name. What it asks of them is something simpler and harder: to pay attention, and to say yes before they know what the question is.

Close your eyes now, if you haven't already. Let your breathing slow.

Somewhere there is a stone room, and an old man snoring like a bellows, and a lamp that has not yet gone out. There is a boy on a mat, with his eyes open, looking up at the dark. He is not afraid. He has been taught the one sentence he needs.

And if, tonight, you hear your name, in the quiet, in the half-sleep, at the edge of a thought, you do not have to run anywhere. You do not have to understand it. You only have to lie still, and say it, the way a boy said it once, nearly three thousand years ago.

Speak. Your servant is listening.

Jethro and the Judges for the Small Cases

Jethro, the priest of Midian, brings Moses his wife and two sons across the desert to the mountain of God, hears everything the Lord has done, and blesses him; and the next morning he watches his son-in-law sit from dawn till dark with the whole people queuing in front of him, and gives him the oldest good advice in the Bible. Told from the Book of Exodus, chapter eighteen.

What's true in this story

Who
Jethro the priest of Midian, Moses, Zipporah, Gershom and Eliezer, Aaron and the elders of Israel, and the able men who were made judges over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Book of Exodus as Scripture; Moses is honoured as a saint and prophet in both traditions; Jethro has no feast day of his own in either
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Book of Exodus, chapter eighteen, told straight in a plain rendering, with Moses' own retelling in Deuteronomy, chapter one; the book gives no date in our years and historians disagree about when the Exodus happened, so none is given
Legend
none told as fact; the different names the Bible gives Moses' father-in-law are mentioned as the text has them, without settling how they fit together
What we invented
the people in the queue and their small cases, the goat, the cooking pot, the tent peg and the boy with the question, Jethro's journey, his thoughts, the talk between him and his grandsons, and every word not given in the chapter are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; nothing is added to what Jethro, Moses or the people do in the book, or to the advice itself
Feast day
Moses the prophet and lawgiver is remembered on the fourth of September in the Orthodox Church and in the Roman Martyrology; Jethro has no feast day

Moses' own retelling, with his words to the new judges, is from Deuteronomy, chapter one. Hobab is from Numbers, chapter ten, and the Kenites from Judges, chapter one.

The Bible calls Moses' father-in-law Reuel in chapter two and Jethro afterwards, and Hobab appears in Numbers, and how the names fit together has been read in more than one way; we have not tried to settle it. The book does not say when or why Moses sent Zipporah and the boys back, and we have not claimed to know. Some readers, ancient and modern, have wondered whether Jethro's visit came before or after the Law was given on the mountain; we have told it where the book puts it.

That the Torah portion beginning with this chapter is called Yitro is the custom of the synagogue.

Jethro's journey, his talk with his grandsons, the goat, the sandal, the cooking pot, the tent peg, the boy with the question about the manna, the sensible man from three tents down, and the next morning in the camp are our own invention. Nothing has been added to Jethro's advice, or to what Moses did with it.

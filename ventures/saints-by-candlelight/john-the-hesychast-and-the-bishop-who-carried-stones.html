John the Hesychast and the Bishop Who Carried Stones

A young bishop in Armenia slips away from his own cathedral, takes ship for Jerusalem, and turns up at Saint Sabbas's lavra in the desert as an ordinary newcomer, carrying water and stones for the builders, until the day Sabbas tries to have him made a priest. The life of Saint John the Hesychast, called John the Silent, from Cyril of Scythopolis, who knew him.

It is early morning in a desert gorge, and the sun has not yet climbed over the eastern rim.

Halfway up the cliff, on a narrow path cut into the rock, a man is walking uphill. He is not young, and he is not old. He is somewhere near forty. It is a land of high valleys and cold winters and rivers that run fast and white in the spring when the snow melts. If you have ever been to the mountains in winter and tried to get warm afterwards by sitting very close to a stove, you have some idea of an Armenian winter. If you have not, imagine your feet, and then imagine them colder.

The year is four hundred and fifty-four. The Roman Empire in the West is in the very last years of its long, sad collapse. In the East, the empire is very much still going, ruled from the great city of Constantinople, and the Church is busy, as it always seems to be, with arguments, councils, bishops, and building.

And in the town of Nicopolis, in Armenia, a baby boy is born into a well-to-do family. His parents are Christians. They are important people in the town. And they call him John.

Cyril does not tell us very much about John's childhood. He tells us that the family was a good one, and comfortable, and that the boy was brought up well. And he tells us that John's parents died when he was still young.

Now, a young man with money and no parents to tell him what to do with it can go one of two ways. You have probably met both kinds. One kind buys a very fast horse. The other kind does something more surprising.

John, at about eighteen years old, did the surprising thing. He took his inheritance, and he built a church. Cyril tells us it was a church of the Mother of God. And next to it he built a small monastery, and he gathered a few other young men who wanted to live a life of prayer, about ten of them, and they lived there together, under his guidance.

Let us pause on that for a moment. Eighteen years old. I do not know what you were doing at eighteen. I know that I would not have trusted myself to run a monastery. I am not sure I would have trusted myself to run a bath. But John did it. And by all accounts, he did it well. He was gentle, and serious, and he prayed, and the little community grew in peace.

Which, of course, meant that people noticed him.

And when people in the ancient Church noticed a young man who was gentle, and serious, and prayed, and could run things, there was usually only one outcome. Somebody would want to make him a bishop.

The somebody, in this case, was the metropolitan of Sebaste. A metropolitan is a senior bishop, the chief bishop of a province, and Sebaste was the chief city of that part of Armenia. Not far away there was a small town called Colonia, and Colonia needed a bishop. And the metropolitan looked around, and saw John, and thought, there he is.

John was about twenty-eight years old.

He did not want it. Cyril makes that very clear. John wanted his small church and his small monastery and his small, quiet life of prayer. But in those days, when the Church chose you for a bishop, it was very hard to say no. People had tried. Some had run away and hidden. Some had been found and brought back. One or two had been ordained more or less while being held by the elbows. John did not run away, not then. He bowed his head, and he was made bishop of Colonia.

And here is something important. He was a good bishop.

He did not treat it as an honour. He treated it as work. He preached. He cared for his people. He kept, Cyril tells us, his monastic way of living, plain food and long prayers, even in the bishop's house. He did not dress up. He did not throw dinners. He did not do any of the things that people sometimes do when they suddenly find themselves important.

Let us imagine him, in the evenings, after a long day of hearing complaints and settling quarrels and signing things. The house is quiet. The candle is lit. And he sits, and he looks at the flame, and he thinks of his little church of the Mother of God, and the ten young men, and the silence. And he prays. And then, very probably, someone knocks at the door with another quarrel.

He did this for about nine years.

Now, a bishop in those days was not only a pastor. He was often caught up in the affairs of the town, and of the province, and sometimes of the empire. And Cyril tells us that John got caught up in a very difficult business. A relative of his by marriage, who was the governor of the province, was making trouble for the Church in Colonia. Cyril does not give every detail, and I am not going to invent the ones he leaves out. But it was serious enough that John decided he had to go all the way to Constantinople, to the court of the emperor Zeno, to sort it out.

Imagine the journey. Weeks of it. Over mountains, along rivers, across the plains of Asia Minor, to the greatest city in the world, with its walls and its palaces and its harbour full of ships. And in the city, waiting. Waiting in corridors. Waiting in anterooms. Waiting for officials to see you, and then waiting for the officials to see the officials who could actually decide things.

If you have ever spent a long afternoon on the telephone being told that your call is important and you will be connected shortly, you have touched the very hem of what it was like to petition the court of Constantinople.

And somewhere in the middle of all that waiting, John made a decision.

We do not know exactly when. We do not know exactly what tipped him over. Perhaps it was the waiting. Perhaps it was seeing, close up, how much of a bishop's life could be eaten up by things that had nothing to do with prayer. Perhaps it was simply that the old longing for silence, which he had carried quietly for nine years, finally became too strong. But what Cyril tells us is this. John did not go home.

He went down to the harbour, quietly, and he found a ship that was sailing for the Holy Land. And he got on it. And he did not tell anybody where he was going.

Now, I want to be fair here, because a bishop leaving his diocese is not a small thing, and you might be wondering whether it was allowed. It is a good question. The Church has always taught that a shepherd should not abandon his sheep. But it has also, from very early on, known men who were made bishops against their will and who, after serving faithfully for years, laid the burden down and went to live as monks. It was not common. It was not encouraged. But it happened, and some of those men are saints. John never pretended he had not been a bishop. As we will see, when it really mattered, he told the truth about it, to the right person. He simply did not go around announcing it.

Let me take you, now, to the lavra of Saint Sabbas, as it was when John first saw it.

A lavra, you may remember, is a particular kind of monastery. It is not one big building with a wall round it. It is a scattering of separate cells, caves and little stone huts, spread out along the sides of a gorge, each with a monk in it, joined together by paths. In the middle there is a church, and a bakery, and a few storerooms. The monks live alone in their cells for five days of the week, making baskets and praying, and then on Saturday and Sunday they all come together for the liturgy, and eat a meal together, and collect their bread and their palm leaves for the next week, and then go back to their cells.

The Great Lavra was in the gorge of the Kidron, the same valley that runs past Jerusalem, a few hours' walk out into the desert. The gorge is deep and steep and dry, with sheer cliffs of pale yellow rock, and the cells were cut into the cliffs, or built onto ledges, so that the whole place looked, from across the valley, like a sort of honeycomb that had been designed by someone with a great deal of faith and not much fear of heights.

By the time John came, the lavra had been going for about eight years, and it was growing fast. Monks were coming from everywhere. From Cappadocia, from Armenia, from Syria, from Egypt, from Greece. There was always building going on. A new cell here, a new cistern there, a new wall to hold up a path that had been washed away in last winter's rain. And there were guests to look after, pilgrims and visitors and men who wanted to try the life. Sabbas, as we know, had a soft spot for building, and a soft spot for guests, and so there was a great deal of both.

And at the heart of it was Sabbas himself, a little over fifty, sun-browned and wiry and cheerful and stubborn, with a countryman's hands and a countryman's way of looking at you as if he were working out how much you could carry.

So, one day in the year four hundred and ninety-one, a stranger came down the path into the gorge, and asked to see the abbot.

Oh, said Domnus, cheerfully. A reader? A cantor?

Sabbas looked at the stranger for a long time. He asked him a few questions. He did not ask, Cyril tells us, anything that John felt bound to answer with the whole truth about Colonia. And at the end of it, he said yes, John could stay.

And then he gave him a job.

Now, Sabbas had a habit with newcomers. He did not hand them a cell and leave them to pray in it straight away. He had learned, the hard way, that a young man who goes straight into solitude before he has learned humility and obedience tends to come out of it a little bit odd. So he started them off with work. Ordinary work. Work for the community.

And at that moment, Cyril tells us, the lavra was building. There was a guesthouse going up, and the builders needed water, and stones, and food. So that is what Sabbas told John to do. Carry water for the builders. Carry stones for the builders. Cook for the builders.

Let us think about that.

A few months earlier, this man had been a bishop. When he walked through the town, people stepped aside. When he spoke in church, everyone listened. He had signed letters that went to the governor. He had stood in the anterooms of the emperor.

And now he was carrying stones up a cliff path, behind a donkey called Patience, in the heat, for a builder who did not know his name.

And the remarkable thing, the thing Cyril wants us to notice, is that he did it gladly.

He did not do it with a long face, as if to say, look at me, look how humble I am being. He did not do it while dropping heavy hints. He simply carried the stones. He carried them carefully. He carried a great many of them. And when the stones were done, he cooked.

The builders have gone back up to their cells, tired and dusty. The sun has gone down behind the western rim of the gorge, and the cliffs have turned from yellow to gold to grey. In the desert monasteries, if you were not eating bread, you were very probably eating lentils, and if you were not eating lentils, you were probably fasting.

Cyril tells us that after John had served the builders, Sabbas gave him more responsibility. For a while, he was made the steward of the lavra, which meant looking after its stores, and its supplies, and its guests. This was a job that needed a cool head, a fair hand, and a talent for saying no to people kindly. It was, if you think about it, rather a lot like being a bishop, only with more sacks of flour.

And John did it so well that Sabbas began to watch him more closely.

Sabbas was not a fool. He had seen a great many monks come and go. He knew the ones who were proud, and the ones who were lazy, and the ones who were sincere but would never quite settle, and the ones who were going to be saints. And he looked at this quiet Armenian, who carried stones like a labourer and ran the stores like an administrator and prayed like a hermit, and he thought, this man is ready for more.

So after John's years of service, Sabbas let him go into solitude. He gave him a cell of his own. And John shut the door, and for five days a week, he spoke to nobody. He prayed, and he worked with his hands, and he kept that stillness, that hesychia, that he had been longing for since he was eighteen years old. And on Saturdays and Sundays, he came down to the church, and stood among the brothers, and sang the psalms, and received Holy Communion, and then went quietly back up the cliff.

He was happy.

And then Sabbas had an idea.

It was a perfectly sensible idea. It was a kind idea. It was the kind of idea a good abbot has about a good monk.

Sabbas thought: this man should be a priest.

The lavra needed priests. There were hundreds of monks, and the liturgy had to be celebrated, and confessions heard, and the sick visited. And here was a man of prayer, and wisdom, and steadiness, who had shown in every job he had been given that he could be trusted. Who better?

So, one day, Sabbas came to John's cell, and knocked, and told him.

We do not know exactly what John said. Cyril does not give us the conversation. But we can imagine his face. The calm, quiet face of a man who has been keeping a secret for several years, and who has just heard a knock at the door of it.

He did not argue. That is the extraordinary thing. He did not say no. He did not say, I cannot, I am not worthy, the usual polite refusals. Because he knew that none of those were the real reason, and he was not a man who said things that were not true.

He simply went with Sabbas, up to Jerusalem, to see the patriarch.

The patriarch of Jerusalem at that time was a man called Elias. He was a good bishop, and a friend of the monks, and especially of Sabbas. And when Sabbas brought his quiet Armenian monk and said, here is a man I would like you to ordain a priest, Elias was very ready to do it.

And then John asked a question.

He asked, Cyril tells us, if he might speak to the patriarch alone. Just for a moment. Privately.

Sabbas, a little surprised, stepped outside.

And there, in a quiet room in Jerusalem, with the door shut, the monk who carried stones told the patriarch the truth.

I was a bishop, he said. I was ordained bishop of Colonia, in Armenia. I served there for years. And then I left, and came here, and became a monk, because I wanted to live in silence before God. I cannot be ordained a priest, because I am already a bishop. And I beg you, he said, do not tell anyone. Let me stay where I am. Let me be what I am now.

Let us imagine the patriarch's face. A man who had seen a great deal in his life. A man who knew how hard bishops sometimes fought to keep their chairs, and how few of them ever willingly gave one up. And here in front of him was a man who had given up his chair, and his name, and his honours, and had been quite content, for years, to carry stones and stir lentils.

Elias was a wise man. He did not make a fuss. He gave his word.

And then he opened the door, and called Sabbas back in.

Here is how Cyril tells what happened next, in our own plain words.

The patriarch said to Sabbas: This man has told me something which means that he cannot be ordained. Let him be. Let him stay in his cell in peace, and do not trouble him about this any more.

And that was all he said.

Now, put yourself in Sabbas's sandals for a moment.

He had brought his best monk to be ordained. The monk had asked to speak to the patriarch privately. And then the patriarch had come out and said, he has told me something that means he cannot be a priest. And would not say what.

What would you think?

You would think, as Sabbas thought, that the monk had confessed something. Some sin, some terrible thing in his past, which barred him from the priesthood. Something he had been hiding all these years, behind the quiet face and the careful hands.

Sabbas went home to the lavra with his heart very heavy. He did not say anything to John. He did not ask. But he was troubled. Cyril tells us that he was deeply grieved, because he had loved and trusted this man, and now he did not know what to think.

And Sabbas did what Sabbas always did, when something troubled him that he could not fix with a trowel or a new cistern. He went to his cave, alone, and he prayed. He prayed, I imagine, something like this. Lord, I do not understand. I thought I knew this man. Show me what I need to know, so that I may not judge him wrongly.

And Cyril tells us that God answered that prayer. That it was made known to Sabbas, as he prayed, that John was a bishop. That the thing he had told the patriarch was not a sin at all, but an honour he had laid down. That, as Cyril puts it in effect, the man was not unworthy of the priesthood. He was already above it.

This is told, I should say, as Cyril tells it, from what he learned from John himself and from the old monks of the lavra. It is the kind of thing that the desert fathers expected of prayer. That if you were truly troubled, and truly asked, God would let you know what you needed to know. Not by magic. Not because Sabbas was a wizard. Simply because he asked, and the Lord was kind.

The years went by, and they were not all peaceful ones.

The lavra of Sabbas had its troubles, as all big families do. I want you to picture that cell, because John lived in it for a very long time. Nearly fifty years.

It is a small room, partly a cave, partly built of stone, clinging to the side of the gorge. There is a door, and a little window. Inside, there is a mat to sleep on, and a jar of water, and a basket of palm leaves for weaving. There is perhaps a small icon, or a cross, and a lamp. In the afternoons, the cell is warm. And in the middle of it, a man. Praying. Weaving baskets. Reciting the psalms, very quietly, one after another. Sleeping a little. Waking in the night to pray again.

Being still.

People sometimes imagine that a hermit's life must be very boring. And perhaps, in a way, for the first few years, it is. But the old monks of the desert said something interesting about that. They said that silence is like water in a jar. When you first pour it, it is cloudy, and full of bits, and you cannot see the bottom. But if you leave it still, and do not shake it, the bits slowly settle, and the water becomes clear. And then, they said, you can see all the way down.

John had a great many years to let the water settle.

And then, in the year five hundred and forty-four or thereabouts, a young man came to the window.

He was about twenty years old. He was from Scythopolis, a town in Galilee, to the north. His name was Cyril. And he had come to Jerusalem to become a monk, and like John, so many years before, he had asked around for where to go. And somebody had said: go and see the old man at the lavra of Sabbas. The one who keeps silence. He will tell you.

So Cyril came. And he stood at the window of the cell. And on the other side of the window there was a very, very old man, ninety years old, with a white beard and a face like a walnut and eyes that were still clear.

We do not know exactly what they said, that first time. But Cyril tells us that John became his guide. That the old man advised him, and prayed for him, and that the young man came back to that window again and again over the years. And he tells us something else. He tells us that much of what he knew about the early days of the lavra, and about Sabbas, who had died twelve years before, and about John himself, he learned from the old man at the window.

Let us imagine one of those visits. A winter afternoon. Cyril is standing outside, with his cloak wrapped round him, stamping his feet a little.

Abba, he says, they tell me that when you first came here, you carried stones for the guesthouse.

The old man nods.

And that you cooked for the builders.

Lentils, says the old man. Mostly lentils.

And that the abba Sabbas wanted to make you a priest, and you went to the patriarch, and then you did not become a priest.

A long pause. The old man looks at the young one through the window.

Who told you that, he says.

The old monks, says Cyril. They said there was something, and nobody knew what.

And the old man is silent for a long time. Long enough that Cyril wonders whether the visit is over. And then, very slowly, he begins to tell.

That too is our imagining. But it must have gone something like that, at some point. Because somebody told Cyril, and it is very hard to believe that anybody else could have told it the way he wrote it down.

And so it was that Cyril wrote the life of John the Hesychast while John was still alive. Which must have been a slightly odd experience for both of them. Imagine finding out that the young monk who comes to your window has been going home and writing down everything you say. It would make most of us a little careful. But I suspect John did not mind very much. He had been nobody in particular for more than sixty years. If a young man wanted to write it down, so that other people would know that carrying stones gladly is a good way to God, then perhaps that was all right.

John the Hesychast lived in his cell until he was one hundred and four years old.

He outlived Sabbas by more than twenty years. He outlived the patriarch Elias, who had kept his secret. He outlived the emperor Zeno, at whose court he had once waited, and the two emperors after him. He outlived, I imagine, Patience the donkey, though I am told that donkeys can live a surprisingly long time, especially the ones who complain.

He outlived Domnus too, probably, if Domnus had existed. And let us imagine, since we invented him, that Domnus grew up, and stopped talking quite so much, and became a quiet old monk himself, and sometimes, when he was very old, he would tell the young ones: you know, when I was a boy here, I peeled onions for a bishop, and I never knew. Never knew. Talked his ear off every evening. And he never said a word.

And the young ones would laugh, and not believe him.

In the end, in about the year five hundred and fifty-eight, the very old man in the cell on the cliff grew weaker, and quieter, and more still. And one day the brothers who brought him his bread found that he had gone home to God, peacefully, in the stillness he had loved, as if he had simply finished one long psalm and begun the next one somewhere else.

He had been born in a comfortable house in Armenia, in the middle of the fifth century. He had been a young founder, and a bishop, and a petitioner at the imperial court. And then he had been a stone-carrier, and a cook, and a steward, and a hermit, and an old man at a window giving advice to a young one. And of all of those things, I think, the ones he would have said mattered most were the quiet ones.

The lavra of Sabbas is still there, in the gorge of the Kidron, clinging to its cliff. Monks are still praying in it, fifteen hundred years later. The cells are still cut into the rock. And if you were to stand on the opposite side of the gorge, very early in the morning, before the sun comes over the eastern rim, and look across at that honeycomb of little doors and windows, you would not be able to say which one was John's.

Which is, I think, exactly how he would have wanted it.

The Church remembers him in both East and West. In the Orthodox Church he is honoured as Saint John the Silent, bishop of Colonia, and his feast is kept on the third of December, just two days before the feast of Saint Sabbas, his abbot, so that the old man and his quiet monk stand very close together in the calendar. In the West, the Roman Martyrology remembers him on the thirteenth of May.

And the word that is his name, hesychia, stillness, has stayed in the language of prayer ever since. It is still used, in monasteries and in ordinary homes, to mean that settling of the water in the jar. The quiet in which you can see all the way down.

So let us end tonight with a little of that quiet.

The sun has gone down behind the western rim of the gorge. The cliffs have gone from gold to grey to blue. In the cells along the cliff, one by one, the little lamps are lit. Somewhere down at the bottom of the gorge, a donkey shuffles in her straw, and sighs, and is still. And in one small cell, an old man sets down his basket, and folds his hands, and says nothing at all.

And the stars come out over the opposite cliff, one by one, very bright.

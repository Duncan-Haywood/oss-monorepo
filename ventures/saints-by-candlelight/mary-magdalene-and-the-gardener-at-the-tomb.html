Mary Magdalene and the Gardener at the Tomb

Before dawn on the first day of the week, Mary of Magdala walks out to a garden with a jar of spices and no plan for the stone. Told straight from the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, with the other Gospels set beside it.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Mary Magdalene, Peter, the beloved disciple, the two angels in the tomb, the risen Lord
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; the account is Scripture for both, and both honour Mary Magdalene, the Orthodox among the myrrh-bearing women and as equal to the apostles, the Catholic Church as the apostle to the apostles
Scripture and history
the account is in the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, verses one to eighteen, and the women's visit to the tomb is told in all four Gospels; Magdala was a real fishing town on the west shore of the Sea of Galilee, and Luke tells that Mary was among the women who supported Jesus out of their own means
Legend
none in the story itself; later stories of Mary's life in France, in Ephesus, or before the emperor with an egg are later tradition and we name that and leave them out; we also name and leave aside the old mixing of Mary Magdalene with the unnamed woman of Luke seven, which the Gospels do not make
What we invented
the cold, the dark, the walk, the smell of the garden, the weight of the jar, Mary's thoughts on the way, the look of the tomb, and the small quiet moments between the verses are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; what John says the people did and said is kept as John gives it, and we add nothing to what it means
Feast day
the twenty-second of July in both traditions, and the Orthodox Church also keeps the Sunday of the Myrrh-Bearers, the third Sunday after Easter; calendars differ, so check your own

Full script

Picture a woman walking in the dark with a jar in her arms, and a problem she has not solved.

The problem is a stone. A big round one, the kind that rolls in a groove across the mouth of a tomb, and it took several men to set it there. She knows this. She watched. She has been thinking about it all the way from the city, and she still has no answer. She has no men with her. She has no crowbar. She has, in her arms, a jar of spices, and in her chest a sort of heavy, patient sorrow, the kind that has stopped crying only because it has run out of room.

She is going anyway. That is the thing to notice. She does not know how she will get in. She is going anyway.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the cold, the dark, the walk, the weight of the jar, and the quiet moments between the verses, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let us start with the woman, because she has a name, and the name has a place in it.

She is called Mary, which is the most common name for a woman in her country, so common that the Gospels are always having to tell their Marys apart. There is Mary the mother of Jesus. There is Mary the sister of Martha. There is Mary the wife of Clopas. And there is this one, whom the Gospels call Mary the Magdalene, which means, simply, Mary of Magdala.

Magdala was a town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, a few miles north of Tiberias. A fishing town. The name seems to come from a word for a tower, and the town was known, in those years, for drying and salting fish and sending them off to be sold. If you were to stand on its shore on a summer morning, you would smell it before you saw it, and you would see boats pulled up on the stones, and nets laid out like long brown lace, and men arguing about the price of sardines. It is a fair guess that Mary grew up with that smell. We do not know. The Gospels do not say how old she was, or whether she had a husband, or what her family did. They give us one thing about her before the morning we are about to describe, and it is Luke who gives it.

Luke, in the eighth chapter, says that Jesus was going about from town to town, proclaiming the good news of the kingdom of God, and that the twelve were with him, and also some women who had been cured of evil spirits and diseases. And he names them. Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out. Joanna, the wife of Chuza, who was a steward of Herod. Susanna. And many others. And then Luke adds a line that is easy to read past, and that I would like you to hold on to. These women, he says, provided for them out of their resources.

Out of their own money. Out of their own pockets. When people tell the story of that little travelling company, the twelve and their teacher on the roads of Galilee, they usually tell the part with the miracles and the sermons. Luke wants us to know that somebody was also paying for the bread.

So that is Mary. A woman who had been in a very dark place and been brought out of it. A woman with enough of her own to help. A follower who did not leave, when a good many others did.

Now, a word of care before we go on. If you have heard old stories about Mary Magdalene, you may have heard that she was the woman who anointed the feet of Jesus in the house of a Pharisee, the one Luke describes in his seventh chapter, a woman of the city, who was a sinner. You may have heard her called a reformed prostitute. I should say plainly that the Gospels do not say this. Luke's woman in chapter seven is not named. The mixing of the two women into one came much later, and it has been repeated for so long that many people take it for the text. The Church, in both traditions, honours Mary Magdalene for what the Gospels actually tell about her, and what they tell is more than enough. Tonight we will stay with the Gospels.

Now let us go back three days.

It is the Friday of the Passover week in Jerusalem. The city is crowded to the walls with pilgrims. There are tents on every hill, and lambs being led through the streets, and everybody is busy with the sort of preparations that happen before a big festival: cleaning out the old bread, buying the new, hurrying to finish things before sunset, because at sunset the Sabbath begins, and when the Sabbath begins nobody does anything.

And on that Friday, outside the city wall, the teacher Mary has followed for so long is put to death.

I am not going to describe it. This is a bedtime story, and the Gospels themselves, which are not squeamish, tell it in remarkably few words. What I will say is this. John tells us who was standing near the cross. His mother. His mother's sister. Mary the wife of Clopas. And Mary Magdalene. The men, by and large, had scattered. The women had not. They stood there, in the worst place in the world, and stayed.

And when it was over, there was very little time. A man named Joseph, from a town called Arimathea, a member of the council, who had been a disciple secretly because he was afraid, went to the governor and asked for the body. And a second man, Nicodemus, who had once come to Jesus by night, brought a great quantity of spices, myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds in weight, John says, which is a very generous amount, and they wound the body in linen cloths with the spices, as the Jewish custom of burial was. There was a garden near the place, John says, and in the garden a new tomb, in which no one had ever been laid. Because it was the day of Preparation, and the tomb was close, they laid him there.

Then a stone was rolled across the opening. Then the sun went down. Then the Sabbath began.

And Mary, who had watched where he was laid, because Mark tells us that Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses saw where the body was placed, went home. And was still. For a whole day. Because that is what the law required, and she was a person who kept it.

I would like you to think about that Saturday for a moment. We do not have a word of it from the Gospels. It is the quietest day in the whole story. A day when nothing happened, and everything that mattered seemed to have ended, and there was nothing whatever to do about it but sit, and wait for the sun to go down.

And so we come to the first day of the week.

It is still dark. John says this specifically. Early, while it was still dark. The other Gospels say that the dawn was just coming, or the sun had risen, and people have spent a great deal of ink reconciling the tellings. We will not try to settle it tonight. It is enough to say that the day has not really begun, and that Mary is awake before it.

She has not slept much. Who could? She has lain on her mat in the dark and listened to the city breathe, and at some point she has given up on sleep, and got up, and found her cloak.

The cold is the kind you get in Jerusalem in the spring, before the sun is up. Not bitter, but damp and thin, and it gets into the sleeves. She puts her feet into her sandals. She has the spices. That is what she is going for. Mark and Luke both tell us that the women had bought spices to anoint the body. Nicodemus had brought a great deal on the Friday, but they had been in such a hurry then, with the Sabbath coming on, that the work had had to be done in a rush. She wants to do it properly. She wants to do the last small kind thing that can be done.

It is a hard sort of kindness, the kind we do for the dead. There is nothing to be gained by it. Nobody will thank you. It will not change anything. And so it is, in a way, the purest sort there is. You do it because you loved them, and because you have nowhere else to put the love.

She goes out into the street. The doors are shut. A dog lifts its head from a doorstep, considers her, and puts it down again. Somewhere a rooster, who has not read the situation, is having a good morning. She walks.

I have said she is thinking about the stone. The other Gospels tell us so. In Mark, the women say to one another on the road, who will roll away the stone for us from the door of the tomb? It is a very practical question, and I find it the most comforting sentence in the story. These are not women with a plan. These are not women who expect a miracle. They have not the faintest idea that anything is about to happen. They are doing the next small kind thing, on a cold morning, with the least possible confidence, and they are quite sure that they will fail at the door.

And they go anyway. I think that is worth a good deal of reflection, on a night when we are tired.

The city gate. The path down. The garden.

You can smell a garden before you see it, at that hour. The wet earth. Crushed leaves. Somebody's herbs, rosemary or thyme or something like it, breathing out in the cool. There is a faint grey beginning behind the hills, not light yet, but the idea of light.

And then the place. The tomb is cut into the rock, as these tombs were: a low opening in a stone face, with a groove running along the front, and a round stone, which has been rolled along the groove to cover the doorway. She has seen it done on Friday. She is looking for it now, and as the grey grows she can see it, a dark shape against the pale rock.

Only the shape is wrong.

John says it simply. She saw that the stone had been taken away from the tomb.

Not rolled a little. Not cracked open. Taken away. The doorway is a black mouth in the rock, and there is nothing in front of it at all.

I do not think she goes in. John does not say that she goes in. She stands there for perhaps a few heartbeats, in the grey, with the jar in her arms, and her mind does what any mind would do, and goes to the nearest explanation. Somebody has been here. Somebody has taken him.

And she runs.

Here I should say something about the other Gospels, because they tell this morning differently, and you may notice it if you know them well.

Matthew says there was an earthquake, and an angel of the Lord rolled back the stone and sat on it. Mark says the women went in and saw a young man sitting on the right side, dressed in a white robe. Luke says there were two men in dazzling clothes. John, tonight's Gospel, has Mary run away at once, without looking in, and meet the angels only later, when she comes back. The four tellings do not match in every detail, and the Church has never pretended that they do. What they agree on is the thing that matters. The tomb was empty. The stone was moved. The women were the first to come. And the first word that anyone heard in that place was a word from heaven, telling them that Jesus had been raised.

Tonight we will follow John. But I wanted you to know that the others are there, standing around the edges of the story, telling it from where they stood.

Now. Mary is running.

She runs the way people run who have no breath to spare. Up the path, through the gate, along the narrow streets, where the first lamps are being lit in the upper rooms and a man is carrying a stack of flat bread on his head. She does not stop for any of it. She knows where she is going. She is going to the house where the men are hiding.

I do not mean this unkindly. It is just what the Gospels say. On the evening of that same day, John tells us, the disciples were behind locked doors for fear of the authorities. They have had a dreadful three days. Their teacher has been arrested and executed. One of them has denied he ever knew him. Another has betrayed him. They are afraid that they will be next. And so there is a house, somewhere in the city, with a bolt on the door, and a few frightened men inside, and a woman coming up the stairs.

She finds Simon Peter. And the other disciple, the one whom Jesus loved. John does not give that disciple's name in his own Gospel, and tradition has long said that it is John himself, and we will leave that where John leaves it.

And she says, and this is the whole of her report:

"They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him."

Notice the we. It is a small word and an interesting one. She says we do not know. Not I. She was not alone at the tomb, perhaps. The other Gospels say there were other women with her. John, in telling it, lets the we stand and does not explain. It is one of the little cracks in the wall of the text, where you can see that there is more behind it than we are told.

And she says them. Not an angel. Not a gardener. Not a miracle. They. Somebody has stolen him. This is Mary's first thought, and it is a perfectly reasonable one, and John records it just as it was. I find that honest. She does not leap to glory. She leaps to the worst thing she can think of, and that is where she begins.

What do Peter and the other disciple do?

They run.

Of course they do. A man who has been told that the body of his teacher is missing does not sit down and finish his bread. They set off together, the two of them, through the streets, out of the gate and down toward the garden, and John tells us something I have always liked, because it is so human. They were running together, but the other disciple outran Peter and reached the tomb first.

Think about that. He tells us who won. John, who wrote this Gospel, probably as an old man, probably after sixty or seventy years of thinking about it, still remembers that he was faster. I cannot decide if this is vanity or just honesty. I think it is honesty, and a certain gentle joke at his own expense. He was younger. He won. He remembers.

And he gets to the tomb, and he stops. He bends down and looks in. He sees the linen cloths lying there. But he does not go in.

Then Peter comes puffing up behind him, and Peter, who is never one to stand at a door when there is a thing to be done, goes straight in. He sees the linen cloths lying there. And the cloth that had been on Jesus' head, not lying with the linen cloths, but rolled up in a place by itself.

Now, this is a very particular detail, and I want to leave it exactly as John leaves it. The cloths are lying there. The head cloth is folded, or rolled, in a separate place. It does not look like a robbery. A thief in a hurry does not pause to fold the bandages. John does not draw any conclusion from it aloud. He just tells us what was seen. We are meant to look at it and think about it for ourselves.

And then the other disciple goes in too. And John says: he saw and believed.

He saw and believed. We are not told exactly what he believed, or how far his belief went. The very next verse says that as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead. So it is a beginning. It is a first small light in the room. It is not yet the day.

And then, John says, the disciples went back to their homes.

Which is such a strange and ordinary sentence. They went home. After all that running, and the cloths, and the believing, they went home. I think they simply did not know what else to do. When you cannot understand a thing, sometimes the only thing to do is go and sit in your own kitchen, and let it settle.

But Mary does not go home.

Hold on to that. The men have looked, and gone, and the garden has gone quiet again. And Mary Magdalene is standing outside the tomb, weeping.

She has come back, you see. She came back behind them, perhaps, not as fast, and she has stopped at the opening, and she cannot go. She cannot go home and she cannot go in. She stands there in the grey light, and now at last she cries, the crying she has been holding for three days, and nobody is watching.

And while she is weeping, John says, she bent over to look into the tomb.

I love that. She bends. The men looked in, and saw, and believed, and went away. Mary stays, and cries, and leans down into the dark, as if the way to learn more were simply to keep looking at the place where he was.

And in the tomb she sees two angels in white, sitting where the body of Jesus had been, one at the head and one at the feet.

I want to be careful here. Angels are angels. They are not dead people, and they do not earn wings, and they are not frightening in the way that ghosts are frightening. John does not call them frightening. He does not say that Mary was afraid. She sees two beings in white, sitting quietly in a small stone room, one at each end of the place where the body had lain. If you have ever seen the little carved box in which the Hebrews kept the covenant, with a figure of a cherub at either end, you might think of that. And perhaps John did. He does not say.

And they speak to her. Their first words are a question, and it is the gentlest question I know.

"Woman, why are you weeping?"

Why are you weeping. As though the angels were a little puzzled, as though they knew the reason, and could see that she did not. As though this were a night when weeping was, for the first time in three days, slightly out of date.

And Mary answers. Not with awe. Not with a shriek. She answers like a person asked a plain question in a plain voice, and she gives them the plain truth.

"They have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him."

My Lord. Notice that. She doesn't say the teacher, or the master, or the body. My Lord. And she says it to two angels in white, in a rock-cut tomb, and she doesn't seem to notice that it is a strange thing to be doing. Her grief has taken up all the room. There is none left over for being astonished.

And then she turns around.

John does not say why. Perhaps she hears a step. Perhaps she feels, as you sometimes do, that someone is behind her. She turns away from the angels, and she sees a man standing there.

It is light by now. Or lighter. The sun is coming up behind the hills. The garden is grey going to gold. And there is a man standing among the plants, a few feet away. He is dressed like a working man. He is not doing anything in particular. He is simply there.

And Mary does not know him.

I want to say something about that, because it is the strangest part of the strange morning. She does not know him. She who knew his voice, and his walk, and the way he held his head, who was at the foot of the cross, who has followed him for years. She turns and looks at him, and with her eyes full of tears and the light in her face, she sees a stranger.

The Gospels return to this again and again. Luke tells of two disciples on a road who walk for hours beside him and do not know him. John tells of the disciples in a boat, a bit later, who see a man on the shore and do not know him. It seems to be part of the way in which he came. Not as a ghost, not as a visiting angel, but as himself, and yet not as they expected. I will not try to explain it. John does not. I only note that it is there, and that Mary is the first to meet it.

And the man says the same words the angels said.

"Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you looking for?"

And Mary, thinking that he is the gardener, and I would like you to notice that John tells us what she was thinking, so that we are in on the secret and she is not, says:

"Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away."

Let us just look at that sentence for a moment. I will take him away.

She has no men. She has no crowbar. She has, I imagine, a jar of spices and a cloak. She is going to carry a grown man's body, on her own, through the streets of Jerusalem, if the gardener will just tell her where it is. I think it is one of the bravest and most foolish things anyone says in the whole of the Gospels. And I think it is exactly why she is the one who is there.

She is not afraid of the soldiers. She is not afraid of the authorities. She is not afraid of what is going to happen to her if she is caught. She just wants to be in the same place as him again. Even if he is dead. Even if all she can do is to carry him somewhere where he can be buried properly, and sit beside him.

And I want you to think about the man in the garden, who is about to speak. Because he has heard her say it. I will take him away. And there is a place, I think, in the heart of the Gospel, where those words are heard, and kept.

Now the story comes to its single quiet moment.

The man says one word.

He says her name.

"Mary."

That is all. Not a speech. Not a blaze of light. Not a long explanation. Just her name, said in a voice she knows. And I would like you to imagine, as we go slower, how it must have sounded. All her life she has been called Mary by a great many people. In the market. In the house. By her mother, calling her in from the street. But there was one voice that said it a particular way, and she has not heard it for three days, and she is hearing it now.

John does not tell us what happened in her face. He does not need to. He tells us only what she did. She turned, he says, and said to him in Hebrew, "Rabbouni!" which means Teacher.

She turned. Notice that. She had turned already, to see the gardener. Now she turns again, and John does not say whether this is a literal turning or the turning of a heart, and I suspect it is both. She turns toward him. And what comes out of her is not a sermon. It is the old, familiar, very ordinary word that a student says to a teacher. Rabbouni. My Teacher. The one I know.

There is the whole of Easter, in one word. There is nothing more to be said, and nothing more that she could say. He is alive. And he knows her name.

Let us slow down.

The garden is quite light now, and the birds, who are rarely told anything about the course of history, have begun to sing. There is a woman standing in the path, and a man in front of her, and the stone is lying open behind them, and the sun is on the wet leaves.

And Jesus says to her something that has puzzled readers for two thousand years.

In the old translations it is: do not touch me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father. In the Latin it became a famous phrase, and a great many painters have painted it, the woman on her knees, the man with his hand raised, a little distance between them. But the Greek word is not quite "do not touch." It is closer to "stop holding on to me," or "do not keep clinging to me." Some translators say it that way. Some keep the older phrasing. We will not argue about it tonight. I will only say what I think is plain from the surrounding words, and that is that she has reached out, or fallen at his feet, and taken hold, as anyone would. And he is not rebuking her. He is telling her that things are about to be different. He is not going to be here in the old way. She does not need to cling, because he is not going away from her; he is going to the Father, and in a way she does not yet understand, that will be nearer, not farther.

And then he gives her work.

"But go to my brothers and tell them, I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God."

My brothers. It is a very tender word. These are the men who ran away. The man who denied him. The men who were behind a locked door. And he calls them brothers, and sends the news to them, and sends it by her.

I would like you to notice who he trusts. He does not send an angel. He does not appear to them first. He does not write them a letter. He sends the woman who stood at the cross, who walked out in the dark with a jar of spices, who said I will take him away. She is the first person to see him risen, and she is the first one to be sent. The old writers of the Church called her, for this reason, the apostle to the apostles. That is a Latin phrase, and it is used in the Catholic tradition, and the Orthodox Church honours her as equal to the apostles, and the two titles come to the same thing. She was sent, and she went.

And she goes.

I like to think that she goes slowly at first. That she can hardly walk. That she stands there, in the garden, for a moment after he is gone, with the light on her face, and her hands full of nothing, because at some point in all that she has put the jar down, and has forgotten where.

The spices, by the way, are never mentioned again. Not in John. I think they sit there in the garden, unopened, beside the path. The most carefully prepared gift in the whole of the story, and it was not needed. He was not there to be anointed. He was somewhere else, and he was alive.

Then she picks up her skirts, and she runs again. It is the second run of the morning. The first was in sorrow, and this one is not. She goes up the path, and through the gate, and into the streets, where the market is waking and a boy is pulling a goat along on a rope, and nobody looks at her twice, because nobody knows what has happened.

She arrives at the house. She goes up the stairs. The men look up, and I imagine that they see her face before she says a word.

And John gives us her report, which is the shortest and greatest in the whole of the New Testament.

"I have seen the Lord."

And then, John says, she told them that he had said these things to her.

That is all. Five words. I have seen the Lord. Not I have a theory. Not I think I may have. I have seen him. She does not say it in a hush. She says it in the voice of a woman who has been told to say it, by someone who has asked her to, and who has no intention of being anything but exact.

And what do the men say? John does not tell us. Luke tells us, in his own account, that when the women reported what they had seen, the words seemed to the apostles an idle tale, and they did not believe them. It is not very flattering to the apostles, and the Gospel records it anyway. I think this is one of the reasons we can trust the Gospels. They tell us, quite calmly, that the first witnesses of the greatest event in history were not believed, and that the people who did not believe them were the ones who would later be sent to the ends of the earth.

But that comes later. The evening of that day is another story, with a locked door, and a greeting of peace, and a room full of men who are, at last, no longer afraid. We will leave it for another night.

Let us slow down now. Let us come in.

The morning has become a day, and the day has moved on. The garden is empty. The stone leans open in its groove. A bird has found its way into the tomb, and out again, and does not seem to think it a remarkable place. The sun is warm on the path. Somebody, somewhere, is hanging washing on a roof.

And in a room in the city, a woman is sitting, and she is not weeping. She is only sitting very still, with her hands in her lap, and her eyes wide, as people do who have been told something so large that it will take them years to take in. The men are talking, over her head, in low and urgent voices. Peter is asking her a question. The other disciple is quiet. Outside, the noise of the Passover week goes on and on, the whole great crowded city, not yet knowing anything at all.

And she has the name, the one word, the one voice, folded away somewhere in her where it will keep.

What became of Mary Magdalene? The Gospels tell us she was at the cross, and at the tomb, and then, in Acts, there is a list of those gathered in prayer after the Ascension, and she is not named in it, though the women are. After that, we have no certain word. Old stories grew up, in the East and in the West, about where she went and what she did, and they are lovely in their way, and they disagree with one another, and the Gospels do not support them. We have no wish to put them in her mouth tonight. What the Gospels give us, and what the Church in both East and West has loved for two thousand years, is one morning. A woman, a garden, a name. It is enough.

Her feast is kept on the twenty-second of July, in the Catholic and in the Orthodox calendars, and in the Orthodox Church there is also a Sunday after Easter given to the myrrh-bearers, the women who came with spices to the tomb, and who were the first to be told. You may like to look for it next spring.

Here is a thought to take to bed with you.

Mary did not know how she would get past the stone. She walked out in the dark with no plan, and with a jar of spices that she would never use, to do a last small kindness for someone who, as far as she knew, was gone. And the stone had been moved before she arrived.

Most of us will never be asked to do anything very large. But a good many of us, some mornings, are walking toward a stone. A hard conversation. A grave to visit. A situation with no solution that we can see. And we go because we cannot stay away, and we ask ourselves, who will roll away the stone for us, and we have no answer.

It may be worth remembering that the stone was already gone. Not because she was clever. Not because she had worked out how. Only because the one she loved had gone ahead of her, and was already waiting in the garden, and knew her name.

You are known, too. That is the whole of this story, I think. Whatever the day was, whatever you are carrying, someone knows your name and says it the way no one else does.

And now you can let it go. You can let the day go. The garden is quiet, and the light is coming up on the leaves, and the whole wide world is waking, very slowly, to something good.

Here are the notes for the curious.

The story of Mary Magdalene and the risen Lord in the garden is told in the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, verses one to eighteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Everything that Mary, Peter, the other disciple, the two angels and Jesus say and do in the story is kept as John gives it, including the stone taken away, the running, the cloths and the head cloth rolled up apart, the angels at the head and the foot, the question twice asked, the mistaking of Jesus for the gardener, the speaking of her name, her answer in Hebrew, the words about the Father and the brothers, and her report. The matter of what Mary Magdalene was like before is taken from Luke, chapter eight, verses one to three. The women with spices come from Mark and Luke, and the question about who would roll away the stone from Mark. The burial in the garden is from John, chapter nineteen. Matthew, Mark and Luke tell the morning differently in some details, the earthquake, the young man, the two men in dazzling clothes, and we have named that, and said that the Church has never made the four tellings match.

We invented the cold, the dog and the rooster, the smell of the garden, the weight of the jar, the sound of the washing on the roof, the thoughts of Mary on the way, and the quiet scene at the end. The way the English phrase "do not touch me" differs from the sense of the Greek is a matter on which translators differ, and we have said so and not settled it. We have said that the Gospels do not say that Mary Magdalene was the woman of Luke chapter seven, a mixing that came later, and that the Gospels do not support it. We do not know how old Mary was, whether she married, or what became of her after the days of the Gospels. Later stories of her life in France, at Ephesus, or before the emperor with an egg are later tradition, and we have named that and left them out. That the beloved disciple was John is tradition, which we have noted and not argued. The title apostle to the apostles belongs to the Catholic tradition and equal to the apostles to the Orthodox, and we have honoured both and argued neither.

That's the story of Mary Magdalene and the Gardener at the Tomb for tonight. Her feast is the twenty-second of July in both the Catholic and Orthodox calendars, and the Orthodox also keep the Sunday of the Myrrh-Bearers after Easter, and calendars differ, so you will want to check your own. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

And here is a blessing. May the Lord who called Mary by her name call you by yours. May you be given the courage to go out in the dark with no plan and a small kindness in your arms. May the stone you are afraid of be already moved. May whatever you are mourning tonight be held, and answered, in the morning. May there be light on the leaves, and a friend in the garden, and a word that is only for you. And may you sleep like a woman who has been sent with good news, and who is in no hurry at all.

Goodnight, and God bless.

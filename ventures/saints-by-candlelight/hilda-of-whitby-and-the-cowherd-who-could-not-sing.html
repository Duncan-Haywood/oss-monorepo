Hilda of Whitby and the Cowherd Who Could Not Sing

On a cliff above the North Sea, an abbess who advises kings meets an elderly cowherd who has just woken up able to sing. Saint Hilda of Whitby, the stableman Caedmon, and the first English poem, as Bede tells it.

What's true in this story

Who
Saint Hilda of Whitby, Caedmon, the reeve of Whitby, the brothers and sisters of the double monastery, an unnamed man in a dream, a number of cattle
Tradition
Catholic saint; honoured by many Orthodox Christians as a saint of the early English church
Scripture and history
Hilda was a real woman, born in 614 into the Northumbrian royal family, baptised in 627, abbess first of Hartlepool and then of Whitby, where the great Council of 664 was held; she died on 17 November 680 after a long illness; Caedmon is the first English poet known by name, and Bede wrote about him about fifty years after his death
Legend
the vision of a bell and angels seen by a nun at a distant house on the night of her death is reported by Bede from the nun herself; it is told here as Bede reports it
What we invented
the cowherd's thoughts, the feast and its jokes, the weather, the conversations in the abbess's room, the name of nothing but the people Bede names, and the small talk of the brothers are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; Bede does not describe the feast, and he gives the abbess no words at all in this scene
Feast day
17 November in the Catholic calendar, the day of her death in 680; Orthodox calendars that keep the saints of the early English church name her as well, on days that vary

So, as the harp gets to the man two seats away, he gets up, quietly, picks up his cloak, and slips out through the door into the dark.

It is called Streoneshalh at the time, which is a mouthful, and later people will call it Whitby. It stands on the Yorkshire coast, on a headland with the North Sea on one side and a steep little river valley on the other, and the wind up there has a kind of personality. It arrives early and it leaves late and it never apologises.

On the top of that cliff, in the year six hundred and fifty-seven, a woman founded a monastery.

Her name was Hilda.

Now you may have heard it said that history is made by kings, and in the north of England in those days there were certainly plenty of kings. There were so many that it was hard to keep track of them. They fought, they married each other's cousins, they fought again, and every few years one of them was killed, and another was crowned, and everyone shuffled over.

But Hilda was no king. She was something stranger and, as it turned out, longer-lasting. She was the person that the kings came to ask.

Let me tell you about her, as Bede tells it, because it is a good life, and you might as well know who is about to enter the story.

She was born in six hundred and fourteen, into the royal family of Northumbria. Her great-uncle was King Edwin, the very same Edwin who had his whole court argue the matter of Christianity round a fire, and who famously said that human life was like a sparrow flying through a lighted hall on a winter night, in one door and out the other, and that if the new faith could tell us where it came from and where it went, he would follow it. He did follow it. He was baptised at Easter in six hundred and twenty-seven, in a small wooden church at York, and the little girl Hilda, who was about thirteen, was baptised in the same season.

So she grew up in the first generation of Christians in her part of England. Everything about the faith was still new, and a good deal of it was still being worked out, and she was not too proud to learn.

She lived for thirty-three years as a noblewoman, doing what a noblewoman did, whatever that was, and Bede says almost nothing about those years, except that she lived them nobly. And then, when she was thirty-three, she decided to leave England and join her sister in a convent in Gaul, on the other side of the sea. She meant to go for good.

She had her bags packed, so to speak. She had made her plans. And then a bishop called Aidan, an Irish monk, a gentle, tireless man who had come over from the island of Iona to teach the Northumbrians, called her back. He gave her a small plot of land by the river Wear, and a handful of companions, and he said, in effect, Hilda, do it here. We need you here.

She did. A year later he made her abbess of the convent of Hartlepool, which had been started by a woman called Heiu. And Aidan and his friends, Bede tells us, visited her often, and loved her for her wisdom, and taught her all they could.

That is the first thing to know about Hilda. She was a woman who changed her mind about her own plans when she was asked to, and she did not think of it as giving anything up.

Now let's go to Whitby.

When Hilda arrived at the cliff, there was nothing there but grass and gulls and a very good view.

So she did what founders do. She walked the ground, and she prayed, and she marked out where the church would be and where the kitchen would be and where the guest house would be. She got hold of timber. She got hold of masons, and a large number of stubborn cart horses. And over the next few years, a monastery rose out of the headland, wood and thatch and stone, with a bell on the roof that could be heard, on a clear night, a very long way along the coast.

It was what they called a double monastery. That means that two communities lived on the same cliff, a house of women and a house of men, each with their own buildings and their own prayers, and the abbess over both. This was not unusual in those days, in the north of England and in Gaul. It meant that the monastery was something like a small town, with sisters and brothers, and priests and farmers, and visitors, and a lot of people who just came because a meal and a bed were to be had.

And at the head of the whole thing, Bede says, was Hilda.

I want you to picture how she ran it, because it is the key to the rest of our story. Bede is quite clear about it. She taught everyone to keep peace and charity, and to keep nothing as private property, but to hold everything in common, as the first Christians did in the Acts of the Apostles. She made them study the Scriptures. She insisted that all her people, the sisters and the brothers, should learn the Bible and practise good works. And she was, as Bede puts it, so wise that not only ordinary people, but kings and princes would come and ask her for advice, and she gave it.

A monastery with no private property. A monastery in which a king's cousin and a shepherd's daughter ate at the same table and learned the same psalms. A monastery with a school, in which, over the years, five men were taught who would go on to become bishops.

And all the people who knew her, Bede says, even those who lived far away, called her Mother.

That is a wonderful word to be called, in the middle of a world full of men with swords. I think it tells you what the cliff felt like to live on.

Now. In the lands that belonged to the monastery, there was a man who looked after the animals.

Bede doesn't tell us how old he was. He tells us that he had spent most of his life as a layman, in the world, and that he had never learned any songs. This was a problem in a land where songs were how people remembered everything. Kings had singers. Warriors had their battles sung. Feasts had songs the way modern parties have music, and a man who could not sing was a man who sat quietly at the edge of things.

His name was Caedmon.

I'll tell you what I think he was like, and I want to say clearly that this is my invention, not Bede's. I imagine a big, slow, careful man, quiet in the way that people who spend their days with cows are quiet. Someone who talked to the animals more than to people, and was not at all sure that they didn't understand him. A man who got up in the dark and went to bed early, and who knew the name of every cow in his herd and her mother's name too, and her temper, and which of them would stand still for milking and which would kick the pail over out of principle.

He would have been thought a decent, steady, slightly invisible man, the kind everyone depends on and nobody thinks about.

And he had a secret, which was not really a secret. He loved songs. He was moved by them. He used to stand outside the hall, I like to think, when the music was going, with a bucket in each hand, listening, feeling something move in his chest like a fish in a pond. But he had never once been able to sing. When he tried, nothing came out but a sort of embarrassed hum. And he had come to terms with that, the way people do. It was simply something he was not.

So, on the night we began with, when the feast was in full swing and the harp started coming down the table, and he knew that his turn was three seats away, and then two, he did what he had done at every feast he could remember. He left.

Bede puts it simply. When the harp was passed to him, he got up from the supper, went out, and went home.

Now, he did not go home, exactly. His work was with the animals, and that night it was his turn to watch the byre, the long low barn where the cattle were kept. So he went across the yard in the dark, with the wind coming off the sea and rattling the thatch, and he went in among the warm bulks of the cows, which breathed and shifted and made the comfortable noises that cows make, and he sat down in the straw.

I think he sat there for a while with his arms round his knees. Perhaps he was ashamed. Perhaps he was just tired. Perhaps he was thinking, as people do, that it was a strange thing that a whole table of folk could do easily what he couldn't do at all, and that there was no use in minding.

And then he lay down in the straw, and went to sleep.

And as he slept, a man stood by him.

Bede doesn't say what the man looked like. He says only that someone came to him in a dream, and greeted him by name, and said:

"Caedmon, sing me something."

Imagine that. You are asleep in a barn. A stranger is standing over you in a light that did not come from any lamp. He knows your name. And he asks you the one thing in the world you cannot do.

Caedmon answered, as honestly as he could. He said, "I cannot sing. That is why I left the feast and came here."

You can almost hear him, can't you? Not angry. Not frightened. Just explaining, politely, the way you would to someone who has the wrong address. I'm sorry, I think you've got the wrong man. I'm the one who can't.

But the stranger said, "Nevertheless, you can sing to me."

And Caedmon said, "What should I sing?"

And the stranger said:

"Sing about the beginning of created things."

That is all. That is the whole request. Not sing something clever. Not sing something that will impress the abbess. Sing about the beginning of things. Sing about how it all started, the thing you have been looking at all your life, the sky over the cattle and the sea at the foot of the cliff.

And Caedmon, in the dream, opened his mouth.

And he sang.

I want to tell you what he sang, because we still have it. It is only nine lines long. It is in the old language of the north of England, which is the grandfather of the English we speak, and it is the oldest poem in English whose author we know by name.

I'll give you the sense of it in modern words, and not the sound, because the sound is another country.

It goes something like this.

Now we must praise the keeper of the heavenly kingdom, the power of the Maker and the thought of his mind, the work of the Father of glory, how he, the eternal Lord, began every wonder.

He first made, for the children of earth, heaven as a roof, the holy Creator. Then the middle world, the guardian of mankind, the eternal Lord, made afterwards, the land for the people, the Lord Almighty.

That is all of it. No battle, no gold. A cowherd looks up at the sky, and down at the ground, and says what he sees: someone made the roof, and someone made the floor, and it was all done for us.

Bede notes, in a careful sort of footnote, that this is the sense and not the exact order of the words, because even the best poet cannot translate his own verse from one language into another without losing the beauty of it. He wrote that in Latin, and I think he meant it as a compliment.

Caedmon sang it in the dream. And then, Bede says, he woke up.

Think for a moment about what that morning was like.

He lay in the straw. The cows were awake. It was cold and grey and ordinary. And he remembered the dream, which in itself is a strange thing, because most of us can't, and he found, to his surprise, that he remembered the song.

Every word of it.

So he tried it. Quietly, in the byre, to the cows.

He opened his mouth, and the words were there, and the tune was there, and he sang the nine lines of the song from beginning to end. And then, being a practical man, he added a few more lines, in the same style, praising God. They came easily. They seemed to be lying around, waiting for him, like tools in a shed he had not known he owned.

I imagine the cows looking round. One of them would have blinked. That is the sort of audience he was used to.

And then, Bede says, he went and told the reeve.

The reeve was the man in charge of the farm, the steward who answered to the abbess, and Caedmon's immediate boss. He was not a poetical person, I don't think. He was a person with accounts to keep and a long list of things to be done before supper. So Caedmon came to him, a little breathless, and said, in effect, Sir, something has happened, and I need you to listen to this.

And he sang it.

The reeve, to his credit, did not laugh. He did not say, Caedmon, you've been at the ale. He listened to the whole thing, and he understood in some part of him that this was not an ordinary morning, and he did the one thing a sensible steward could do with something that was far above his own pay.

He took Caedmon to the abbess.

So now we come to the room we've been walking towards all evening.

It is Hilda's room, probably, or the chapter house, or a corner of the great hall, we can't be sure. The abbess is there, and with her, Bede says, some of the learned people of the house. By learned I mean the people who had spent years studying Scripture and Latin and the interpretation of the old books, the kind of people who knew what a poem ought to sound like and were not easy to impress.

And in front of them is a cowherd, who is trying to hold his hat politely, and who has been told to say it all again.

He tells them about the dream. He tells them about the man in the barn, and the question, and the beginning of created things. And he sings the song.

I wonder what that silence was like.

I have a theory. I think it was the kind of silence that comes when a roomful of intelligent people each privately decide, at the same moment, not to say the first clever thing that comes to mind.

Because Hilda was not a naive woman. She had spent her life among kings and bishops and people who claimed to have heard from heaven. She knew that dreams are dreams, and that a good story is not the same as a gift from God. And she knew the other thing too, which is that God is not in the habit of consulting the educated before He chooses someone. She had read the Gospels. She knew where the shepherds were when the angels came.

So she did what a wise person does when faced with something marvellous and not yet understood. She tested it.

She and her scholars read Caedmon a passage of sacred history. A piece of the Bible, some teaching, something with real content in it. And they said to him, Go home, think about it, and see whether you can put it into verse. Come back tomorrow and let us hear.

That is a hard thing to ask of anyone. Imagine being asked, on the strength of one lucky night, to produce a second poem, to order, by morning, on a subject you did not choose, in front of the people who will decide what to make of you.

And off he went, back to the byre, probably, and the straw, and the cows.

The next morning he came back.

He had done it. He had turned the passage into verse, and not poor verse either, but good, graceful verse, Bede says, that sang beautifully. He recited it to the abbess and her scholars, and when he was finished there was a very different kind of silence.

Hilda understood at once. Bede's words are that she saw that the gift of grace was from God. And she did the remarkable thing, the thing that makes me love her. She did not hold on to him as a prodigy. She did not make him a court entertainer for the great house. She did not charge admission.

She told him to give up his old life, if he chose to, and become a monk.

Think about what that meant. The man was a cowherd. He had no Latin. He had no letters, I suppose. He had come to the monastery as a working hand, and she was inviting him to be one of the brothers, to sit in the choir, to learn the psalms, to be fed and taught and honoured as a member of the community.

And he said yes.

She welcomed him in, Bede tells us, with all her people, and she gave orders that he should be taught the whole course of sacred history. And Caedmon stood and listened, as the teachers read, and he took it all in the way a cow takes in a meadow, slowly, and thoroughly, and for a very long time. Then he would go off and turn it into the most melodious verse, which his teachers, who were learned and clever, would then sit down and write out, and learn from him, rather than the other way round.

The cowherd taught the scholars.

He sang about the creation of the world, and the origin of the human race, and the whole story of Genesis. He sang about the going out of Israel from Egypt, and the entry into the promised land. He sang about the Lord's incarnation, His passion and resurrection and ascension into heaven. He sang about the coming of the Holy Spirit, and the teaching of the apostles. And he sang, Bede says, about the terror of the judgment to come, and the horror of the pains of hell, and the sweetness of the heavenly kingdom.

All in his own English. All in the tongue of the people who worked in the fields and ate in the hall.

I should say something about why that mattered, because it is easy to miss. In those days, the language of the Church was Latin. The Bible was in Latin, the Mass was in Latin, and the learned men wrote in Latin. If you were an ordinary farmer who did not know it, then the great story of your own salvation was a thing that happened over your head, in a language you could not follow.

Caedmon changed that. For the first time that we know of, somebody took the great Christian story and sang it in English, in the old, strong, alliterative English line the people already loved. The songs of the heroes were suddenly sharing the stage with the song of Christ.

And what is remarkable is how it happened. It did not happen because a bishop thought it was a good idea. It happened because a man who couldn't sing fell asleep in a barn, and an abbess was wise enough to tell the difference between an accident and a gift.

Now, there is a part of this story I find very moving, and it comes near the end.

Bede says that Caedmon went on like this for the rest of his life. He did not become proud. He did not collect honours. He was, Bede writes, a very religious man, who submitted himself humbly to the discipline of the rule, and he was always burning with zeal. And he could never write poetry that was frivolous or idle. He sang only of religion, because, as Bede says, it was fitting for a religious man to do so.

He grew old in the monastery. He lived among the brothers of the house, and I like to think that he did a bit of wandering at feasts, now and then, with a cup in his hand and a harp in front of him that he was perfectly happy to take.

And then, when the time came, Bede says, he knew.

Some days before his death, Caedmon felt a small illness come upon him, nothing dramatic, and he said to the brother who looked after the sick, Prepare a place for me to lie down. It was a calm request, and the brother, who was surprised, since the old man did not look ill, did as he asked.

That evening, near midnight, Caedmon asked: Is the Eucharist here?

They said, Why do you need it? You are not going to die. You are so cheerful, talking to us as if you were in perfect health.

And Caedmon said, Bring me the Eucharist anyway.

When they had brought it, he took it in his hand, and he asked a question that I think is one of the loveliest things in all of Bede. He asked, "Are you all at peace with me? Do any of you have any complaint against me, any anger?"

And they answered, Not at all. We are all at peace with you. Everyone is.

And then they asked him the same question, and he said, I am at peace with all the servants of God.

And then, when he had received the Eucharist, and had strengthened himself for the journey, he asked how long it was until the brothers would be woken for the night prayers.

They told him: not long.

"Good," he said. "Let us wait for them."

He signed himself with the cross, laid his head on the pillow, closed his eyes for a moment, and in silence, Bede says, ended his life.

And so, the man who had been unable to sing a note, who had left the feast, who had hidden among the cows, went out of this life the way he had come into the song: quietly, and sure that someone was waiting.

And what of Hilda?

She lived on for some years after that. She led the house at Whitby for the rest of her life. Her greatest public moment, perhaps, was in six hundred and sixty-four, when kings and bishops from all over the north came to her monastery to settle a bitter argument over when to keep Easter. The monks who followed the customs of Iona, as Hilda herself did, and the clergy who followed the practice of Rome, argued for days. We won't go into the arguments tonight, which were long and technical and, for a bedtime story, not very restful. It is enough to say that the decision was made, that Hilda accepted it with grace and went on shepherding her house, and that the quarrel did not tear the Church in the north apart, which, considering how much anger there was in that hall, is a small miracle in its own right.

But, in the last six years of her life, she was ill.

A fever came upon her, Bede says, and it burned inside her, and it never completely left. For six years it stayed with her. And in all that time, he tells us, she never stopped teaching, never stopped giving thanks. She made her whole community, the sisters and the brothers, serve God in their health and thank Him in their sickness, and she herself did both for the last six years of her life, quietly, in her bed or in her chair, until at last the illness reached her heart.

She died on the seventeenth of November, in the year six hundred and eighty. She was sixty-six.

There is one more thing that Bede tells us, and I will tell it exactly as he does, because it is a gentle thing and I do not want to dress it up.

On the night that Hilda died, there was a nun in a monastery that Hilda had founded, about thirteen miles away, in a place called Hackness. Her name was Begu, and she had given thirty years of her life to the service of God. And she opened her eyes, Bede says, and she saw the roof of the house seem to be lifted away, and a great light pouring down from above, and in the light, the soul of Hilda being carried up to heaven by angels.

Begu woke the others, and the sisters came to the church and prayed. And in the morning, brothers arrived from Whitby, with the news that the abbess had died in the night.

I don't know what to say about that. Bede reports it, and he reports it from Begu herself, and from the sisters who were there, and I think we can take it as they meant it. The people who loved her saw her go up. Perhaps they all did, in whatever way each of them could. And the bell they heard was ringing for her, and the angels, as they always do, were not far from her.

I find it a comfort to think that in the last, bright, strange hour of a good woman's life, a sister in a quiet room many miles away could hear the bell.

So here is where we leave them.

A cliff by the sea. A bell on the roof of a house in which kings are given advice, and cows are given names. An abbess in a bed by a window, and the whole house gathered round her. Most of us have a song we think we cannot sing. It may be a kind word, or an apology, or a prayer we are shy of saying, or a task that seems far too big and far too grand for someone like us. And we get up quietly from the table, and we go out into the dark.

But Caedmon's story says: the one who asks you to sing already knows you cannot. He asks anyway. And he does not ask for something clever. He asks for the plainest thing in the world. And Hilda's story says: be the person who notices. Be the one who, when a stranger turns up in the morning with a trembling little song, does not laugh, and does not take it for her own, but listens, and tests it, and tells the truth about it, and says, Come in. There is room for you here.

Notes for the curious.

Saint Hilda of Whitby is a real person, and unusually well recorded for her time. Almost everything we know comes from one chapter of Bede's Ecclesiastical History, completed around the year seven hundred and thirty-one, which draws on the memories of people who knew her. She was born in six hundred and fourteen, baptised in six hundred and twenty-seven, abbess of Hartlepool by about six hundred and forty-nine, founder of the monastery at Whitby in six hundred and fifty-seven, and she died on the seventeenth of November, six hundred and eighty. The Council of Whitby, in six hundred and sixty-four, was held at her monastery. She is a saint of the Catholic Church, and many Orthodox Christians honour her as one of the saints of the early English church, from before the break between East and West.

Caedmon is the first English poet we know by name. The story of the dream, the reeve, the abbess and the death comes from Bede's chapter twenty-four of the fourth book. The nine-line poem, called Caedmon's Hymn, survives in the old Northumbrian English in the margins of some of the earliest manuscripts of Bede's History, copied within a few years of his death, and is one of the oldest pieces of writing in English. Bede himself gave it in Latin only, and said plainly that it was a translation of the sense. Scholars have debated how much of the later poetry attributed to Caedmon is really his, and the answer is probably none of it; the nine lines are all we can be fairly sure of.

The vision of Begu is told by Bede in chapter twenty-three. It is a report of a private experience by a nun at a distant house, and is told here as a report.

What we invented: the opening feast and its jokes, the cowherd's feelings and his love of his animals, the weather on the cliff, the surprise of the reeve, most of the atmosphere of the abbess's room, and every thought that we gave to anyone. Bede tells us what Caedmon sang, and that the learned men tested him, and that Hilda welcomed him as a monk, but he gives her no words at all in the scene, and we have been careful not to put any in her mouth. The modern English version of the hymn is our own rendering of the sense of the old song.

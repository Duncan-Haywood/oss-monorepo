The Wise Men and the Star Over Bethlehem

Travellers from the east who read the sky, a worried king in Jerusalem, a prophecy found in a scroll, and a house in Bethlehem where they kneel and open their gifts. Matthew, chapter two, told straight, with a bedtime telling of the road and the waiting.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
the wise men (the Magi), King Herod the Great, the chief priests and scribes of Jerusalem, Mary, the child Jesus, Joseph, an angel in a dream
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both honour the Magi and both keep their visit in the Christmas season, the Catholic Church at the Epiphany, the Orthodox Church within the feast of the Nativity itself
Scripture and history
the story is told only in the Gospel according to Matthew, chapter two, verses one to twelve, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture; Herod the Great, who died in or near the year four before the common era, is known from the historian Josephus
Legend
later tradition says there were three of them, that they were kings, that they were named Caspar, Melchior and Balthasar, and that their bones lie in Cologne; none of that is in Matthew, and we say so in the notes and leave it out of the story
What we invented
the road, the nights of watching, the talk in the caravan, the waiting at the gate in Jerusalem and the stop outside Bethlehem are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; the question of the Magi, the words of Herod, the answer of the scribes and the dream follow Matthew in our own plain wording, and Matthew gives the Magi no names and no number
Feast day
the Catholic Church keeps the Epiphany of the Lord on the sixth of January, or on a Sunday near it in many countries; the Orthodox Church keeps the adoration of the Magi within the feast of the Nativity, on the twenty-fifth of December by its own calendars, and keeps the sixth of January as the Theophany, the Baptism of the Lord

Sources

  • The Gospel according to Matthew, chapter two, verses one to twelve, the only account of the Magi
  • The Book of Micah, chapter five, verse two, which the scribes quote, and the Second Book of Samuel, chapter five, verse two, which Matthew joins to it
  • The Book of Numbers, chapter twenty-four, verse seventeen, a star out of Jacob, which many Christian writers have connected with this story as a reading
  • The Book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter sixty, verses one to six, the nations coming to the light with gold and frankincense, which many Christian writers have connected with it as a reading
  • Flavius Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews, book seventeen, for the character of Herod and the date of his death
  • Origen, Against Celsus, book one, and Irenaeus, Against Heresies, book three, for the early Christian readings of the three gifts, given as readings only

Full script

Imagine being paid to look at the sky.

Not for fun. As a job, with a reputation, a long training and a small group of very serious colleagues. Every clear night for most of your life you go up on the roof, you write down what is where, and the next night you write it down again. You are not looking for anything dramatic. You are looking for the dull, patient kind of thing, a planet that is a little late, a star that is a little early, the sort of detail that nobody else would ever notice. And then one night, you see something that is not in any of your tables.

That is tonight's story. And it starts with a man who very nearly did not go.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the visit of the wise men, from the second chapter of Matthew's Gospel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Matthew tells it in only twelve verses, so a few small things are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original: the road, the nights of watching, the talk of the travellers, and the waiting at the edge of the town. Matthew does not tell us how many wise men there were, or what they were called, and neither shall we. What the wise men ask, what Herod says and what the scribes answer follow Matthew in plain words. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

First, who were they?

Matthew calls them magoi, and he says they came from the east. That is the whole description. The Greek word is the plural of magos, and it is the root of our word magic, and also of our word magician, which is why you will sometimes see this story illustrated with pointy hats and wands. Please put the wand away. That is not what the word meant, or not mainly. In the ancient world a magos was a learned man of a particular kind. The word began as the name of a priestly class in Persia. By Matthew's day it was used much more widely, and it could mean a priest, or an interpreter of dreams, or a student of the stars, and sometimes, I am sorry to say, a fraud who took money from the credulous. It was a word that could be said with respect or with a sneer, according to who was saying it.

Matthew uses it with respect. These men are the good ones. They are people who study the sky, and who are honest enough to follow what they see, wherever it leads.

Where did they come from? Matthew says only, from the east. Later writers offered guesses, Persia, Babylon, Arabia, and these are guesses, and each of them has something to be said for it. Babylon had kept careful records of the sky for centuries. Persia was the home of the old learned priests. Arabia was where the incense came from. We cannot choose, and the Gospel does not need us to.

And how many were they? Here I want to be very clear, because most of us were taught otherwise. Matthew does not say. Not three, not two, not twelve. The number three comes from the gifts. There are three gifts named, gold and frankincense and myrrh, and it was an easy step to think, one gift, one man. Some of the very early Christian writers did just that. But it is a reading and not a fact, and some old eastern traditions counted twelve. So tonight there are simply travellers. A company. And as for the kings, the idea that they were kings came later, partly because a psalm and a passage in Isaiah speak of kings bringing gifts, and partly because it is a good story. In Matthew they are not kings. The only king in the chapter is the other one.

Now, the sky.

Matthew gives us only a few words about what they saw. They say, we saw his star at its rising. Or perhaps, in its rising, or when it rose. The Greek is a little open, and the scholars have argued about it for centuries. But it is a star that is his, that belongs to a newborn king of the Jews, and they saw it in the east, or while they were in the east, and that was enough to make them pack.

Let me tell you what the Gospel does not say. It does not say what the star was. It does not say it was a comet, or a planet, or two planets meeting, or a new star that flared up and faded, or a miracle that was never a physical object at all. People have proposed every one of these. There is a famous idea that in the year seven before the common era, the planets Jupiter and Saturn met three times in the constellation Pisces, in one year, which is a rare and handsome sight. The astronomer Johannes Kepler wrote about something like it four hundred years ago. It is a lovely idea, and it might even be right, and I would be very careful about saying so, because nobody can show that Matthew's star was that. The old Fathers of the Church were more cautious than some modern authors. Some of them said, this star was a special sign, appointed by God for this purpose, and the sky itself bowed to the child. We shall leave it there. We shall say it was a star, as Matthew did, and that it was theirs to read, and that it said something to people who had spent their lives learning to listen to the sky.

There is also an old and gentle connection that Christian readers have always noticed. Long before, in the book of Numbers, a prophet who was not even an Israelite, a man called Balaam who came from the east, had said, a star shall come out of Jacob, and a sceptre shall rise out of Israel. Matthew does not quote it. He does not need to. But many readers have heard it behind the story, like a bell a long way off. I mention it as a reading, because it is, and a good one. The Gospel itself simply says the star was seen.

And I find it moving that the first people in Matthew's Gospel to notice the Messiah's birth are outsiders. They are not priests of the Temple, or scribes of the Law, or neighbours. They are strangers, from a different country and a different religion, who had never heard of the Law of Moses, and who had read what they were able to read. God, it seems, speaks to people in the language they can understand. To shepherds, he sent an angel. To these men, he sent a star.

Let us imagine the roof.

I will tell you plainly that this part is ours, and a guess. Somewhere in the east, on a flat roof, on a clear night, there are some people. I will not number them. There is a lamp, and a table with a very old set of clay tablets, or a roll of papyrus, or whatever they used, and a water jar, and a blanket, because the desert is cold at night. There is an old person, who is the senior one, and a younger one who does the writing, and perhaps someone else who just likes being up there.

And one of them says, that is not right.

That is how it goes with these things. Nobody shouts. Nobody says, behold. Somebody notices a small error in the pattern, and says, that is not right, in the voice of someone who has just spotted a misspelling. And the others look. And one says, it is very bright. And another says, it was not there last month. And then a long silence, because they all know what each of them is thinking, and nobody wants to be the first one to say it.

What are they thinking? We do not know. Matthew does not tell us how they linked the star to the Jewish king. Some think they knew the Jewish scriptures, because there had been Jewish communities in the east for centuries, ever since the exile in Babylon, and some of the learned Jews there would certainly have talked with the learned stargazers. It is a reasonable guess, and it is only a guess. What the Gospel tells us is the result. They saw the star, they understood it to mean a king of the Jews had been born, and they decided to go.

And here is a thing to think about as you lie there. They did not know the way. They did not know the language, the customs, the road, the politics, or the weather. They knew one thing, that something had happened, and that they were meant to be there. It was a very long journey. We are not told how long, but a journey of many hundreds of miles on foot or with beasts of burden, in that world, with packing and arranging and resting, could easily take weeks or even months. They were not young, I suspect. A study of the sky is not a young person's job. They went anyway.

Now the road.

Again, ours. Let us picture the packing. There are the gifts, wrapped carefully, in whatever one wrapped such things in. Matthew will tell us in a moment what they are, and why they are not the sort of thing you toss in a bag. There is food. There is a tent, perhaps. There are servants, though Matthew does not mention them, because the people who carry the water are never mentioned in the Gospels, or in most books. And there are the animals. I know I said there would be no camels, because Matthew does not give any, and I am going to keep to that. But I will say that people in that part of the world did travel by caravan, and that a caravan of the learned would not be a small thing, and that you may picture whatever beast you like.

They travel mostly by night, perhaps, when it is cool, with the star ahead. That is a lovely thing to imagine, though I cannot promise it. Perhaps they travel by day, and only watch at night, as a navigator might check his position. What I think is true, and what the story shows, is that for some stretch of the journey the star was out of sight. Matthew says nothing about it until the very end, and when it comes back, he says they rejoiced exceedingly with great joy. It is the strongest sentence in the chapter. Nobody rejoices exceedingly about something that was always there. They are glad because it had gone.

So I like to think there was a long middle stretch of the journey when the sky was just the sky, and the men looked up every night and saw only the usual, and had nothing but their own decision to go on. And they went on. And that, I think, is part of why the Church has loved them. Following a bright star is easy. Anyone would. They kept on when it went dim.

And they ended up, in the end, not at the small town they were aiming for, but at the capital. Which is the next thing.

They arrived in Jerusalem, and we have to say something about the city, and about the king.

The king was Herod, whom history calls Herod the Great, and I have to say that the title is a bit misleading. He was great in the sense of building great things. He rebuilt the Temple of Jerusalem on a much grander scale, and built harbours and fortresses and cities, and he reigned for about thirty-three years. The Jewish historian Josephus, who wrote about him a century later, describes a brilliant, suspicious and increasingly cruel man. He was not himself descended from the old royal line. He had been appointed by the Romans. He had a great fear, all his life, of being replaced. Josephus says that he had members of his own family put to death over suspicions that they were plotting against him. He died, as we said in the last episode, in or near the year four before the common era, in great pain, and a very unpopular man.

Hold that picture. A very powerful king, who knows he is unloved, and who is very old, and very afraid. And into his city, one day, come strangers from the east, asking a question.

And the question is this. Matthew gives it in a single sentence. Where is the child who has been born king of the Jews? For we saw his star at its rising, and have come to worship him.

I want you to notice how good a question it is. There is no malice in it. These men are not spies and not rebels. They are scholars, who have followed a sign, and who have come to the obvious place, to the capital, to the palace, to ask the king. Where would you look for a newborn king, if not in the king's own house? It is a perfectly sensible thing to do. And it is, as the story shows, the one thing that could not have been more dangerous.

Because there is only one king of the Jews at the time, and he is sitting in the palace. And someone has just walked in and asked where the real one is.

Matthew gives the next reaction in one short sentence, and I think it is one of the most revealing in the whole Gospel. When Herod the king heard this, he was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him.

Troubled. The Greek word is the same one used for a stormy sea. It means shaken, stirred up, thrown into disorder. Herod is afraid, and it is not hard to see why. And, says Matthew, all Jerusalem with him. Why should the whole city be afraid? Because everyone knew what Herod was like when he was afraid. An anxious Herod meant arrests, and trials, and sudden deaths. The people of the city might well want a Messiah, in the long run, but they had good reason not to want one announced tonight.

So there is a curious contrast, and the Gospel does not point it out, it just sets it down. On the one hand, strangers from a far country, who see a new star and are filled with a quiet desire to go and kneel. And on the other hand, the king and the capital, who have the Temple and the scrolls and the whole tradition, and who hear the same news and get nervous.

What does Herod do? He is a clever man, and here is a careful move. He does not decide anything. He calls a meeting. He gathers all the chief priests and scribes of the people, which is to say the experts in the scriptures, and asks them where the Christ, that is the Messiah, is to be born.

And they know. They do not even have to look it up. They say, in Bethlehem of Judea, for so it is written by the prophet. And they quote. And Matthew gives us the quotation, which is a joining of two old texts. There is the prophet Micah, who wrote some seven hundred years before, you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah, are by no means least among the rulers of Judah. And there is an older line, from the days of King David, you shall be shepherd of my people Israel. Bethlehem was David's own town. Of course it was Bethlehem.

Now I want you to think about this scene for a moment, because there is something quietly remarkable in it. These scribes know exactly where the Messiah is to be born. They can say it at once. They have the prophecy by heart. And the strangers from the east, who have no such scriptures, have come hundreds of miles. And the scribes, who are six miles away, do not go. Not one of them. Matthew does not say that they were wicked. He says nothing at all about what they did next. It is simply there, the gap between knowing the answer and going to see.

I do not think we should be too hard on them. They had a king who was in a dangerous mood. They had their jobs and their families. And it is a thing that is easy to do, to know the right text and not to walk six miles. I do it myself, often. It is a thing to think about, tonight, at the end of the day, if you have a moment. What is the thing that I know, and have not gone to see?

Now Herod calls the wise men aside.

Matthew says that he summoned them secretly, which is a word that should make you sit up. The meeting is private. Not a word of it goes to the scribes. And he asks them, carefully, the exact time when the star had appeared. That is the first detail Herod wants, because the time is useful. The child will have been born at about that time, and so Herod can work out how old the child is.

I would like you to remember that. Remember it, because it matters at the end of the story, and in the next story. Herod is gathering information for a purpose.

And then he sends them to Bethlehem. He says, go and search diligently for the child, and when you have found him, bring me word, that I too may come and worship him.

And I think the old writers were right to hear something in that. It sounds so gentle. It sounds so respectful. That I too may come and worship him. Matthew does not comment. He lets the king's words stand there, and he lets us hear them, and we can feel the cold under them, because we know what kind of king this is.

And the wise men, who have no reason to doubt him, nod and say thank you, and set off. They are not fools. But they are guests in a foreign country, and they have been given directions by the host. It would have been rude to say no.

Let us go back to the dark.

I imagine them leaving the city, at evening perhaps, through a gate and down a road, with the lamps of Jerusalem behind them. Matthew does not say whether they went at night, but I like to think that was when it happened. It is a short road, as these things go. Bethlehem is about six miles south. A good walk for an afternoon. And on it, I think, there is a long silence. Perhaps they are tired. Perhaps they are thinking about the king's face. Perhaps one of them is saying, I did not like him, and another is saying, I did not like the way he asked about the time.

Perhaps. We are guessing again. What Matthew tells us is that after they had heard the king, they went on their way. And here is the sentence. And behold, the star that they had seen at its rising went before them, until it came to rest over the place where the child was.

Behold. The word Matthew uses when something remarkable happens. The star that they had seen in the east, the star that had been missing, is there again. Perhaps it was there all along, behind the lamps of the city. Perhaps it came back at that moment. Matthew does not say. And the Gospel gives the reaction I quoted earlier. When they saw the star, they rejoiced exceedingly with great joy.

Exceedingly, with great joy. It is, I think, as strong a way as a writer can say it. In the whole Gospel, there is nobody else who is said to rejoice quite like this. And that is a joy that has been waiting a long time. A joy that has crossed a desert and a city and a lie.

The star stops, and the story slows, and now we come to the house.

Matthew says, and this is one of the details that people often miss, they went into the house. Not the stable. Not the cave. The house. The shepherds, in Luke's Gospel, find the child in a manger, on the night of the birth. But the wise men, in Matthew, come into a house, and some time later. How much later, we are not told. Weeks, perhaps. Months. Perhaps up to two years, since Herod will count that way. Many a Christmas card puts the wise men and the shepherds together at the stable on the same night, and it is a lovely picture, but it is a blending of two Gospels. We can enjoy the picture and also know that the Gospels themselves keep them apart.

So they come to the door of a house in Bethlehem. I will not tell you what it looked like, because Matthew does not, and anything I said would be a guess. It is an ordinary house, I suppose, in an ordinary town, with an ordinary door. And someone has to knock. And someone has to open it.

And I would like to stop here for a moment, and think of Mary. Or of Joseph, for that matter. A family in a small house. They have had a hard year, with a long journey and a birth away from home. And there is a knock at the door in the evening, and when it opens, there is a company of strangers in foreign clothes, who have come from a very long way, and who are standing, perfectly politely, on the doorstep.

What do you say?

I do not know. Matthew does not tell us. What he tells us is what the visitors do. And it is the most important thing in the whole chapter.

They saw the child with Mary his mother, and they fell down and worshiped him.

That is the sentence. Let us take it slowly. They saw the child with Mary his mother. The child is a small boy, and he is with his mother. That is all they see. There is no throne, no crown, no soldiers, no gold, no courtiers. There is a young woman and a small child, in an ordinary house. And I think this is the moment the whole story has been building to. They have come for a king, and they find a child in a house, and they do not hesitate. They fall down.

The word translated worshiped is a strong one. It is used for the kind of reverence that people give to a king, or to God. Matthew has chosen it on purpose, and he uses it again in the Gospel, of people who kneel before Jesus. These wise men, who studied the sky for a living, and who came a long way for a sign, kneel in a small room in front of a child, and give him the honour that is due to God.

And then they open their treasures. Matthew says they presented him with gifts, gold and frankincense and myrrh.

Let us take the gifts one at a time, because they are interesting, and because the early Christians loved to think about them.

Gold needs little explanation. It was the gift for a king, and in the ancient world it was also a gift of great practical value. Frankincense is the resin of a tree that grows in southern Arabia and in the Horn of Africa. It was burned as incense, in temples, as a sign of prayer. It was very costly, and it was traded along the same roads the travellers had walked. And myrrh is another resin, also from Arabia and Africa, used in perfumes and oils, and, in that world, in the preparation of the dead.

Now the early Church did not take the gifts as a mere list. In the second and third centuries, writers like Irenaeus and Origen suggested that the gold was for a king, the frankincense for God, and the myrrh for a man who would die. It is a beautiful reading, and it has been repeated in carol after carol. But I should say, as I always do, that it is a reading. Matthew himself does not explain the gifts. He just names them. He lets them lie there in the lamplight. And we can think about them, and enjoy them, and be glad of what the Church has seen in them, without pretending that Matthew said it.

There is another old reading, and a more practical one, which I mentioned in the last episode. That these costly gifts, given to a poor family in a small house, would have been a very great help for a long journey to a foreign country. We have no way to prove it. But it fits, and a great many people have felt that the God who sent the gifts also knew what the road would need.

Now let me take a breath, and tell you what I like to imagine of the next hour. I will tell you right now that this part is entirely ours, and that it is small.

The visitors are in the house. They have given their gifts, and the child, who is a small child, is doing what small children do, which is to be interested in the shiny thing. I like to think he held out his hand for the gold. I like to think he put a bit of the resin to his nose, and made a face. These are the guesses of a narrator with a soft heart, and they change nothing in the story.

The family brings out what they have. A little bread, some dates, a jug of water, because in that country, when anyone comes to your door, you feed them. And the visitors sit on the floor, in the low light, and I think they say very little. They have come a very long way, and there is not much that can be said at the end of it. They look at the child. The child looks at them. And somewhere in the room, I suspect, there is an old and tired man who is looking at a child, and thinking, so it was true.

Then, in my picture, someone says that they must lie down. It is late. And they are given somewhere to sleep, a corner of the house or a place in the courtyard. And they lie in the dark, and they do not talk. They are very tired, and very happy. And then, as Matthew tells us, something happens that changes the road home.

The dream is told in a single sentence. And being warned in a dream not to return to Herod, they departed to their own country by another way.

Notice the pattern, because it runs all through the first two chapters of Matthew. God speaks through dreams. Joseph is warned in a dream, and is warned again, and a third time. And now the wise men, who are from another people, are warned in the same way. It is God speaking in the quiet of the night, in the one place where nobody can see.

And they obey. We are not told what the dream was. We are not told whether they discussed it in the morning, or whether anyone argued. Matthew says only that they took another way. They do not go back to Jerusalem. They do not return to Herod. They do not break their word because they have no word to break, they said they would bring him news, and I think we can say that, as honest men, they would have wished to keep it. But God gave them a clear reason, and they listened.

And so they vanish. They leave the story in a single clause, and we never hear of them again. Matthew does not tell us their names. Nobody records where they went, or whether the star was seen again, or what they said to their families. There is no sequel. They are the most mysterious figures in the Gospel, and also, I think, among the most loved. They come into the story out of nowhere, they say what they came to say, they do what they came to do, and they go home another way.

That is a phrase that has been quoted by a great many writers, and it is not hard to see why. They went home by another way. After you have met the child, you cannot go back the way you came.

Now let us wind down. And I want to say something about what happened afterward, as gently as I can.

In the next scene in Matthew, which is the one we told in the last episode, Joseph is warned to take the child and his mother and flee to Egypt, because Herod will search for the child to destroy him. And Matthew tells, in a few quiet lines, of what Herod did when he saw that he had been tricked. It is a hard passage, about the children of Bethlehem, and the Church honours those children as the Holy Innocents. We told it briefly then and we will not tell it again tonight. This is a night for the wise men, and for the child in the house, and for the star.

But I do want to say this, because I think it is part of the story. There is a shadow behind this gentle scene, and the Gospel does not hide it. The wise men kneel in the lamplight, and a few miles away a frightened king is sitting in a palace, and has already begun to plan. Matthew puts those two things side by side on purpose. He wants us to see them both. There is a child who is worshipped by strangers, and a king who is afraid. And the whole of the Gospel that follows is the story of how those two meet.

And yet, for tonight, the shadow stays outside. The door is closed. The lamp is lit. And a small boy in a small house is being looked at, with great love, by a company of people who have come across the world to see him.

I find myself thinking about the sky they left. All those years of looking, all that care, all those tables of figures. And at the end of it, a small face in a small room. I think that is one of the quiet lessons of the story. That what we are looking for, in all our study, and all our effort, is not far away and not grand. It is nearer than we thought, and smaller, and much more tender. And it has been waiting, patiently, in a house, for us to arrive.

Now the notes for the curious.

The whole of tonight's story is in the Gospel according to Matthew, chapter two, verses one to twelve. It is a short passage, and I would recommend reading it, slowly, once, tonight or tomorrow, and noticing how much is not there.

What we have kept from Matthew: the magoi, from the east, coming to Jerusalem; their question, where is the child who has been born king of the Jews, for we saw his star at its rising and have come to worship him; Herod troubled, and all Jerusalem with him; the gathering of the chief priests and scribes; their answer, in Bethlehem of Judea, with the words of the prophet; Herod's secret meeting, the question about the time of the star's appearing, and the instruction to search diligently and bring word; the star going before them until it stood over the place; their great joy; the house, the child with Mary his mother, the kneeling and the worship; the gifts of gold, frankincense and myrrh; the warning in a dream; and the return by another way.

What we flagged as a reading, or as later tradition. The number three is inferred from the three gifts and is not in Matthew. Their being kings is a later idea, helped by readings of Psalm seventy-two and Isaiah sixty, which many Christian writers connected with this story. The names Caspar, Melchior and Balthasar appear in writings several centuries after the Gospel, and are a tradition and not Scripture. The relics said to be theirs, which are honoured in the cathedral of Cologne, are a later tradition, and we leave them alone. The identification of the star with a meeting of Jupiter and Saturn, or with a comet, or with any one event, is a proposal that nobody can prove, and we have called it that. The Fathers' reading of the gifts as king, God and death is a reading. The idea that the gold paid for the road to Egypt is a reading. And the connection to Balaam's star in Numbers is a reading that many have noticed, and Matthew does not make it. The place of the Magi's homeland, in Persia or Babylon or Arabia, is a guess.

A note on the timing. Luke's shepherds find the child in a manger on the night of the birth. Matthew's Magi come to a house, and at a time that Matthew does not give. The phrase that Herod asked the exact time of the star, and the later order that reaches children of two years and under, have led many readers to think that some months, perhaps even as much as two years, had passed. It is a reasonable inference and it is only an inference.

A note on the feast. The Catholic Church keeps the Epiphany, which means a showing, on the sixth of January, or on the Sunday near it in many countries, and the story of the Magi is its chief Gospel, the showing of the child to the nations. The Orthodox Church keeps the visit of the Magi within the feast of the Nativity, as in its hymns, and keeps the sixth of January for the Theophany, which is the baptism of the Lord. Both Churches honour the Magi, and each keeps them in its own way and its own season, and we have simply said so.

What we invented: the roof and the lamp and the first quiet words, that is not right; the long middle stretch of the journey with the sky empty; the packing and the talk of the travellers; the long silence on the road to Bethlehem; the guess at what the family felt when they heard the knock; the child reaching for the gold and wrinkling his nose at the resin; the bread and dates and water; the lying down in the courtyard. None of it is in Matthew, and none of it changes what happens or what it means. Matthew gives the Magi only the one line of speech, and Herod only the two, and those follow his Gospel in plain words.

One honesty note on the history. We said that the Jewish historian Josephus describes Herod as suspicious and cruel, and that is what he says. We also said that Herod rebuilt the Temple on a grander scale, and that is well known. Herod's death in or near the year four before the common era is the usual dating, and scholars differ by a year or so. We have taken nothing about the Magi from outside Matthew, because nothing outside him can be trusted to tell us about them.

For further reading, the scene just after this one is in the same chapter, in Matthew, chapter two, verses thirteen to twenty-three, and we told it in our episode Joseph and the Night Road to Egypt. The scene just before it, in Luke, is our episode The Shepherds and the Night Watch Above Bethlehem. Listen to them in whatever order you like. The Gospels do not mind.

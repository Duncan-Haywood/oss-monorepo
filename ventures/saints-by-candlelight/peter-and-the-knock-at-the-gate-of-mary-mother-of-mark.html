Peter and the Knock at the Gate of Mary, Mother of Mark

An apostle sleeps in chains between two soldiers, an angel wakes him with a tap on the side, and a servant girl called Rhoda is so happy to hear his voice at the gate that she forgets to open it. Told straight from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twelve, verses one to seventeen.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Peter the apostle, the angel of the Lord, Rhoda, Mary the mother of John Mark, King Herod Agrippa
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; the account is Scripture for both, and both traditions honour Peter as an apostle and a saint
Scripture and history
the account is in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twelve, verses one to seventeen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture; Herod Agrippa the First, grandson of Herod the Great, really did rule Judea from the year forty-one to the year forty-four, and the arrest falls in that time
Legend
none in the story itself; later traditions say that the house of Mary stood where a church stands today in Jerusalem, and that Peter's chains were kept and venerated, but those are later traditions that we name and do not rely on
What we invented
the weather, the sounds of the city, the look of the cell, the soldiers' small talk, the room full of people, the supper, what Rhoda was doing before she heard the knock, and the quiet moments between the verses are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; what Luke says the angel, Peter, Rhoda and the others did and said is kept as Luke gives it, and we add nothing to what it means
Feast day
Catholic calendars keep the feast of Saint Peter in Chains on the first of August, and the Orthodox keep the veneration of the honourable chains of Saint Peter on the sixteenth of January, though calendars differ and we advise checking your own; we claim nothing more precise

She does not open it. She puts her mouth close to the wood and says, who is there? And a voice answers from the other side. And she knows the voice. She would know it anywhere. And she is so astonished, and so happy, that she does something that you and I would probably do as well. She turns around and runs away, and leaves the man standing in the street.

The city is Jerusalem. The year is somewhere around forty-four of the common era, which is about a dozen years after the first Easter. The country at this time has a king. This is unusual, because for most of the years around then the Romans ran things through governors, and the governors were not much loved. But for three short years, from about the year forty-one to the year forty-four, the Romans handed Judea to a king of the old royal family. His name was Herod Agrippa, which is confusing, because there were a lot of Herods, and they were all related, and several of them were nasty. This one was a grandson of the Herod who, in the story of the Christmas star, was afraid of a baby. Agrippa had grown up in Rome, among the emperor's family and friends. He had a gift for being liked by whoever was in charge. And now that he was in charge himself, he wanted to be liked at home as well.

Here is a thing worth noticing about people who want to be liked. They watch the crowd. They find out what the crowd wants, and they give it to them. And in Jerusalem in those years, there were a good many people who were uneasy about the new movement of Jesus's followers, who did not like how fast it was growing, and who would be glad to see someone put a stop to it.

So Herod Agrippa did what a man does who wants to please the crowd. Luke puts it very briefly, and we will put it just as briefly. He laid violent hands on some who belonged to the church. And he killed James, the brother of John, with the sword.

That is all that Luke says about James. It is one short sentence. It is a sad sentence, because James was one of the first friends Jesus called, one of the sons of Zebedee, one of the three who had been on the mountain and in the garden. And so when we meet Peter in the story, we are meeting a man who has just lost a friend.

Luke goes on. When Herod saw that it pleased the Jews, he proceeded to arrest Peter also.

Notice that word, pleased. It was not hatred, exactly, that moved Herod. It was that he saw it made people pleased. Sometimes the hardest things in the world happen because somebody wanted to be popular.

And it was during the days of Unleavened Bread. That is the week of Passover, the great spring festival, when the city is crowded with pilgrims and the air smells of baking and of lambs. Herod had Peter seized and put in prison, and he handed him over to four squads of four soldiers each to guard him. That makes sixteen soldiers for one fisherman. It is a great many. It tells you what kind of a man Herod took Peter to be, and also something about how nervous he was, because Peter had been in prison before, and had got out, and Herod did not mean to be embarrassed twice.

And Luke tells us what Herod was planning. He intended, after the Passover, to bring him out to the people. The festival was a time for feasts and not for trials, and so Peter was to be kept until it was over. That means that Peter had a number of days. He knew, and everyone in the city knew, that those days would come to an end.

So Peter was kept in prison. And then Luke gives us the line that the whole story turns on, in six plain words. But earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church.

Let us go and look at the prison, because Luke wants us to see it.

We do not know exactly which prison this was. There was a fortress in Jerusalem, the Antonia, built beside the temple, and there was Herod's own palace, and either could have had a cell. We shall not pretend to know. Let us only imagine that it is a stone room, small and dry, and cool in the daytime and cold at night, with a door of heavy wood, and with a smell of old straw. There is a little window high up, which lets in a thin grey light in the mornings. And on the floor there is a man lying down.

He is not alone. Luke is careful about this. Peter was sleeping, he says, between two soldiers, bound with two chains, and sentries before the door were keeping the prison.

So the picture is this. On the left, a soldier, chained to Peter's left wrist. On the right, a soldier, chained to Peter's right wrist. Two more soldiers outside the door of the cell. And here is the thing we want to stop on. Peter is asleep.

Luke does not say, Peter was awake all night, pacing, going over what he might say. He does not say, Peter lay there trembling. He says that Peter was sleeping, and he gives us the picture of it, and then he tells us the way the angel will have to wake him. This is the very night before Herod meant to bring him out. It is the last night, as far as anyone knows. And he is asleep, soundly enough that an angel has to nudge him.

How can a man sleep like that? We do not know, and Luke does not say. We might guess a few things. Perhaps he was tired, because he had been up for many nights, and his body simply gave out. Perhaps he had said his prayers, and said them again, and found that he had nothing left in his hands to hold. Perhaps he had learned something by this time about the difference between the things that are in your power and the things that are not, and had put the second sort down, the way you put down a heavy basket at the end of a long walk.

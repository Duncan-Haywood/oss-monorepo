Thecla and the Window at Iconium

A girl who sits at an upstairs window for three days listening to a short, bald stranger preach next door, a bracelet given to a doorkeeper, a silver mirror given to a jailer, a storm that puts out a fire, a family fasting in an empty tomb, a lioness who lies down at a prisoner's feet, and a queen who takes in a stranger as her daughter. The old story of Saint Thecla of Iconium, from the second-century Acts of Paul and Thecla, told as the legend it is.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Saint Thecla of Iconium, Saint Paul the Apostle, Theocleia her mother, Thamyris who was to marry her, Onesiphorus with his wife Lectra and their sons Simmias and Zeno, Demas and Hermogenes, the governor Castelius, Alexander of Antioch, and Tryphaena, a wealthy woman of Antioch whom the story calls a queen
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; in the Orthodox Church she is honoured as Protomartyr among women and Equal to the Apostles
Scripture and history
Paul's preaching at Iconium in the Book of Acts, chapter fourteen; the names Demas, Hermogenes, Onesiphorus and Alexander from the Second Letter to Timothy; Tertullian's report, written about the year two hundred, that a presbyter in Asia wrote the Acts of Paul out of love for Paul and was removed from his office; the great shrine of Saint Thecla near Seleucia, visited by the pilgrim Egeria in three hundred and eighty-four, where Gregory of Nazianzus also stayed for some years; and the secret name Thecla given in the family to Saint Macrina, as her brother Gregory of Nyssa reports
Legend
the whole of Thecla's own story, from the window to her last years at Seleucia, comes from the Acts of Paul and Thecla, a second-century book that the Church has never counted as Scripture; her later life in a cave near Seleucia and the rock that opened for her come from still later tellings, and the mountain at Maaloula is a local legend
What we invented
most of the dialogue, including the conversations with Theocleia, Thamyris, the doorkeeper, the jailer, Onesiphorus's boys, Tryphaena and Paul, and the narrator's pictures of the houses, the roads, the tomb and the arena, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; the Acts are given in our own plain renderings, not quoted from a published translation; we have left out or softened several of the harder scenes of the original for bedtime
Feast day
the twenty-fourth of September in the Orthodox Church; the twenty-third of September in the older Roman calendar and in many places in the West, including Tarragona in Spain, where she is the patron saint

Full script

It is evening in a town in the hills, and a girl is sitting at an upstairs window.

She has been sitting there since the morning. She was sitting there yesterday, too, and the day before. Her supper is on a tray behind her, and it has gone cold. Her mother has been up the stairs four times. Her nurse has been up the stairs more times than anyone has counted. The girl has not moved.

Across the narrow street, in the courtyard of the house next door, a short, bald man with bowed legs and eyebrows that meet in the middle is talking to a crowd of people. He is not shouting. He does not need to. The courtyard is very quiet, and the girl at the window can hear every word.

She leans a little further out, with her elbows on the sill, and listens.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the old story of Saint Thecla of Iconium, from a book written in the second century called the Acts of Paul and Thecla, which is not Scripture, and which Christians have loved and argued about for eighteen hundred years, and most of the small conversations in it are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Tonight we are going to a town called Iconium.

If you went there now you would find a big, busy Turkish city called Konya, out in the middle of the high, flat country of central Anatolia, with mountains on the horizon and very cold winters and very hot summers. Konya is famous for many things. It is famous for its carpets, and for a great poet who lived there in the Middle Ages, and for a kind of long flat bread baked with meat on top that people there will tell you, firmly, is better than anything you can get anywhere else. I have no way of checking this. I am inclined to believe them.

But we are going back much further than that. We are going back to the middle of the first century, about twenty years after the Resurrection, when Iconium was a Roman town on one of the great roads that ran across Asia Minor. It had a market, and a theatre, and temples, and good stone houses with courtyards for the families who could afford them, and a governor who sat in judgment and did his best not to have any trouble.

We know from the Book of Acts, which is Scripture, that the Apostle Paul really did come to Iconium. Saint Luke tells us that Paul and Barnabas preached there, in the synagogue, and that a great number of people believed, and that the town was divided about them, and that in the end they had to leave in a hurry because some people were planning to throw stones at them. That is all Luke says. It is a few lines in chapter fourteen.

The story we are telling tonight is not from the Book of Acts. It is from a different book, written well over a hundred years later, called the Acts of Paul. It is a long, colourful, adventurous book, full of journeys and prisons and speeches and lions. Only some of it survives. And the part that has always been the best loved, the part that was copied again and again on its own, in Greek and Latin and Syriac and Armenian and Coptic and Ethiopic and Arabic and Slavonic, is the story of a girl from Iconium called Thecla.

Now. I want to be very clear about this book before we go any further, because honesty is the house rule on this show.

The Acts of Paul and Thecla is not in the Bible. The Church has never read it as Scripture. It was written well after the Apostles had died. And we actually know a little about who wrote it, because a writer from North Africa called Tertullian, about the year two hundred, tells us. He says a presbyter, that is a priest, somewhere in Asia, wrote the Acts of Paul. When he was found out, he admitted it, and he said he had done it out of love for Paul. And he was removed from his office.

Which is a very strange and rather touching thing to know about a book. It is a bit like finding a note tucked inside the back cover saying, I'm sorry, I made this up, but I meant well.

And yet. The Church did not throw Thecla away. Over the next few centuries, Christians in both East and West came to honour Thecla as a real woman and a saint. There was a great shrine to her, near the southern coast of what is now Turkey, and pilgrims walked for weeks to get there. Great bishops prayed there. Mothers named their daughters after her. Her name went into the old Roman calendar and into the Orthodox calendar, where she is called by two magnificent titles, Protomartyr among women, and Equal to the Apostles.

So what is history here, and what is legend? Probably this. There was a real woman, early in the life of the Church, in that part of the world, who heard the Gospel and gave her whole life to it, and whose memory was kept and loved. And around that memory, as happens, a story grew. And the story we have is the one the presbyter wrote down, out of love.

So tonight, we will tell it as what it is. An old story. A legend. A very good one. And at every step I will try to tell you which bits are the story, and which bits we have added, and where the bits that are true come in.

All right. Iconium. A house with a courtyard, on a street of good stone houses. And next door, a house with an upstairs window.

The story begins on a road outside the town.

A man called Onesiphorus is standing at the side of the road, early in the morning, with his wife Lectra and their two boys, Simmias and Zeno. Onesiphorus is a Christian. He lives in Iconium, and he has heard that Paul is coming, and he has gone out to the crossroads on the royal road, the road that leads to Lystra, to meet him and bring him home.

The trouble is that Onesiphorus has never actually seen Paul.

So he has brought a description. A friend called Titus has told him what Paul looks like. And the old book gives us Titus's description, and it is one of the most famous things in it, because it is the only description of what Paul looked like that has come down to us from the early centuries. I should say straight away that we do not know whether it is accurate. But here it is, as the book gives it.

A man small in size. Bald-headed. Bandy-legged. Strongly built. With eyebrows that meet in the middle, and a nose that is rather long. And full of grace. For sometimes he looked like a man, and sometimes he had the face of an angel.

Which is, if you think about it, a rather wonderful description. It starts like a police notice and ends like a hymn.

So the family stands at the crossroads, and watches people coming along the road, and Onesiphorus checks each one against the list.

"That one?" says Simmias.

"Too tall," says his father.

"That one?" says Zeno.

"Has hair," says his father.

"That one has hair too, but only a bit."

"That," says Onesiphorus, "is a woman with a basket of onions."

And then, the book says, they saw Paul coming. And they knew him. And he saw Onesiphorus, and smiled, and Onesiphorus said, "Greetings, servant of the blessed God," and Paul said, "Grace be with you and with your house." And they walked back into the town together, the little bald man in the middle, and the two boys running ahead to tell everyone.

There were two other men walking with Paul. The book calls them Demas and Hermogenes, and it tells us straight away that they were full of hypocrisy, and that they were only pretending to love him. Now, here is a small thing that I enjoy. If you open the real Second Letter to Timothy, in the New Testament, near the end, you will find a short sad list of names. A Demas who deserted Paul. A Hermogenes who turned away from him. An Onesiphorus who was not ashamed of his chains and looked for him and found him. An Alexander who did him a great deal of harm. The presbyter who wrote our story had clearly read that letter very carefully. And he took the names and gave them all parts to play, the faithful ones and the faithless ones. Keep an eye out for Alexander. He turns up later.

So Paul came into the house of Onesiphorus. And there was bread, and there was prayer, and people heard he was there, and came. And the next morning, and the morning after that, Paul stood in the courtyard of Onesiphorus's house and talked about God.

The book gives us his sermon. It is a list of blessings, rather like the Beatitudes, but longer. Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God. Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy. Blessed are those who have kept their baptism pure. And many more like that. There is a great deal about purity, and about giving your whole heart to God, and about the resurrection.

And next door, at an upstairs window, sat Thecla.

Thecla was young. The book calls her a virgin, which in this sort of story means she was a girl not yet married. Her mother's name was Theocleia. She was from a good family, one of the first families of Iconium, and she was engaged to be married to a young man called Thamyris, who was also from a good family, and who was, as far as we can tell, very pleased with the arrangement.

And her window looked straight across into the courtyard where Paul was talking.

The book says she sat at that window for three days and three nights. She did not come down to eat. She did not come down to drink. She did not, so far as anyone could see, sleep. She just sat, and listened. She could not see Paul's face very well from where she was. But she could hear him. And she heard about the God who made everything, and about Jesus, and about the resurrection, and about giving your whole heart to God. And she listened the way you listen to the one piece of news you have been waiting for all your life without knowing it.

Her mother was beside herself.

You have to have some sympathy for Theocleia. She had a daughter who was going to make an excellent marriage, and the girl had suddenly stopped eating and planted herself at the window like a cat watching a bird. And the bird was a bald stranger next door saying peculiar things about the resurrection of the dead.

So Theocleia sent for Thamyris.

He came hurrying, the book says, very happy, because he thought he was being sent for to set a date for the wedding. And Theocleia met him at the door and said something like this.

"Thamyris. I have to tell you something strange. For three days and three nights Thecla has not got up from that window. Not to eat. Not to drink. She is staring out at that man next door as if she were bound to the window. She is clinging to it like a spider in its web. He is a stranger, and he goes around saying that there is only one God and that people should live in purity, and she has drunk it all in. Go and talk to her. She is yours, after all. She will listen to you."

I like that the old book has the mother call her a spider at the window. It has the ring of something a real mother would say.

So Thamyris went up the stairs, and stood behind Thecla, and was very gentle with her.

"Thecla," he said. "My Thecla. What is the matter? Why are you sitting like this? Turn round and look at me. It's only me. Come away from the window."

And her mother and her nurse and the servants all stood about saying, Thecla, Thecla, come away, come and eat something, come and be sensible.

And Thecla did not turn round. The book says she kept her eyes fixed on the courtyard and went on listening to Paul.

And downstairs, the whole house began to weep. Thamyris wept because he was losing the girl he was going to marry. Theocleia wept because she was losing her daughter. And the maidservants wept because everyone else was weeping, which is the way of households.

Thamyris went out into the street, feeling very sorry for himself, and stood outside the gate of Onesiphorus's house, and watched people going in and out. And he saw the two men, Demas and Hermogenes, come out.

And he went up to them and said, "Who is this man in there? What is he? He is making all the young women of the town refuse to marry. I will give you money if you tell me about him."

And Demas and Hermogenes, who as you remember were only pretending to love Paul, said, "We don't know exactly who he is. But we know what to do. Take him before the governor, Castelius. Tell him this man is persuading the crowds to follow a new teaching, the teaching of the Christians. The governor will deal with him. And then you can have your Thecla back."

They also, the book says, had supper with Thamyris that night, at his expense, and drank a good deal of his wine. I mention it only so that you know what sort of people they were.

So the next morning Thamyris came to the gate of Onesiphorus's house with officials and a crowd of men with clubs, and he said to Paul, "You have ruined the city of Iconium. You have taken away the girl I was going to marry. Come with us to the governor."

And the crowd shouted, "Away with the magician! He has corrupted all our wives!"

Paul went with them. He did not argue. He was, after all, very used to this. It was not his first crowd, and it would not be his last.

And the governor, Castelius, sat in his seat of judgment, and looked down at the small bald man, and said, "Who are you, and what do you teach? These are serious accusations."

And Paul lifted up his voice and said, as the book tells it in our own plain words, "Since I am being asked today what I teach, listen. The living God, the God who needs nothing from anyone, but who wants us to be saved, has sent me to draw people away from corruption and impurity and death, so that they may not sin any more. That is why God sent his Son, Jesus Christ, whom I preach, and in whom I teach people to put their hope. He alone had compassion on a world that had lost its way. If that is what I am accused of, governor, then I am guilty."

And the governor, who was not a bad man, only a busy one, said, "Put him in prison. I will hear him again, more carefully, when I have time."

That night, Thecla came down from her window.

She waited until the house was asleep. Then she took off her bracelets. The book does not say how many. I like to imagine they were gold, and rather heavy, the sort of bracelets a girl from one of the first families of Iconium would have been given for her betrothal. She took them off one by one, and she went down the stairs very quietly, and out into the street.

At the door of her own house there was a doorkeeper. She gave him the bracelets, and he opened the door, and he did not ask any questions, and I expect he was very careful not to remember anything about it the next day.

And she went through the dark town to the prison.

At the prison there was a jailer. She had nothing left but a mirror. It was a silver mirror, the old book says, the kind a young woman would have kept in her room to see her face in. And she gave it to the jailer, and he let her in.

I think that is one of the loveliest small details in all the old stories. She gave away her bracelets to get out of her house, and she gave away her mirror to get into the prison. And from that night on, for the rest of the story, she does not seem to need either of them.

And she went in to where Paul was sitting in the dark, and sat down on the floor at his feet, and listened to him.

He talked to her about God. About Christ. About the greatness of God's love. The book says she kissed his chains, out of love for the Gospel he preached. And her faith grew all through that night, the way a fire grows when someone has been quietly feeding it.

Meanwhile, at home, it was morning, and Thecla's bed was empty.

You can imagine the scene. Theocleia in the doorway of an empty room. The nurse saying she was there last night, I swear she was there last night. Thamyris arriving in a fury. The whole family and all the servants out in the streets, looking for her, as if she were lost.

And then someone remembered the doorkeeper. And the doorkeeper, after a certain amount of persuasion, remembered that a young lady had gone out in the night, and that she had gone towards the prison.

So they went to the prison. And there she was, sitting on the floor at Paul's feet, as quiet and as happy as if she had been sitting there all her life.

They took both of them before the governor.

Paul they brought in chains. Thecla, the book says, would not come at first. When they went to fetch her, she was lying on the floor of the prison, in the very place where Paul had sat teaching, as though she could not bear to leave it. They had to lift her up and bring her.

The governor looked at Paul, and then at the girl, and said to Paul, "What is this? Why are you going about the city doing this?" And Paul did not answer him straight away. The crowd outside, which had grown, was shouting.

Then the governor turned to Thecla, and spoke to her more kindly.

"Thecla," he said. "Why won't you marry Thamyris, as the law of the Iconians says you should?"

And Thecla did not answer. She did not say a word. She stood there, the book says, with her eyes on Paul.

Now, I have to tell you that what happens next is hard. I am going to tell it gently.

Her mother, Theocleia, out of grief and anger and terrible embarrassment in front of the whole town, cried out a dreadful thing. She cried out that her daughter should be burned, so that all the other women of the town who had listened to Paul would be afraid.

I think we should be gentle with Theocleia. People say terrible things when they are frightened. And the old story does not leave her there. You will see.

And the governor, who was greatly troubled, gave his judgment. Paul was to be beaten and sent out of the city. And Thecla, because she would not answer, and because the crowd was howling, was to be burned in the theatre.

They took her to the theatre.

Iconium had a theatre, as every Roman town of any size did, a great half-circle of stone seats rising up a hill, with a flat space at the bottom. The whole town came. And Thecla was brought out into the middle.

And here is something the book says that I want you to hear. Thecla looked up at all those seats, all those faces, looking for one face. The book says she was looking for Paul, the way a lamb in the wilderness looks for the shepherd.

And she saw him. She saw, sitting in the crowd, someone who looked just like Paul. And she said to herself, Paul has come to watch over me, so that I do not lose heart. And she kept her eyes on him. And then, the book says, she understood that it was not Paul at all. It was the Lord, who had come to her in Paul's likeness. And as she watched, he rose and went up into heaven.

So she was not afraid any more.

The men of the town had built a great pile of wood. And Thecla came to it, and she made the sign of the cross, and she went up onto it.

And they lit it.

And the fire went up, high and roaring, and the book says that it did not touch her. It went up all around her, and it did not touch her.

And then God, the book says, had compassion on her. There was a great rumbling, deep under the ground. And a cloud came over the theatre, a great dark cloud full of water and hail. And it opened. And it poured down, so hard and so suddenly that the whole crowd ran for cover, slipping on the wet stone seats, holding their cloaks over their heads. And the fire went out. Hissing and spitting and steaming, the whole great pile of wood went out.

And in the rain and the confusion and the steam, with everybody running and nobody watching, Thecla came down off the wet wood, and walked out of the theatre, and was gone.

Now, I want to say clearly, as we always do on this show, that in the story, Thecla does not do this. She does not make the rain. She makes the sign of the cross and she trusts God, and God sends the rain. That is how it always is with the saints. They pray. God acts.

Meanwhile, where was Paul?

When they had beaten him and put him out of the town, he had not gone far. He had gone with Onesiphorus and Lectra and the two boys, who had left their house in Iconium to go with him, a little way out along the road towards a place called Daphne. And there, by the side of the road, they had found a tomb.

I should explain about the tomb. In those days, people were not buried inside towns. They were buried along the roads just outside, and families with money would have a tomb cut into the rock, or built of stone, a sort of little room with a door. Sometimes such a tomb would be made and stand empty for years, waiting. And this tomb was new. Nobody had been laid in it yet. It was simply a dry, quiet stone room by the side of the road, out of the wind.

So that is where they were. Paul, and a whole family, camping in an empty tomb. And Paul was praying and fasting for Thecla. He had been fasting for six days.

On about the sixth day, the boys were hungry.

They were boys. They had been very good. They had walked a long way and been very patient and listened to a great deal of praying. And now they said, as boys will, "Paul. We are hungry. We haven't got anything to buy bread with."

Because Onesiphorus, when he left his house to follow Paul, had left everything behind.

So Paul took off his cloak. His outer cloak, the warm one. And he gave it to the elder boy, and said, "Go, child. Sell this, and buy some bread, and bring it back."

So the boy went off down the road with Paul's cloak over his arm.

And on the road, he met Thecla.

"Thecla!" he said. "Where are you going?"

And she said, "I am looking for Paul. I got away from the fire."

And the boy, who was perhaps ten, and had been thinking of nothing but bread for a whole day, forgot all about the bread and said, "Come on! Come with me! I'll take you to him. He has been praying for you for six days and he hasn't eaten anything. Come on!"

And we might imagine that he did, after all, go and sell the cloak and buy the bread, because a boy who has been hungry for a day does not forget bread for long. And the old book tells us that when they all sat down together in the tomb, there were five loaves, and some vegetables, and salt, and water.

And when they came to the tomb, Paul was on his knees, praying. And the book gives us his prayer. He was saying, "Father of Christ, do not let the fire touch Thecla. Stand by her, for she is yours."

And Thecla, standing behind him in the doorway of the tomb, said, "Father, maker of heaven and earth, Father of your beloved Son Jesus Christ, I thank you, because you have saved me from the fire, so that I could see Paul again."

And Paul got up and turned round and saw her.

There was great joy in that tomb. Paul, and Onesiphorus, and Lectra, and the boys, and Thecla. And they sat down on the floor together and shared the five loaves and the vegetables and the salt and the water. And they were glad, the book says, with a great gladness, because of the holy works of Christ.

It must have been a very small, very crowded supper, in a stone room by the side of a road, with the light coming in through the door. I think it may have been the happiest meal in the whole story.

And after supper, Thecla said to Paul, "I will cut my hair short, and I will follow you wherever you go."

And Paul looked at her, and was careful. He said something like this. "These are hard times, Thecla. And you are young, and you are beautiful. I am afraid that another trial might come to you, worse than the first, and that you might not be able to bear it."

And Thecla said, "Then give me the seal in Christ. Give me baptism, and no trial will touch me."

And Paul said, "Be patient, Thecla. You will receive the water."

That is a strange answer, and people have puzzled over it ever since. Why didn't he baptise her there and then? The story doesn't really say. I think the writer is simply setting up what happens next. But I also think there is something true in it about Paul, the real Paul of the letters, who was very aware of how hard the road of a Christian could be, and who did not want anyone to walk it lightly.

Then Onesiphorus and his family went home to Iconium. And Paul set off on the road again, towards Antioch. And Thecla went with him.

I have to stop for a moment and say which Antioch, because there were at least two of them, and the book does not say. There was the great city of Antioch in Syria, one of the biggest cities in the whole Roman world, where the disciples were first called Christians. And there was a smaller Antioch, in Pisidia, not so very far from Iconium, where Paul had also preached. Scholars have argued about which one the book means for a long time. I am not going to settle it tonight. It was an Antioch. It was bigger than Iconium. It had an arena.

As they came into the city, a man called Alexander saw Thecla.

The book calls him one of the first men of the city. He was rich, and important, and he was in charge of the public games that were about to be held. And he saw Thecla in the street, and he wanted her. He tried to buy her from Paul, as if she were a slave, offering money and presents. And then, when that came to nothing, he simply tried to take hold of her, there in the street, in front of everybody.

And Thecla, who had been through the fire, was not going to stand for that.

She said, "Do not force a stranger! Do not force a servant of God! I am one of the first women of the Iconians, and because I would not marry Thamyris, I have been driven out of my city."

And then, the book says, she took hold of Alexander's cloak and tore it. And she pulled the crown off his head. He was wearing a golden crown, the kind an official in charge of the games would wear, with the emperor's image on it. And she knocked it off, into the street. And there he stood, with a torn cloak and no crown, in front of everybody.

I have to be honest. I think every listener who has ever heard this story, in every century, has cheered quietly at that bit. It is not the sort of thing that usually happens in a saint's life. But there it is.

Alexander did not cheer. He was furious, and humiliated, and he did what powerful men do. He dragged her before the governor of Antioch. And the governor, because Alexander was important and because Thecla had insulted the emperor's image on the crown, condemned her to the beasts, in the games that Alexander himself was about to put on.

And the women of Antioch, who had watched the whole thing, were outraged. They stood outside the governor's house and shouted, "This is a wicked judgment! An unholy judgment!"

And Thecla asked the governor for one thing. She asked to be kept safe, and kept pure, until the day of the games.

So the governor had to find somewhere to keep her. And a woman came forward.

Her name was Tryphaena. She was rich, and a widow, and the old book calls her a queen and says she was a kinswoman of the emperor. And she had lost her own daughter not long before. Her daughter had died, and the house was empty, and Tryphaena was grieving.

And Tryphaena said, "I will take her."

Here is a little thing for the history lovers. There really was a Queen Tryphaena in that part of the world, in Paul's time. Her name was Antonia Tryphaena. She was a queen of Pontus and Thrace, the great-granddaughter of Mark Antony, and so related to the emperor's family. People have found her coins. Whether the presbyter who wrote our story had heard of her, and borrowed her, the way he borrowed the names from the Letter to Timothy, nobody knows. It is a nice thought.

So Thecla went to live in Tryphaena's house. And something lovely happened there.

Tryphaena had expected, I suppose, to look after a prisoner. Instead she found a daughter. Thecla was gentle with her, and prayed with her, and talked to her about God. And the grief in that house began to lift. And by the end of the story, Tryphaena is calling Thecla her second child.

"You are my child now," she says to her. "You are my second daughter. Pray for me."

And when the day of the games came, and the soldiers came to Tryphaena's door to take Thecla to the arena, Tryphaena went with her, holding her hand, all the way.

Now I am going to tell you about the arena, and I am going to tell it gently, the way the story deserves.

I want to stop and say something careful here, because this is the most famous and the most argued-about moment in the whole story.

The Church does not teach that anyone can baptise themselves. Baptism is given to us. Someone else pours the water, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. So what do we do with this moment? I think we do what the Church has done for a very long time. We do not take it as a lesson in how baptism is done, which it is not. Our main source tonight is the Acts of Paul and Thecla, part of a longer second-century book called the Acts of Paul. The easiest English translations to find are in J. K.

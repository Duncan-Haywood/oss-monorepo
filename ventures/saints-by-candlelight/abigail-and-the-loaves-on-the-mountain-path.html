Abigail and the Loaves on the Mountain Path

A rich sheep farmer insults David and his hungry men at shearing time, and David straps on his sword to do something he will regret for the rest of his life, until a woman on a donkey comes down the mountain path with two hundred loaves and the wisest speech in the Books of Samuel. And then he takes the food from her hand. All of it, the two hundred loaves and the wine and the figs and the raisins. And he says to her, go up to your house in peace. See, I have listened to your voice, and I have granted your request.

And Abigail climbs back on her donkey. And David and his four hundred men, with their swords still on but their oath unmade, turn around and go back down into the wilderness, with the donkeys and the bread.

And I like to think that the young men ate very well that night, out under the stars. And that somewhere among them were the ten who had been sent up to Carmel that morning, sitting by the fire with a cake of figs in each hand, and grinning.

Now. We are not quite finished. Because there is still Nabal.

Abigail rides home, back up the mountain path, in the evening light. And she comes into the farm at Carmel. And Nabal is still at his feast.

The book says that he was holding a feast in his house, like the feast of a king. Which is a little joke, I think. Nabal is not the king. David is going to be the king. But here is Nabal, feasting like one, with no idea at all how close he came to the end.

And his heart was merry within him, the book says, because he was very drunk.

And so Abigail tells him nothing. Not a word, not a little or a lot, until the morning.

That, too, is wisdom. There is no point in telling something important to a man who will not remember it. There is no point starting a conversation that will turn into a shouting match in front of the shearers. She lets the night go by.

And in the morning, when the wine had gone out of Nabal, she sat down with him, and she told him everything.

About the shepherd who came to her. And the loaves, and the donkeys, and the mountain path. And the four hundred men with their swords. And how close it had been. And what he had very nearly brought down on every man and boy in the house.

And the book says that his heart died within him, and he became like a stone.

We do not know exactly what that means. Perhaps it was fear, at last, when it was too late to matter. Perhaps it was an illness of the body, brought on by the shock, or by the feasting. The book does not say, and I will not pretend to know.

What it does say is this. About ten days later, the Lord struck Nabal, and he died.

And that is all. The book does not linger over it, and neither will we. I will only say that the story is careful about one thing. David did not do it. Abigail did not do it. Nobody on that farm lifted a hand. The judgment was left where David himself had once said it belonged, with God.

When David heard that Nabal was dead, he said, blessed be the Lord, who has judged my cause, and has kept his servant back from doing wrong.

And notice, again, what he is grateful for. Not mainly that Nabal is gone. He is grateful that he himself was kept back from doing wrong. That is the thing that stays with him. He knows, now, that he was very nearly a different man.

And then, the book says, David sent to Abigail, to ask her to be his wife.

His servants came up to Carmel, to the farm, and found her, and said, David has sent us to you, to take you to be his wife.

And here is Abigail's answer, the last words she speaks in the whole Bible. She rose, and she bowed down with her face to the ground, and she said, look, your servant is ready to be a slave, to wash the feet of my lord's servants.

That is a very old-fashioned way of saying yes. It is the most humble yes you can imagine. She, who had just stopped an army with a speech, offers to wash the feet of the men who brought the message.

And she hurried. The book uses that word again. She hurried, just as she had hurried with the loaves. She got up, and climbed onto her donkey, I like to think the same sensible donkey, with five of her young women walking behind her. And she followed David's messengers down the mountain path, the same path, into the wilderness. And she became his wife.

I should say one more thing honestly here, because the book itself says it in the next breath. David had more than one wife. The Old Testament tells us plainly about the kings of Israel and their large households, and it does not pretend those households were happy. Some of the saddest stories in the Books of Samuel come out of them. Our Lord, when he was asked about marriage, pointed back past all of that to the beginning, to the one man and the one woman whom God made for each other. So we will not hold up that part of David's life as a pattern. We will just tell you that Abigail went with him, and leave it there.

And now it is time to slow down.

Abigail went with David into the wilderness, and into the hard years that followed. She went with him when he lived among the Philistines, at a town called Ziklag, on the edge of the southern desert. Once, while David and his men were away, raiders came and burned the town and carried off all the women and children, Abigail among them. And David pursued the raiders across the desert, and found them, and brought back every single person who had been taken. Nothing was missing, the book says, small or great. Which is very nearly what David's young men had once said to Nabal about his shepherds.

And then, at last, the long waiting ended. Saul died in battle in the north, on the hills of Gilboa. And there, in Hebron, the men of Judah came and anointed David king.

The boy who had been anointed in secret, with his brothers looking on, was anointed again, in front of everyone.

And in Hebron, Abigail had a son. His name is given as Chileab in one book, and as Daniel in another, and we know nothing else about him at all, except that he was hers and David's.

And after that, the Bible says nothing more about Abigail. We do not know when she died, or where she was buried. She simply goes quiet, the way the hills go quiet in the evening.

But I like to think of her, in Hebron, in the years after. An older woman, by then. And I like to think that every so often, when King David came in from some meeting with his advisors, red in the face, ready to do something hasty about a rude ambassador or an ungrateful town, somebody quietly sent for Abigail.

And I like to think he remembered the mountain path, and the woman kneeling in the road, and her voice saying, this will not be a weight on your heart.

That is my own guess. The book does not say it. But I do not think a man forgets a day like that.

The Jewish people never forgot her either. In the old Jewish tradition written down in the Talmud, the rabbis counted seven women of the Bible as prophetesses. There is Sarah, and Miriam, and Deborah, and Hannah, whom we met on this podcast praying at Shiloh. And there is Huldah, and Esther. And there, among them, is Abigail. Because she saw what God was going to do with David, years before it happened, and said it out loud on a mountain path. That is a tradition, not part of the Bible itself, but it is a lovely one.

And Christians have loved her too, as one of the great peacemakers of the Old Testament. Blessed are the peacemakers, said Our Lord, for they shall be called children of God. And I can think of very few people in the whole of Scripture who made peace more quickly, or with more bread.

Saint Paul once wrote to the Christians in Rome, do not repay anyone evil for evil. Never avenge yourselves, but leave room for the wrath of God. If your enemy is hungry, feed him. Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.

Abigail never read Saint Paul, of course. She lived a thousand years too early. But she knew all of that, somehow, on the morning she loaded up the donkeys.

So let us leave her there, on the path, in the late light.

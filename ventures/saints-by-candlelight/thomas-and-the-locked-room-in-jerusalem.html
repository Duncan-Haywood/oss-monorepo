Thomas and the Locked Room in Jerusalem

On the evening of Easter day the disciples sit behind a bolted door, and Thomas is not there. A week later he is. Told straight from the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, verses nineteen to thirty-one, with the rest of what John tells about Thomas set beside it.

Full script

Picture a room with the door bolted, and eleven people inside who would very much like it to stay that way.

It is the evening of a very long day. Nobody has had a proper meal. A lamp is burning low on a shelf, and someone has put a cloth over the one small window, so that no light leaks out into the street. When a cart rattles past outside, every head turns. When it fades away, every head turns back, and nobody says anything about it.

They are not planning anything. That is the thing to notice. They are not planning, or arguing, or praying out loud. They are just sitting very close together, the way people do when a house is cold and the news is bad, and they have, between them, one single piece of news that nobody knows what to do with.

And one of them is missing.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the room, the lamp, the sounds from the street, and the talk while the disciples wait, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let us start with the man himself, because he has a bad reputation, and he does not deserve it.

His name is Thomas. John tells us that he was also called Didymus, which is the Greek word for twin. So when the old writers say Thomas Didymus, they are really saying Thomas the Twin, which is a bit like saying Tom Twin, and it is a little odd. Nobody knows whose twin he was. The Gospels do not say. There is no brother or sister of his in any of the stories. Some later writers had guesses, and we will leave the guesses where we found them. All we know is that a man was called the Twin, and nobody wrote down why, and I think that is an oversight.

What we do know about him, we know mostly from John. Matthew, Mark and Luke each put his name in the list of the Twelve, and that is all. Just a name in a list, somewhere in the middle, with Philip and Bartholomew. But John gives him a voice. Three times before the evening we are about to tell, John lets him speak. And if you read those three places together, a certain kind of man begins to show.

It is not the kind of man people usually mean when they say Doubting Thomas.

The first place is at a village near Jerusalem, called Bethany.

A man called Lazarus is ill there. He is a friend of Jesus. His sisters, Martha and Mary, send word to Jesus, and Jesus waits two more days before he moves. And then he says to his disciples, let us go back to Judea.

Now the disciples know what that means, and they say so. Teacher, they say, the people there were only just trying to stone you, and you are going back? It is a perfectly reasonable question. If a doctor told you that a certain town had it in for you, you would not choose that town for your holiday.

Jesus says that Lazarus has fallen asleep and he is going to wake him. The disciples take this the simple way. Well, they say, if he is only asleep, he will be fine. And then Jesus tells them plainly that Lazarus has died.

And here is where Thomas speaks. John gives us one line. He says it to his fellow disciples, and it goes, in our own plain words: Let us also go, that we may die with him.

Stop and listen to that for a moment.

There is nothing doubtful in it. It is not a clever line, or a cautious one. It is not the kind of thing a man says if he is trying to protect himself. Thomas thinks that going to Judea is dangerous. He thinks it will probably end badly. And he says, in effect, well then, we will all go, and if it ends badly, it ends badly with him.

You can read the line in more than one way. Some readers hear a man who is gloomy, a man who always expects the worst, and I think there may be something of that in it. But a gloomy man who says, let us go anyway, is not the same as a gloomy man who stays at home. Thomas is the one who sees the danger most clearly, and he is the one who does not turn back. That is a kind of courage. It is, if anything, the more difficult kind, because it is the courage of somebody who is not pretending to himself that everything will be fine.

Hold on to that. We will need it in a little while.

The second place is at a supper, on the night before everything goes wrong.

Jesus has been talking to the disciples for some time, and the mood in the room has become very heavy. He tells them that he is going away, to prepare a place for them, and that they know the way to where he is going.

And Thomas says, in our own words: Lord, we do not know where you are going. So how can we know the way?

Now think about who else in that room was probably thinking the same thing, and thinking it quietly, and not saying it. I would put money on it, if I were a person who put money on things. I would say that at least half the table had exactly the same question. Where is he going? What does the way mean? How do we get there? And only one of them was willing to look foolish for the sake of finding out.

That is Thomas. He is the one who asks the plain question out loud.

And look at what the question brings. Jesus answers him with one of the best-known sentences in the whole Gospel. I am the way, and the truth, and the life. Nobody comes to the Father except through me. The church has been reading that sentence for two thousand years, in every language there is, and we have it because a man in a crowded room did not nod politely and pretend he understood.

I think that is worth a small cheer, quietly, so as not to wake anyone.

The third place comes later, and it is in Galilee, but we will get to it after the evening we are here for. Hold on, we will come back to it.

First, the evening itself.

It is Easter day, though nobody in the room would use that name yet. It is the first day of the week. Early that morning, Mary of Magdala has come running with her astonishing news. Peter and the other disciple have run to the tomb and seen the linen cloths lying there, and the tomb empty. Mary has said, I have seen the Lord. And the disciples, as far as John tells it, have not known what to make of that.

They are afraid. John says so in a single plain line. It is evening, and the doors of the place where the disciples are have been shut, for fear of the authorities. John's own words there are, for fear of the Jews, and we should say a word about that, because it can be heard wrongly. Jesus was a Jew, and so were Mary and Peter and Thomas and every person in that room. John is using a kind of shorthand for the leaders in Jerusalem who had just seen Jesus put to death, and who might, the disciples feared, come next for his followers. It is the fear of a small group of friends who have just watched something terrible happen to the man they followed, and who think they may be next. That is all. We say it kindly, and we move on.

So the door is shut. Perhaps it is bolted. John says only that it is shut, and later that it is locked, and we will take him at his word. The lamp is low. The bread, if there is any, has not been touched.

And inside the room, there are ten of them. Not eleven, as I said a moment ago. Let me count again. Judas is gone, and that leaves eleven of the Twelve, and one of the eleven is Thomas, and Thomas, for reasons John does not tell us, is not there. So there are ten in the room. Ten, plus whoever else was with them. Luke tells us there were others with them too. We will keep it simple and say ten, and the others.

Why is Thomas away?

The honest answer is that nobody knows. John does not say. Perhaps he had gone to find bread. Perhaps he was walking the streets because he could not sit still. Perhaps he could not bear to be in a room where everyone was whispering. Perhaps he went to look at the tomb himself. Every one of those is a guess, and I mean to leave them as guesses. All we have is the plain fact: he was not there.

I will tell you what I think, though, and I will say plainly that it is only what I think. I think that a man who said, let us go and die with him, is a man who was hit very hard by what had just happened. He had been ready to die beside his friend, and he had not been given the chance. His friend had gone alone. And it may be that he could not stand to sit still with it.

But that is only me. Back to John.

So the ten are in the room, and the lamp is low, and the cart goes by outside.

I would like to tell you what they say to each other. John does not give it. Nothing in the Gospel says they spoke at all. But rooms like that are not silent, and I think we can allow ourselves a little of what it might have sounded like, as long as we say plainly that it is invented.

Someone says that the bread is stale. Someone else says it is the same bread they had yesterday, so of course it is stale. Someone laughs, and then stops laughing, because it does not feel right to laugh. Someone says, did you hear that, and everyone listens, and it is only a dog. Someone says that they ought to put out the lamp, and someone else says that if they put out the lamp, they will have to sit in the dark, and nobody wants that.

Peter is sitting by the wall. He has not said much all day. There is a great weight on him, because of the courtyard, and the fire, and the three times, and I mean to leave that story alone, because it is not tonight's. He is simply there, with his hands between his knees.

Mary has told them what she saw. They have heard it, and they do not know where to put it. It is too large. It is like being handed a ship and being asked to put it in a cupboard.

And then, with no sound at all, and no knock, and no creak from the bolt, there is somebody standing in the middle of the room.

John says it plainly: Jesus came and stood among them.

Notice that John does not describe how he got in. The door is shut, and the bolt is still on, and then Jesus is there. John does not explain it. He does not tell us what the room looked like in that moment, or how the lamp flickered, or what the disciples did with their hands. He says, Jesus came and stood among them, and then he gives us the words.

He said, Peace be with you.

That is the first thing. Not a rebuke. Not, where were you on Friday. Not a speech. Peace be with you.

And then, John says, he showed them his hands and his side.

I want to stay on this for a moment, because it is going to matter in a minute. The first thing the risen Lord does, when he comes into a room full of frightened friends, is show them the marks. He does not hide them. He does not say, we need not mention that. He shows them, so that they can see that it is really him, and that it is really the same body that was nailed there. John says: then the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord.

Glad. That is the word. Not frightened, now. Glad.

And he says it again. Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you.

And then something John tells in one short sentence. He breathed on them, and said, Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them. If you retain the sins of any, they are retained.

Both our traditions have a great deal to say about those words, and they are not tonight's business. We will only say that they are in the Gospel, that they are among the most serious sentences in it, and that he gave them in a room where a few hours earlier the people he was speaking to had been too afraid to unbolt a door.

And then, as quietly as he came, he was gone.

Now we come to Thomas.

Night has come. Somebody has been sent to bring the door open, or the door has been opened by a knock on the right signal, we do not know, but at some point Thomas comes in. We can imagine the street still in his hair, and a cold air coming in behind him. They are all talking at once.

We have seen the Lord, they say.

Now, I want you to hear how they say it. Not one of them says, it was a ghost. Not one says, we think we saw something. They say it the way you would say it if you had been given the best news in the world and could not stop yourself. We have seen the Lord. They say it again, perhaps. John says, so the other disciples told him, we have seen the Lord. Told him. Not argued. Told.

And Thomas looks at them.

What does he do? What would you do?

Think of the position he is in. Ten friends, people he knows and trusts, are all telling him the same impossible thing. And he is the only one who was not there. Every one of them has something he does not have, and that is the one thing, the sight of the Lord, that he would give the whole world to have.

Now there are two ways to hear what he says next. One way is to say, he is a hard-hearted man who refuses to believe his friends. The other way is to say, he is a man who loves Jesus so much that he is afraid to believe it, in case it turns out to be false and he has to lose him a second time.

I think the second is nearer the truth. I think that a man who had said, let us go and die with him, was a man who had given everything to this, and had had it taken from him on a Friday afternoon. And now ten people are telling him it is undone, and he cannot bear to hope.

So he says what he says. John gives it in full. Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger in the mark of the nails, and place my hand in his side, I will not believe.

It is a hard sentence. It is fair to say it is a hard sentence. And I do not think we should pretend it is not. But look at what Thomas is asking for. He does not ask for something new. He does not ask for a wonder greater than the ones the others have had. He asks for exactly what they were given. Remember that, the first thing Jesus did, in the locked room, was to show them his hands and his side. Thomas is asking for no more than that. He wants to be put on the same footing as his friends.

And something else. The marks are the one thing Thomas names. He does not say, unless I see him walking. He does not say, unless I see him in glory. He says, unless I see the marks of the nails. In other words, the thing he needs to know is that it is the same one. The one who was crucified. Not a kinder, easier, cleaner version. The same. And that is, if you think about it, a rather faithful thing to ask.

Now the week goes by.

John gives it in one short line. Eight days later. That is all. Eight days, with no word of what happened in them.

I find that very moving, in a quiet way. Eight days. And the ten who had seen him did not throw Thomas out. Thomas did not leave. That is the thing to notice about those eight days. The man who said, I will not believe, is still in the room. They are still together. They still eat the same bread. There is nobody saying, well, if you do not believe, you can go. And Thomas is not saying, well, if you are all so sure, I shall find somebody more sensible to spend my time with.

For a week, a group of people who disagree about the most important question in the world sit down to supper together. I do not think it is too much to say that this is the first thing the church ever did.

And I will tell you what I think they talked about. I think they talked about everything else. The weather. Whose turn it was to go for water. Whether the roof was going to hold. What they were going to do about money. Because when the large thing is too large to speak of, people speak of small things, and it is a kind of kindness.

Eight days later, then. Again it is the first day of the week. The disciples are inside, John says, and Thomas is with them.

And the door is shut. Again.

John says it twice, and he means us to notice. They are still afraid. A week after the best news of their lives, they are still behind a closed door. That does not seem to me a failure. It seems very human. It takes more than eight days to get over being afraid. It took them, as the Acts of the Apostles tells it, a good deal longer than that, and a good deal more than that.

And again, with no knock and no sound, Jesus came and stood among them, and said, Peace be with you.

The very same words.

And then he turns, and John gives us one of the most tender moments in the whole Gospel. The Lord turns to Thomas, and he says: Put your finger here, and see my hands. And put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not be unbelieving, but believing.

Look at what that is. The Lord was not in the room eight days ago. He was not standing beside Thomas when Thomas said those words, in whatever corner of the city he said them. And yet he gives them back to him, one by one. The finger. The hands. The side. He knows what Thomas asked for, nearly word for word.

And he does not say, how dare you. He does not say, shame on you. He does not even say, I told you so. He says, here. Here it is. Come and see.

It is the same gentleness that the ten had met in the first room. Peace be with you. Here are my hands.

Now I want to be careful, because here is a place where a great many pictures, and a great many sermons, have gone slightly past what John wrote.

Does Thomas touch him?

Go back and read it. John does not say that he does. He tells us that Jesus offered. He tells us what Thomas said. But he does not write, and Thomas put his finger in the marks. A great many painters have shown it. Some of the most famous pictures in the world show Thomas with his finger in the wound. And it may be that he did. It may be that he did not. John leaves it open. Some of the old readers took it one way, and some took it the other, and both of our traditions have both sorts of readers.

What John does tell us is what Thomas says. And he says it at once. There is no pause that John records, and no argument. Thomas answers him.

And what Thomas says is this. My Lord and my God.

Five words, in English. In the Greek, a little fewer. And it is, I think, the highest thing anybody says to Jesus in the whole of the Gospel of John. It is at the end of the book, nearly, and the book has been building toward it from the first page, where it says that the Word was with God and the Word was God. And now, at last, a human being in a room with a door shut, says it face to face.

And who says it? Not the one who was always sure. Not the one who found the empty tomb first. It is the man who said, I will not believe. The one who asked for the plain evidence, and was given it, and who, in that very moment, went further than anyone had gone. He does not say, my teacher. He does not say, my friend, though he is both. He says, my Lord and my God.

If you ever need a reason to think that honest doubt is not the enemy of faith, you could do worse than that sentence. Thomas asked his question out loud, and he was answered, and the answer brought him to the greatest confession in the book.

Then Jesus says one more thing, and it is for us.

Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed.

Now, I would like to say something about how this sentence is sometimes used, because it can be heard in a sour way. Some people hear it as a little scolding. They hear, you were wrong to ask, Thomas, and the better people are the ones who never ask. But that is not what it says, and it is not what the Lord does. He has just spent a week, or at least the end of a week, answering the very question Thomas asked, in the very words Thomas used. If it were a scolding, it would be a strange one, delivered by showing him his hands.

I think we should hear it the other way round. It is a blessing, said over everyone who comes after. Over the people who were not in the room. Over you, and over me, and over everyone who has ever heard this story, in a language that did not exist then, from somebody who had it from somebody who had it from somebody who was there. We were not in the room. We were not given the hands and the side. And the Lord, standing there, looking past Thomas and out through the closed door and down all the years, says that we are blessed.

I find that rather lovely. He was thinking of us, in that room, with the lamp low.

Now John does something I always like. He stops the story and speaks to the reader directly.

Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, he says, which are not written in this book. But these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name.

That is the end of the chapter. In fact, most readers think it was the original end of the whole book, before the last chapter was added. And I like how plain it is. John admits that he left things out. There was a great deal more. He picked what he picked, and he picked it for a reason, and the reason is you. You, in whatever room you are in. He wrote it down so that you could hear it.

Which is why we are telling it tonight.

Now, we said we would come back to the third place, and here it is.

It is in the last chapter of John, in Galilee, and it is a small thing. Seven of the disciples are together by the Sea of Galilee. Peter says, I am going fishing, and the others say, we will come with you. John lists who is there, and the list goes: Simon Peter, Thomas called the Twin, Nathanael from Cana, the sons of Zebedee, and two others.

Look at that. Thomas is right there, in the second place in the list. The man who wouldn't believe, a few weeks later, is out in a boat with the rest of them. Nobody has thrown him out. He has not run away. He is simply one of the group, hauling nets, as if there had never been a problem at all. And I think that is the best ending there could be for the story of a man who asked a hard question. He asked it, he was answered, and then he went fishing with his friends.

And the Acts of the Apostles, after the Ascension, lists the Eleven who went up to the upper room in Jerusalem and stayed together in prayer. And Thomas is named there, too. He is in the room. Not behind a locked door, this time, but in prayer, and waiting.

Now let us slow down. It is late, and the lamp is nearly out.

What became of Thomas, afterwards? The honest answer is that the Gospels do not tell us. The Acts of the Apostles does not tell us either. After the list in the upper room, the New Testament does not name him again.

What we have after that is tradition, and we should say plainly that it is tradition. The oldest and the most widely held of the stories says that Thomas travelled far to the east, further than any of the others, and that he brought the Gospel to the south of India, where a community of Christians has lived ever since. They are called the Saint Thomas Christians, and they are still there, in Kerala, and they honour him as the one who first told them. Their churches are among the most ancient in the world, and they trace themselves back to him. How much of the early story can be proved by documents is something that scholars still discuss, and we will not take a side. We only say that there is a very old and very loved tradition, that a living people hold it, and that it is a lovely thing to be remembered by.

The same tradition says that he died there, and that he is buried far from the room where he saw the hands and the side. We will leave that as the old story tells it, and we will not add any more.

The Catholic Church keeps his feast on the third of July. The Orthodox Church keeps the Sunday after Easter as Thomas Sunday, when this very Gospel is read, and the whole church hears again about the locked door, and the hands, and the five words. And the Orthodox keep another day for him in October. So he is remembered in both our traditions, in more than one place in the year, as the apostle who said what he honestly needed to say, and was given what he honestly needed, and then said the greatest thing of all.

And there is a small thing I should like to say before we go.

Most people, when they hear the name Thomas, think of the doubt. But the Gospel remembers three things about him. It remembers that he said, let us go and die with him. It remembers that he asked the plain question that gave us, I am the way, the truth and the life. And it remembers that he said, my Lord and my God. And I think that is the real Thomas. A man brave enough to follow into danger, honest enough to say when he did not understand, and honest enough, in the end, to say the whole truth out loud when he saw it.

If we are lucky, there is a little of him in all of us.

The wind is dropping outside. The streets of the old city have gone quiet. In the room, the lamp is almost out, and the eleven are asleep, with their backs to the wall. The door is shut. The bolt is on. And on the shelf, where somebody left it, is the last piece of bread.

Nobody is afraid now. Or if they are, it is the small, slow fear of people who know the morning is coming.

Peace be with you. That is what he said, twice, and it is still what he says.

Rest now.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is told from the Gospel of John, chapter twenty, verses nineteen to thirty-one, with the other places where John shows Thomas, in chapters eleven, fourteen and twenty-one. It is Scripture for both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches, and we told it straight.

What is Scripture: the shut doors, the appearance of Jesus, the words Peace be with you, the showing of the hands and the side, the sending of the disciples and the breathing of the Spirit, Thomas's absence and his words, the eight days, the second appearance, the offer of the finger and the hand, Thomas's answer, the blessing on those who have not seen, and John's own closing lines.

What we flagged as not in the text: John does not say that Thomas touched the wounds, only that Jesus offered, and we left it open as John does. John does not say why Thomas was absent. The phrase "for fear of the Jews" is John's own, and we explained it as the fear of the authorities in Jerusalem, since every person in the room was a Jew. The nature of the forgiving and retaining of sins is a matter of much careful teaching in both traditions, and we did not try to explain it.

What we invented: the room, the lamp, the cloth over the window, the bread, the carts in the street, the talk among the disciples, Peter by the wall, the guesses about where Thomas went, and what the group talked about during the eight days. None of it changes what happens or what it means.

What is tradition: Thomas carrying the Gospel to India, and the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala, are a very old and honoured tradition, and we named it as such and left it there. The reason he was called the Twin is not given in the Gospels.

For further reading, the Gospel of John itself is the best place, and Luke chapter twenty-four, verses thirty-six to forty-three, tells a nearby evening in its own way.

That's the story of Thomas for tonight.

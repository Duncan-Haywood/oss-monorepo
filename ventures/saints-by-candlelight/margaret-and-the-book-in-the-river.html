Margaret and the Book in the River

A princess who grew up in Hungary, fled England after ten sixty-six and was blown into the Firth of Forth marries a rough Scottish king who cannot read, feeds nine orphans every morning with her own spoon, builds a free ferry for pilgrims, and loses her favourite book in a river. Saint Margaret of Scotland, from the Life written by her own priest.

What's true in this story

Who
Saint Margaret of Scotland, King Malcolm the Third of Scotland, her brother Edgar the Atheling, her priest and biographer Turgot, her children, an invented orphan girl called Bethoc, an invented ferryman called Aed, an invented servant called Wulfric who drops the book
Tradition
Catholic; Margaret lived after the parting of East and West in ten fifty-four and was canonised by Pope Innocent the Fourth in twelve fifty, so her feast is kept in the Catholic calendar; Scots of many churches have long honoured her
Scripture and history
her birth in exile in Hungary, her family's return to England in ten fifty-seven, the flight to Scotland after the Norman conquest, her marriage to Malcolm about ten seventy, the founding of the church at Dunfermline with monks sent by Archbishop Lanfranc, the nine orphans, the poor fed in the royal hall, the ransoming of captives, the houses and free ships for pilgrims crossing the Forth, the king who could not read kissing her books, the deaths of Malcolm and their son Edward near Alnwick and her own death at Edinburgh three days later in November ten ninety-three, all from Turgot's Life written for her daughter, with the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Lanfranc's letter
Legend
the book found unharmed in the river is Turgot's report and is told as his report, though the book itself survives in the Bodleian Library at Oxford with an old poem about its rescue; her landing place at Saint Margaret's Hope, the name Queensferry, the cave at Dunfermline and the chapel in Edinburgh Castle as her own chapel are local or later tradition and labelled so; the mist that hid her body as it was carried from the castle is a later chronicler's story and is told as one
What we invented
Bethoc the orphan, Aed the ferryman and Wulfric the servant, everything they say, the conversations of the king and queen, the weather, the walk to the ford and Bethoc's later life are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original
Feast day
the sixteenth of November in the Roman calendar, the day of her death; for centuries before nineteen sixty-nine she was kept on the tenth of June

Imagine a river in Scotland, a long time ago, on a grey and windy morning.

It is not a deep river, but it is quick and brown and cold, and it chatters over stones. There is a ford, a shallow place where people cross, and a little group of travellers is splashing across it. Horses. Packs. A priest holding his robe up out of the water with a face of great suffering.

And at the back, a young servant, wet to the knees, with a bundle held tight against his chest. Inside the bundle, wrapped in cloth, is the most precious thing he has ever been trusted to carry. A book. A small book of the Gospels, with gold letters inside it, and a cover set with jewels, and it belongs to the queen.

Halfway across, his foot slips on a stone. He staggers. He rights himself. He laughs at himself, a little shakily, and goes on.

He does not notice, not for a long time, that the bundle in his arms is now a good deal lighter than it was.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the life of Saint Margaret, queen of Scotland, told mostly from the book her own priest wrote about her a few years after she died, and the story of the book in the river is his report, and a little orphan girl, a ferryman and the servant who dropped the book, with everything they say, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Margaret is one of those saints whose life starts somewhere you would not expect, and ends somewhere else entirely.

She was an English princess. But she was not born in England. She was born in Hungary, about the year ten forty-five, a very long way from any English field.

Here is why. Her grandfather was an English king, called Edmund Ironside, which is a fine name for a king and was, I think, fairly earned. He died young, in the year ten sixteen, and a Danish king called Cnut took the throne of England. Edmund's little sons were babies, and babies with a claim to a throne are, I am sorry to say, not safe in the house of the man who has taken it. So the babies were sent away. Far away. And after a long journey, one of them, Edward, grew up at the court of the king of Hungary.

There he married a lady called Agatha. Historians still argue about exactly who her family were, and I am not going to settle it for them tonight. And they had three children: a girl called Margaret, another girl called Cristina, and a boy called Edgar.

So Margaret's first language may have been something else, and her first winters were Hungarian winters, and her first churches were Hungarian churches. If you had told her as a little girl that she would one day be buried in Scotland, she would probably have asked you where that was.

Then, when she was about twelve, a message came. The old king of England, Edward the Confessor, who had no children, wanted his long-lost nephew to come home. So in ten fifty-seven the whole family made the long journey back across Europe to England.

And then, almost as soon as they arrived, before he had even met the king, Margaret's father died.

It is a sad beginning. The narrator does not want to hurry past it. A girl of twelve in a new country, with a new language, in a cold palace, and her father gone. The old sources do not tell us how she felt, but I think we can guess, and I think it is worth remembering, because Margaret grew up to be a woman who always noticed the children who had lost somebody.

She was brought up at the English court. She learned to read, which was not as common as you might think, even for princesses. She read the psalms and the Gospels. She was serious and devout and, people said, very quick.

And then came ten sixty-six.

You may know that year. Most people in England know it, the way people know their own birthday. In ten sixty-six the old king died, and a nobleman called Harold took the crown, and then Duke William of Normandy crossed the sea with an army, and at the battle of Hastings Harold was killed. For a few weeks some of the English lords said that young Edgar, Margaret's brother, should be king. But there was not much a teenage boy could do against William the Conqueror, and Edgar bent the knee.

It did not feel safe to stay. And so, a year or two later, Edgar and his mother and his sisters slipped away and went north, to the kingdom of Scotland.

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, which was a sort of running diary kept by English monks, year by year, tells this part of the story. It says that the family came to Scotland, and that the king of Scotland, Malcolm, received them kindly.

There is an old tradition about how they arrived. It says their ship was blown by storms up into the wide sea-inlet called the Firth of Forth, and that they came ashore at a sheltered little bay on the north side, which ever since has been called Saint Margaret's Hope. That is local tradition, and a lovely one, and the bay is still there, though I am afraid it now has a large dockyard next to it, which rather spoils the romance.

Now let us meet the king.

Malcolm, King of Scots, was a big, rough, practical man in his late thirties. He had had an adventurous life. When he was a boy his father, King Duncan, had been killed by a rival called Macbeth. Yes, that Macbeth. The real one was a good deal less theatrical than the one in the play, but that is a story for another night. And so Malcolm had grown up in exile in England, and much later had come back north with an army and won his throne.

Because of those years in England, he spoke English as well as his own Gaelic. Remember that, because it will matter.

He was a soldier. He was good at fighting, and at riding, and at shouting. He could not read. Not a word. Kings often could not, then, and nobody thought much worse of him for it. He had people for that.

And he looked at the serious young English princess who had come ashore in his kingdom, and he decided that he would like to marry her.

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says something rather surprising here. It says that Margaret did not want to. Her brother and his men were against it at first, and Margaret herself refused. She said that she would rather have no husband at all, if God would allow it, and give her whole life to Him, perhaps in a convent.

I like that the Chronicle bothers to tell us this. It means that she had a will of her own, and a plan of her own, and that the plan was God.

But Malcolm was persistent, and the family was a family of exiles with nowhere much else to go, and in the end, about the year ten seventy, Margaret married him, at a place called Dunfermline, in Fife, where the king had a tower.

And here is the surprising thing. It turned out to be a very good marriage.

We know Margaret so well because of one man. His name was Turgot. He was a monk, and later the prior of the great monastery at Durham, and later still a bishop in Scotland. For years he was Margaret's priest and confessor, which means that he heard her confessions and gave her advice, and knew her better, perhaps, than anybody except her husband.

After she died, her daughter asked him to write down everything he remembered about her mother. And so he did. He wrote it as a letter to the daughter, and it is warm and admiring and sometimes surprisingly funny, and almost everything in tonight's story comes from it.

Now let me introduce somebody who is not in Turgot's book, because I made her up.

Her name is Bethoc. In our story she is about four years old, with hair that sticks out in every direction like a startled hedgehog. Her mother and father died in a hard winter, of a fever, and she has been living with an old woman in the village under the king's tower at Dunfermline, who is kind but very poor and very tired.

And one morning, the old woman takes Bethoc by the hand, and brings her up the hill, and in through a gate, past a guard with a spear who looks enormous to Bethoc, and into a warm room with a fire in it.

There are other children there. Eight of them, all small, all thin, all looking at the fire as if they have never seen one so big. And then a door opens, and a lady comes in.

She is not dressed the way Bethoc expects a queen to be dressed. She is dressed well, but simply, and she has a soft voice, and she sits down on a stool by the fire, quite low, and looks at the children one by one as if she is learning them.

"This is Bethoc," says the old woman, and gives Bethoc a little push.

"Good morning, Bethoc," says the queen. Her Gaelic is careful and not very good, and she says Bethoc's name slightly wrong, the way a foreigner does. "Are you hungry?"

Bethoc nods. She is always hungry.

And this next part is true. It is in Turgot's book, and it is one of the loveliest things in it.

Every single morning, Turgot says, nine little orphans were brought to the queen. And she had soft food made for them, the kind of food that small children like, a sort of warm porridge or pap. And she did not think it beneath her, he says, to take each child up on her knee, and to feed them herself, putting the food into their mouths with her very own spoon. The same spoon she ate with.

So Bethoc, who in our story has had a very hard winter, finds herself sitting on the knee of the queen of Scotland, being fed warm porridge off a silver spoon, while the queen says, in her careful, slightly wrong Gaelic, "Another? Yes? Good girl. Slowly. There is plenty."

Bethoc does not know that this is unusual. She is four. She thinks perhaps this is what queens are for.

And the narrator, who is a good deal older than four, thinks she may be right.

It was not only the nine orphans.

Turgot tells us that Margaret's whole day was arranged around prayer and around the poor. She prayed for long hours, and she fasted so much that it damaged her health, which Turgot, who was fond of her, mentions with some worry. And whenever she went out, the poor and the widows and the orphans came crowding round her, because they knew that nobody went away from the queen empty-handed.

When she had given away everything she had with her, she would borrow from the people around her. The noblemen and ladies of her court learned to carry a little extra, because the queen might turn round at any moment and say, "Have you anything? This woman needs it more than you do."

And when that ran out, she went to the king's treasury.

This is one of my favourite things in Turgot's book. He says that Margaret would sometimes take money from the king's own treasure to give to the poor. And the king knew perfectly well that she did it. He would pretend not to notice. And sometimes he would catch her, with the coins in her hand, and he would laugh, and pretend to be very stern, and threaten to have her arrested and tried for theft.

Let us imagine one of those evenings.

The king comes into the room where the money chest is kept, stamping the snow off his boots, and finds his wife standing by the open chest with her hands behind her back.

"Good evening," says the king.

"Good evening," says the queen.

"What have you got behind your back?"

"Nothing of any importance."

"It jingles."

"It is a very small amount of nothing."

The king folds his great arms. "Do you know what the penalty is, in my kingdom, for robbing the king?"

"I imagine it is severe."

"Very severe." He holds out his hand. She puts her hand in his, with the coins still in it. He looks at them. He closes her fingers back over them. "Who is it for?"

"There is a man at the gate whose cow has died. He has five children."

"Then you had better take him a little more than that," says the king, and goes off to find his supper.

That conversation is ours, of course. But the laughing and the pretend threats are Turgot's, and I think the old soldier must have been rather proud of his wife, to make jokes about it.

There were other changes too.

Margaret had grown up in the courts of Hungary and England, and the court of Scotland was, let us say, a little rough around the edges. Turgot tells us that she brought in merchants with fine cloth, and that the king's household began to dress and behave more grandly, and that the king's dinners became more orderly. The king's table, he says, began to be served on gold and silver dishes, which I imagine took some of the old warriors a while to get used to.

She also founded a proper church at Dunfermline, where she had been married, and dedicated it to the Holy Trinity. And she wrote to the Archbishop of Canterbury, a learned old Italian called Lanfranc, asking him for monks to come and serve there. We still have his letter back to her. It is very courteous, and a little flowery, and he says he is sending three monks, and that she must treat them well and use them to do good work. One of them was called Goldwine. I find it oddly touching that we know the name of a monk who walked from Canterbury to Fife nine hundred and fifty years ago.

And she did one more thing that the Scots remembered.

Remember that the king could speak both English and Gaelic. Turgot tells us that Margaret held long meetings with the Scottish clergy about the way some things were done in the Church in Scotland, for example the day on which Lent should begin, and keeping Sunday as a day of rest from work. Margaret spoke English. Most of the Scottish clergy spoke Gaelic. And so the king, the rough soldier who could not read, sat in the middle and translated for them both.

I like to picture it. A long table. A row of bearded Gaelic priests along one side, a slim English queen at the end, arguing politely about the calendar, and in between them, a large king in a fur cloak, saying, "She says... no, wait, let me get this right... she says..." And probably, now and again, adding a few words of his own.

And now, the books.

Turgot says that the king, who could not read, loved to see his wife reading. He saw how she loved her books, how she held them and turned the pages and prayed from them. And so, though he could not read a word in them, he would pick up her books and turn them over in his big hands, and look at them, and kiss them.

And sometimes, when he knew there was one she especially loved, he would quietly take it away and send for a goldsmith, and have its cover decorated with gold and jewels, and then give it back to her as a present.

I think that might be one of the most romantic things I have ever heard about a medieval king. He could not share the words with her. So he made the books beautiful.

One of those books was a small book of the Gospels. Not the whole Gospels, but the readings from them that were used at Mass, along with pictures of the four Gospel writers, painted in bright colours. It had gold capital letters inside. It had a jewelled cover. It was, Turgot tells us, the book she loved best of all.

And this is where our servant comes in.

Our servant is called Wulfric. He is invented. He is about sixteen, an English boy who came north with the queen's household, and he is so proud to have been trusted with the queen's little Gospel book on a journey that he has hardly let go of it for three days.

The journey is going well. There is a small party: a priest, a couple of men on horseback, Wulfric on foot with the bundle, and they are crossing the countryside on the queen's business. And then they come to a river.

It is a quick river, brown with rain from the hills. There is a ford, a shallow place with stones under the water. The horsemen go across without trouble. The priest gathers up his robe, says a short prayer which may not have been entirely about the river, and splashes across.

Wulfric follows. The water comes up past his knees and it is so cold that it takes his breath away. Halfway across, his foot turns on a slippery stone, and he lurches, and grabs at a rock, and rights himself, and laughs at himself, and goes on.

He reaches the other side. He shakes the water off his legs. They walk on for a mile. Perhaps two.

And then the priest says, "Wulfric. Where is the book?"

Wulfric looks down at his arms. There is the cloth. There is the bundle. He unwraps it.

There is no book in it.

I will spare you most of the next few minutes. There was some shouting. There was some praying. Wulfric went white and then red and then white again. And they all turned round and went back to the river at a run, Wulfric well in front.

Here, at last, is Turgot's own story. He says that the person carrying the book was crossing a ford, and that the book slipped out of its wrappings and fell into the middle of the stream, and that the bearer did not notice and went on his way. And later, when he wanted to take the book out, he found it gone. He searched for it for a long time, and could not find it.

And then, at last, it was found. It was lying open at the bottom of the river. All the time it had been there, Turgot says, the rushing water had been tossing its leaves back and forth, and the little coverings of silk which protected the gold letters had been torn away by the current.

Now you know, and I know, what happens to a book with painted pages if you leave it at the bottom of a river. Ink runs. Paint washes away. Pages swell up and stick together, and the whole thing turns into a sort of soggy brick.

But when they lifted this book out of the water, Turgot says, it was unharmed. The pages were white and the letters were whole, as if they had never been in the water at all. Only, he says, on the very last leaves, there was a tiny mark of damp that you could hardly see.

The book was brought back to the queen, and Turgot, who tells this story as a sign of God's love for her, says that she thanked God for it with even greater love than before.

Let us imagine Wulfric bringing it back.

He comes into the queen's room, still damp at the edges, holding the book out in front of him with both hands as if it might explode, and goes down on one knee.

"My lady," he says. "I have to tell you something."

And he tells her everything. The ford, the stone, the missing book, the running back, the long search in the cold water. And at the end, he holds it out.

Margaret takes it. She opens it. She turns the pages, slowly. The gold letters glint in the firelight. The painted Gospel writers look calmly out of their pages, as if they have done nothing more alarming that week than sit for their portraits.

She is quiet for a long time.

"Were you afraid?" she says at last.

"Yes, my lady."

"Of me?"

Wulfric thinks about it honestly. "Of the king, mostly, my lady."

And the queen laughs, which is not a thing Wulfric expected. "Then I shall tell the king that it was found," she says, "and not that it was lost. Go and put on some dry clothes. And Wulfric. Thank you for going back."

That conversation, again, is our invention. But I wanted you to know that Turgot's Margaret was not the kind of person who would have been angrier about a book than glad about a boy.

Now, I promised you honesty about what is history and what is legend. Turgot tells the story of the book as a wonder, and he believed it, and he was writing only a few years later, about a woman he knew well. He also tells it with care, as a thing that happened, not a thing he has decorated. So we tell it as his report.

But here is the extraordinary part.

The book still exists.

In the nineteenth century, a small, battered, old Gospel book turned up at a sale in London and was bought by the Bodleian Library, the great library at Oxford. At the front of it, written in an old hand from very near her own time, is a little Latin poem. And the poem tells how this book was carried by a man who let it fall into a river, unawares, and how it was found lying in the water, and how, when it was drawn out, it was unharmed, except for a little damp, and how it belonged to the holy queen Margaret and to the king.

Somebody read the poem and realised what they were holding. It was her book. The very one.

It is still in Oxford tonight. And the four Gospel writers are still sitting in their painted frames, looking out, nine hundred and fifty years on, perfectly calm.

I have always thought that is one of the nicest things about Margaret's story. You can go and look at the book.

There is one more of Margaret's works I want to tell you about, and for this one we will go down to the sea.

In Margaret's day, one of the great places of pilgrimage in Scotland was Saint Andrews, on the coast of Fife, where people believed some relics of Saint Andrew the Apostle were kept. Pilgrims came from all over, from England and from the south of Scotland. But the south of Scotland and Fife were separated by the Firth of Forth, the wide grey sea-inlet that Margaret's own ship had come into all those years ago. And to cross it, you needed a boat. And boats cost money. And the poorer pilgrims often did not have any.

Turgot tells us what Margaret did. On both shores of the sea that divides Lothian from Scotland, he says, she built houses, so that the pilgrims, and the poor, could shelter and rest after their journey. And she arranged for there to be ships, so that they could be carried across. And the passage was free. Nobody was to be charged a penny. The queen paid for it all.

The two little towns at either end of that crossing are still called South Queensferry and North Queensferry, and people have said for many centuries that the queen in their name is Margaret. Today there are three enormous bridges across the Forth at that very place, one of them a famous red railway bridge like a great iron dragon, and the ferry is long gone. But the name stayed.

Let us meet our ferryman. He is invented, and his name is Aed. He is broad and weathered and has a beard like a gorse bush, and he has been rowing across the Forth since he was a boy.

One windy afternoon a family of pilgrims comes down to the shore. A man, a woman, a grandmother, three children, all footsore and dusty from the road, and when they see the grey choppy water and the big boat, the man begins anxiously counting the few coins in his purse.

"How much to cross?" he says.

Aed shakes his head. "Nothing."

The man frowns. "Nothing at all?"

"Nothing at all. It is the queen's ferry. She pays. Get in. Mind the grandmother. Sit in the middle, little one, and hold on to your mother."

"Why would a queen pay for us?"

Aed takes up his oars and squints at the far shore. "I asked her steward the same thing, the first year. He said the queen said that the road to Saint Andrews belongs to God, and God does not charge a toll." He pulls on the oars, and the boat surges out into the waves. "Then he said she also said not to let anybody fall in. So sit still."

And the children, who have never been in a boat before, sit very still for nearly a minute, and then lean over the side to look at the seals.

The years went by, as years do, even in stories.

Margaret and Malcolm had eight children, six boys and two girls. Turgot tells us that she brought them up very carefully, and that she taught them to love God and to be honest, and to treat the poor well. Three of the boys, Edgar, Alexander and David, each became king of Scotland in turn, and David in particular was remembered as a good and pious king, who founded abbeys all over the country. One of the girls, Edith, married King Henry the First of England, and became a queen herself, and was loved for her kindness to the poor. She was the daughter who asked Turgot to write down her mother's life.

And in our story, little Bethoc grew up.

She did not stay four, of course. She grew tall, and her hedgehog hair calmed down a little, and when she was old enough she went to work in the queen's own rooms. Turgot tells us that Margaret's rooms were like a workshop, where well-born ladies sat and embroidered vestments for the churches, the robes the priests wore at Mass, and altar cloths, in silk and gold thread. Our Bethoc sat among them, and learned to sew, and became very good at it.

And sometimes, in the mornings, when a new little orphan was brought in, frightened and thin, Bethoc would be the one to take their hand and bring them to the fire, and say, in her own good Gaelic, which was a great deal better than the queen's, "Don't worry. She is going to feed you. She has a spoon."

Turgot tells us that in the seasons of Advent and Lent, Margaret's charity became even bigger. Every day in those seasons, three hundred poor people were brought into the royal hall, and seated all round it. And then the doors were shut, so that only the servants and the chaplains were there, and the king and queen came in, and they served the poor themselves. The king served on one side of the hall and the queen on the other, carrying dishes and pouring drinks, like servants at a feast.

She also cared for captives. Malcolm was a warrior king, and in his wars with England his armies took English people captive and carried them north, to work as servants or worse. Margaret, Turgot says, sent people secretly to find out which captives were being treated most harshly, and she paid their ransom and set them free.

Think about that for a moment. She was an English princess married to a Scottish king who raided England. It must have been a very complicated thing to be. She did not solve the complicated thing. She was not able to stop the wars. But she could find out which English prisoners were the most miserable, and buy them back one at a time, and she did.

And now the story slows down, and gets quieter, because we have come to the end of Margaret's life.

In the autumn of ten ninety-three, Margaret was ill. She had been ill for some time, perhaps from all those years of fasting, and she was lying in her room in the castle at Edinburgh, high up on its great rock.

That November, King Malcolm had gone south with an army, into England, as he had done many times before. And near a place called Alnwick, in Northumberland, on the thirteenth of November, he was killed in an ambush, and so was their eldest son, Edward.

Up in the castle, Margaret did not know. But Turgot, who had the story from the priest who stayed with her to the end, says that on that very day she was sad and quiet, and said to the people around her that perhaps some great evil had fallen on the kingdom of Scotland that day.

Three days later, she felt that she was very near the end. She went into her little oratory, her private chapel, and heard Mass, and received Holy Communion. Then she lay down on her bed, and asked for a cross to be brought to her. It was a precious cross that she had brought with her from England, called the Black Rood, which held what people believed was a piece of the Cross of Christ. She held it, and kissed it, and said the psalms quietly.

And then one of her sons came in. Later writers say it was Edgar. He had come straight from the army, and he had terrible news, and he did not know how to say it.

She asked him how his father was, and his brother. He could not bring himself to tell her, and he said they were well.

But she looked at him, and she knew. And she said, I know, my son, I know.

And then, Turgot says, she lifted her hands to heaven, and she thanked God. She said, in words like these: I thank you, almighty God, that you have willed that I should suffer such sorrow at my death, so that, as I hope, it may cleanse me of my sins.

And then she began to pray one of the prayers that the priest says at Mass, just before Communion, a prayer she must have heard every day of her life. Lord Jesus Christ, who by the will of the Father, and the work of the Holy Spirit, gave life to the world by your death, deliver me.

And as she said, deliver me, she went home to God.

It was the sixteenth of November, ten ninety-three. She was about forty-eight years old.

They carried her body down from the castle on the rock to bury her at Dunfermline, in the church of the Holy Trinity that she had founded, where she had been married.

There is a later story, told by a chronicler two or three hundred years afterwards, that the castle was surrounded at that time by enemies, and that a thick sea mist came rolling in over the rock, and hid the little procession, so that it went quietly down and away and nobody saw it. It is a later chronicler's story, not Turgot's, and we cannot know if it is true. But anyone who has stood on Edinburgh's castle rock on a November evening, when the mist comes in off the Forth, will find it very easy to picture.

Her son David, when he was king, rebuilt the church at Dunfermline into a great abbey. In twelve fifty, more than a hundred and fifty years after she died, Pope Innocent the Fourth declared her a saint, and her body was moved to a new shrine in the abbey. The ruins of the shrine are still there, beside the church at Dunfermline, under the open sky.

On the top of the castle rock in Edinburgh there is a tiny stone chapel, the oldest building in the whole city. It is called Saint Margaret's Chapel. The old tradition said it was her own chapel, where she prayed. Most historians now think it was built a few years after her death by her son David, in her memory. Either way, it is a small, plain, quiet room, with thick walls and little windows, and on most days somebody puts fresh flowers on the altar. And in Dunfermline, down under a car park, of all places, there is a little cave by a stream where, the local tradition says, the queen used to go to pray alone.

And then there is the little book, in Oxford, that once spent an afternoon at the bottom of a river.

So, tonight, if you are in Scotland, there are bridges over the Forth with the lights of the trains going across them, where a queen once paid for a boat. There is a small chapel on a rock with flowers on the altar. And somewhere in a library, in the dark, there is a little book of the Gospels lying closed, with gold letters inside it that a river could not wash away.

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source is the Life of Saint Margaret by Turgot, her priest and confessor, later prior of Durham and bishop of Saint Andrews, written a few years after her death as a letter to her daughter, Queen Edith, also called Matilda. Some scholars think the version we have was revised a little later, but it goes back to Turgot. From it come the nine orphans and the spoon, the borrowing and the treasury and the king's pretend threats, the king who could not read kissing her books and having them covered in gold, the meetings with the clergy with the king translating, the three hundred poor in the hall, the ransomed captives, the houses and free ships at the Forth, the embroidery room, the book in the river, and her last days and last prayer. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle tells of the family's flight north, her reluctance to marry and the deaths at Alnwick. Archbishop Lanfranc's letter to her survives.

What is history. Her birth in Hungary, the return of ten fifty-seven, the flight after ten sixty-six, the marriage, the church at Dunfermline, her charity, the deaths of November ten ninety-three, and her canonisation in twelve fifty. The Gospel book is in the Bodleian Library, with the old poem about its rescue.

What is legend or later tradition. The book's escape from the water is Turgot's report, told as his. The landing at Saint Margaret's Hope, the name Queensferry, the cave, the chapel as her own and the mist at her funeral are later or local tradition.

What we invented. Bethoc, Aed and Wulfric, everything they say, the king and queen's conversations, the weather, and Bethoc's life.

That's the story of Saint Margaret of Scotland for tonight. Her feast is on the sixteenth of November, the day she died, and Scots have long honoured her as one of the patrons of their country.

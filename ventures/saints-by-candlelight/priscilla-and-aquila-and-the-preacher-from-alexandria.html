Priscilla and Aquila and the Preacher from Alexandria

Priscilla and Aquila open a new workshop in Ephesus, wave Paul off to Syria, and one Sabbath hear a brilliant young preacher from Alexandria who has almost everything right. Instead of correcting him in public, they take him home. Told straight from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter eighteen, with Paul's own words about the friend who watered what he planted.

Full script

It is a warm evening in a big city by the sea, and three people are sitting round a low table in the back room of a workshop.

The room smells of leather and lamp oil and fish stew. Pieces of hide hang drying from the beams overhead, like washing that has given up hope. On a shelf there is a row of awls and knives, and a big ball of waxed thread.

Two of the people at the table are a married couple in their middle years, with strong, needle-scarred hands. The third is a young man, a guest, with fine clothes, a fine voice, and the slightly dazed look of someone who has been talking brilliantly all afternoon and has only just stopped.

The wife refills his cup. She sets the jug down. And she says, very gently, "Apollos, can we ask you something? About baptism?"

The young man puts down his bread. He looks from one of them to the other.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter eighteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, with a few lines from Saint Paul's letters, and the old widow Doris, the boy Nikon from last time, the supper and a few of the small conversations are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Last time on Saints by Candlelight, we spent a year and a half in Corinth, in a back-street workshop, with Saint Paul and two tentmakers called Aquila and Priscilla. They had been thrown out of Rome by the emperor. Paul had walked in from Athens, tired and frightened and broke. The three of them sewed together all week and argued about the Scriptures all Sabbath, and a church grew up round them, in the houses and the workshops and the house next door to the synagogue.

At the very end of that story, Paul decided it was time to move on. And so did his friends. They went down together to the little port of Cenchreae, on the eastern side of the isthmus, and there someone in the party had his hair cut off, because of a vow. Luke's Greek leaves it slightly unclear whose hair it was, though most people think it was Paul's. And then the three of them got on a ship and sailed east.

Tonight we follow that ship. Not to Syria, where Paul was going, but to the place where it stopped on the way, and where Priscilla and Aquila got off.

That place was Ephesus.

Let me tell you about Ephesus, because if Corinth was a loud, rich, new port, Ephesus was a loud, rich, old one, and much bigger.

It sat on the western coast of what is now Turkey, the region the Romans called Asia. Not the whole continent, as we mean it now. To the Romans, Asia was a province, about the size of a small country, full of old Greek cities, olive groves, vineyards and mountains running down to the sea. And of all those cities, Ephesus was the greatest. The Roman governor lived there. Ships came in from every part of the Mediterranean. Roads went out from it east into the heart of the land. It was one of the biggest cities in the whole Roman world.

It had a long main street, paved with marble, with colonnades on both sides to keep the sun off the shoppers. It had baths, and fountains, and a market square the size of several football pitches. Cut into the side of a hill there was a huge open-air theatre, with seats for something like twenty-four thousand people, which is about the size of a fair-sized modern football stadium. It will come into another story, on another night, when it is full of people shouting.

And just outside the city stood the thing Ephesus was most famous for, the temple of the goddess Artemis.

The ancient world kept a list of seven wonders, the seven most astonishing things that human beings had ever built. The great pyramid was on the list. So was the lighthouse at Alexandria, which we will meet in a moment. And so was the temple of Artemis at Ephesus. It was enormous, much bigger than the Parthenon in Athens, with more than a hundred marble columns, each one about as tall as a five-storey building. Pilgrims came from all over the world to see it. Silversmiths in the city made little models of it to sell to them, which is roughly the ancient equivalent of a snow globe, and they did very well out of it. That, too, is a story for another night.

But there was something else about Ephesus that the people who lived there did not much like to talk about. The city stood near the mouth of a river, and the river brought down mud. Every year, a little more mud. The harbour kept silting up, and the Ephesians kept dredging it out, and the river kept winning. A Greek geographer called Strabo, who wrote at about the time Our Lord was born, tells us that one ruler had tried to fix the problem by narrowing the harbour mouth, and only made it worse. Today, if you visit the ruins of Ephesus, you will find the old harbour is a marsh, and the sea is several miles away, out of sight. The river won in the end. Rivers usually do.

But in our story the harbour is still open, the ships are still coming in, and one of them is bringing two tentmakers with very little luggage, apart from a great deal of tools.

The ship came in on a bright morning, and tied up at the quay among the grain ships and the wine ships, and the gangplank went down, and Priscilla and Aquila stood on the dock with their bundles and looked up at the city.

"Well," said Aquila. "Here we are again."

He had a point. In a few short years these two had lived in Pontus, where Aquila was born, on the south shore of the Black Sea. Then Rome. Then Corinth. And now Ephesus. If you have ever moved house, you will know that it is one of the most tiring things a person can do. These two did it so often that they must have got very good at packing.

Paul was with them, but only for a few days. His ship was going on to Syria, and he meant to be on it when it left. So he did what he always did when he arrived anywhere. As soon as the Sabbath came round, he went to the synagogue.

Ephesus had a large Jewish community, settled there for generations, with its own rights and its own meeting places. And Luke tells us that Paul went in and reasoned with them. Which, knowing Paul, means he talked for a long time, with a great many quotations from the prophets, and a great deal of waving his hands.

And here is a surprise. In city after city, up to now, when Paul talked in the synagogue, there had been trouble before long. Arguments, shouting, people being thrown out. But not in Ephesus, not this time. This time, Luke says, the people listening asked him to stay longer.

And Paul said no.

I think that must have been hard for him. Here, for once, was a door wide open. But he had somewhere he needed to be. So he said goodbye to them, and he made them a promise. I will come back to you, he said, if God wills.

Some of the later copies of Acts add another line here, in which Paul says that he must by all means keep the coming feast in Jerusalem. The oldest copies do not have it, so we will not put it in his mouth. Either way, he had to go.

So, a few days later, Priscilla and Aquila went down to the harbour again, and stood on the quay, and watched Paul's ship pull away, and turn south, and get smaller.

"He'll be back," said Aquila.

"He said if God wills," said Priscilla.

"Then he'll be back," said Aquila. "God usually does, with Paul."

And they turned round, and walked back up into the city, to find somewhere to live.

Luke does not tell us how they found their new workshop, so we will have to imagine it.

I imagine that they spent a few days walking the back streets, the way they had in Corinth, looking for the part of town where the leatherworkers and the weavers and the rope makers were. I imagine that they asked at the synagogue whether anyone knew of a shop to let. And I imagine that someone sent them to a little old widow called Doris.

Doris is our invention, I should say. She is not in Acts. But somebody must have let them a room, and I like to think it was someone like her.

Doris owned a narrow shop on a side street, with a living space behind it and a flat roof above it. Her husband had been a saddler, and had died a few years before, and the shop had stood empty since, with dust on the benches and a family of sparrows in the rafters. She was a small, sharp woman of about seventy, who wore a great many bracelets and had opinions on everything.

"Tentmakers," she said, looking at them. "From where?"

"Corinth," said Aquila.

"And before that, Rome," said Priscilla.

"And before that, Pontus," said Aquila.

Doris narrowed her eyes. "You move about a lot."

"Not always by choice," said Priscilla.

Doris thought about this. Then she looked at their hands, which were covered in the small scars and the hard places that you get from years of pushing a needle through leather. And she seemed to make up her mind.

"The rent is due on the first of the month," she said. "No goats in the shop. And the sparrows stay. They were here before you."

And so, the old Greek way, with a handshake and a long list of conditions about sparrows, Priscilla and Aquila became Ephesians.

They swept the floor. They put up hooks for the hides. They laid out the knives and the awls and the big curved needles, and the ball of waxed thread. They found the shop that sold the best goat-hair cloth, and the tanner who did the best hides, and the baker who did not burn the bread. Within a month, customers were coming in: a sea captain who wanted a new awning, a merchant who needed tents for a caravan going inland, a man with a hole in his sail who wanted it mended by yesterday.

And on the Sabbath, they went to the synagogue, and sat at the back, and listened.

And on the other evenings, people began to come round to the shop after work. A few at first. Then more. A neighbour who had heard them talking. A customer who had asked what they were humming. Someone Paul had spoken to in the synagogue, who wanted to hear more. They would sit on the benches, among the hides, and Priscilla and Aquila would tell them about Jesus of Nazareth, who had died and risen, and about what had happened in Jerusalem at Pentecost, and about all that Paul had taught them during those long days of sewing in Corinth.

They were not preachers, exactly. They were not apostles. They were a married couple with a business to run. But somehow, wherever they went, a church seemed to start meeting in their house. It had happened in Corinth. It was beginning to happen in Ephesus. And it would happen again, years later, in Rome.

Doris, I imagine, noticed the visitors, and listened at the door, and pretended she wasn't. But that is her business, and we will leave her to it.

Now we have to leave Ephesus for a while, and go south, across the sea, to Egypt, to a great city at the mouth of the Nile. Because that is where the young man in our cold open came from.

The city was Alexandria.

Alexandria had been founded by Alexander the Great himself, nearly four hundred years before our story, and named after him, which was the kind of thing Alexander liked to do. He founded quite a few cities called Alexandria. This was the one that lasted.

By the time of our story it was one of the two greatest cities in the world, the other being Rome, and in some ways Alexandria thought it was the greater. It was a city of scholars. It had the Museum, which was not a museum in our sense but something more like a university, where learned men were paid to study and argue and write. It had the great library, famous across the world, which tried to collect a copy of every book ever written. And out in the harbour, on an island, stood the lighthouse, the Pharos, another of the seven wonders, a great stone tower with a fire burning on top of it at night, so that ships could see it far out at sea.

And Alexandria had a very large Jewish community. One of its most famous members, a scholar called Philo, who lived at the same time as our story, tells us that the city was divided into five districts, named after the first five letters of the Greek alphabet, and that two of the five were called the Jewish quarters. That is a lot of people. There may well have been more Jews in Alexandria than in Jerusalem itself.

They spoke Greek, mostly, not Hebrew. And so, some two or three hundred years before our story, the Jews of Alexandria had translated their Scriptures into Greek. There is a lovely old story about how it was done, which says that seventy-two scholars, six from each of the twelve tribes, were brought from Jerusalem, and finished the whole job in seventy-two days. That story is a legend, from a book called the Letter of Aristeas, and the real work almost certainly took much longer and was done by many hands. But the translation was real, and it was very good, and it is still called the Septuagint, from the Latin word for seventy, in honour of the legend. It was the Bible that the first Christians read. When the apostles quote the Old Testament in their letters, they are very often quoting the Septuagint, made in Alexandria.

So, if you were a bright young Jewish man growing up in Alexandria, you grew up with the Scriptures in Greek, and with Greek learning all around you, and with teachers who loved to find deep meanings in every word of Moses and the prophets. Philo himself wrote book after book doing exactly that.

And sometime around the years of our story, a young man called Apollos left Alexandria and came to Ephesus.

Here is how Luke introduces him. I will give it to you in plain words, because every phrase matters.

A Jew named Apollos, Luke says, a native of Alexandria, came to Ephesus. He was an eloquent man, well versed in the Scriptures. He had been instructed in the way of the Lord. And being fervent in spirit, he spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus, though he knew only the baptism of John.

Let us take that slowly.

Eloquent. The Greek word can mean eloquent, or it can mean learned, and with Apollos it probably meant both. He was a fine speaker. The kind of man who could hold a room. The kind of man people came back to hear again.

Well versed in the Scriptures. Literally, mighty in the Scriptures. He knew them, deeply, and could use them. If you have ever met someone who can find the right verse for anything, at once, without looking it up, that was Apollos.

Fervent in spirit. The Greek word here is lovely. It means boiling. Apollos was boiling in spirit. He was on fire. Some readers take it to mean the Holy Spirit, burning in him. Others take it to mean his own spirit, his own enthusiasm. Either way, he was not a dull man.

He taught accurately the things concerning Jesus. So he knew about Jesus. He knew a great deal, and what he said was right.

And yet. He knew only the baptism of John.

You will remember John the Baptist, the prophet in the wilderness, who came before Jesus and baptized people in the Jordan river, as a sign that they were sorry for their sins and wanted to turn back to God. And John told them that someone greater was coming after him, who would baptize them not with water only, but with the Holy Spirit.

Somehow, somewhere, Apollos had learned about John, and about Jesus, and had come to believe. But some part of the story had not reached him. Luke does not tell us which part, exactly. He tells us only that Apollos knew the baptism of John, and not, it seems, the baptism that the apostles had been giving since Pentecost, in the name of Jesus, with the gift of the Holy Spirit.

How could that happen? Nobody knows for sure. John the Baptist had a great many disciples, and some of them travelled far, and kept on teaching what he had taught them. One very old copy of Acts, a manuscript called Codex Bezae, adds a small line here saying that Apollos had been taught in his own country, which would mean that there were believers of some kind in Alexandria already. But that line is not in most of the old copies, and so we cannot lean on it. The honest answer is that we do not know how Apollos came to know what he knew. We only know that he knew a great deal, and was missing something, and did not know he was missing it.

Which, if we are honest, is true of most of us, a good deal of the time.

So Apollos came to Ephesus. And on the Sabbath, he went to the synagogue. And he stood up, and he spoke.

It was, I imagine, a very good sermon.

Picture the synagogue in Ephesus on that Sabbath morning. A plain room, with benches round the walls, and a raised place in the middle for the reader, and a cupboard where the scrolls were kept. The men on one side, perhaps, the women on the other, or at the back. The windows open, because it is warm, and the sound of the street coming in faintly, a donkey complaining, a child laughing, a man selling figs.

And at the back, on a bench, two tentmakers.

The scroll was read. And then the young visitor from Alexandria was invited to speak, as visitors often were. And he stood up, and he began.

He began, I imagine, quietly, with the prophets. With the promises God had made to Abraham, and to David. With Isaiah, and the voice crying in the wilderness, prepare the way of the Lord. And then he spoke about John, the voice in the wilderness, who had come to prepare the way. And about the one John had pointed to. Jesus of Nazareth.

And his voice rose, and the room went very still.

Luke says that Apollos spoke boldly. He did not hedge. He did not soften it for his audience. He showed them, from their own Scriptures, all the things concerning Jesus. And when he finished, I imagine there was that particular silence that comes after someone has said something very well, before anyone quite knows what to say back.

At the back of the room, Aquila leaned over to his wife.

"He's very good," he whispered.

"He's wonderful," whispered Priscilla.

There was a pause.

"But," said Aquila.

"Yes," said Priscilla. "But."

Because they had both noticed. They had sat in the workshop in Corinth for a year and a half with Paul, listening to him teach, day after day, over the stitching. They knew the whole story, as the apostles told it. And they could hear, in the young man's beautiful sermon, a gap. Something left out. Not something wrong, exactly. Something not yet there.

Now. Here is the moment I want you to notice. Because there were several things Priscilla and Aquila could have done.

They could have stood up, there and then, in the synagogue, and said, excuse me, young man, you have missed something out. That would have been true. But it would have been unkind, and it would have embarrassed him in front of a whole roomful of strangers on his very first Sabbath in the city. And people who are embarrassed in public do not usually learn very much. They mostly just get defensive.

They could have gone round afterwards telling people, that new preacher, very fine voice, but he hasn't got it quite right, you know. That would also have been true. And it would have been the beginning of a great deal of gossip, and no good to anyone, least of all Apollos.

Or they could have said nothing at all. He's doing well enough, they could have said. He's a scholar from Alexandria. Who are we, a couple of tentmakers, to correct a man like that?

They did none of those things.

Luke tells us exactly what they did, in one short line. When Priscilla and Aquila heard him, Luke says, they took him aside, and explained to him the way of God more accurately.

They took him aside. The Greek word means something like, they took him to themselves. They brought him in. They took him home.

Walking back from the synagogue that day, I imagine, they talked it over.

"We should ask him to supper," said Priscilla.

"Doris will want to know who he is," said Aquila.

"Doris always wants to know who everyone is."

"And what will we say to him?"

Priscilla thought about it, as they went round the corner by the fountain.

"We'll say what Paul said to us," she said. "All of it. From the beginning. And we'll let him ask questions. He's clever. He'll see it for himself."

"And if he's offended?"

"Then he's offended," said Priscilla. "But I don't think he will be. Did you see his face, when he was talking about John? He doesn't want to win arguments. He wants to know the truth. People like that don't mind being told more of it."

So that evening, when the Sabbath was over, Aquila went to find the young man, and found him at the house where he was lodging, and said, with a craftsman's directness, "My wife and I heard you this morning. We'd like you to come and eat with us. We have some things to tell you about Jesus that I think you'd like to hear."

And Apollos, to his great credit, came.

And so we come back to where we started.

The back room of the workshop. The hides hanging from the beams. The lamp. The fish stew, which in my imagination Priscilla had made with whatever the fishermen at the harbour had left over that evening, plus a great deal of garlic. A loaf of bread, from the baker who did not burn it. A jug of watered wine. And three people round the table.

They talked about Alexandria, at first. About the lighthouse, and the library, and whether the scholars at the Museum really did spend all day arguing about commas. Aquila talked about Pontus, and the Black Sea, where the fish are different. Priscilla talked about Rome, and about Paul, and about the long months in Corinth, and Apollos listened with great interest, because he had heard of Paul, and wanted to know everything about him.

And then, when the stew was finished, and the bread was nearly gone, Priscilla refilled his cup, and set down the jug, and said, very gently, "Apollos, can we ask you something? About baptism?"

The young man put down his bread. He looked from one of them to the other.

"Of course," he said.

And they began.

Now, I am going to be careful here, and you will see why. Luke does not tell us what was said at that table. He tells us only that Priscilla and Aquila explained to Apollos the way of God more accurately. More accurately. Not, you had it all wrong. Just, more accurately. Filling in. Rounding out. Taking what he had, which was good, and showing him the rest.

So I will not put words in their mouths that Luke did not put there. But we can say, from the rest of the book of Acts, the sort of thing the apostles taught, and the sort of thing Priscilla and Aquila had heard from Paul, night after night, over the stitching. That Jesus, whom John had pointed to, had been crucified, and had risen from the dead, and had appeared to his friends, and had gone up to his Father. That he had promised to send the Holy Spirit. That on the day of Pentecost, in Jerusalem, the Spirit had come, like wind and like fire, on the apostles and on all who were with them. And that from that day, those who believed were baptized, not only with John's baptism of repentance, but in the name of Jesus, and received the gift of the Holy Spirit.

I imagine it went on late. I imagine the lamp had to be filled again. I imagine Apollos asked a great many questions, because clever people do, and that some of them were very good questions, which Priscilla and Aquila could not quite answer, and said so, and said that Paul would know, when he came back. I imagine that at some point the young man went very quiet, and sat looking at the lamp for a long while, the way people do when something they have half known for a long time suddenly comes into focus.

And I imagine that at the end of it, he said something like, "Thank you."

Whether Apollos was baptized again, Luke does not say. In the next chapter of Acts there is a story about some other believers at Ephesus who had received only John's baptism, and who were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus. But Luke does not say that about Apollos, and so we will not say it either. What Luke tells us is what happened next, and it is a lovely thing.

Because Apollos did not go off in a sulk. He did not go and find another synagogue where nobody would correct him. He stayed. He learned. He became part of the little church that met among the hides in Priscilla and Aquila's workshop, or so I like to think. And the believers in Ephesus, as we are about to see, came to trust him.

And after a while, Apollos decided that he wanted to go across the sea to Achaia, to Greece, which almost certainly means Corinth. Perhaps Priscilla and Aquila had told him so much about the church there that he wanted to see it. Perhaps he felt that a city full of people who argued about ideas could use a man who was good at arguing about ideas. Luke does not say why. He says only that Apollos wished to cross over.

And the believers at Ephesus did something very practical, and very kind. They encouraged him to go. And they wrote a letter.

In the old world, if you turned up in a strange city and said, hello, I am a Christian teacher, let me preach to you, people had no way of knowing who you were. You might be genuine. You might be a fraud. You might be a perfectly nice person with some very odd ideas. So the early churches had a custom. When someone travelled from one church to another, the first church wrote a letter, saying, this is our brother, we know him, we trust him, please welcome him. Paul mentions letters like that in his second letter to the Corinthians, a little crossly, because some people had turned up in Corinth with letters and he had none, and he thought that rather silly, since the Corinthians themselves were his letter.

So the believers at Ephesus wrote Apollos a letter of recommendation, and I think it is quite likely that Priscilla and Aquila had a hand in it. Please welcome him, it would have said. And off he sailed, west across the sea, the way Priscilla and Aquila had come.

Now let us go back to Corinth for a moment, and see an old friend.

Nikon, if you remember, was the boy in our last story who ran errands for the workshop in Corinth and sat on a basket in the doorway listening to Paul. He is our invention, and so is this little scene. But I like to think of him, a year or so older now, a bit taller, standing on the quay at Cenchreae on the morning the ship from Ephesus came in, because someone had told him that a visitor was coming with a letter from Priscilla and Aquila.

He watched the passengers come down the gangplank. A merchant. Two sailors. A woman with a basket of chickens. And then a young man in good clothes, with a scroll in his hand, looking about him with great interest.

"Are you Apollos?" said Nikon.

"I am," said the young man. "And you must be Nikon. Priscilla said you would be here. She said you would be the one asking questions."

Nikon was so pleased that she had remembered him that he forgot to be shy, and talked all the way up the road to the city, about the workshop, and Paul, and the governor who had said no, and how the church met at Titius Justus's house now, and how big Corinth was, and whether Ephesus was bigger. And Apollos laughed, and listened, and asked him questions back.

And then they came to the house where the believers met, and the letter from Ephesus was read out, and the church in Corinth welcomed him.

And here is what Luke says about what happened next. When Apollos arrived, Luke says, he greatly helped those who through grace had believed. For he powerfully refuted the Jews in public, showing by the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus.

He greatly helped. That is what Priscilla and Aquila's supper did. The man they took aside, and gently set right, went on to strengthen a whole church. They taught one person, at one table, on one evening. And through him they taught a city.

Of course, it was not all smooth. It never is, with Corinth.

A year or two later, Paul came back to Ephesus, just as he had promised, if God willed. He stayed there a long time, more than two years. And while he was there, news came across the sea from Corinth, and some of it was not good. The church had started to split into parties. Some people were saying, I belong to Paul. Others were saying, I belong to Apollos. Others said, I belong to Cephas, which is Saint Peter. And some, rather smugly I suspect, were saying, well, I belong to Christ.

It is easy to imagine how it happened. Paul was a powerful thinker, but by his own account, and by the account of his critics, he was not always an impressive speaker. Apollos, on the other hand, was eloquent, and polished, and had all that Alexandrian learning. Some of the Corinthians, who were very fond of a good speech, must have compared the two, and taken sides, the way people do about their favourite football team.

Now. If you had been Paul, and you heard that people in a church you had founded were saying, oh, Apollos is much better than Paul, how would you have felt? A little hurt, perhaps. A little jealous. You might have been tempted to write back and point out, rather sharply, who had been there first.

Paul did write back. It is the letter we call the First Letter to the Corinthians, and he wrote it from Ephesus. And here is what he said about Apollos. I will give it to you in plain words.

What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants, through whom you came to believe, as the Lord gave to each. I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the growth. So neither the one who plants nor the one who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth. We are God's fellow workers. You are God's field.

I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth.

There is no jealousy in that at all. Paul does not run Apollos down. He puts them side by side, two gardeners in the same garden, one with a spade, one with a watering can, and both of them looking up at the one who makes things grow. A little later in the same letter, he says that he has applied all this to himself and Apollos for the Corinthians' benefit, so that they would learn not to puff themselves up, one against another.

And then, near the end of the letter, there is a little line that I find very touching. As for our brother Apollos, Paul writes, I strongly urged him to go to you with the other brothers, but it was not at all his will to go now. He will come when he has the opportunity.

So Apollos was there with Paul in Ephesus, or near it, when the letter was written. The two of them, whom the Corinthians had tried to set against each other, were together. Paul calls him our brother. And Paul had begged him to go back to Corinth, and Apollos had said, not now. Luke and Paul do not tell us why. But many readers have thought that perhaps Apollos did not want to go back while there was a party in Corinth using his name against his friend. That is only a reading. But it would fit the man who, years before, had sat at a supper table in Ephesus and let a couple of tentmakers teach him.

And Priscilla and Aquila? They were there too. At the very end of that same letter to Corinth, after all the arguing and all the advice, Paul sends greetings. The churches of Asia greet you, he writes. Aquila and Prisca, together with the church in their house, greet you warmly in the Lord.

The church in their house. In Ephesus, too, it had happened. The workshop with the hides on the beams, and the sparrows in the rafters, had become a church.

Prisca, by the way, is what Paul always calls her. Luke calls her Priscilla, which is the friendly, everyday form of the same name, a bit like the difference between Elizabeth and Lizzie. And there is one more small thing about her name that I cannot resist telling you. In the oldest copies of Acts, in the verse where the couple take Apollos aside, Priscilla's name comes first. In some of the later copies, the order is swapped, and Aquila comes first. Some of the old copyists, it seems, thought it was more proper to put the husband's name in front. Luke did not. And neither, most of the time, did Paul.

After Ephesus, when the emperor's order had lapsed, the two of them went back to Rome, as we heard last time, and the church met in their house there too. And later still, when Paul was writing what may have been one of his very last letters, to Timothy, he sent his greetings, near the end, to Prisca and Aquila. They were among the last people on his mind.

As for Apollos, he appears in the New Testament just once more. In a short letter to his friend Titus, on the island of Crete, Paul writes, do your best to send Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way, and see that they lack nothing. So he was still travelling, still teaching, still a friend of Paul's, years after the parties in Corinth. After that, Scripture does not mention him again.

Later tradition remembered him as one of the Seventy, the wider circle of the Lord's disciples, and made him a bishop, though Luke and Paul say nothing about that. And a great many people, ever since, have wondered whether Apollos might have written one of the books of the New Testament. The Letter to the Hebrews does not say who wrote it. It is written in beautiful, polished Greek, full of the Scriptures, full of the kind of deep reading of Moses and the priesthood that an Alexandrian scholar might have loved. Martin Luther, fifteen hundred years later, guessed that Apollos wrote it. Others in the early Church guessed Barnabas, or Luke, or Clement, or Paul himself. Nobody knows. It is a guess, and we will leave it as a guess. But it is a nice guess.

The Orthodox Church remembers Saint Apollos on the eighth of December, in the company of some others of the Seventy, among them a Saint Sosthenes, whom that tradition takes to be the same Sosthenes we met last time being hustled about in front of the governor's platform at Corinth. And Saints Aquila and Priscilla are remembered, as we heard last time, on the eighth of July in the West and the thirteenth of February in the East.

Ephesus is ruins now. The temple of Artemis, one of the seven wonders of the world, is a single column standing in a marshy field, put back together from fallen pieces, with a stork's nest on top of it. The great theatre is still there, and in summer the tourists sit in it, and eat ice cream, and take photographs. The marble street is still there, and you can walk down it, past the fountains and the shop fronts, and try to guess which narrow doorway might once have been a tentmaker's.

And the harbour is a marsh, with reeds in it, and frogs, and the sea a long way off.

But I like to think of that one evening, in a little back room, with the lamp and the stew and the hides on the beams. A clever young man, and two working people with scarred hands, who knew something he didn't, and told him, kindly, at their own table, so that nobody else had to hear. And then sent him off, with a letter and their blessing, to go and do more good than they could do themselves.

Most of us, most of the time, are not Paul. And not Apollos, either. Most of us are more like Priscilla and Aquila. Quiet people, with ordinary work to do, and a table, and a lamp. And it turns out that is quite enough to be going on with.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter eighteen, verses eighteen to twenty-eight, with the start of chapter nineteen. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept, in our own plain rendering. Luke does not tell us what Priscilla and Aquila said to Apollos, only that they explained the way of God to him more accurately, so we have not invented their teaching; we have only sketched what the apostles taught elsewhere in Acts. Whether Apollos was baptized again, and how he came to know only John's baptism, Luke does not say, and neither have we.

Paul's line about keeping the feast in Jerusalem is in many later copies of Acts but not the oldest. The note in Codex Bezae that Apollos was taught in his own country is in that one manuscript. Whether fervent in spirit means his own spirit or the Holy Spirit is read both ways.

Paul's words about planting and watering, the parties at Corinth, Apollos not wishing to go, and the greetings from Aquila and Prisca and the church in their house, are from the First Letter to the Corinthians, which Paul wrote from Ephesus. Zenas and Apollos are in the Letter to Titus. The reason Apollos stayed away from Corinth is a reading, not a fact. His bishopric is later tradition, and Luther's guess about Hebrews is a guess.

Philo describes the Jewish quarters of Alexandria. The seventy-two translators are a legend from the Letter of Aristeas. Strabo describes the silting harbour at Ephesus.

Doris, her shop and her sparrows, Nikon meeting Apollos at the harbour, the stew, and every word at the supper table and on the walk home, are our own invention, and so are everybody's thoughts.

That's the story of Saints Priscilla and Aquila and the preacher from Alexandria for tonight.

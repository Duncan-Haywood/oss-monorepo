Kentigern and the Four Signs of Glasgow

The sixth-century bishop the Scots call Mungo, "dear one", and the four small stories that sit on the coat of arms of Glasgow: a robin, a tree, a bell and a fish with a ring in its mouth. A warm, funny bedtime telling of the old legends, with plain honesty about how little is certain.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Saint Kentigern (Mungo), Saint Serf, Thenew, King Rhydderch, Queen Languoreth, Saint Columba
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; Kentigern is a saint of the undivided Church in Britain, patron of the city of Glasgow, honoured in both traditions
Scripture and history
that a holy bishop called Kentigern worked among the British people of the kingdom of Strathclyde in the later sixth century and was buried where Glasgow Cathedral now stands is the firm core; he is mentioned in the old Welsh and Scottish records and a church was built over his grave
Legend
the robin, the tree, the bell and the fish are all legends, written down in two Lives, a fragment from about the year eleven hundred and a long Life by the monk Jocelyn of Furness around eleven hundred and eighty, some six hundred years after he died; they are told here as the old story says, never as documented history
What we invented
the narrator's jokes, the weather, the small conversations between Kentigern, Serf, the queen and the king, and the scenes of the market and the river are our own invention, told in the spirit of the old legends; no new miracle is attributed to the saint
Feast day
the thirteenth of January in the Catholic calendar of Scotland; the same day is kept by Orthodox Christians who honour the saints of the British Isles

Sources

  • Jocelyn of Furness, Life of Saint Kentigern, about 1180, the main source for the four signs
  • The anonymous fragment of a Life of Saint Kentigern, about 1100, preserved in the British Library
  • Adomnan of Iona, Life of Saint Columba, which has Columba as a contemporary in the north of Britain
  • The Annales Cambriae and other early Welsh records for the dates of the kings of the north
  • The coat of arms of the city of Glasgow, whose four emblems preserve the legends

Full script

There is a bird on the table, and it is not moving, and eleven small boys are standing in a circle looking at it with their mouths open.

It is a robin. It is quite dead. Its head has come off, and one of the boys, the one with the most guilty face, is holding it in two hands the way you hold something you have broken in somebody else's house. And a quiet old man in a long brown habit has just walked in, and put down his candle, and is looking at the bird, and then at the boy, and then at the bird again, and saying, in the voice of someone who has had a long day, "Well. Let's see what we can do."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is of Saint Kentigern, the patron of Glasgow, whom the Scots call Mungo, and it comes from old legends written down long after his death; we will say which parts are which as we go. A few of the small conversations, and most of the weather, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let me start with a confession. Of all the saints we have talked about on this show, Kentigern is one of the ones we know least about, and one of the ones people love most. Those two facts go together more often than you would think.

Here is what we know for certain, which will not take long. In the second half of the sixth century, in the north of Britain, there was a bishop called Kentigern. He worked among the British people, who spoke a language much like early Welsh, and who lived in a kingdom called Strathclyde, which ran along the River Clyde. He founded a church beside a little stream, on a green hill, in a place the old records call Glasgu, which means something like the green hollow. He died, and was buried there. And over his grave, six hundred years later, men began to build a great stone cathedral, and around the cathedral grew a town, and the town became a city, and the city is Glasgow. That is the whole of the firm history. A bishop, a river, a grave, and a city that grew up round it like ivy.

Everything else is story. And the stories are lovely.

They were written down about six hundred years after he died, by monks who loved him, in two documents. The first is a fragment, a few pages from around the year eleven hundred. The second is a long Life, written about eleven hundred and eighty by a monk called Jocelyn, at a place called Furness, a long way south, in what is now England. Jocelyn was a careful man by the standards of his time, and he says so himself. He says he found an older book that was written in a rough, difficult style, and a second one, full of good things but a little hard to follow, and that he has done his best to put the two together in good Latin. I find that endearing. He is basically the first editor who ever wrote a note saying, I have tidied this up, please do not be cross.

So, when this story says "the old story says", it means Jocelyn, and the monks before him. We will not pretend it is anything else. A legend is a way a people tell each other what a person was like. This one says Kentigern was kind, patient, brave and funny, and I see no reason to argue with that.

Let me set the scene.

Imagine Scotland in the year five hundred and fifty or so. It is not Scotland yet. The Romans have been gone for a hundred and fifty years, and they have left behind roads that nobody can quite maintain and a few walls that nobody quite remembers building. The country is a patchwork of small kingdoms, each with its own king, its own hall, its own feuds, and its own opinion of the neighbours. In the north, the Picts. In the west, the Irish settlers, who are about to become the Scots. In the south and the middle, the Britons, the old inhabitants, who have been Christians for some generations now, and who think of themselves as the proper descendants of Rome, which is a little optimistic of them.

The Christians of the time are scattered. There are no big parishes with a bishop and a diocesan office and a newsletter. There is a monk here, a hermit there, a little church of wood and wattle, a cross carved on a standing stone. Faith travels the way news travels, from one person to the next, along the river and the road. A holy man with a good voice and a pair of strong legs could do a great deal, and a good many of them did.

The weather, I should say, is exactly what you think it is. It is Scotland. It is raining. It has been raining since the Romans left, and the people have developed a great many words for it, and none of them is a compliment.

And there, in the middle of this rain, we begin.

The old story says that Kentigern's mother was called Thenew, and that she was the daughter of a king in the east, and that she came to a time of great trouble, and was cast out by her own people. We do not need the details, and the monks themselves were not sure of them. What matters is where she ended up. The story says that she was sent out to sea in a little boat, and the boat drifted across the great estuary, and came ashore at a place called Culross, on the north side of the Firth of Forth, where a holy man named Serf had a small monastery.

Saint Serf. Hold that name, because he is the kind of person every bedtime story needs: a man of great gentleness and mild, constant surprise. He was an old monk by then, with a fringe of white hair round a bald head, and a habit of looking at the sky as if it owed him an explanation. He had a little monastery, a few huts, a church, a vegetable garden, and a school for boys. He was the sort of teacher who does not so much give lessons as arrange for you to be standing near something wonderful when it happens.

He found the young woman on the beach, and he did not ask her the long list of questions that other people had. He gave her a place by the fire, and a bowl of something hot, and later, when her baby was born, he baptised him. And he looked at the child, and said, in the old British tongue, the words that became his name for ever. He called him Mungo.

Mungo. It means dear one, or dear friend. It is what you would call a favourite nephew, or a puppy, or a boy you are very fond of. The name Kentigern is the grand name, the bishop's name, which means something like lord or chief of the household. But on the streets of Glasgow, for fourteen hundred years, he has been Mungo, which is to say, our dear one. I think there is something rather wonderful about a city that has kept calling its patron saint by a pet name.

So the boy grew up at Culross. He learned his letters. He learned the psalms. He learned how to carry water, and how to mend a fence, and how to be quiet in the morning, and he was, the monks say, a good boy: cheerful and gentle and quick. And that, of course, is where the trouble began, because there is nothing a group of other boys finds harder to forgive than a boy who is good and also liked.

Which brings us back to the robin.

Serf had a pet. It was a robin, a plain brown-and-orange little bird that lived in the church, hopped about on the benches, and took crumbs from his hand. Every monastery, I think, has one animal that is secretly in charge, and at Culross it was the robin. It sat on Serf's shoulder during the psalms. It ate from his bowl. Serf was fond of it in the way that a person who has given up almost everything is allowed to be fond of one small thing.

Now, the other boys at the school were jealous of Mungo, because the old man plainly loved him, and so, one day, they hatched a plan. A bad one. They caught the robin, in a quiet moment, while Mungo was at the other end of the garden. And in the scuffle, as you will have guessed, the bird died.

They were horrified. Boys always are, afterwards. They stood there in the little room with the dead robin on the table, and the loudest of them said, the way boys do, quick, we have to blame somebody. And the somebody they chose was Mungo. They put the bird in his hands when he came in from the garden, and they ran to fetch Serf.

I would like you to picture that scene properly, because it is the first of our four signs. A small boy standing in a small room, with a dead bird in his hands, and the whole class looking at him. He has not done it. He knows he has not done it. But nobody is going to believe him, and the other boys are each other's witnesses, and the most important man in his world is about to walk through the door.

And here the old story is good. It does not say that Mungo shouted, or accused them, or called down a punishment. It says that he did something very ordinary, and very hard. He picked up the bird, and he made the sign of the cross over it, and he prayed. Quietly. As he might say grace at supper.

And the bird opened its eyes, and shook itself, and flew up on to the beam, and began to sing.

The old story says the Lord restored the robin, because Mungo asked, and that is the right way to say it. Not Mungo did a miracle. The Lord did it, in answer to a boy's prayer. And when Serf came in, there was nothing to see but eleven very pale schoolboys, one calm one, and a small bird on a beam, singing its head off.

I do not know what Serf said. The old sources do not tell us, which I think is an oversight. I like to imagine that he looked at the bird, and then at the boys, and then at the bird, and said, "I see," in the voice of a man who has understood everything and has decided to say nothing. And that the eleven boys were excused from the afternoon lesson, and were given the pots to scrub, and that they scrubbed them in a very respectful silence.

That is the first sign. And that is why, on the coat of arms of Glasgow, if you look carefully, there is a little bird.

Now, the old story has one more tale from the school at Culross, and it is the one that gives Glasgow its tree.

Serf had a custom, which was that the lamp in the church must never go out. Not for a night. Not for an hour. It was a sign that Christ was in the place. It was, I suppose, the sixth-century equivalent of a pilot light. And the boys took turns to keep it. One night it was Mungo's turn, and the jealous boys, who had not forgiven him for the robin, waited until he was asleep, and crept in, and put the fire out.

Now, in a cold, wet country without matches, this was a problem. You could not pop next door and borrow a light. It was the middle of winter. The nearest fire was miles away. And in the morning, the old story says, Mungo woke in the dark, found the ashes cold, and understood at once what had been done.

He did not wake the others. He did not call for Serf. He went out into the frozen wood behind the church, in the half dark, and found a frost-covered branch of hazel. He broke it off. He held it in his hands, and he prayed, and the branch caught fire, and burned with a clear steady flame, and he carried it back into the church, and lit the lamp.

That is the second sign, the tree. The monks tell it a little differently from one another, and the shield does not say, but what the city holds on to is the sense of the thing: that a frozen, dead-looking branch, in the hands of someone who prays, can turn out to have been alive all along. In the oldest rhyme of the city it is called the tree that never grew, which I find a very Scottish way of describing a miracle, as if it were a complaint.

I think that is the real shape of all four of the signs. If you listen carefully, you will find that none of them is about how clever Mungo was. They are about how he treated people who had let somebody down, or even wronged him: he put the thing right, quietly, and said nothing.

Mungo stayed at Culross for some years more. He grew up. He became a young monk, and then, as these things go, a bit too well known. People came to ask Serf about the boy. People came to ask the boy about everything. And Mungo, who was by nature a quiet and rather shy man, began to feel the walls of the little monastery getting close.

There comes a day in every life when you have to leave the place where you were loved, and go to a place where you will have to love people yourself. For Mungo it was a morning in early spring. He went to see Serf, in the small room beside the church, and he said it as plainly as he could. He said that he thought he was meant to go west.

Serf did not argue. I think he had always known. He said something I like very much, which is not in any book but ought to be: "Well, I did not bring you up to keep you." And he gave him his blessing, and a loaf, and a stick, and Mungo set out.

He went west, across the country, with his stick and his loaf and his habit, towards the River Clyde. He walked for days. And he came, in the end, to a little settlement beside a stream, where there was a cross, set up long before by Saint Ninian's followers, and a graveyard, and a green hill. He found the people there were Christians, but only just. There was no church to speak of. There was no priest. The graveyard was kept by an old holy man, who was dying, and who looked at Mungo and said, in the way that the dying sometimes do, "Ah. There you are."

And so Mungo stayed. He took up the old man's work. He built a church out of wood and wicker, on the green hill, beside the stream. And the stream was called the Molendinar, and the place was called Glasgu, and a handful of people began to come.

The old story says that the little church at Glasgu grew. People came to it from up and down the river. They came for the prayers, and the bread, and the quiet. And in time, the monks say, the people of the place agreed that the humble man in the brown habit ought to be made a bishop. This was, I think we can say, the sixth-century equivalent of being told you've been promoted, when all you wanted was to get on with the vegetables.

He was consecrated, the old story says, by a bishop who came over from Ireland, with the right words and the oil, and Mungo became the bishop of the Britons along the Clyde. He accepted. He did not refuse, and he did not boast. He just went on being the same man, with a heavier staff.

And the people loved him. And that, as it did at Culross, led to some trouble. Because the king of the country at that time, the old story says, was not very pleased.

His name was Morken. He was a stern and suspicious sort of king, and he did not like the idea of a holy man who had more of the people's hearts than he did. He was not an evil man, in the way the old stories sometimes make villains. He was a tired and anxious one, with a leaking roof and a court full of flatterers. And he decided that the best thing for the kingdom, and for his own peace of mind, was for the bishop to go.

I do not want to make too much of this. The legend is brief, and the monks who wrote it were not trying to write a political thriller. What it says is that the king was hostile, that there was a quarrel, and that Mungo left. He went south, into Wales, and spent some years there, and he was received with great kindness by the Welsh saints, and he built a monastery at a place called Llanelwy, which is now Saint Asaph.

That is a nice detail. I like that in the middle of the story, the saint goes to Wales and gets on very well with everybody, and that the Welsh are still proud of it. I think you can tell a great deal about a man from the places that remember him fondly.

I think I should stop here, and let the room get a little quieter.

You have been listening for a good while, and the next part of the story is a story about a ring, and a queen, and a king, and a fish, and it is the funniest of the four. So I would like to tell it slowly.

Here is the setting. We are in the great hall of King Rhydderch. It is the evening. The fire is bright, and the bench is full, and there is a feast on. The king's household is there, the knights, the poets, the harper, the steward, and the dogs. There is a good deal of mead. There is a good deal of noise.

And the king's wife, Queen Languoreth, has a ring. It is a gold ring, a gift from her husband, and it is the sort of ring that a king gives a queen: heavy, plain, and carrying the whole weight of a marriage. She wears it always.

But on this particular day, the old story says, she did something a little foolish. There was a young knight at the court, a pleasant man, whom she liked, and who was in some difficulty, and she was kind to him. And when he admired the ring, she, in the warmth of the moment, gave it to him as a gift.

Now. A queen should not give away her husband's ring. She knew it, I think, the moment she had done it. And the young knight, who was a good deal less wise than he looked, did not know what to do with it. He took it down to the river. It was a warm afternoon. He lay down on the bank by the Clyde, and he took the ring off to look at it, and he fell asleep.

And the king, who happened to be walking by, saw the ring.

He did not say a word. He knew the ring. He took it very gently off the sleeping man's hand, and he walked away with it. And he threw it, the old story says, into the river. It sank. It was gone. And the king turned, and went back up the hill to the hall, with a face like a closed door.

When he came in, he sat down at the high table, and looked at his wife, and said, quite pleasantly, "My dear. Where is the ring I gave you?"

Now the queen was a clever woman, and a frightened one, and she could see that her husband already knew. She said it was upstairs. She said it was being mended. She said she would fetch it. And the king said, in the same pleasant voice, "Of course. Please do. I would like to see it. Tomorrow, by noon."

She went out of the hall in a state I will not describe. She sent a messenger to the young knight, who woke up by the river and found his hand empty. She sent another messenger to the only person in the kingdom she thought might help. She sent for the bishop.

Mungo came. I like to imagine him arriving breathless from the hill, in his brown habit, with mud on the hem. She told him everything, in a rush, all at once, the way you tell the truth to someone you trust. And Mungo listened. He did not interrupt. He did not say what a foolish thing to do. He looked at her, and I think he saw how afraid she was, and how sorry.

Then he said, "Wait here."

He went down to the river, alone. He walked along the bank in the evening light. He found a fisherman, the old story says, in a boat, and he said, "Would you catch me a salmon?" And the fisherman, who knew the bishop, said, "Of course," and cast his line. And a salmon came up. A big, handsome Clyde salmon, silver and heavy, gleaming in the dusk.

And Mungo took the fish, and carried it back up the hill, still wet. And in its mouth, the old story says, was a gold ring.

Hold that picture for a moment. A bishop walking up a hill in the half dark with a large salmon in his arms. It is not a dignified image. It is, I think, the least dignified picture of a saint in all of hagiography, and I love it for that. Nobody in Scotland ever had to be told that holiness is solemn.

He gave the ring to the queen. And the old story says, quietly, that the queen went to her husband the next morning, at noon, with the ring on her hand. And the king looked at it, and looked at her, and I think he understood a great many things at once. He had been angry, and he had been wrong to throw it. He had thought the worst, and the worst was not true. He asked her pardon. She asked his. They both stood there in the great hall in front of the whole court, being very sorry, and the young knight, who was hiding behind a pillar, came out and was sorry as well.

And the story ends there. No punishment, no scene, no villain. A king, a queen, and a bishop who knew how to find a ring in a river, and a marriage that was mended, and a young knight who learned to keep his hands to himself.

That is the third sign, the fish with the ring in its mouth. It is on the coat of arms of Glasgow, and it is the most famous thing about the city, and it is a story about forgiveness, again. You will notice the pattern.

The last sign is the bell, and it is short.

The old story says that Mungo, when he was an old man, was given a bell. A small handbell, which was rung to call the people to prayer. The monks tell it differently: that he brought it from Rome, that it was given him by the pope, that an angel gave it, that it was found. I think we can say, honestly, that nobody knows. What the legend wants us to remember is that the bishop had a bell, and that when it rang, people came.

In the centuries afterwards, the bell of Saint Mungo was carried through the streets of Glasgow by a town crier. When someone died, or a feast began, or a market was opened, or a proclamation made, the bell was rung, and people stopped what they were doing and listened. It is on the shield, the last of the four. A bird, a tree, a bell and a fish. And I like to think, if you were a stranger walking into Glasgow, in the year twelve hundred, and you asked what the four things on the city's arms meant, someone would have told you, with great patience and a bit of a grin, the whole story, and then made you a cup of tea.

Now, I should tell you about one more person, because the old story does.

Late in his life, the story says, Kentigern met Saint Columba. Columba, as you will remember from another episode, was the great Irish monk who had set up his monastery on the island of Iona, and who was, in those years, one of the most famous holy men in the whole of the north. They were contemporaries, near enough. They were in the same country, doing the same work, and I think it would have been strange if they had never met.

The old story says they did. They met, the story says, beside a stream, and the two of them exchanged their staffs. Kentigern gave Columba his, and Columba gave Kentigern his. And they talked, for a long time, about God, about the work, about the people they were trying to look after. And then they parted.

I like this. I don't know if it happened, and Jocelyn tells it with some hesitation, but I like it. Two old men by a stream in the rain. Two walking sticks. A whole country that was only just beginning to be a country. And two quiet men, neither of whom needed to be the most important person in the conversation.

Now. We've come a long way. The fire is low, and the hall is quiet, and I think it's time to slow right down.

Let us go back, one last time, to the green hill beside the stream.

It is the thirteenth of January, in the year the old records give as six hundred and three, though we cannot be certain. Kentigern is an old man now, very old, perhaps eighty or more. He has been a bishop for a long time. He has seen kings come and go. He has been into exile, and home again. He has buried friends and baptised their grandchildren. And he has, the old story says, grown very tired.

On this particular morning, he has been brought, the monks say, to a warm bath, to ease his bones. It was an old custom. It was a way of keeping a very elderly person comfortable in the cold. And there, in the warm water, with the steam rising and the candles lit and the monks beside him, Kentigern went to sleep. And did not wake up.

That is the old story's account of his death. It is peaceful. It is unremarkable. There is no battle, no martyrdom, no scene. A tired old man in a warm room, among people who loved him. I think most of us would take it.

And they buried him on the green hill. They buried him in the place he had chosen, beside the stream, in the earth of Glasgu. And the people came from far and near, and they stood round the grave, and they did not say very much.

And when they had gone, and the monks had gone, the sun came out. It was January in Scotland, so it was a thin, pale, watery sun, but it was there. It shone on the new grave, and on the green hill, and on the little stream. And a robin, I like to think, a small brown-and-orange one, came and sat on the stone, and began to sing.

Over the next six hundred years, a church was built on the place. And then a bigger church. And then, in the twelfth century, a great cathedral, which is still there, and which still stands over his grave, in the crypt, in the dark, by a little well. The city grew up around it. There are now a good many people in Glasgow, and a good many universities, and a good many football clubs, and on all their old official papers there is a small bird, a tree, a bell and a fish.

Here is the rhyme, and I will say it slowly, because it is an old one and I would like it to be said properly at least once.

Here is the bird that never flew, here is the tree that never grew, here is the bell that never rang, and here is the fish that never swam.

Which is to say: here are the things that, by every rule, should not have happened. And they happened, the city says, in this place, to this man, for these reasons. They happened because someone was kind to a boy, kind to a young monk, kind to a queen. They are stories about the way mercy gets into the world, one small deed at a time.

The fire is low now. The candle's nearly out. The stream is going on down the hill in the dark, as it has done for fourteen hundred years, and somewhere in the cathedral crypt a lamp is burning. It has not gone out. And in a little while, if you have not already, you will be asleep.

Now, notes for the curious, which we do at the end of every episode, so that you know what you have just been told.

What is certain is small. There was a bishop called Kentigern in the kingdom of Strathclyde in the later sixth century. He is named in early Welsh and Scottish records. He was buried where Glasgow Cathedral now stands, and the cathedral, the city and its name grew from that. The traditional date of his death is the thirteenth of January, about the year six hundred and three, but the date is a later tradition.

The stories come from two Lives. The first is a fragment from about eleven hundred. The second is the Life by Jocelyn of Furness, written about eleven hundred and eighty at the request of a bishop of Glasgow. Jocelyn was drawing on older material, but he was writing six hundred years after the events. The robin, the fire from the hazel branch, the tree, the ring and the fish, the bell, the meeting with Columba: all of these are legends, told here as the old story says. In particular, the meeting with Columba is doubtful, and some of the details of Kentigern's mother Thenew are told differently in the two Lives; we have kept to the gentlest and said little. The rhyme about the bird, tree, bell and fish is of much later date, and is not from the Lives at all.

What this episode made up is the colour: the weather, the schoolboys' faces, Serf's quiet remarks, the dinner in the great hall, the frightened queen, the bishop with the salmon in his arms, the old man's last morning. None of those are in the sources. And we have not added any miracle that Jocelyn does not report.

One last note. In the legends, Kentigern is shown again and again forgiving people who have failed. That may be the monks' own picture of what a bishop ought to be. If so, it is a good one, and it has lasted.

Solomon and the House That Heaven Cannot Contain

King Solomon builds the Temple in Jerusalem, with cedar floated down the coast from Lebanon and stone cut so far away that no hammer is heard on the hill, and then, in the autumn, the ark is carried in, the singers and trumpeters sound as one, a cloud fills the house, and the king kneels before everyone and prays that the God whom heaven cannot contain will hear from heaven and forgive. Told from the First Book of Kings and the Second Book of Chronicles.

Full script

It is autumn, on a hill in Jerusalem, and a great many people are standing very still.

There are trumpets. A hundred and twenty of them, held by priests in a long gleaming row. There are cymbals, and harps, and lyres, and singers in white linen, more singers than you have ever seen in one place. And they have all just done something that is very hard to do. They have all made one sound. Not a hundred and twenty trumpets and a crowd of singers. One sound, as if one enormous voice were singing a single short line. He is good. His love lasts for ever.

And then the sound stops. And in the stopping, something fills the new building on the top of the hill. It is a cloud. It comes from nowhere, and it fills the whole house, from the golden floor to the cedar ceiling, so thickly that the priests who were going in cannot go in. They stand at the door, holding their things, not knowing what to do with their hands.

And the king, who has waited seven years for this morning, looks at the cloud, and says, very quietly, something that makes everyone near him turn and look at him.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the building and the dedication of King Solomon's Temple, from the First Book of Kings and the Second Book of Chronicles, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a quarryman, his little daughter and a sailor from Tyre, and everything they say, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Last time on Saints by Candlelight, we were in Jerusalem with the pilgrim Egeria, sixteen hundred years ago, at the great autumn feast when the church of the Resurrection was dedicated. And Egeria, standing in the crowd, remembered something from the Bible. She wrote to her sisters at home that this was the same kind of day as the day on which holy Solomon, when he had finished the house of God, stood before the altar and prayed. And then, as you may remember, her letter stopped, on the fourth day of the feast.

So tonight, we are going back. A long way back. Not sixteen hundred years, but about three thousand. To the day she was thinking of.

Let me tell you who Solomon was.

He was the son of King David. You will remember David, the shepherd boy with the sling and the five smooth stones, who became the king of Israel, and who played the harp, and wrote songs, and danced in front of the ark of God with all his might, which his wife did not think was very kingly of him. David made Jerusalem his city. He brought the ark of the covenant there, the golden chest that held the stone tablets Moses had brought down from the mountain. And he put it in a tent.

And one day, David was sitting in his fine new palace of cedar wood, and he looked out at the tent, and he felt uncomfortable. He said to the prophet Nathan, "Look. I live in a house of cedar, and the ark of God lives in a tent." And Nathan said, at first, "Go and do whatever is in your heart." But that night the word of the Lord came to Nathan, and the answer was gentler and stranger than anyone expected. It went something like this. "Did I ever ask anyone for a house of cedar? I have been going about in a tent since I brought my people out of Egypt. You will not build me a house, David. I will build you a house. A house of children, and grandchildren. And your son will build a house for my name."

The First Book of Chronicles adds the reason. David had been a man of war all his life. He had shed a great deal of blood. And the house of God was to be built by a man of peace. In fact, the name Solomon sounds like the Hebrew word shalom, which means peace. So David gathered things instead. He gathered stone, and iron for nails, and bronze beyond weighing, and cedar logs beyond counting. He drew up the plans. He organised the priests and the singers. He did everything except build it. Which, if you have ever done the hard part of a job and then handed it on to someone else to finish and get the credit, you will understand is a kind of holiness all of its own.

And then David died, an old man, and Solomon became king.

Now, Solomon is famous for one thing above all, and it happened near the beginning.

Early in his reign, Solomon went to a high place called Gibeon to offer sacrifice. And that night, in a dream, the Lord appeared to him and said, "Ask. What shall I give you?"

Think about that for a moment. You are young. You have just become king. And God says, ask for anything.

Most of us, I suspect, would have a list. Solomon did not ask for a long life, or for riches, or for his enemies to be defeated. He said, more or less, "I am only a little child. I do not know how to go out or come in. And here I am in the middle of a great people. So give your servant an understanding heart, to judge your people, and to tell good from bad."

And the Lord was pleased. He said, "Because you asked for this, and not for long life or riches or the lives of your enemies, I will give you a wise and understanding heart. And I will give you the things you did not ask for too."

Solomon woke up. And it was a dream. And the Bible says, so simply, that he went to Jerusalem and stood before the ark, and offered sacrifices, and made a feast for all his servants. Which I think is exactly the right thing to do when God has spoken to you in your sleep. Say thank you, and have people round for dinner.

You probably know the story that comes next, about the two mothers and the one baby, and the wise king who knew which one was the real mother because she would rather give the baby away than see it harmed. That is from the same chapter. It is how the people of Israel found out that their king had been given wisdom. But it is a story for another night.

Tonight is about the house.

In the fourth year of his reign, in the spring, in the month the old calendar called Ziv, which means something like the month of flowers, Solomon began to build.

The place was a hill on the north side of the city, above the old town of David. Before it was anything else, it had been a threshing floor, a flat open place where a man called Ornan, or Araunah, used to beat the grain from the straw and let the wind carry the chaff away. David had bought it from him. And the Second Book of Chronicles gives the hill a name that would have made any listener sit up. Mount Moriah. The same name as the land of Moriah, where long ago Abraham had gone up a mountain with his son Isaac and found that God would provide. Chronicles does not spell out the connection. Later Jewish and Christian readers did, and have been thinking about it ever since.

Now, the first thing you need for a temple is wood. And the best wood in that part of the world grew in Lebanon, in the mountains to the north, where the great cedars stood. Cedar is lovely wood. It is red-gold, and it smells sweet, and insects dislike it, and it does not rot. If you have ever opened an old cedar chest, you will know the smell.

The cedars of Lebanon belonged, more or less, to the king of Tyre, a great trading city on the coast, whose name was Hiram. Hiram had been a friend of David. So Solomon sent him a message. "You know my father wanted to build a house for the name of the Lord his God, and could not, because of the wars. But now the Lord has given me peace on every side. Not an enemy, not a misfortune. So I mean to build. Will you let your people cut cedars for me? My servants will work alongside your servants, and I will pay them whatever you say. Because, as you know, there is nobody among us who knows how to cut timber like the people of Sidon."

That last line is a compliment, and a true one. Hiram was delighted. He wrote back. "I will do everything you want about the cedar and the cypress. My servants will bring the logs down from Lebanon to the sea, and I will make them into rafts, and float them along the coast to whatever place you tell me. And there I will break them up, and you can take them away."

And in return, every year, Solomon sent Hiram wheat and oil. A great deal of wheat. Twenty thousand measures of it, the Bible says, and twenty measures of the finest, purest oil. Tyre was a city on a rock in the sea, and it was full of sailors and merchants and very short of fields. So this was a good arrangement. Wood for bread. Which, if you think about it, is how a lot of the world has always worked.

Let me take you now to the coast, to a raft.

The Second Book of Chronicles names the place where the rafts came ashore. It was Joppa, the port that we now call Jaffa, the same harbour that Jonah sailed from when he was running away, and where, a thousand years later, Saint Peter would see a sheet let down from heaven. From there, the timber was hauled up into the hills to Jerusalem.

Imagine, then, a man called Abdi. He is our own invention, so you can imagine him however you like. I see him as a short, brown, cheerful sailor from Tyre, with a beard full of salt, who has spent most of his life on the water and does not entirely trust anything that keeps still.

Abdi's job is to ride the rafts. The logs are lashed together in great bundles, and towed, or poled, or nudged along the shore by little boats, down past the cliffs and the river mouths and the fishing villages, from Lebanon to Joppa, which is a long way. And somebody has to stand on top of them. Somebody has to walk up and down the floating logs, with a pole, keeping an eye on the ropes, and shouting at the boats, and not falling in.

"The trick," Abdi tells anyone who will listen, "is to think like a log. A log does not want to go to Jerusalem. A log wants to go wherever the sea goes. So you do not argue with it. You just make sure the sea and Jerusalem are going the same way."

He is very proud of this saying. He has said it about four hundred times. Nobody else on the rafts thinks it is as wise as he does.

At Joppa, the rafts are taken apart, and the logs are dragged ashore, and the men from Israel take over. And here is something remarkable that the First Book of Kings tells us.

Solomon raised a levy of workers from all Israel, thirty thousand men. And he did not send them all to Lebanon at once. He sent them in turns. Ten thousand a month. Each man spent one month in Lebanon, cutting and hauling with the men of Tyre, and then two months at home. One month away, two months home. Over and over, for years.

I like this very much. It is such a sensible arrangement. Somebody, perhaps the man in charge of the levy, whose name was Adoniram, sat down and thought, these men have families, and fields, and roofs that leak, and children who need to be reminded what their fathers look like. A month away is long enough.

The second thing you need for a temple is stone.

And here we come to the verse I have been looking forward to all week. It is in the sixth chapter of the First Book of Kings, and it goes like this, in a plain rendering.

"The house, while it was being built, was built of stone made ready at the quarry. And there was neither hammer, nor axe, nor any tool of iron, heard in the house while it was being built."

Think about that. A great building site, for seven years, in the middle of a city. And it was quiet.

All the noise happened somewhere else. Somewhere out in the hills, at the quarries, the great stones were cut and shaped and dressed and measured, and then brought to the hill and set in place, one on top of another, already finished. On the hill itself, there was only the sound of ropes, and feet, and men saying "left a bit". Nobody chipped. Nobody hammered. The house of God went up in silence.

The Bible does not explain why. It just says that it was so. People have wondered about it for three thousand years. Perhaps it was because the altar of the Lord, in the law of Moses, was not to be cut with iron tools, and Solomon wanted the same reverence for the whole house. Perhaps it was simply to keep the holy place quiet. The book does not say, and I will not pretend that it does. But I think it is one of the most beautiful details in all of Scripture.

So let us go out to the quarry. Let us go and find the noise.

There is a man in the hill country called Yoash. He is our own invention too. He is a stonecutter, one of the eighty thousand hewers that the First Book of Kings says Solomon had working in the hills. Eighty thousand. That is a lot of hewers. If they had all hammered at once, I imagine you could have heard it in Egypt.

Yoash has a daughter called Tirzah, who is seven years old, and who brings him his lunch every day in a basket. Bread, and cheese, and olives, and, on good days, figs.

The quarry is a great white wound in the side of a hill, all ledges and steps and dust. The stone is limestone, pale and creamy, soft enough to cut when it first comes out of the ground and hard enough, once it has dried in the air, to last for a very long time. The men cut along lines. They drive wooden wedges into grooves and soak them with water until the wood swells and the stone cracks. They square the blocks with chisels and picks. They measure, and measure again. And it is loud. It is very, very loud.

"Why do we make all the noise here?" Tirzah asks one day, sitting on a block, swinging her feet, with her hands over her ears.

"So that there is no noise there," says Yoash, and he points with his chisel, east, towards Jerusalem, which you cannot see from the quarry.

"Why can't there be noise there?"

Yoash thinks about this. He is not a priest. He is not a learned man. He cuts stone.

"You know when your grandmother is asleep," he says at last, "and we all go round the house in our bare feet, and talk in whispers?"

"Yes."

"It is a bit like that. Except the other way round. It is not that God is asleep. It is that we are making a house for him, and you do not hammer in someone's house when they are there. You get everything ready outside, and then you bring it in quietly."

Tirzah thinks about this. "But he isn't there yet."

"No," says Yoash. "Not yet."

"So how will we know when he comes?"

Yoash does not have an answer to that. He gives her a fig instead, which, in my experience, is what fathers do when they do not have an answer, and it works more often than you would think.

Every block that Yoash cuts is numbered, in a way. Not with numbers, perhaps, but with marks, and lists, and the memory of the men in charge. Each stone has a place waiting for it on the hill. It is cut to fit that place, and no other. And when it is carried away, on rollers and sledges and the backs of a great many patient men, Yoash never sees where it goes.

That is what I think about, when I think about Yoash. He cut stone for seven years for a house he had never seen. He never heard the stones being set. He never saw them in their places. He only knew that they had gone, and that they had been needed, and that somewhere, quietly, they fitted.

There are a great many people in the world like Yoash. People who make the noisy, dusty part of something good, and never see the finished thing. Nurses on night shifts. Mothers at three in the morning. People who stack chairs after the party. I think the verse about the hammer is for them as well.

Now, what did the house look like when it was finished?

You might be imagining something enormous. A great cathedral, with towers and domes. But Solomon's Temple was not, by our standards, very large. The First Book of Kings gives its measurements in cubits, and a cubit is the length from your elbow to the tip of your middle finger, about half a metre, or a foot and a half. The house was sixty cubits long, twenty cubits wide, and thirty cubits high. That is about thirty metres long, ten metres wide and fifteen metres high. Roughly the size of a good stone parish church in a country village.

It was not built for crowds to go inside. The crowds stood in the courtyards around it. The house itself was for the priests, and the innermost room was for nobody at all, except the high priest, once a year.

At the front there was a porch. Then a long room, the holy place, panelled from floor to ceiling in cedar, so that, the Bible says, no stone was seen. The cedar was carved with gourds and open flowers. The floor was of cypress. And everything, walls and floor and ceiling, was overlaid with gold.

And at the far end, behind doors of olive wood, there was a smaller room, a perfect cube, twenty cubits long and twenty wide and twenty high. This was the inner sanctuary, the holy of holies, where the ark was to rest. And in it Solomon set two great cherubim, carved from olive wood and covered in gold, each one ten cubits high. Each cherub had two wings, and each wing was five cubits long. And they stood side by side, with their wings spread wide, so that one wing of the first cherub touched one wall, and one wing of the second touched the other wall, and in the very middle of the room, their other wings touched each other.

I want you to picture that. A room of gold, in the dark. No window. Two great figures standing with their wings outstretched, wall to wall, wingtip to wingtip, like a pair of arms opened wide, waiting.

The cherubim, I should say, are not the plump little babies with wings that you see on Christmas cards. In the Bible, cherubim are tremendous, mysterious beings who stand guard at holy places. They guarded the way back into Eden with a flaming sword. They are angels of a very high order, and they are nothing like a cherub on a greetings card at all. So if you are picturing a pair of chubby toddlers, please picture something much taller and more solemn.

All the walls of the house, the Bible says, were carved with cherubim and palm trees and open flowers, in the inner room and the outer. So that walking into it must have been a little like walking back into a garden. A garden of gold and cedar, with angels standing among the palms. Which, if you remember how the Bible begins, in a garden, with God walking in it in the cool of the day, is perhaps not an accident.

Then there was the bronze.

For the bronze work, Solomon sent to Tyre for another man called Hiram. Not the king. A different Hiram. Which must have caused a great deal of confusion in the letters. The Bible tells us that this Hiram was the son of a widow from one of the tribes of Israel, and that his father was a man of Tyre, a worker in bronze. So he was half Israelite and half Tyrian, a man from two peoples, and he was, the book says, full of wisdom and understanding and skill for every kind of work in bronze. The word used for his wisdom is the same word used for Solomon's. Which I find rather lovely. The king had wisdom for judging, and the metalworker had wisdom for bronze, and the Bible does not seem to think one sort was more holy than the other.

Hiram cast two great bronze pillars for the porch, each eighteen cubits high, with tops shaped like lilies and hung with chains and pomegranates. And he gave them names, which is not a thing people usually do to pillars. The one on the right he called Jachin, which means something like "he will establish". And the one on the left he called Boaz, which means something like "in him is strength". So anyone walking up to the house walked between two bronze sentences.

And he made the great sea.

The sea was a basin. An enormous round basin of cast bronze, ten cubits across from rim to rim, five cubits high, and thirty cubits round. Its rim was curved outward like the edge of a cup, or like a lily flower. It held, the First Book of Kings says, two thousand baths of water, and the Second Book of Chronicles says three thousand, which is a great deal either way. It was for the priests to wash in. And it stood on the backs of twelve bronze oxen. Three facing north, three facing west, three facing south and three facing east, with all their tails pointing inwards, to the middle.

And I want you to think about how you make a thing like that. A single piece of bronze, five metres across. You cannot cast it in a workshop. You need a great mould in the ground. So Hiram took his work out of the city, down into the Jordan valley, to a place between Succoth and Zarethan, where the clay was good, and he cast the bronze there, in the clay ground. Imagine the furnaces. The smoke. The glowing river of metal pouring into a hole in the earth. The men standing back with their arms over their faces. And then the long, anxious wait for it to cool, and the digging out, and the moment of seeing whether it had come out whole.

Then he had to bring it up to Jerusalem. The Bible does not tell us how. Somebody must have had a very difficult week.

There was so much bronze in the end, the book says, that Solomon did not weigh it. It could not be weighed. Which I think is the ancient equivalent of the builder saying, "Don't ask."

It took seven years.

The foundation was laid in the fourth year of Solomon's reign, in the month of flowers, in the spring. And the house was finished in the eleventh year, in the month called Bul, the eighth month, in the autumn. Seven years and a bit, of cedar rafts and quiet stones and bronze pouring in the valley.

In the very next verse after the one that tells us it took seven years, the book mentions that Solomon spent thirteen years building his own palace. I am not going to say anything about this. I am just going to leave it there, and let you think about it.

When the house was finished, it was not dedicated at once. There was a wait. Perhaps they were waiting for the right feast. Perhaps there was a great deal of polishing to do. Whatever the reason, the dedication came in the seventh month of the year, the month called Ethanim, in the autumn, at the time of the great feast, when the harvest was in and the people of Israel came up to Jerusalem anyway.

And so, from every part of the country, they came.

Yoash came. Of course he did. I would not have brought him all this way otherwise.

He came with his wife and his old mother and Tirzah, who was by now nine, walking up out of the hills along the road the stones had taken. They slept on the way under an olive tree. They saw, as they got near the city, more people than Tirzah had known there were in the world. People from the far north, from up by the road to Hamath. People from the far south, from down by the Brook of Egypt, where the desert begins. Farmers and shepherds and fishermen and potters, and the elders of every tribe, and the heads of every family, all crowding up towards Jerusalem with their animals and their children and their bundles of food.

And somewhere in the crowd, I like to think, was a short, brown man with a salty beard, who had come all the way down from Tyre to see what his logs had become, and was finding, to his great discomfort, that the ground was not moving.

I like to think that Abdi and Yoash met. That they found themselves standing next to each other, two working men in a crowd, and got talking, as people do.

"Wood," says Abdi, pointing at himself.

"Stone," says Yoash, pointing at himself.

And they look at each other, and laugh, and shake hands. And Tirzah, standing between them, looks up at both of them and says, "Is God here yet?"

And neither of them knows what to say.

The first thing that happened was the bringing of the ark.

All this time, the ark of the covenant had been in the tent that David had pitched for it, down in the old city of David, on the hill of Zion. Now it was to be carried up to its new house.

The priests lifted it by its long poles, onto their shoulders, as the law of Moses said it was to be carried. And they brought it up, with the old tent of meeting, and all the holy things that had been in the tent. And King Solomon and the whole assembly of Israel went in front of the ark, and as they went they sacrificed sheep and oxen, so many, the book says, that they could not be counted or numbered.

They brought it up the hill. Into the courtyard. Up the steps. Between the bronze pillars, between Jachin and Boaz, he will establish and in him is strength. Through the porch. Down the long golden room, past the cedar carved with flowers. Through the olive-wood doors. And into the dark gold room at the end. And they set it down under the wings of the cherubim, so that the great outstretched wings spread over it and its poles, like a covering.

And here the First Book of Kings gives us a funny little detail, the sort of detail that you only put in if you were really there, or knew someone who was. The poles of the ark were so long that their ends could be seen from the holy place, the long room in front of the sanctuary. They stuck out a little, through the doorway. But they could not be seen from outside the house. And the writer adds, "and they are there to this day."

I love this. Somebody, years later, had stood in that long golden room and seen the ends of two old wooden poles poking out of the inner doorway, and thought, that is worth writing down.

And then the book says something quieter. "There was nothing in the ark except the two tablets of stone, which Moses put there at Horeb, when the Lord made a covenant with the people of Israel, when they came out of the land of Egypt."

That is all. Two flat stones, with words on them. Nothing else. Not gold. Not treasure. Just the words of the covenant, the promise between God and his people, written in stone, and carried through the desert for forty years, and kept in a tent for a few hundred more, and now set down, at last, in the middle of the most beautiful building anyone in Israel had ever seen. Which is, I think, exactly where they belonged. The most precious thing in the house was a promise.

And then the priests came out of the holy place.

And the Second Book of Chronicles, which loves music, tells us what happened next, and it is the moment from the beginning of the story.

The Levites who were singers, Asaph and Heman and Jeduthun and their sons and their brothers, stood at the east end of the altar, dressed in fine white linen, with cymbals and harps and lyres. With them were a hundred and twenty priests, blowing trumpets. And the book says that it was the duty of the trumpeters and the singers to make themselves heard as one sound, in praise and thanksgiving to the Lord.

As one sound.

If you have ever been in a choir, you will know how hard that is. Somebody always comes in a fraction early. Somebody's trumpet is a little sharp. But on that day, the book says, it happened. The trumpets and the cymbals and the instruments and the voices all lifted up together, and they sang the line that the people of Israel had sung for generations, and would sing for generations more.

"For he is good. For his steadfast love endures for ever."

And at that moment, the house, the house of the Lord, was filled with a cloud. So that the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud. For the glory of the Lord filled the house of God.

In the crowd, Tirzah tugs at her father's sleeve. She cannot see much. She is nine, and everyone is taller than her. But she can see the top of the house, gold against the blue autumn sky. And she can see that something has changed. The priests at the door are not moving. Nobody is moving. The singing has stopped. And in the courtyard, thousands and thousands of people are quieter than she has ever heard thousands of people be.

She does not ask her question again. She does not need to.

Yoash picks her up and puts her on his shoulders, so she can see over the heads. And she sees the great doors of the house, and the priests standing still in front of them, and through the doors, something like a mist, a soft brightness, a cloud that is somehow both light and dark, filling the place where the golden room is.

And Yoash, who has never seen any of the stones he cut set in their places, and never will, stands in the crowd with his daughter on his shoulders and finds, to his surprise, that he is crying.

And then the king spoke.

The First Book of Kings tells us his first words, and they are very short, and very strange. Solomon looked at the cloud filling the house, and said, "The Lord has said that he would dwell in thick darkness."

He was remembering, I think, Mount Sinai. When Moses went up the mountain to meet God, he went into a thick darkness, a cloud on the mountain. And in the desert, the cloud had led the people, and rested on the tent of meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the tent. So when Solomon saw the cloud in the Temple, he knew what it was. It was the same cloud. The God of Sinai, the God of the desert, the God of the tent, had come into the house.

Then Solomon turned round, to face the people. And he blessed them, all of them, standing there in the courtyard. And he told them what we have already heard. That his father David had wanted to build a house for the name of the Lord, and that God had said, your son will build it. "And now," said Solomon, "the Lord has kept his promise. I have risen in the place of David my father, and I sit on the throne of Israel, as the Lord promised, and I have built the house for the name of the Lord, the God of Israel. And there I have made a place for the ark, in which is the covenant of the Lord."

And then he did something that kings in the ancient world did not usually do.

The Second Book of Chronicles tells us that Solomon had made a little platform of bronze, five cubits long and five cubits wide and three cubits high, and set it in the middle of the courtyard, so that everyone could see him. He stood on it. And then, in front of the whole assembly of Israel, he knelt down. The king knelt. On a bronze platform, in the middle of the crowd, where everyone could see. And he spread out his hands towards heaven, and prayed.

Solomon's prayer is long. It fills most of the eighth chapter of the First Book of Kings, and most of the sixth chapter of the Second Book of Chronicles. I will not read you all of it. But I want to give you the heart of it, in a plain rendering, because it is one of the great prayers of the Bible, and because I think it is, quite possibly, the best thing anyone has ever said at the opening of a building.

He began by praising God. "Lord, God of Israel, there is no God like you, in heaven above or on earth beneath, keeping covenant and steadfast love with your servants, who walk before you with all their heart."

And then he said the thing that made everyone turn and look at him. The thing Egeria remembered, sixteen hundred years ago, standing in her own crowd in Jerusalem.

"But will God really live on the earth? Look. Heaven, and the highest heaven, cannot contain you. How much less this house that I have built."

Think about that. He has spent seven years and a mountain of gold and a forest of cedar on this house. It is the most magnificent thing his people have ever made. The cloud of God's glory has just filled it, in front of everyone. And the very first thing he says to God, at the top of his voice, is: of course this house cannot hold you. Nothing can. The whole sky cannot hold you. This is a very small house.

And yet. And yet, he goes on, look kindly on this house. "Let your eyes be open towards this house, night and day, towards the place of which you said, my name shall be there. And listen to the prayer that your servant prays towards this place. And listen to the prayers of your people Israel, when they pray towards this place. Hear in heaven, your dwelling place. And when you hear, forgive."

When you hear, forgive. That is the refrain of the whole prayer. Solomon says it again and again, in different words. Hear in heaven, and forgive.

And then he goes through a list. And it is such a human list. It is a list of all the things that can go wrong.

When someone wrongs their neighbour, and has to come and swear an oath at the altar, hear in heaven, and judge between them, and do what is right.

When your people are beaten by an enemy, because they have sinned against you, and they turn back to you, and pray in this house, hear in heaven, and forgive.

When the sky is shut up, and there is no rain, and they pray towards this place, and turn from their sin, hear in heaven, and forgive, and teach them the good way, and send rain on your land.

When there is famine, or plague, or blight, or mildew, or locusts, or caterpillars, or any sickness at all, whatever prayer any one of your people makes, each knowing the sorrow of their own heart, and stretching out their hands towards this house, hear in heaven, and forgive, and act. Because you, you only, know the hearts of all the children of men.

And then, in the middle of the list, there comes a part I did not expect, the first time I read it. Solomon prays for foreigners.

"And also the foreigner," he says, "who is not of your people Israel, who comes from a far country because of your name, because they will hear of your great name, and your mighty hand, and your outstretched arm, when they come and pray towards this house, hear in heaven, your dwelling place, and do whatever the foreigner asks of you. So that all the peoples of the earth may know your name."

I thought of Abdi, when I read that. The short brown sailor from Tyre, who was not one of the people of Israel, standing in the crowd with the salt in his beard. And I thought of every pilgrim who has ever walked a long way to stand in a holy place in a country that was not their own. I thought of Egeria, who came from the far west of the world. Solomon, on the first day, before any of them had come, had already prayed for them.

And then, near the end, the most honest part of all.

"If they sin against you," says Solomon, "for there is no one who does not sin, and you are angry with them, and give them to an enemy, so that they are carried away captive to a land far off, and then in that land they come to their senses, and turn back, and pray to you, saying, we have sinned, we have done wrong, we have acted wickedly, and they turn back to you with all their heart, and pray towards their land, and the city you chose, and the house I have built for your name, then hear in heaven their prayer, and forgive your people."

For there is no one who does not sin.

Solomon says it in the middle of the happiest day of his life. In the middle of the crowd, in the middle of the music, with the cloud of glory in the house. He does not pretend. He knows that he will sin, and his people will sin, and that one day things will go wrong, very wrong. And he does not ask God to make sure that never happens. He asks God to listen when they come back.

I think this is why the prayer is still read when churches are dedicated, three thousand years later. Because it is not a prayer for a perfect people in a perfect building. It is a prayer for people like us. People who will get things wrong, and come back, and need to be heard, and forgiven.

And when Solomon had finished praying, the Second Book of Chronicles tells us, fire came down from heaven and consumed the burnt offering and the sacrifices. And the glory of the Lord filled the house. So that the priests could not go in.

And all the people of Israel, seeing the fire come down, and the glory of the Lord on the house, bowed down with their faces to the ground, on the stone pavement, and worshipped, and gave thanks to the Lord. And they said the line again. All of them, this time. Not the trained singers. Everyone.

"For he is good. For his steadfast love endures for ever."

And then Solomon stood up from his knees, on his little bronze platform, and blessed the whole assembly once more, in a loud voice. And he said, "Blessed be the Lord, who has given rest to his people Israel, as he promised. Not one word has failed of all his good promises, which he spoke through Moses his servant." And he prayed that God would be with them, as he was with their fathers, and never leave them, and turn their hearts towards him. "So that all the peoples of the earth may know that the Lord is God. There is no other."

In the crowd, Tirzah is still on her father's shoulders. Her face is pressed into his hair. She has her eyes shut, because everyone else has their faces on the ground, and she cannot get down, so she has done the next best thing.

"Abba," she whispers. "Is he here now?"

And Yoash, with his face against the warm stones of the pavement, stones that someone else cut, in some other quarry, says, "Yes. I think he is."

"In the gold room?"

Yoash thinks about what the king said. Heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you. He is not a learned man. He cuts stone.

"In the gold room," he says. "And everywhere else as well. But he is letting us find him here. Which is kind of him. Since we went to all this trouble."

And Tirzah, satisfied, falls asleep on his shoulders, in the middle of the most important day in the history of her people. Which, if you are listening to this in bed, and your eyes are getting heavy, you are very welcome to do as well. I will keep telling the story, quietly, and you can catch up another night.

There was a great feast afterwards. The book tells us of so many sacrifices that the bronze altar was too small to hold them all, and Solomon had to set apart the middle of the courtyard as well. Twenty-two thousand oxen, the First Book of Kings says, and a hundred and twenty thousand sheep. Which, since the peace offerings were eaten by the people who offered them, means that there was a very great deal of roast meat in Jerusalem that week, and a very great many happy families eating it.

They kept the feast for seven days. And then they kept the feast of the autumn for seven days more, so that it was fourteen days in all, and on the day after that, there was a solemn assembly. And then, the Bible says, Solomon sent the people away to their homes.

And they went, the book says, blessing the king. Joyful and glad of heart, for all the goodness that the Lord had shown to David his servant, and to Solomon, and to Israel his people.

So picture the roads out of Jerusalem, at the end of the autumn feast. People going north, towards Hamath. People going south, towards the desert. Shepherds and farmers and potters and fishermen. Tired children asleep on donkeys. Old people walking slowly, and talking about what they had seen, and telling each other, again, about the cloud. And a stonecutter from the hill country, walking home along the road the stones had come by, with his old mother on a donkey and his daughter on his shoulders, singing quietly, to himself, the only line of the song he could remember. He is good. His love lasts for ever.

And somewhere on the road to Joppa, a short brown sailor, very glad to be going back to the sea, where the ground keeps properly still by moving all the time.

And then, one more thing happened. And it happened at night.

After all the feasting was done, and the people had gone home, and Solomon had finished everything he had wanted to do, the Lord appeared to Solomon a second time, as he had appeared to him long ago at Gibeon. In the night. In a dream, perhaps. The Bible says only that he appeared to him by night.

And the Lord said, "I have heard your prayer. I have heard the plea you made before me. I have consecrated this house which you have built, and put my name there for ever. My eyes and my heart will be there for all time."

And then the Lord said something that the Second Book of Chronicles remembers, and that people have prayed with ever since, in hard times. It is God's answer to the long list of things that can go wrong.

"When I shut up the sky so that there is no rain, or send the locust, or plague among my people, if my people who are called by my name will humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and heal their land. Now my eyes will be open, and my ears attentive, to the prayer that is made in this place."

There was a warning too. The Lord told Solomon that if he, or his children, turned away and went after other gods, then even this beautiful house would become a heap of ruins, and people passing by would ask why. That was part of the answer. God does not hide it. But the promise comes first, and it is the promise that people have remembered.

And now we slow down. The story is told, and what is left is the long afterwards.

Solomon reigned for forty years. He became famous for his wisdom, far beyond Israel, so that a queen came all the way from Sheba to ask him hard questions, which is a story for another night. He wrote proverbs, the Bible says, three thousand of them, and songs, more than a thousand, and he talked about trees, from the cedar of Lebanon to the little plant that grows out of a wall, and about animals and birds and creeping things and fish.

I have to tell you, honestly, that Solomon's story does not end as brightly as it began. The First Book of Kings tells us that when he was old, he loved many foreign wives, and they turned his heart away after their gods, so that his heart was not wholly true to the Lord, as his father David's had been. It is a sad chapter. The wise young king who asked for an understanding heart did not keep it whole to the end. The Bible does not hide that, and I will not hide it either. But it does make me think of his own prayer. There is no one who does not sin. He knew that, on the best day of his life. I hope, and the Church leaves it to God, that he remembered the rest of what he prayed. Hear in heaven. And when you hear, forgive.

The house stood for nearly four hundred years. It was repaired, and neglected, and repaired again. And then, about five hundred and eighty-seven years before Christ, the armies of Babylon came, and the city fell, and the Temple was burnt, and the bronze pillars and the great sea were broken up and carried away. Just as the warning had said. And just as the prayer had said, the people were taken captive to a land far off, and there, many of them came to their senses, and turned back, and prayed towards Jerusalem. And seventy years or so later, they came home, and built the house again, smaller and plainer, and the old people who remembered the first house wept when they saw the new one.

What happened to the ark, Scripture does not tell us plainly. The Second Book of Maccabees has a story, which it says it found in old records, that the prophet Jeremiah, before the city fell, took the tent and the ark and the altar of incense out to the mountain where Moses had gone up to see the promised land, and hid them in a cave, and sealed the entrance. And when some of his companions tried to mark the way, they could not find it again. And Jeremiah told them that the place would stay unknown until God gathers his people together again, and shows his mercy. And then, the book says, the glory of the Lord and the cloud would appear, as they did in the time of Moses, and as when Solomon asked that the place should be specially consecrated.

So somewhere, according to that story, on a mountain in the east, in a sealed cave, two old poles and a golden chest and two flat stones are waiting, very quietly, in the dark, under no cherubim at all. I find I do not mind that very much. The promise was never really in the box.

About a thousand years after Solomon, when the Temple had been rebuilt and rebuilt again, a teacher from Galilee said to a crowd, "Something greater than Solomon is here." And on a hillside, he told people not to worry about what they would wear. "Look at the lilies of the field. They don't work, or spin. And yet even Solomon in all his glory was not dressed like one of these."

And later still, Saint Stephen, the first martyr, standing before the council, said, "Solomon built a house for him. But the Most High does not live in houses made by hands." Which is exactly, if you think about it, what Solomon himself had said. Heaven cannot contain you. How much less this house.

And at the very end of the Bible, in the vision of the new Jerusalem, the city that comes down from heaven, Saint John says that he saw no temple in it at all. Because its temple is the Lord God himself, and the Lamb. No house needed, any more. Everything has become the house.

And in between, in every town and village of the world, Christians build churches. Small ones and great ones. Wooden ones and stone ones. And when a church is finished, they dedicate it. They bring the people, and the bishop, and they pray. And all those prayers look back, in one way or another, to the day on the hill in Jerusalem, when a young king knelt down in front of everyone and said, this house cannot hold you. But please. Listen to us here. And forgive.

And every one of those churches has its Yoash. Someone who cut the stone, or laid the bricks, or carried the timber, or swept up afterwards, and went home, and was not mentioned in any book.

I hope they were there on the day. I hope somebody put them on their shoulders, so they could see.

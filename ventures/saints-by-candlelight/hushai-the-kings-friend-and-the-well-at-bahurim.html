Hushai the King's Friend and the Well at Bahurim

When Absalom seizes Jerusalem and King David flees barefoot over the Mount of Olives, David's old friend Hushai the Archite meets him at the top with his coat torn and is sent straight back into the city, to stand in Absalom's council and talk down the cleverest adviser in Israel; then two young messengers hide in a well under a heap of grain at Bahurim, and the king crosses the Jordan before dawn. Told from the Second Book of Samuel, chapters fifteen to seventeen.

And when two frightened young men she had perhaps never seen before came running into her yard, with soldiers behind them, she hid them, and lied to the soldiers' faces, and did it so well that they went away.

She did not have to. Nobody would have blamed her if she had said, not here, keep running. She chose to be brave, in her own yard, with a cloth and a handful of grain.

She reminds me of Rahab of Jericho, who hid the two spies on her roof under stalks of flax, and sent the king's men off the wrong way. It is the same sort of courage. The quiet kind. The kind that happens in kitchens and courtyards, and that nobody writes songs about, but that kingdoms turn on.

When the soldiers had gone, the two young men climbed up out of the well. I imagine her lifting the cloth, and the grain sliding off, and two dusty faces blinking up at the sky. I imagine them saying thank you, and her saying, go on, go, before they come back.

And they ran. All the way down the road to the king.

It is a long road. From Jerusalem down to the Jordan is more than twenty miles, and it is downhill nearly all the way, more than three thousand feet of downhill, from the cool hills to the hot, low valley where the river runs down towards the Dead Sea, the lowest place on the face of the earth. It is a road through bare, brown, empty desert. Rock and scrub and silence. At night it would have been very dark, and very quiet, and full of stars.

And there, near the river, at the place the book calls the fords of the wilderness, they found King David and his people, camped for the night, as he had said they would be.

And they gave him the message. Get up, they said. Cross the water quickly. This is what Ahithophel has advised against you.

And David got up. And all the people with him got up. And they crossed the Jordan.

The book says that by the time the morning light came, there was not one of them left who had not crossed.

I like to picture that night crossing. The river is not very wide there, and in that season perhaps not very deep, but it is fast and brown and cold, and they had to get everybody over. The soldiers. The servants. The old people. The children, carried on shoulders. The donkeys, protesting the way donkeys do. All in the dark, by starlight, with the water pulling at their legs. And then on the far bank, wet and shivering, everybody counting heads. Is everyone here? Is everyone over?

And then the sky going grey in the east, over the hills of Gilead. And everyone was.

And there we will leave them. Quietly now.

Back in Jerusalem, Ahithophel saw that his advice had not been followed. And he understood, I think, much better than Absalom did, what it meant. He saddled his donkey, and went home to his own town of Giloh, and set his house in order. And there he died, by his own hand, and was buried in the tomb of his father.

It is one of the saddest sentences in the book. The cleverest man in Israel, who had always been right, could not bear to be wrong once. We will leave him there, gently, and leave him to God, who knows every heart.

Christian readers have often heard Ahithophel's voice in the psalms. There is a line in Psalm forty-one, even my close friend, in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me. And another in Psalm fifty-five, it was not an enemy who taunted me, but you, my companion, my familiar friend. We used to take sweet counsel together, and walk in the house of God with the crowd. Nobody can be sure those psalms were written about Ahithophel. But at the Last Supper, on the night before his Passion, Our Lord quoted that line from Psalm forty-one about another friend who had eaten his bread. And so Ahithophel, the counsellor who left his king, has become in Christian memory a kind of shadow of Judas, a long way ahead of him in time.

And David? He went on to a town called Mahanaim, on the far side of the river. And there, as we told a few nights ago, an old man called Barzillai came out to meet him, with two other friends, bringing beds and basins and wheat and barley and lentils and honey and cheese, because, they said, the people are hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness.

The rest of the story of Absalom's rebellion is a sad one, and we will tell it on another night. It ends in a battle in a forest, and two runners racing to bring the news, and a father weeping for his son. Absalom had his army from Dan to Beersheba, in the end, just as Hushai had promised him. And it was not enough.

And Hushai? After he sends the message to the priests, he simply disappears from the story. The book never mentions him again, except in that list in Chronicles, Hushai the Archite, the king's friend. There is a man called Baana son of Hushai among King Solomon's officers, years later, and some have wondered whether he was the old friend's son. It is possible. The name is not rare. We cannot be sure.

But I like to think that when it was all over, and David came back across the Jordan, and up the long road, and over the Mount of Olives, and down into his city again, there was an old man waiting for him somewhere near the top of the hill. Not in a torn coat this time. Just waiting. And that they did not need to say very much at all.

Because that is what a friend does. He goes where you send him. He does the hard thing. And then he waits for you to come home.

Mahanaim and Barzillai follow straight after, in verses twenty-four to twenty-nine. We told it straight, in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation.

Some things are uncertain. Nobody is quite sure where the fords of the wilderness were, or where Bahurim stood, though it was somewhere east of the Mount of Olives on the road down to the river. The words for the crushed grain on the cloth and the brook of water are unusual ones, and we gave their likely sense. Whether the king's friend was an official title is debated. The maidservant, the boy and the woman of Bahurim are unnamed in the book, and so they are unnamed here. Ahithophel's advice about the king's household is mentioned but not told, because this is a bedtime show.

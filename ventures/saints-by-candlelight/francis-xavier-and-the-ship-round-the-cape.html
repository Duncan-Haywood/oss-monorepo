Francis Xavier and the Ship Round the Cape

A proud young nobleman from Navarre who meant to be a canon in Pamplona shares a room in Paris with a shepherd and a limping old student, says yes in an evening when somebody else falls ill, and spends thirteen months on a ship to India washing his own shirts. The Paris years and the road east of Saint Francis Xavier, from his own letters.

Full script

It is the middle of the night, somewhere in the middle of the ocean, and the ship is rolling.

It rolls to the left, slowly, and everything on board that is not tied down slides to the left. Then it rolls to the right, slowly, and everything slides back. A tin cup. A shoe. A sleeping sailor who has not quite noticed. Above the deck, the great square sails are dark against the stars, and the wind hums in the ropes like somebody humming a tune they cannot quite remember.

Down in the waist of the ship, by a lantern, a boy of about twelve is sitting on a coil of rope, feeling very sorry for himself. He is the ship's boy. He has been sick over the side three times today, and he would like very much to go home.

And beside him, with his sleeves rolled up and a bucket at his feet, a thin man with a dark beard is wringing out a shirt. It is not his own shirt. It belongs to a soldier who has a fever and cannot wash it himself.

The boy watches him for a while. Then he says, "Father. Are you really a nobleman?"

"I'm afraid so," says the man, and hangs the shirt on a rope to dry. "But it wears off. Give it time."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the early life and the first great journey of Saint Francis Xavier, told mostly from his own letters, which are some of the most cheerful and urgent letters a saint ever wrote, and the ship's boy, and nearly everything anybody says out loud, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

If you have been listening to this show for the last few nights, you have already met two of the people in this story. We told you about Ignatius of Loyola, the soldier with the broken leg who gave away his sword. And we told you about Peter Faber, the shepherd boy from the mountains of Savoy who greeted the angel of every town he walked into. And both times, out of the corner of the story, there was a third young man. Handsome. Athletic. Ambitious. Not at all sure he wanted anything to do with either of them.

Tonight is his night.

He was born in a castle. That sounds grand, and it was, a little. The castle of Xavier stands on a low hill in Navarre, which is a small, green, hilly kingdom tucked into the corner where Spain meets France, at the western end of the Pyrenees mountains. It is still there. You can visit it. It has a square tower and thick stone walls, and from the top you can see fields, and a river, and on a clear day the blue line of the mountains to the north.

The family name was really de Jasso. Xavier was the name of the castle, which came from his mother's side, and over time the castle's name was the one that stuck to him, the way a nickname sticks. His father, Juan de Jasso, was a learned man, a doctor of law, and an important adviser to the king and queen of Navarre. His mother, María de Azpilcueta, was a lady of the old local nobility, devout and practical, and the castle had come to the family through her. Francis was the youngest of five children. He was born on the seventh of April, in fifteen hundred and six.

Now, here is the thing you need to know about Navarre in those years. It was about to stop being a kingdom.

When Francis was six years old, the armies of Ferdinand of Aragon, the great king of Spain, marched in and took it. The king and queen of Navarre fled north over the mountains. And the families who had been loyal to them, the Jasso family very much among them, found themselves on the losing side.

It went on for years. Francis's father died when the boy was nine. His two older brothers, Miguel and Juan, fought for the old royal family, more than once, and lost, more than once. And the new rulers, to make sure the castles of Navarre could never again be used against them, ordered their walls and towers to be pulled down. The castle of Xavier lost its outer walls and the tops of its towers. The family kept the house, but not the castle it used to be.

And here is a small twist that the storytellers love. One of the soldiers fighting on the other side, the Spanish side, against the French and the Navarrese, at the siege of Pamplona in fifteen twenty-one, was a young Basque gentleman called Inigo of Loyola, who got his leg broken by a cannonball. Francis was fifteen at the time, at home. He did not know Inigo existed. But one day he would share a bedroom with him.

So picture Francis at nineteen.

He is tall for his time, and strong, and quick. He is good at games, at running and jumping, and he knows he is good at them. He is clever, and he knows that too. He is cheerful and warm and very easy to like. And he has a plan.

The plan goes like this. The family has lost a great deal: land, money, standing. The brothers have tried to win it back with swords and it has not worked. So Francis will try another way. He will go to the best university in the world, which in fifteen twenty-five meant Paris. He will get his degrees. He will come home a learned man, and he will become a canon of the cathedral of Pamplona, which was a comfortable and respectable position in the Church, with an income and a fine seat in the choir and a great deal of standing in the town. And the family name of Xavier will be honoured again.

It was a perfectly sensible plan. Lots of younger sons of good families had exactly that plan. There was nothing especially holy about it, and nothing especially wicked either. It was a career.

So in the autumn of fifteen twenty-five, Francis rode north, over the mountains, and up through France, to Paris.

Paris in fifteen twenty-five was the great city of students. There were thousands of them, from every country in Europe, packed into the narrow streets on the left bank of the river Seine, in a part of the city that is still called the Latin Quarter, because Latin was what they all spoke to each other. They lived in colleges, which were something between a school, a boarding house and a small monastery. The days started very early, around four in the morning, with lectures in cold rooms, and went on until evening. The food was famously bad. The students were famously rowdy. There were fights between colleges, fights between nations, and a great many arguments about philosophy that went on long into the night, often in taverns.

Francis joined the College of Sainte-Barbe, which means Saint Barbara. And he was given a room to share with another new student. A small, serious, rather anxious young man from the mountains of Savoy, who had been a shepherd boy until he was ten, and had cried until his parents let him go to school.

Peter Faber.

You might think they would not get on. A nobleman from a castle and a shepherd from a mountain farm. But they did. They got on very well indeed. Peter was gentle and kind and very clever, and Francis was warm and funny and generous, and they became the best of friends. They studied together. They went through the long years of the course together. And four years later, when they were nearly at the end of the course, about to become Masters of Arts, with Francis hoping to start teaching philosophy himself, the two of them were still sharing a room.

Which is why, in the autumn of fifteen twenty-nine, when the college needed a place to put a new student, it put him in with them.

The new student was thirty-eight years old.

He was short, and he limped, and he was poor. He had arrived in Paris with almost nothing, begged for his living, and was starting his studies all over again, with boys half his age. He spoke Spanish with a Basque accent. He prayed a great deal. And he had a way of looking at you, kindly and steadily, as if he could see straight through all your plans to whatever you actually wanted underneath them.

His name was Inigo. Later, he would be better known as Ignatius.

Peter liked him at once. Peter was struggling with all sorts of worries and scruples, and Ignatius sat with him, and listened, and helped him, slowly, to find his peace. And in return Peter helped Ignatius with his Latin and his philosophy. That is Peter's story, and we have told it.

But Francis did not like him. Not at all.

Think about it from where Francis was standing. He was twenty-three. He was about to be a Master of Arts and a teacher, a young man on his way up. He had a plan, a good plan, and it involved meeting the right people, and being seen with the right people. And here in his room was this odd, limping, middle-aged beggar, who talked about God at every opportunity, and who, worse, had a habit of persuading clever young students to give away everything they owned. There had already been a scandal about it in Paris. Some people thought Ignatius was a dangerous influence. Francis rather agreed with them.

So Francis was polite. He was always polite. But he kept his distance. He laughed at Ignatius a bit, with his friends. He went on with his teaching and his plans.

And Ignatius, for his part, did not argue. He did not lecture. He did not push. He simply went on being kind. When Francis was short of money, which was often, because his family's money had mostly gone, Ignatius quietly helped him. When Francis began teaching and needed students for his lectures, Ignatius went out and found him some. He praised Francis's teaching. He was pleased when Francis did well. And every now and then, the old story says, when Francis was talking about his career and his plans and the fine things that were coming to him, Ignatius would say, quietly, a line from the Gospel.

"What does it profit a man, Francis, to gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?"

And Francis would laugh, and change the subject.

And then, a few weeks later, Ignatius would say it again.

I should say that this is the old story, as the early Jesuits told it. We do not have Francis's own account of how he changed his mind. But we know that he did. It took a long time. It took something like three or four years. Ignatius was not in a hurry. And somewhere in those years, something in Francis began to shift. Perhaps it was that question, lodged in his head like a splinter. Perhaps it was watching Peter, his best friend, become calmer and happier and more alive. Perhaps it was simply that it is very hard to go on disliking somebody who keeps being kind to you.

Whatever it was, by about fifteen thirty-three, the proud young nobleman from Navarre had come round.

There is a hill on the north side of Paris called Montmartre. In those days it was outside the city, a green hill with windmills and vineyards and a little chapel on the slope. And early on the morning of the fifteenth of August, fifteen thirty-four, the feast of the Assumption of Our Lady, seven young men climbed up to that chapel together.

There was Ignatius. There was Peter Faber, who had just been ordained a priest, the only priest among them, so he said the Mass. There was Francis. And there were four others, all students: two clever young Spaniards called Diego Laínez and Alfonso Salmerón, a fiery Spaniard called Nicolás Bobadilla, and a cheerful Portuguese called Simão Rodrigues.

And in that little chapel, at that Mass, the seven of them made vows. They promised to live in poverty. They promised to go to Jerusalem, if they could, and spend their lives there helping souls. And if they could not get to Jerusalem within a year, they promised to go to Rome instead, and put themselves at the disposal of the Pope, and go wherever he sent them.

Then they went down the hill and had a picnic by a spring. I like that detail very much. Seven young men who have just given their whole lives to God, sitting on the grass eating bread and drinking water from a spring and talking about the future. Nobody there had the faintest idea how big that future would be.

A few weeks later, when the university was on holiday, Francis made what Ignatius called the Spiritual Exercises. These were a set of prayers and meditations, about thirty days long, that Ignatius had worked out from his own experience. You go away somewhere quiet, and you pray through the life of Christ, day by day, with someone to guide you, and at the end you are supposed to know, a little better, what God is asking of you.

Francis threw himself into it with the same energy he had once put into running and jumping. He hardly ate. And there is a story, which his friend Simão Rodrigues wrote down many years later, about the walk the companions made two years after that, in the winter of fifteen thirty-six, all the way from Paris to Venice, over the Alps, on foot, in snow. Francis, the story goes, was still ashamed of how proud he had been of his athletic body. So before the journey he tied thin cords tightly around his arms and legs, as a private penance, and walked on them for days. The cords dug in. His legs swelled up. In the end he could not walk, and the others had to get a surgeon, who said there was nothing to be done. And then, the story says, after a night of prayer, the swelling went down, and the cords came loose, and Francis got up and walked on.

It is told as the old account tells it. And I will add, as the narrator, that this is not a thing to try at home. The young are often very hard on themselves when they first fall in love with God. The older saints tend to be gentler. Francis grew gentler too, as you will hear.

They never got to Jerusalem.

They waited in Venice for a ship to the Holy Land, and while they waited they worked in the city's hospitals, especially the one called the Incurables, where the people nobody else would look after were looked after. Francis emptied bedpans. He washed sores. He sat up at night with the dying. This was the young man who had wanted to be a canon of Pamplona with a fine seat in the choir. On the twenty-fourth of June, fifteen thirty-seven, he was ordained a priest in Venice, together with Ignatius and some of the others.

But that year, and the next, no pilgrim ship sailed. There was war at sea between Venice and the Turks, and the route to the Holy Land was closed. So, as they had promised at Montmartre, they went to Rome, and offered themselves to the Pope, Paul the Third. And the Pope, who was a shrewd old man, took one look at this little band of clever, poor, energetic priests and decided to keep them. In the autumn of fifteen forty he approved them as a new religious order. They called themselves the Company of Jesus. Most of the world now calls them the Jesuits.

But even before that, the requests had started coming in.

One of them came from the King of Portugal, John the Third. Portugal in those years had ships and trading posts all round the coasts of Africa and India and beyond, and the King was worried, quite rightly, that the people in his lands overseas were being badly served. Many of the Portuguese out there were living wild and greedy lives. There were people who had been baptised and then left with no one to teach them anything. The King wanted good priests. He had heard of this new group in Rome. Could he have six?

Ignatius is supposed to have said, more or less, that if he gave six to Portugal, there would be four left for the rest of the world. He could spare two.

So he chose two. Simão Rodrigues, the cheerful Portuguese, was one. And the other was Nicolás Bobadilla, the fiery Spaniard. Simão set off ahead by sea. Bobadilla was to follow, with the Portuguese ambassador, Pedro Mascarenhas, who was travelling back to Lisbon overland.

And then, just as the ambassador was about to leave, Bobadilla fell ill.

This next scene is one of the most famous in the history of the Jesuits, and I should tell you that it comes to us from the early accounts, not from Francis's own pen. But it rings true, and here it is.

It was March, fifteen forty. Ignatius was ill himself, in bed. The ambassador was leaving Rome the very next day. Bobadilla could not travel. And Francis, who was working as Ignatius's secretary at the time, came into the room.

And Ignatius looked up at him, and said, in Spanish, something like this.

"Francis. You know that two of us have to go to India, by order of His Holiness. Bobadilla can't. This is your job."

And Francis, the old account says, answered at once.

"Good. Here I am."

That is all. No long conversation. No asking for time to think. The young man who had once had such careful plans for his life, who had argued and resisted and kept his distance for years, now said yes in two short sentences, in an evening, to a journey to the other side of the world from which he would never come back.

The old story says he spent that evening mending his old cassock, and saying goodbye to his friends. He left a few small notes behind, including one giving his vote, in advance, for whoever would be chosen as head of the new order. He said he thought it should be Ignatius. And the next morning he rode out of Rome with the ambassador's party, taking almost nothing with him but a breviary and a few books.

He never saw Ignatius again. He never saw Peter Faber again either. But he never stopped writing to them.

It is a long way from Rome to Lisbon. Up through Italy, over the mountains into France, down through Spain, and across into Portugal. The ambassador's party took about three months. Francis, by all accounts, made himself useful the whole way. He looked after the servants and the grooms. He heard confessions. He made people laugh. By the time they reached Lisbon, the ambassador was his firm friend.

And in Lisbon there was a problem of a happy kind. The King and the court liked Francis and Simão so much, and the people of the city came to their preaching and their confessionals in such crowds, that the King did not want to let them go. Why send these two to India, when they were doing so much good here? In the end there was a compromise. Simão would stay in Portugal. Francis would go.

He was given two companions for the journey. One was an Italian priest called Paul of Camerino, a kind, simple, good-hearted man. The other was a young Portuguese called Francis Mansilhas, who was not yet a priest. Francis also carried letters from the Pope naming him his representative in the East, which was a grand title for a man who intended, as he told his friends, to wash his own shirts.

And on the seventh of April, fifteen forty-one, Francis's thirty-fifth birthday, a fleet of five ships sailed out of the river at Lisbon and turned south. Francis was on the biggest of them, the Santiago, which was carrying the new governor of Portuguese India, a nobleman called Martim Afonso de Sousa.

They would be at sea, on and off, for thirteen months.

Now let me tell you what a voyage to India was like in fifteen forty-one, because it is hard to imagine.

There was no Suez Canal, of course. To get from Portugal to India, you sailed south. All the way south. Down the whole length of Africa, which is very long, past the bulge of West Africa, across the equator, out in a great curve into the South Atlantic to catch the right winds, and then round the bottom of the continent, the Cape of Good Hope, where the two oceans meet and the seas are some of the roughest in the world. Then up the east coast of Africa. Then across the Indian Ocean, if the monsoon winds were blowing the right way. And if they were not, you waited. Sometimes for months.

The ships were wooden, and not very large. A big one might carry several hundred people: sailors, soldiers, merchants, officials, servants, a few women and children, and a great many animals, chickens and goats and pigs, for food. The people were packed in. The food was salted meat and hard ship's biscuit, which got weevils in it after a while. The water went green and smelly in the barrels. Near the equator, the ships could be becalmed for weeks, in a heat so thick that the pitch melted out of the seams of the deck. And then, as the months went by, came the sickness that every sailor dreaded, scurvy, which we now know is caused by having no fresh fruit or vegetables, and which made people weak and their gums swell and their old wounds open again. Many people died on every voyage. It was normal.

Into all of this, on board the Santiago, came Francis.

The governor offered him a cabin, and a servant, and a place at his own table. Francis was grateful and polite. And then, as far as the old biographers can tell, he gave most of it away. He spent his days and a good part of his nights below deck, among the sick and the seasick. He fed people who could not feed themselves. He sat with the dying. He taught the children their prayers, and played with them, and made them laugh. He heard confessions, and patched up quarrels, and sat on the deck in the evenings telling stories. And when the governor objected that it was not fitting for the Pope's own representative to be seen washing other people's clothes and his own, Francis is supposed to have said that he would rather set an example than be served.

And this is where, if you will allow me, I am going to tell you about the ship's boy.

We do not know his name. We do not even know that he existed. There were ship's boys on every Portuguese ship, young lads of eleven or twelve or thirteen who fetched and carried and climbed and were sick and homesick and frightened of the sea, and there must have been some on the Santiago. So tonight, for one of them, we are going to borrow a name. We will call him Tomé. And the conversations he has with Francis are ours, not history's. But they are the kind of conversations that the people on that ship remembered having with him.

We left Tomé at the beginning of the story, sitting on a coil of rope in the middle of the night, having been sick over the side three times, asking Francis if he was really a nobleman.

"But why would a nobleman wash a soldier's shirt?" says Tomé.

Francis sits down on the deck beside him. "Well," he says. "The soldier can't do it. He's ill. I can. That seems to be most of the reason."

"But you've got a cabin. The governor said."

"I have," says Francis. "It's a very nice cabin. There's a man down there with a broken leg who's enjoying it very much."

Tomé thinks about this.

"My mother said nobles never do anything for themselves," he says. "She said they have people for everything."

"Your mother is a wise woman," says Francis. "When I was your age, I had people for everything. I thought I'd have people for everything my whole life. I had a plan, you know. I was going to be a very important man. I was going to sit in a very comfortable chair in a cathedral and be bowed to."

"What happened?"

"A man with a limp kept asking me a question."

"What question?"

Francis tells him. And Tomé, who is twelve, thinks about it for a long time, while the ship rolls to the left and rolls to the right and the stars swing slowly back and forth above the mast.

"I don't think I've got a whole world," Tomé says at last. "I've just got a knife, and a shirt, and a little wooden horse my brother made."

"Then you are a long way ahead of where I was," says Francis. "You'll find it much easier." And he gets up, and picks up his bucket, and goes back to work.

The weeks went by. They crossed the equator. They curved out into the South Atlantic. The weather turned cold, and grey, and huge waves came marching up from the south, from somewhere near the ice at the bottom of the world. They rounded the Cape of Good Hope, which, Francis's companions noticed, he did not spend in his cabin either.

Imagine Tomé on one of those days, hanging on to a rope with both hands while the deck tips up like a roof, terrified, soaked through. And Francis beside him, also soaked through, also hanging on.

"Father," shouts Tomé over the wind. "Are you scared?"

"Yes!" Francis shouts back. And then, after a moment, "But I'm also in very good hands. And so are you."

"Whose hands?"

Francis points up, at the grey sky, and the grey sea, and the wild white water. "The same hands as all of this," he shouts. "They're big hands. They've been holding this ocean a long time. They're not going to drop one small boy."

I want to be careful here. The sea is the sea, and on those voyages people did die, and Francis knew it. He was not promising Tomé that nothing bad could ever happen. He was telling him something deeper, which is that whatever happens, we are not alone in it, and we are not dropped. And Tomé, hanging on to his rope, found that this helped, a little. And a little, on a day like that, is a great deal.

By late summer, the fleet had made it up the east coast of Africa to the island of Mozambique, a small, hot, low island just off the coast, where the Portuguese had a fort and a harbour. And there they stopped. The season was too late. The monsoon winds that would carry them across to India had turned against them. They would have to wait for them to turn back. So they waited. For about six months.

It was not a healthy place. The ships had arrived full of sick people, and the island was hot and damp, and fevers spread. Many people died there that winter. And Francis, with Paul and Mansilhas, worked in the little hospital day and night. He nursed the sick. He heard their confessions. He buried the dead. And eventually, perhaps not surprisingly, he caught the fever too, and was very ill for a time. In a letter he wrote from Mozambique to his friends in Rome, he mentions this almost in passing. And then he goes on cheerfully to other news, as if this were a minor inconvenience.

It is one of the lovely things about Francis's letters. He was always much more interested in what was happening to other people than in what was happening to him.

Imagine Tomé coming to the hospital one afternoon, with a little basket of fruit he has been sent with, and finding Francis lying on a straw mattress, grey in the face, with his eyes closed.

"Father?" says Tomé, very quietly.

Francis opens one eye. "Tomé," he says. "Is that fruit?"

"Yes, Father. Oranges. The captain sent them."

"Wonderful," says Francis. "Give them to the man in the corner. He needs them more than I do. No, don't argue. Go on." And he closes his eye again. And then, as Tomé is going, "And come back tomorrow and tell me what the wind is doing."

Tomé came back the next day, and the next. And slowly, the fever passed.

In the spring of fifteen forty-two, the winds turned at last, and the ships sailed north again. They stopped at Malindi, on the coast of what is now Kenya. And then at Socotra, a strange, dry, beautiful island at the mouth of the Red Sea, where Francis met people who called themselves Christians, and said that their faith had come to them from Saint Thomas the Apostle long ago. They had almost nothing left of it, he wrote. No books. Hardly any priests. A few prayers, and the sign of the cross, and a great love of the Apostle. Francis wanted to stay with them very badly. But the governor would not let him. India was waiting.

And on the sixth of May, fifteen forty-two, more than a year after leaving Lisbon, the Santiago sailed into the harbour of Goa, on the west coast of India.

Goa was the capital of the Portuguese in the East. A busy, hot, crowded, noisy city of churches and markets and warehouses, of ships from everywhere, of Portuguese soldiers and Indian merchants and Arab traders, of elephants and palm trees and rain that, when the monsoon came, fell like a waterfall. It must have been overwhelming.

Imagine Tomé standing at the rail as they come in, looking at it all with his mouth open.

"What are you going to do now, Father?" he says.

Francis looks at the city. "I don't know yet," he says. "Go to the hospital, probably. That's usually a good place to start. And find the children. Somebody always needs to teach the children."

"Will I see you again?"

"You'll be going back to Lisbon, I expect, when the ships go," says Francis. "And I will be going wherever I'm sent. So maybe not. But I'll pray for you every day. And you'll pray for me. And then we'll never be very far apart. That's how it works with friends. I've got friends in Rome and Paris that I may never see again in this life, and I talk to them every day."

And Tomé went down the gangplank with the other boys, and into the noise of the harbour, and out of our story. We like to think he made it home, and grew up, and told his own children about the priest on the ship who washed the soldiers' shirts. But that is our wish, not history.

Now Francis's real work began.

In Goa, he did exactly what he had told Tomé. He lived in the hospital, sleeping near the sick. He visited the prisons. And he walked through the streets of the city with a little hand bell, ringing it, and calling out for parents to send their children and their servants to church to learn their prayers. And the children came, in hundreds, partly because of the prayers, and partly, I suspect, because it is very hard for a child to resist a man walking down the street ringing a bell.

Then, after a few months, he went south, to the very tip of India, to a long, hot, sandy coast called the Fishery Coast, where the pearl fishers lived. These were the Paravas, a people who dived for pearls in the shallow sea, and who had been baptised some years before, in great numbers, but had then been left almost entirely alone, with no one who spoke their language to teach them anything at all.

Francis did not speak their language either. It was Tamil, one of the great old languages of India, and he never mastered it. So he did the next best thing. He found people who spoke both Tamil and Portuguese, and with their help, sentence by sentence, he put the Creed, the Our Father, the Hail Mary, the Ten Commandments and a few other prayers into Tamil. Then he learned them off by heart. And then he went from village to village, ringing his bell, and gathering the children, and saying the prayers to them, line by line, and they said them back, and then he told the children to go home and teach their parents. Which, being children, they did, with tremendous enthusiasm.

He slept on the ground. He ate rice and water. He baptised so many babies and children that, in a letter he wrote from the coast of India in January fifteen forty-four, he says, in our plain words, that his arms were often worn out with baptising, and he had lost his voice from saying the Creed and the commandments over and over in their language.

And in that same letter there is a passage that every Jesuit knows, and that a great many university students have heard read aloud to them, rather pointedly, over the centuries.

Francis is writing to his friends in Rome. And he is thinking about Paris. About the University. About the thousands of clever students in the Latin Quarter, arguing about philosophy in the taverns until two in the morning, the way he used to. And he says, again in our plain words, that he often feels like going back to the universities of Europe, and especially to Paris, and running through the streets shouting like a madman at all those learned men who have more learning than love. He wants to tell them how many people out here are waiting, how many children nobody is teaching, and to beg them to stop thinking only about their careers and their comfortable seats in cathedrals, and to come.

He does not say, a comfortable seat in a cathedral in Pamplona. But I think he might have been thinking it.

He never did go back to Paris. But the letters went back. They were copied and passed around and read aloud in colleges all over Europe, and young men read them and set off for India, and Africa, and Brazil, and Japan, because a priest they had never met had written about washing shirts and ringing bells and losing his voice from saying the Creed.

And he never stopped loving his friends. The old account says that he carried with him, folded up next to his heart, the signatures of his companions, cut out from the bottoms of the letters they had sent him, so that he would always have them near. When he wrote to Ignatius, he wrote as a son to a father, and sometimes, it is said, he knelt to read Ignatius's letters. And when Peter Faber died in Rome in fifteen forty-six, Francis did not hear the news for a long time, because letters took a year or more to reach him. When he did hear it, it is said, he began to ask Peter for his prayers, as one asks the saints.

Two boys sharing a room in a cold college in Paris. A shepherd and a nobleman. Neither of them ever saw the other again after Rome. But they never stopped being friends.

And now, slowly, the rest of his life.

He did not stay on the Fishery Coast. He could never stay anywhere long. There was always somewhere else, further on, where nobody had gone. In fifteen forty-five he sailed to Malacca, on the coast of what is now Malaysia, a great trading port. From there he went further east still, to the Spice Islands, the Moluccas, scattered across the sea in what is now Indonesia, islands of cloves and nutmeg and volcanoes and earthquakes. There is a lovely old legend from those years, that once, in a storm, the little crucifix he carried fell into the sea, and the next day, as he walked along the beach, a crab came up out of the water holding it in its claws, and gave it back to him. It is only a legend. But it is the kind of legend that grows up around a man whom everyone, crabs included, apparently wanted to help.

In Malacca, at the end of fifteen forty-seven, he met a Japanese man called Anjirō, who had come all that way to find him, and who told him about Japan. And so, in fifteen forty-nine, Francis went to Japan. He landed at Kagoshima, in the south, on the fifteenth of August, the feast of the Assumption, fifteen years to the day after the seven friends had climbed the hill of Montmartre. I don't think that was an accident. He spent two years there, and came to love the Japanese people very deeply. He said they were the best people he had yet met.

And then he wanted to go to China.

China in those days was closed to foreigners. Nobody was allowed in. But Francis thought that if he could only get in, if he could only talk to the scholars of China, then Japan and all of East Asia would listen. So in fifteen fifty-two he sailed to a small island called Shangchuan, just off the coast of southern China, where Portuguese traders were allowed to stop and do business, and he waited there for a Chinese smuggler who had promised to take him across to the mainland.

The smuggler never came.

And in the autumn, waiting on that island, in a little hut on the hillside looking across the water towards China, Francis fell ill with a fever. He was forty-six. He had a young Chinese companion with him called Antonio, who had grown up in Goa, and who stayed beside him to the end and later told what happened. Francis prayed, and spoke softly, sometimes in languages Antonio did not know, which may have been the Basque of his childhood in the castle of Xavier. And early in the morning of the third of December, fifteen fifty-two, Antonio put a lighted candle into his hand, and Francis went home to God.

He was looking towards China. He never got in. But about thirty years later, others did, following him.

His body was taken back to Goa, where it still rests, in a silver casket in a church called the Basilica of Bom Jesus. He was declared a saint in sixteen twenty-two, on the same day as his friend Ignatius. His feast is the third of December. He is the patron saint of missions, together with a young French Carmelite nun called Thérèse of Lisieux, who never left her convent, and who prayed for missionaries every day. I think he would have liked that pairing very much. The man who went everywhere, and the girl who went nowhere, holding up the same work from both ends.

And the castle of Xavier is still there, on its low hill in Navarre. The towers were restored long ago. Every year, in early March, thousands of people walk there from all over Navarre, on a pilgrimage they call the Javierada, to honour the boy who was born there.

It is evening there now. The fields are going dark, and the river is quiet, and up on the hill the old stone walls are holding the last of the light. Somewhere in that castle, once, there was a little boy who wanted to make his family proud, and who grew up with a plan to sit in a comfortable chair and be bowed to. And God, very patiently, through a shepherd and a limping man and a question from the Gospel, gave him something much better than a plan.

He gave him a whole world. And Francis gave it all away.

And what about you, tonight? You have probably made some plans today. Most of us do. Some of them will work out, and some of them will not, and that is all right. You can put them down now, for the night. You don't need to hold on to them. There are big hands holding the whole of the ocean, and they are not going to drop you.

So rest now. Let the ship roll gently to the left, and gently to the right. Let the stars swing slowly over the mast. You don't need to keep watch. Somebody else is on deck tonight.

That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source tonight is Francis Xavier's own letters. About a hundred and thirty of them survive, and there is a complete English translation by Joseph Costelloe. They are full of energy, affection and practical detail, and very much worth reading. The great life of Francis is the enormous four-volume biography by Georg Schurhammer, which tells you more about the sixteenth-century world than you would think possible. For the Paris years from Ignatius's side, his own Autobiography is the place to go, and for the first Jesuits as a whole, John O'Malley's book The First Jesuits.

What is history. His birth at Xavier in fifteen hundred and six, the conquest of Navarre and his family's losses, his years at Sainte-Barbe with Peter Faber and then Ignatius, his teaching, his slow change of heart, the vows at Montmartre in fifteen thirty-four, the walk to Venice, his ordination there in fifteen thirty-seven, his being sent in Bobadilla's place in fifteen forty, the stay in Lisbon, the voyage that began on his thirty-fifth birthday, the winter at Mozambique and his fever, Socotra, Goa, the bell, the Tamil prayers on the Fishery Coast, the letter about the universities, the later journeys, and his death on Shangchuan with Antonio beside him. The words of his letters are given in our own plain renderings.

What is the old accounts' story. Ignatius's question from the Gospel, the exact words on the evening he was sent, the mended cassock, the cords on his arms and legs, his washing his own clothes on board, the signatures carried by his heart, and his kneeling to read Ignatius's letters come from early Jesuit accounts and the first biographers, and may have grown a little in the telling. The crab is a legend, and we have labelled it so.

What we invented. Tomé the ship's boy, his little wooden horse, the oranges, and nearly every word anybody says out loud. We have also told the story of the Portuguese in the East only from where Francis stood. It was a mixed story, and Francis himself complained in his letters, often and sharply, about the greed and cruelty of some of his countrymen out there.

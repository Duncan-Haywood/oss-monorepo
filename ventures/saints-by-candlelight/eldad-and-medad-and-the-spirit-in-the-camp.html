Eldad and Medad and the Spirit in the Camp

The people in the wilderness are tired of manna and weeping for the cucumbers of Egypt, Moses tells the Lord he cannot carry them all by himself, and seventy elders are called to the tent to share his spirit; but two of them never left the camp, and when they begin to prophesy among the tents a young man runs to tell, Joshua says stop them, and Moses says the kindest thing in the chapter. Told from the Book of Numbers, chapter eleven.

What's true in this story

Who
Moses, Eldad and Medad, Joshua son of Nun, the young man who ran, the seventy elders of Israel, and the people in the camp
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Book of Numbers as Scripture; Moses is honoured as a saint and prophet in both traditions; Eldad and Medad have no feast day of their own in either that we know of
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Book of Numbers, chapter eleven, told straight in a plain rendering; the book gives no date in our years and historians disagree about when the Exodus happened, so none is given
Legend

It is the middle of a hot afternoon in a camp in the desert, and a young man is running.

He is running between the tents, as fast as he can go, which is not very fast, because the tents are close together and there are tent ropes everywhere, and children, and goats, and a woman with a basket who says something to him as he goes past that I will not repeat.

He does not stop. He has news. He runs right out of the camp, and across the open ground, to the place where a great crowd of old men are standing in a ring around a tent, and a cloud is resting over the tent, and there is a strange hush over everybody.

And he pushes his way to the front, all out of breath, and he finds Moses, and he says, "Eldad and Medad! They're prophesying! In the camp!"

And a younger man standing at Moses' side, a soldier, the sort of man who likes things done properly, turns to Moses at once and says, "My lord Moses. Stop them."

And Moses looks at him for a long moment.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the night the Lord shared the spirit of Moses with seventy elders, and two men who stayed at home, from the Book of Numbers, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and the family at the grinding stone, the boy who ran, and the thoughts of the two men in the camp are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Last time on Saints by Candlelight, we sat in the shade of a tent with an old man called Jethro, the priest of Midian, and watched his son-in-law Moses try to settle every quarrel of an entire nation, by himself, from dawn till dark. And Jethro said, very gently, what you are doing is not good. You will wear yourself out, and the people with you. And Moses listened, and chose able men to judge the small cases, and the queue got shorter, and everybody went home to supper.

Well. It would be lovely to tell you that this solved everything for ever.

It did not. Moses was given a lot of good help in his life, and he still had days when the whole thing landed on his shoulders at once. Tonight is one of those days. And tonight, the help comes not from a wise father-in-law, but from the Lord himself, in a way nobody expected, including, it turns out, two men called Eldad and Medad, who were sitting quietly in their tents and minding their own business.

Let me set the scene.

The people of Israel have been camped at the foot of the mountain of God, Mount Sinai, for nearly a year. The Book of Numbers is very precise about it: they set out from Sinai in the second year, in the second month, on the twentieth day of the month. That is the sort of detail the Book of Numbers loves. It is called Numbers, after all. There are whole chapters in it that are lists of tribes and how many men each tribe had, and which tribe camped on the east side, and which on the south, and who carried which bits of the tabernacle when they moved. I am not going to read you those chapters tonight. I want you to stay awake at least until the quails.

But it is worth knowing what the camp looked like, because it matters for our story.

In the middle was the tabernacle, the tent of meeting, where the Lord's presence dwelt among his people. Over it, the book says, there was a cloud by day, and at night the cloud looked like fire. When the cloud lifted, the people packed up and moved. When the cloud settled, they stopped and pitched their tents. Sometimes they stayed a couple of days, sometimes a month, sometimes longer. Nobody could plan anything. Imagine a family holiday where you don't know when you're leaving, or where you're going, or how long you'll stay when you get there, and there are perhaps two million of you. That is the wilderness.

Around the tabernacle camped the Levites, who looked after it. And around them, in a great square, camped the twelve tribes, three on each side, each with its own banner. It must have been an astonishing sight from the top of a hill. A city of tents, in a perfect pattern, in the middle of nowhere.

There is one more thing you need to know about the camp, and it is the most important thing of all. It is what they ate.

Every morning, when the dew lifted, there was something on the ground. Small, round, white, fine as frost. The first time the people saw it, back in the Book of Exodus, they said to one another, "What is it?" And in Hebrew, what is it is something like man hu. And so they called it manna. Which means, if you like, the food called what-is-it. I think that is one of the best names for a food ever given.

Moses told them, this is the bread the Lord has given you to eat. Gather as much as each of you needs. And they did, every morning, for forty years. It melted when the sun grew hot. It kept for one day only, except on the day before the Sabbath, when they gathered twice as much and it kept for two. If you tried to keep it longer, it went off, and the book says, with great frankness, that it bred worms and stank. I imagine you only tried that once.

So there they are. A whole people, fed every morning by the hand of God, with bread from heaven, in the middle of a desert where nothing grows.

And they are about to complain about it.

The trouble starts almost as soon as they leave Sinai.

Three days' march out from the mountain, the book says, the people complained, and it was evil in the ears of the Lord. It does not tell us what they complained about. Probably the walking. Three days of walking in the desert, after a year sitting still, would make anybody's feet complain. And the Lord was angry, and fire from the Lord burned at the edges of the camp.

And the people cried out to Moses, and Moses prayed to the Lord, and the fire died down.

That is how it so often goes in these books. The people grumble. The Lord is angry. Moses prays. And the Lord listens to Moses. Moses spends a great deal of the wilderness standing between the people and the consequences of their own grumbling, and you will notice that he never once says, well, you brought it on yourselves. He just prays.

They called that place Taberah, which means burning. They did not stay long.

And then, a little further on, came the cucumbers.

The book says it began with what it calls the mixed crowd among them. When the people left Egypt, the Book of Exodus tells us, a mixed crowd went up with them, people who were not of the twelve tribes but who had thrown in their lot with Israel and come along. And the craving started there, the book says, and then it spread to everybody. Which is exactly how a craving works, of course. It doesn't matter who starts it. Somebody at the next tent says, wouldn't it be nice to have a proper meal for once, and before you know it, the whole street is talking about it.

So let me take you to one tent, on one evening. This family is our own invention, but I suspect every family in the camp had a version of this conversation.

The mother is sitting at the grinding stone outside the tent, grinding manna. The book tells us they did this. They ground it in hand mills, or pounded it in a mortar, and boiled it in pots, and made it into cakes. And the cakes, the book says, tasted like cakes baked with oil. The Book of Exodus says it tasted like wafers made with honey. So it was, at least, a pleasant food. Not a punishment. Somewhere between a pancake and a shortbread biscuit, perhaps.

But it was the same food. Every morning. For a year.

"What's for supper?" says the youngest son, who knows perfectly well what's for supper.

"Manna," says his mother.

"What kind of manna?"

"Boiled manna," says his mother. "Yesterday was baked manna. Tomorrow will be manna cakes. The day after, I thought I might try pounding it, for a change."

"Could we have it with something?"

"With what?"

And the boy thinks very hard. And the boy has no idea, because he was very small when they left Egypt, and he cannot really remember anything else.

But his grandfather can. His grandfather is lying on a mat in the doorway of the tent, and he opens one eye, and he says, "Fish."

And that's how it starts.

Because the book tells us exactly what they said, and it is one of the most human speeches in the whole Bible. They wept, and they said, "If only we had meat to eat! We remember the fish we used to eat in Egypt, for nothing. And the cucumbers. And the melons. And the leeks, and the onions, and the garlic. But now our strength is dried up. There is nothing at all. Nothing but this manna, wherever we look."

I want you to notice a few things about that speech.

First, they remember the fish they ate in Egypt for nothing. For nothing! They were slaves in Egypt. They made bricks without straw. Their baby boys were thrown in the river. And now, a year later, out in the desert, what they remember is that the fish was free. Memory is a very strange thing. It lets go of the whip and holds on to the onions.

Second, it is a wonderfully specific list. Not just food. Cucumbers. Melons. Leeks. Onions. Garlic. You can almost smell it. If you have ever been away from home for a long time, you know this feeling. It is never food in general you miss. It is one particular thing. Your grandmother's soup. A certain kind of bread from a certain shop. The book has kept that list for three thousand years, and I think it kept it because it is true.

And third, they say there is nothing. Nothing at all. And outside every tent, on the ground, every single morning, there is bread from heaven.

That's the bit that hurts, really. Not that they wanted a change. Anybody would want a change. But that they looked at the daily miracle and called it nothing.

And the book says that Moses heard the people weeping, family by family, each at the door of his tent. All through the camp. A whole city of tents, and at every doorway, people crying for cucumbers.

And the Lord was very angry.

And Moses, for once, was very unhappy too.

Now comes a part of the story that I love, and it surprises people when they first read it.

Moses prays. But it is not a calm, holy, faraway sort of prayer. It is the prayer of a man at the very end of his rope. And the Bible gives it to us, every word, without tidying it up.

He says, "Why have you treated your servant so badly? Why have I not found favour in your sight, that you put the weight of this whole people on me? Did I conceive all these people? Did I give birth to them, that you should say to me, carry them in your arms, the way a nurse carries a baby, all the way to the land you promised to their fathers? Where am I supposed to get meat for all these people? They keep weeping at me and saying, give us meat to eat. I cannot carry all these people by myself. It is too heavy for me. If this is how you are going to treat me, then please, kill me now, if I have found any favour with you, and let me not see my own misery."

That is quite a prayer.

I want to say something about it, because it might worry you. Is Moses allowed to talk to God like that?

And the answer, from the whole of Scripture, seems to be yes. Not to stop trusting him, and not to turn away from him. But to tell him the truth. Moses does not go off and sulk. He does not pretend everything is fine. He goes straight to the Lord, and he says, this is too much for me. And the Psalms are full of prayers like that, and so is the Book of Job, and so is the prophet Jeremiah. The Lord would rather have an honest prayer than a tidy one.

And I want you to notice the picture Moses uses. A nurse, carrying a baby. A whole nation of grown men and women, and Moses feels as if he is carrying them on his hip, all the way across the desert, while they cry because they want their dinner. Anybody who has ever looked after a small child at the end of a long day knows exactly how Moses felt.

Did I give birth to them? he says. As if to say, they're yours, Lord, not mine.

Which is, of course, true. They are the Lord's people. And that is exactly where the Lord's answer begins.

The Lord does not tell Moses off. He does not say, how dare you. He says, in effect, you are right. You cannot carry them by yourself. So you won't.

He says, "Gather for me seventy men from the elders of Israel, men you know to be elders of the people and leaders over them. Bring them to the tent of meeting, and let them stand there with you. And I will come down and speak with you there. And I will take some of the spirit that is on you, and put it on them. And they will carry the weight of the people with you, so that you do not carry it by yourself alone."

Do you hear the echo? Last time, it was Jethro, saying you cannot do this alone, the thing is too heavy for you, let others help you carry it. Now it is the Lord himself saying it. Only this time the help is not just good organisation. It is the Lord's own spirit, the same spirit that was on Moses, shared out.

And then the Lord answers the other complaint too. The meat.

"Tell the people," he says, "make yourselves holy for tomorrow, and you will eat meat. You wept in my hearing and said, if only we had meat, we were better off in Egypt. So the Lord will give you meat, and you will eat it. Not one day. Not two days, not five, not ten, not twenty. A whole month. Until it comes out of your nose, and you are sick of it. Because you have rejected the Lord who is among you, and wept before him, and said, why did we ever leave Egypt?"

And that, I'm afraid, is a slightly alarming promise. If you've ever been told, you want sweets, fine, you can have the whole bag, you'll know the tone.

And Moses, being Moses, does the arithmetic.

"Lord," he says, "there are six hundred thousand men on foot here. And you say you'll give them meat for a whole month? If every flock and every herd were slaughtered, would it be enough? If every fish in the sea were caught for them, would it be enough?"

Which is a fair question. You have to admire a man who, in the middle of being promised a miracle, stops to check the numbers.

And the Lord says one short sentence back to him, which I think is one of the best sentences in the book.

"Is the Lord's arm too short?"

Is the Lord's arm too short. As if to say, you've seen what I can do. Who parted the sea? Who sends the bread every morning? Now you'll see whether my word comes true for you or not.

And Moses doesn't argue any more.

So Moses goes out, and tells the people what the Lord has said. And then he starts choosing.

The Lord said seventy, and he said they should be men Moses already knew to be elders and leaders. Not strangers. Not whoever put their hand up first. Men he had seen at work.

And I like to think Moses enjoyed this part, a little. Because he did know them. He had been with these people for a year now. He knew which of the old men had settled a quarrel between neighbours without anybody raising their voice. He knew which ones had sat up with a sick child for somebody else's family. Some of them, perhaps, were among the judges Jethro had told him to choose. And there was another seventy, too, that he will have remembered, way back at the mountain, in the Book of Exodus, when Moses and Aaron and seventy of the elders of Israel went up the mountain and saw the God of Israel, and ate and drank in his presence, and lived. Whether these were the same seventy, the book does not say. But it would not surprise me.

So the names are written down. Seventy names. And the word goes round the camp. If your name is on the list, come out to the tent of meeting.

And the tent of meeting, I should explain, seems to have been outside the camp at this point. Or at least, the book speaks of the seventy going out to it. That matters, because of what happens next.

Now let me tell you about two of the men on the list.

Their names were Eldad and Medad.

And I have to be very honest with you about how much we know about them. We know their names. We know their names were on the list. We know they did not go out to the tent. And we know what happened to them anyway. That is all. Four facts. The Bible does not tell us whether they were brothers or friends or neighbours. It does not tell us how old they were, or which tribe they were from, or what they looked like. And it does not tell us why they stayed in the camp.

People have wondered about that last one for a very long time. Some of the old Jewish teachers, whose wondering was written down in the Talmud many centuries later, guessed that Eldad and Medad stayed behind out of humility. They thought, who are we, to stand beside Moses at the tent of the Lord? Let the others go. We are not worthy of this. And they stayed at home.

That is a guess, and it is labelled as a guess. But it is a lovely one, and I am going to use it tonight, because it fits what happens next so well.

So picture it. All over the camp, old men are getting up, and putting on their best cloaks, and saying goodbye to their families, and walking out between the tents toward the tent of meeting, with a kind of nervous solemnity, the way people walk into a church for an ordination.

And in one tent, somewhere in the middle of the camp, there is an old man called Eldad, sitting on a mat. And he is not putting on his best cloak.

"Aren't you going?" says his wife. This is our own invention, of course. The Bible does not mention his wife.

"No," says Eldad.

"Your name's on the list."

"I know."

"Moses wrote your name on the list himself."

"I know. I think he made a mistake. He meant someone else. There must be another Eldad. Somebody more suitable."

"There is no other Eldad."

"Well, then," says Eldad, and he picks up a bit of rope he has been meaning to mend for a week, and he starts mending it. And his wife looks at him the way wives have looked at husbands since the beginning of time, and goes off to grind the manna.

And somewhere else in the camp, perhaps two tents down, perhaps on the other side of the whole camp, there is a man called Medad. And he is doing something very similar. Perhaps he is mending a sandal. Perhaps he is helping his daughter with the goats. Perhaps he is just sitting very quietly in the shade, with his eyes closed, and his heart going too fast, and thinking, not me. Surely not me. Let it be the others.

And out at the tent, sixty-eight men gather in a ring.

And then the Lord comes down.

The book says it very simply. The Lord came down in the cloud and spoke to Moses. And he took some of the spirit that was on Moses, and put it on the seventy elders. And when the spirit rested on them, they prophesied.

What does that mean, they prophesied? Not, I think, that they started telling the future. In the old books, prophesying is something wider than that. It means being so full of the spirit of God that you speak out, and sometimes sing out, words that come from God, praising him, declaring what he has done. There are stories later, in the time of the prophet Samuel, of whole bands of prophets coming down a hill with harps and drums and pipes, prophesying as they go. It was something you could see and hear. Something unmistakable. Everyone standing round the tent would have known at once that something had happened to these old men.

And then the book adds a few little words. In our plain rendering: and they did not do it again.

That is, it seems to have happened that once. It was a sign. A visible sign, to everybody, and to the elders themselves, that the Lord had really put his spirit on them, and that from now on they were to share the weight of the people with Moses. They did not go around prophesying for the rest of their lives. They went back to the camp and got on with the work.

I should tell you that the Hebrew of those words can also be read a different way, and some old translations took it to mean the opposite, that they did not stop. Which shows you that even the people who translate the Bible sometimes have to scratch their heads. Either way, the point is the same. The spirit really came. The help was real.

But what about the two men who weren't there?

Back in the camp, in the shade of his tent, Eldad is mending his rope.

And the spirit of the Lord comes to rest on him there.

The book doesn't describe it. It just says, the spirit rested on them, they were among those who had been written down, but they had not gone out to the tent, and they prophesied in the camp.

So I shall not describe it either, except to say that Eldad's wife came running round the side of the tent at the sound of it, and dropped the manna, and stood there with her hands over her mouth. And then she probably went and got the neighbours. And the neighbours got their neighbours. And over in Medad's corner of the camp, exactly the same thing was happening.

Two old men who had thought, not me. And the Lord had said, yes. You. Wherever you are sitting.

You cannot hide from the Lord's call by staying at home. He knows where your tent is. He wrote your name on the list.

And that is when the young man ran.

We do not know his name. The Bible just calls him a young man, a lad. I picture him as about fourteen, the sort of boy who is always the first to know everything that happens in the street, and always the first to tell everybody. Every village has one.

And off he goes, between the tents, over the ropes, round the goats, past the woman with the basket, out across the open ground, to the ring of old men round the tent, and he finds Moses, and he gasps it out.

"Eldad and Medad are prophesying in the camp!"

And now let me tell you about the man at Moses' side.

His name is Joshua, the son of Nun. You may know him already. Much later, after Moses has died, it is Joshua who will lead the people across the Jordan into the promised land. At this point in the story, he is Moses' assistant, his right hand. The book says he had served Moses from his youth. He was the one who led the army at Rephidim, while Moses held up his arms on the hill. He was the one who went partway up Mount Sinai with Moses and waited there. When Moses went into the tent to speak with the Lord, Joshua would stay behind at the tent, and not leave it.

Joshua is loyal. Joshua is brave. Joshua is devoted to Moses, utterly. And Joshua is the kind of person who likes things done properly.

So when he hears that two men have been given the spirit somewhere else, not at the tent, not in front of Moses, not where they were told to be, Joshua is upset. And he turns to Moses straight away, and he says, "My lord Moses. Stop them."

I don't think Joshua was being wicked. I think he was being protective. He was thinking, this is Moses' spirit. It was supposed to be shared out here, in the proper place, in the proper way, with Moses standing in the middle. And now two men who could not even be bothered to turn up are prophesying in the camp, and people will say, what do we need Moses for? It isn't fair. It isn't orderly. It isn't right.

And Moses looks at him for a long moment.

And then he says the kindest thing in the whole chapter.

"Are you jealous for my sake?"

That's the first half. And you can hear in it, I think, an older man's fondness for a younger man who has got it wrong for the right reasons. Joshua, are you worried about me? Are you worried somebody is going to take something away from me?

And then the second half.

"I wish that all the Lord's people were prophets. I wish the Lord would put his spirit on every one of them."

All of them.

Think of who is saying this. This is Moses, who not long ago was praying to die because he could not carry these people any more. These weepy, grumbling, cucumber-wanting people. And the Lord has just lifted part of the weight off his shoulders. And now somebody is telling him that two more people have the spirit than he was expecting.

And instead of being worried that his authority is shrinking, Moses is glad. He doesn't want to be the only one. He never wanted to be the only one. He would be delighted if the whole camp, every man and woman and child, the grandfather on his mat and the boy who wanted something with his manna and the woman with the basket, if every single one of them were full of the Lord's spirit.

What a thing to wish for. And what a thing to hear from the greatest prophet of them all.

And then, the book says, Moses went back into the camp, he and the elders of Israel. Back among the tents. And I like to think, though the book doesn't say so, that on the way he stopped at Eldad's tent, and at Medad's, and put his hand on their shoulders, and said, you see? It wasn't a mistake.

And now, the quails. Because the Lord had promised meat, and the Lord keeps his promises, even the alarming ones.

The book says that a wind went out from the Lord, and brought quails from the sea, and let them fall all around the camp. A day's journey on this side, and a day's journey on that side, all around, and about two cubits deep over the ground. A cubit is about the length from your elbow to the tip of your fingers. So two cubits is about the height of a child's shoulder.

The old translators read that in two ways too. Some thought the quails lay heaped on the ground that deep. Others thought it meant the birds were flying that low, about waist high, so that you could simply reach out and catch them. Either way, it was a very great many quails.

And I should say, quails really do this. Every spring and autumn, enormous flocks of quail migrate across that part of the world, over the sea, and they arrive at the coast exhausted, flying very low, and they come down on the land in great numbers. People along those coasts caught them in nets for thousands of years. The Lord, you might say, used a wind and a bird that already knew the way. He often does work like that. Through the ordinary things he made, at exactly the right moment.

And the people went out, and gathered quails. All that day. All that night. All the next day. The book says that the one who gathered least gathered ten homers. A homer seems to have meant a donkey-load. So the very least anybody gathered was ten donkey-loads of quails. Which is far more than anybody could possibly eat, and they spread them out all round the camp to dry.

And here the story turns sad, and I shall tell it quietly, the way the book tells it.

While the meat was still between their teeth, the Lord's anger came on the people, and many of them died there. And they called the place Kibroth-hattaavah, which means the graves of craving, because there they buried the people who had craved.

I don't think the lesson is that it is wrong to want meat. The Lord gave them the meat. The lesson, I think, is about what the craving had done to them. It had made them look at bread from heaven and call it nothing. It had made them weep that they ever left Egypt, that they ever left slavery. And when the meat came, they fell on it as if the Lord had never fed them at all. The craving was not for quails, really. It was a craving to go back. And that is the one thing you cannot do, on the road to the promised land.

And then the people set out again, from the graves of craving, and moved on to a place called Hazeroth. And the cloud went before them by day, and the fire by night. And every morning, on the ground, there was manna.

And I think, perhaps, after that, they looked at it a little differently.

I wonder what happened to Eldad and Medad. The Bible never mentions them again. Like the seventy, perhaps they never prophesied again in their lives. They will have gone back to mending ropes and sandals and herding goats, and sitting as elders when somebody needed a quarrel settled, and carrying a share of the weight with Moses. Quietly. The way most of the Lord's work is done.

There was a book, long ago, that carried their names. It is lost now. All we have of it is one line, quoted in an early Christian writing called the Shepherd of Hermas. And the line is this: the Lord is near to those who turn to him. Which is not a bad thing to have as your only surviving sentence.

But their story did not end with them.

Hundreds of years later, a prophet called Joel heard the Lord make a promise. In days to come, the Lord said, I will pour out my spirit on everyone. Your sons and your daughters shall prophesy. Your old men shall dream dreams, and your young men shall see visions. Even on the servants, the men and the women, I will pour out my spirit.

That is Moses' wish. I wish that all the Lord's people were prophets. The Lord had heard it. And he had not forgotten.

And hundreds of years after Joel, on a feast day in Jerusalem, a group of fishermen and their friends were sitting together in an upper room, waiting, as they had been told to wait. And there came a sound like a rushing wind, and something like tongues of fire, and the Holy Spirit came upon every one of them. Not just one leader. All of them. And they went out into the street, praising God in every language under heaven, and a crowd gathered. And Peter stood up and told the crowd, this is what the prophet Joel was talking about. I will pour out my spirit on everyone.

And one more thing. In the Gospels, there is a day when John, one of the twelve apostles, comes to Jesus rather pleased with himself, and says, Teacher, we saw a man driving out demons in your name, and we told him to stop, because he wasn't one of us.

And Jesus says, do not stop him. Whoever is not against us is for us.

My lord Moses, stop them. Do not stop him. A thousand years apart, and the same lesson. The Lord's spirit is not a possession, to be guarded. It is a gift, to be glad about. Wherever it turns up. Even in the middle of the camp, in an old man's tent, while he is mending a bit of rope.

So tonight, wherever you are sitting, or lying, in your own tent in the middle of the camp, you might remember that the Lord knows where you are. He has written your name on the list. And he does not need you to be at the front, or in the proper place, or feeling worthy, to find you.

And you might remember Moses, worn out, telling the Lord the plain truth. And the Lord, not angry with him for it, but sharing out the weight.

And you might, if you like, think of the manna one more time. Small and white on the ground, every morning, without fail. Easy to call it nothing. Easy to forget it was a miracle at all.

And then you might go to sleep, and not have to carry anything by yourself.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapter eleven, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. The departure from Sinai and the order of the camp are from chapters one, two and ten. The manna, its name and its keeping are from Exodus, chapter sixteen, and the mixed crowd from Exodus, chapter twelve. The seventy elders on the mountain are from Exodus, chapter twenty-four. Joshua at Rephidim, on the mountain and at the tent is from Exodus, chapters seventeen, twenty-four and thirty-three, and the band of prophets from First Samuel, chapter ten. Jethro's advice is from Exodus, chapter eighteen, and Moses' own retelling of the choosing of leaders from Deuteronomy, chapter one.

The words usually rendered they did no more, about the seventy prophesying, can also be read as they did not cease, and both readings are old. The two cubits of quails have also been read in two ways, as we said. A homer was a large dry measure; that the word seems to mean a donkey-load is a common explanation, not a certain one. Quail migration over the region is real; we have not claimed it explains the story away.

The guess that Eldad and Medad stayed behind out of humility comes from old Jewish teachers and is recorded in the Talmud; it is a guess, not Scripture. A lost book under the names of Eldad and Medad is quoted once in the Shepherd of Hermas, an early Christian writing, and nothing more of it survives. The prophecy is Joel, chapter two; Pentecost is Acts, chapter two; and the man driving out demons is in Mark, chapter nine, and Luke, chapter nine.

The family at the grinding stone, the grandfather, Eldad's wife and the rope, Medad's corner of the camp, the boy who ran and his age, and Moses stopping at their tents are our own invention.

That's the story of Eldad and Medad and the spirit in the camp for tonight.

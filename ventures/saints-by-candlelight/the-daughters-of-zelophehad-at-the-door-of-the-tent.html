The Daughters of Zelophehad at the Door of the Tent

Five women are walking across a camp.

It is early in the morning, and the camp is enormous. Tents in every direction, as far as you can see, in rows and blocks, with little lanes between them where children are already running and goats are already being difficult. Smoke from cooking fires goes straight up into a pale sky.

People look up as they go past. A man mending a sandal stops with the needle halfway through. Two old women at a fire stop talking. A small boy, holding a very unhappy chicken, stands and stares.

So God told Moses and Eleazar the priest to count the people.

They had done this once before, at the beginning of the journey, forty years earlier at Mount Sinai. But almost none of the people from that first count were still alive. A whole generation had grown old and died in the wilderness. The people in this camp were their children and grandchildren. So it was time for a new count, and this one had a very particular purpose.

The land was going to be shared out.

The Book of Numbers is very clear about how. Each tribe would get a share, and the big tribes would get a bigger share and the small tribes a smaller one, and the exact places would be decided by lot, which means something like drawing names out of a hat, so that nobody could say the leaders had kept the best bits for their friends. And within each tribe, the land would be shared among the clans, and within each clan among the families. And each family's land would be passed down, from a father to his sons, generation after generation, and it would stay in the family for ever.

That was the important thing, for these people. Not just having land. Having land that had your family's name on it. A field your grandfather had ploughed, that your grandson would plough. For a people who had spent forty years in tents, that must have sounded like heaven.

The count was done tribe by tribe, and it is written out in full in chapter twenty-six of Numbers. It is a long list. Now, before we go on, I want to be careful about something, because it would be very easy to tell this story as if the people of Israel were villains for not thinking of daughters, and that would not be fair either.

In those days, all over the ancient world, land usually went from fathers to sons. It was not something anybody had sat down and decided to be unkind about. It was just the way things were done, the way the sun comes up in the east. A daughter would usually marry, and go to live with her husband's family, on her husband's family's land, and her children would belong to that family. So the land went to the sons, and the sons looked after their sisters and their mother, and that was how it was supposed to work.

It was a system with a gap in it. And the gap was exactly the shape of a family like this one. Five daughters. No sons. Nobody to carry the name.

And these five sisters were standing in the gap.

A long time afterwards, nearly eight hundred years before Christ, in the city of Samaria, the capital of the northern kingdom, a clerk in the royal storehouses kept records of jars of wine and oil coming in from the villages round about. The old Jewish teachers loved these five sisters. In the Talmud, the great book of rabbinic discussion written down many centuries later, the rabbis say that the daughters of Zelophehad were wise women. They knew the law, and they knew how to read it, and they were good women too. Wise, and learned, and righteous. That is not a bad thing to have said about you by people who argued about everything.

They were not queens or prophetesses. They did not part any seas or bring down any walls. Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty-six, verse thirty-three, where the five sisters are named in the count, and chapter twenty-seven, verses one to eleven, where they come to the door of the tent of meeting, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Their three sentences, Moses bringing their case before the Lord, the words "the daughters of Zelophehad are right", and the law of inheritance that follows are all the book's own, told plainly. Moses on the mountain and Joshua chosen are the rest of chapter twenty-seven. The question from the leaders of Gilead, the answer that they may marry whom they think best within their father's tribe, and the second list of names in a different order are chapter thirty-six, the last chapter of Numbers. The sisters before Eleazar and Joshua and the ten portions are in Joshua, chapter seventeen. Korah's rebellion is Numbers, chapter sixteen, and the daughters of Job are in Job, chapter forty-two.

The Talmud's praise of the sisters is in the tractate Bava Batra. Rabbi Akiva's opinion that their father was the man found gathering sticks on the sabbath, in Numbers fifteen, is in the tractate Shabbat, and another rabbi rebuked him for it there, so we have not told it as fact. The Samaria ostraca are real, and the likeness of names is a scholars' observation, not a proof.

And the sisters' characters, their talk in the tent the night before, the boy with the chicken, the man at the back of the crowd, and the hug at the corner are our own invention.

They have no feast day of their own, but the Orthodox Church remembers all the righteous of the Old Testament on the Sundays of the Forefathers before Christmas, and Moses the prophet on the fourth of September.

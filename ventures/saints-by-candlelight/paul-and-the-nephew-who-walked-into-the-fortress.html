Paul and the Nephew Who Walked into the Fortress

Saint Paul stands before the council in Jerusalem, is struck on the mouth, sets the Pharisees and the Sadducees arguing about the resurrection, and hears the Lord say take courage in the night; then more than forty men swear not to eat until he is dead, his sister's son hears of it, and a Roman commander sends him away to Caesarea before midnight with four hundred and seventy soldiers. Told straight from the Acts of the Apostles, chapters twenty-two and twenty-three.

And Luke notices, too, that the commander said to provide mounts, in the plural. More than one horse for Paul. Perhaps so he could change horses on the way. Perhaps for his things. Perhaps, though Luke does not say so, for a companion. Either way, the commander was not going to have Paul walking.

Then the commander sat down and wrote a letter.

Luke gives us the whole letter. And I want to read it to you, in our own plain words, because it is a wonderful letter, and because it is the first time we hear the commander's name.

It says this.

Claudius Lysias, to His Excellency the governor Felix, greetings.

This man was seized by the Jews, and was about to be killed by them, when I came on the scene with my troops and rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman citizen.

And because I wanted to find out what they were accusing him of, I brought him down to their council. And I found that he was accused about questions of their own law, but there was nothing against him that deserved death or chains.

And when I was told that there would be a plot against the man, I sent him to you at once. And I have told his accusers to state their case against him before you.

That is the letter.

Now, did you notice anything?

Having learned that he was a Roman citizen, I rescued him.

That is not quite what happened, is it. What happened, if you remember, is that the commander rescued him first, and wondered if he was an Egyptian troublemaker. And then chained him. And then ordered him to be whipped. And only found out he was a Roman citizen when Paul mentioned it, rather pointedly, while they were tying him up.

But that is not how it reads in the letter. In the letter, the commander is a dashing officer who heard that a Roman citizen was in trouble and rode to the rescue. And the bit about the whip has somehow been left out.

I find this very comforting. Nineteen hundred and fifty years ago, there was a man writing a report to his boss, and tidying up the story just a little bit so that he came out of it looking good. People have not changed very much. And Luke, who must have got hold of a copy of the letter somehow, or heard it read out at Caesarea, put it into his book without a word of comment. He just let it sit there, so that we could notice it ourselves. Which I think is very funny, and very gentle.

And the important part of the letter is true. Nothing against him that deserved death or chains. A Roman officer, who had no reason to like Paul at all, wrote that down and sent it to the governor. And Paul would need that letter, later on.

So now we come back to where we started. The courtyard of the fortress, at nine o'clock at night. The torches. The horses blowing. Four hundred and seventy men trying to be quiet.

And Paul being helped up into the saddle.

And the gate opened, and they went out.

I do not know which way they went. The road from Jerusalem down to the coast goes west and north, out of the hills, and one old way runs down past a place called Beth-horon, down a long steep ridge, where, a very long time before, Joshua had chased the kings of the Amorites, on the day the sun stood still over Gibeon. It is a likely road. But Luke does not name it, so I am only guessing.

Let us imagine it, though. Our own invention, all of it.

Let us imagine that the moon was up. Not full, but bright enough. The road pale in front of them, going down and down between the terraces, the olive trees silver on either side. The sound of four hundred and seventy pairs of feet, or rather four hundred feet and seventy horses, which is a different sort of sound. The clink of buckles. A horse blowing. A centurion's voice, quiet, at the front.

And Paul, on his horse, in the middle of it all, with a soldier on either side.

He had been bound in Jerusalem, just as Agabus had said. And now he was going back down to Caesarea, back to the city by the sea, where, not two weeks before, his friends had wept and begged him not to go up. Bound, and with a guard of honour that a king might envy.

I wonder what he thought about, on that ride. I wonder if he thought about the road to Damascus, the other time a lot of soldiers had travelled a long road with him at the centre of it, and he had been the one with the letters, and the power to arrest. And now here he was, the prisoner, and the soldiers were there to keep him alive.

I wonder if he thought about his sister's boy, and prayed for him.

And I think, most of all, he thought about those words in the barracks. Take courage. You must bear witness in Rome too.

And I think, somewhere on that long slope down out of the hills, with the stars going round overhead and the horse rocking under him, he very probably fell asleep in the saddle. Which is a thing that tired travellers have always done, and is not recommended, but there were four hundred and seventy people making sure he did not fall off.

They went on all night. And Luke says they brought him by night as far as Antipatris.

Antipatris was a town at the edge of the hills, where the high ground runs down into the flat coastal plain. It was built at a place with springs, where a river rises. It was a very old place, much older than its name. But King Herod the Great had rebuilt it, and named it after his father, whose name was Antipater. So it was, if you like, Antipater-town. Herod liked naming things after his family. He named a tower in Jerusalem after his brother, and another after his wife, and the Antonia fortress itself, where Paul had just come from, after his friend Mark Antony. If he had had a dog, I expect he would have named a city after the dog.

Now, Antipatris is a long way from Jerusalem. Something like forty miles. And people who know about marching have wondered ever since how the foot soldiers managed to walk forty miles in a single night, some of it downhill on a bad road, with their armour on. It would be a very hard march. Perhaps they went faster than we think. Perhaps they went only part of the way that night, and Luke has simply run the journey together. Perhaps the soldiers on foot went with them only for the dangerous first stretch, out of the hills, and the rest happened in the morning. We do not know. Luke does not say. All he tells us is where they got to.

And at Antipatris, the next day, the soldiers on foot turned round and went back to the fortress in Jerusalem. Because once you are out of the hills, and onto the flat open plain, there is nowhere for forty men to hide. You can see anyone coming for miles. So the commander's orders, I imagine, were to take the foot soldiers as far as the edge of the hills, and then let the horsemen go on alone.

So the seventy horsemen went on with Paul. Across the plain. Another twenty-five miles or so, north and west, towards the sea.

And I like to think that the morning was bright, and that there was a wind off the sea, and that Paul smelled salt before he saw the water. That is our invention. But it is the sort of thing that happens on the road to Caesarea.

And so they came to Caesarea.

Caesarea was Herod's other great city. He had built it on the coast, on a stretch of open shore with no proper harbour, and then, because there was no harbour, he had built one, out into the open sea, with great stone breakwaters, which was one of the marvels of the age. There was a theatre, and a racecourse, and a temple to the emperor, and aqueducts bringing water down from the hills. And it was where the Roman governor lived, because the Romans, very sensibly, preferred the sea breeze to the politics of Jerusalem.

The horsemen rode in through the gates, and delivered the letter to the governor, and handed Paul over to him.

The governor's name was Antonius Felix. And I am afraid I have to tell you that he was not a nice man either. He had been born a slave, and freed, and he had a brother called Pallas who was one of the most powerful men at the emperor's court in Rome, which, people said, is how Felix had got the job. The Roman historian Tacitus, who did not like him at all, said that Felix used the power of a king with the mind of a slave. Which is a very Roman sort of insult, and not a very kind one, but I am afraid it tells you how people felt about him.

Felix read the letter. And he asked Paul one question. He asked him what province he came from.

That is the sort of question an official asks while he is working out whether something is his problem. If Paul had come from a province that belonged to some other governor, Felix might have been able to send him on somewhere else, and let somebody else deal with all this.

And Paul said, Cilicia.

And Felix, I imagine, sighed. Because Cilicia was close enough, and Roman enough, that there was no way of passing it on.

So he said, I will hear your case when your accusers have arrived too.

And he gave orders for Paul to be kept under guard in Herod's palace.

And that is where the chapter ends.

The palace, most archaeologists think, was on a little rocky point that sticks out into the sea, at the edge of the city. Herod had built it there so that the waves would come right up to the walls. It had a pool in the middle of it, of fresh water, with the salt sea all round, and, from what the diggers have found, painted walls and patterned floors, and a great hall where the governor gave judgments.

And it is there, in that palace by the sea, that Paul would stay. Not for a few days. For two whole years.

The high priest Ananias came down to Caesarea five days later, with a lawyer, to make the case against him. That is in the next chapter, and we will save it for another night. Felix listened, and put off deciding, and listened again, and put off deciding again. He brought his wife Drusilla to hear Paul talk about faith in Christ, and about justice, and self-control, and the judgment to come. And Luke says that Felix became frightened, and said, that is enough for now, I will send for you again when I have a moment. Which I think may be the most honest thing anyone in this story ever says.

And after two years Felix was called back to Rome, and another governor came. And Paul, at last, said the words that would take him where the Lord had promised. He said, I appeal to Caesar. And he went to Rome. By ship, of course. With a shipwreck, of course. That was always going to happen.

And the forty men, who had sworn not to eat or drink until Paul was dead?

Acts does not say. Luke never mentions them again.

But I am fairly confident that none of them starved. A later book of Jewish law, the Mishnah, written down a century and a half afterwards, has a little list of the kinds of vows that the teachers said could be let go. And one of them is a vow that circumstances made it impossible to keep. I suspect that, about two days after that night, a great many very hungry men in Jerusalem discovered that their vow came under that heading. And I hope they had a very good dinner. And I hope, somewhere in the middle of it, one or two of them stopped, with the bread in their hands, and wondered whether God had been trying to tell them something.

As for Ananias, he did not stay high priest very long. Some years later, when the great war against Rome broke out, he came to a sad end in the troubles in the city, as many people did. And a few years after that, the temple and the fortress were both gone.

But Caesarea is still there. Not the city, exactly, but its stones. The harbour is mostly under the sea now, and divers swim over the old breakwaters. And the rocky point where Herod's palace seems to have stood is still there, with the outline of the pool, and the sea still coming up round it, on a windy day, just as it did when Paul looked out of his window.

And in the year nineteen sixty-one, archaeologists digging in the old theatre at Caesarea turned over a block of stone that had been reused in some later building, and found letters cut into it. And when they read the letters, they found the name of a Roman governor, from about thirty years before Paul arrived. The governor's name was Pontius Pilate. It is the only stone inscription with his name on it that anyone has ever found. It is in a museum in Jerusalem now, and you can go and look at it.

So the governors came and went, in the palace by the sea. Pilate, and Felix, and the ones after. And their names are mostly on stones, or in the pages of historians who did not like them very much.

And the man they kept in that palace for two years, under guard, the short man with the bruise on his cheek, who rode down out of the hills one night in the middle of four hundred and seventy soldiers. His letters are read out loud, every Sunday, in churches all over the world.

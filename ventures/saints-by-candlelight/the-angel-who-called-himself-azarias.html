They picked up a good part of the population and moved it somewhere else, on the theory that people who have been uprooted from their land and their neighbours are less likely to organise a rebellion. It was effective, and it was cruel, and it is how a great many people from the northern kingdom of Israel found themselves living in Nineveh, a very long way from the hills of Galilee.

The old commentators say that the Book of Tobit is about many things. About marriage, and how it begins with prayer. About almsgiving, and how nothing given to the poor is ever lost. About honouring your father and mother. About how God hears the prayers of people who think they've been forgotten.

But I think, at bedtime, it's mostly about this. You are not walking alone. Not on the road to Media, not by the river at dusk, not in the dark room where you can't see anything. There is someone walking with you, whose name means "the Lord helps." You may not know who he is. He may seem like an ordinary companion. But he knows the road. He knows every road. And he will bring you home.

Some notes, for the curious.

The Book of Tobit comes to us in several ancient versions, mostly in Greek, with fragments in Aramaic and Hebrew found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran. Tonight's telling follows the longer Greek text, which is the one most modern Catholic Bibles translate. The detail of the dog running ahead and wagging his tail comes from Saint Jerome's Latin translation, the Vulgate.

Catholics read Tobit as one of the deuterocanonical books of the Old Testament, and Orthodox Christians count it among the books of their Old Testament as well. Many Protestant Bibles leave it out, or print it in a separate section, so if you've never come across it, that may be why.

Everything that happens in tonight's story is in the book. The fish, the gall, the grave in the garden, the fourteen days of feasting, and Raphael's words at the end are all there. What we added are small things: some of the conversations on the road, the barking, the speculation about catfish, and the narrator's very strong opinion that the dog was medium-sized and enormously pleased to be there.

Saint Raphael is one of three angels named in the Catholic Bible, with Saint Michael and Saint Gabriel. Catholics keep the feast of the three archangels on the twenty-ninth of September. The Orthodox Church honours them, with all the bodiless powers of heaven, at the Synaxis of the Archangels on the eighth of November. Raphael has long been asked to pray for travellers, for the sick, for people looking for a husband or wife, and, for obvious reasons, for people who work with fish.

That's the story of Tobias, Sarah and the Archangel Raphael for tonight. His feast is on the twenty-ninth of September in the Catholic Church and the eighth of November in the Orthodox Church.

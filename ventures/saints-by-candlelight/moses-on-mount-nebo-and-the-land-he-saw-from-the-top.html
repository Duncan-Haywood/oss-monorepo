Moses on Mount Nebo and the Land He Saw from the Top

Forty years after the grapes of Eshcol, the people of Israel are camped on the plains of Moab with the Jordan in front of them, and Moses, a hundred and twenty years old, gives them his last words, a song and a blessing, lays his hands on Joshua, and climbs Mount Nebo alone, where the Lord shows him the whole promised land he will not walk into. Told from the Book of Deuteronomy, chapters thirty-one to thirty-four, with Numbers and Joshua.

Tradition

Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Book of Deuteronomy, chapters three, thirty and thirty-one to thirty-four, with Numbers twenty and twenty-seven and Joshua one to five, told straight in a plain rendering; the books give no date in our years and historians disagree about when the Exodus happened, so none is given
Legend
none told as fact; the Jewish tradition that Moses died by the kiss of God is named as a later reading of the Hebrew, and the Christian tradition that the Transfiguration happened on Mount Tabor is named as tradition
What we invented

Feast day
the Orthodox Church remembers the holy prophet and God-seer Moses on the fourth of September, and the Roman Martyrology of the Catholic Church names him on the same day

Full script

It is very early in the morning, and an old man is walking up a mountain by himself.

He is a hundred and twenty years old. That is not a figure of speech. And yet he is not leaning on anyone. He has a staff in his hand, but he is not really leaning on that either. He is just walking, steadily, the way a shepherd walks, up the stony slope, with the dew still on the thistles and the light coming up behind him out of the desert.

Far below him, on the plain, there is a camp so big it looks like a town. Tents in rows. Thin smoke going up from the first fires. And in front of the camp, a line of green, where a river runs through a deep valley. And beyond the river, the hills on the other side, still grey, waiting for the sun.

The old man does not look back at the camp. He keeps walking. Up and up, to the top.

And when he gets to the top, the Lord shows him everything.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the last days of Moses, his farewell, and the land he saw from the top of Mount Nebo, from the Book of Deuteronomy, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a grandmother and her grandson in the camp, and everything they say, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Last time on Saints by Candlelight, twelve men went up from the wilderness to look at the land the Lord had promised. They came back after forty days with a bunch of grapes so big it took two of them to carry it on a pole. Ten of them said, we are like grasshoppers next to the people there. Two of them, Joshua and Caleb, said, let us go up at once, because we are well able. And the people believed the ten. And so the Lord said that the whole frightened generation would wander in the wilderness for forty years, a year for every day, until their children were grown, and that it would be the children who went in.

Well. The forty years are up.

Tonight we are on the other side of all that wandering. And the children have grown up.

Let me tell you where we are.

If you stand on the eastern side of the river Jordan, a little north of where it runs into the Dead Sea, you are in one of the lowest places on the dry surface of the earth. The Dead Sea lies more than fourteen hundred feet below the level of the ocean. It is hot down there, a thick, heavy heat, and the air smells faintly of salt and minerals, like a very large and very old bath.

The Bible calls this flat land by the river the plains of Moab. And behind the plains, to the east, the ground rises up in a long wall of brown hills, the range the Bible calls Abarim, which means, more or less, the mountains on the other side. And one of those hills, standing out a little from the rest, is called Nebo. Its highest shoulder, the one that looks out over the valley, is called Pisgah.

From the top of Nebo, you are more than two thousand feet above the sea, and the whole Jordan valley lies spread out below you like a map on a table. On a clear day you can see the green of the oasis at Jericho, on the far side of the river, and the hills of the west rising behind it, ridge after ridge, until they fade into the haze.

A few weeks ago on this show, a pilgrim called Egeria climbed this very mountain, in the year three hundred and eighty-something, with a party of holy men and, for part of the way, a donkey, and asked to be shown what Moses saw. Tonight we go back much further than Egeria. Tonight we are there at the very first time anyone looked at that view and was told what it meant.

The camp of Israel is spread out over the plain. It is enormous. Hundreds of thousands of people, according to the counts in the Book of Numbers, with their flocks and their herds and their tents of black goat hair, and their children, and their grandmothers.

They still eat manna. Every morning except the Sabbath, it lies on the ground around the camp like frost, small and white, and they gather it in baskets, and it tastes, the Book of Exodus says, like wafers made with honey. They have eaten it for forty years. The Bible says plainly that they ate it until they came to the border of the land of Canaan. So this is nearly the last of it, though nobody in the camp knows exactly when it will stop.

And almost nobody in the camp remembers Egypt.

That is the strange thing about this crowd. Everyone who was grown up when they came out of Egypt, everyone who was twenty or older on the day the spies came back, is gone. That whole generation has been buried, one by one, in the sand of the wilderness, over forty years. Of all the grown men who were counted in that old census, only two are left. Joshua and Caleb. And Moses himself.

Almost everybody else is younger. And the oldest of them were only children when they walked out of Egypt.

One of those children, if you will let me invent her, was a girl called Atarah.

She was perhaps four or five years old on the night they left Egypt, and she is not a girl any more. She is a grandmother, with grey in her hair and strong brown hands from forty years of putting up tents and taking them down again. And she has a grandson called Ezer, who is eight, and who has never lived anywhere except in a tent, and never eaten anything for breakfast except manna, and never seen a river as big as this one until this month.

Ezer is fascinated by the river.

"Is it true," he says, one evening, sitting in the door of the tent with his knees under his chin, "that you walked through the sea?"

"Yes," says Atarah.

"Through the actual sea."

"Through the actual sea. On dry ground. With the water standing up on both sides like walls."

"What was it like?"

Atarah thinks about it. It is a long time ago, and she was very small. "I remember it was night," she says. "I remember it was windy. A great wind, all night, from the east. And I remember my father carrying me on his shoulders, and holding on to his hair. And I remember the ground was wet, but it was not mud. I remember the sound. Like a waterfall, on both sides, but further away than you would think." She smiles. "And I remember that I was more worried about my sandal, which had come off, than about the sea."

Ezer considers this. "Did you get the sandal back?"

"My mother had it in her bag the whole time. She had picked it up without telling me."

"That's very like Mother," says Ezer, who has met Atarah's daughter.

"It runs in the family," says Atarah.

They look out across the plain. In the evening light the river is a dark line of trees, and beyond it, the hills of the promised land are turning gold, and then pink, and then a soft blue.

"So why," says Ezer, "isn't Moses coming with us?"

Ah. Atarah has been waiting for that question. Everybody in the camp has been asking it.

Here is the answer the Bible gives. It is not a long story, and it is a sad one, so I will tell it gently.

Near the end of the forty years, the people came back to a place called Kadesh, in the wilderness of Zin. And there, the Book of Numbers tells us in one short sentence, Miriam died, Moses's sister, who readers have always understood was the girl who once watched over her baby brother in a basket among the reeds, and who had led the women in dancing with a tambourine on the far shore of the sea. And they buried her there.

And at Kadesh there was no water. And the people did what the people had done many times before. They grumbled. They said to Moses and Aaron, why did you bring us out of Egypt to this terrible place, where there are no figs and no vines and no pomegranates, and not even any water to drink?

And Moses and Aaron went to the tent of meeting, and fell on their faces. And the glory of the Lord appeared. And the Lord said to Moses: Take the staff, and gather the people together, you and Aaron your brother, and speak to the rock before their eyes, and it will give out its water.

And Moses took the staff. And he and Aaron gathered the people in front of the rock. And Moses said to them: Listen, you rebels. Are we to bring water for you out of this rock?

And Moses lifted up his hand, and struck the rock with his staff, twice. And water came out, a great deal of it, and the people drank, and their animals drank.

And the Lord said to Moses and Aaron: Because you did not trust in me, to show my holiness in the eyes of the people, you will not bring this assembly into the land that I have given them.

That is what happened at the waters that were called Meribah, which means quarrelling.

People have argued for a very long time about exactly what Moses did wrong. Was it that he struck the rock when he had been told to speak to it? Was it that he struck it twice? Was it that he said, are we to bring water, as if it were his doing and Aaron's, and not God's? Was it the anger? The Bible gives the Lord's own words, you did not trust in me, to show my holiness, and does not explain them further. And in the Book of Deuteronomy, Moses himself, remembering it, says something else as well. He says to the people, the Lord was angry with me too, because of you. As if the long weariness of forty years with a grumbling people had worn something thin in him at last.

I will not try to settle it tonight. Better scholars than I have not. What I will say is that the Bible does not hide it. The greatest prophet in Israel, the man who spoke with God face to face, made a mistake near the end of a long life, and it cost him the one thing he had wanted for forty years. And the Bible tells us so, plainly, and does not make excuses, and goes on loving him anyway.

A little later, at a mountain called Hor, on the edge of the land of Edom, Aaron died too. Moses went up the mountain with his brother and with Aaron's son Eleazar, and took off Aaron's priestly robes and put them on Eleazar, and Aaron died there on the top of the mountain. And Moses and Eleazar came down. And all the people wept for Aaron for thirty days.

So by the time the camp reached the plains of Moab, Moses was the last of the three. His sister, gone. His brother, gone. And he knew he would be next.

And here is the part that I think is the bravest thing in the whole story.

Moses asked anyway.

In the Book of Deuteronomy, he tells the people about it himself. He says: I pleaded with the Lord at that time. I said, O Lord God, you have only just begun to show your servant your greatness and your mighty hand. What god is there in heaven or on earth who can do deeds like yours? Please. Let me go over, and see the good land beyond the Jordan, that good hill country, and Lebanon.

It is a very human prayer. You have only just begun, he says. As if a hundred and twenty years, and the plagues, and the sea, and the mountain, and the manna, and the water from the rock, were only the first chapter, and he wanted to see how the story went on.

And the Lord said no.

But listen to how he said no. Moses tells it like this. The Lord said to me: Enough. Speak to me no more about this. Go up to the top of Pisgah, and lift up your eyes to the west and to the north and to the south and to the east, and see it with your own eyes. Because you will not go over this Jordan. But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him, because he will go over at the head of this people, and he will give them the land that you will see.

Enough, the Lord said. But also: go up and look. And: look after Joshua.

Atarah, in her tent, explains this to Ezer as best she can.

"So he can't go in," says Ezer. "But he's allowed to look at it."

"He's allowed to see all of it," says Atarah. "From the top of the mountain. More of it than any of us will ever see."

Ezer thinks about this for a while. "I would rather go in," he says.

"Yes," says Atarah. "So would I. So would Moses, I think." She pulls the blanket up around his shoulders, because the evenings on the plain are cooler than the days. "But he didn't stop praying to God because God said no. And God didn't stop talking to him because he asked. That's worth remembering."

And now, for some weeks, on the plains of Moab, Moses talks.

The whole Book of Deuteronomy is, more or less, his farewell. It is the longest goodbye in the Bible. He goes back over the forty years, all of it, the good and the bad, the sea and the mountain and the golden calf and the spies and the long wandering. And he gives them the Law again, from the beginning, for this new generation who were children or not yet born when it was first given.

That is where the book gets its name, by the way. Deuteronomy is a Greek word, and it means, roughly, the second law, or the law repeated. Moses is saying it all again, for the children.

Some of the most famous words in the whole Bible are in these speeches. Hear, O Israel, Moses says. The Lord our God is one Lord. And you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength. More than a thousand years later, when somebody asked Jesus which commandment was the first of all, those were the words he gave in answer.

And near the very end of his speeches, Moses says this, and I think Atarah would have remembered it. He says: This commandment that I give you today is not too hard for you, and it is not far away. It is not up in heaven, so that you should say, who will go up to heaven for us and bring it down to us, so that we can hear it and do it? And it is not beyond the sea, so that you should say, who will cross the sea for us and bring it back? No. The word is very near you. It is in your mouth, and in your heart, so that you can do it.

And then: I have set before you today life and death, the blessing and the curse. So choose life, so that you and your children may live, loving the Lord your God, listening to his voice, and holding fast to him. Because that means life for you, and length of days.

Choose life. Ezer, who is eight, likes that bit. It is short, and he can remember it.

One day, Moses calls all Israel together, and says something that makes the whole crowd go quiet.

He says: I am a hundred and twenty years old today. I can no longer go out and come in. And the Lord has said to me, you shall not go over this Jordan.

I can no longer go out and come in. You may remember those words from a few episodes ago. Young King Solomon, centuries later, in his dream at Gibeon, says to God, I am only a little child, and I do not know how to go out or come in. It is an old Hebrew way of talking about leading people, going out in front of them and bringing them home again. Solomon, at the start of his life, says, I don't know how yet. Moses, at the end of his, says, I can't any more. Not because his legs have given out, as we will see. But because the Lord has said it is time for somebody else.

And then Moses calls Joshua.

Joshua is not young either, by now. He has been Moses's assistant since the days of the battle with the Amalekites, at the very beginning of the wandering. He was the young man who stayed in the tent of meeting when Moses went out. He was one of the two good spies. He has waited forty years for this.

And Moses, the Book of Numbers tells us, had already asked the Lord for him. When the Lord first told Moses that he would see the land but not enter it, Moses did not argue for himself, that time. He said this instead: Let the Lord, the God of the spirits of all flesh, appoint a man over the congregation, who will go out in front of them and come in in front of them, and lead them out and bring them in, so that the people of the Lord may not be like sheep that have no shepherd.

Sheep that have no shepherd. Moses had been a shepherd, for forty years, in Midian, before ever he saw the burning bush. He knew what happens to sheep with no one to look after them. And the Gospel of Saint Mark tells us that when Jesus, long afterwards, got out of a boat and saw a great crowd waiting for him on the shore, he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. Same words. I don't think that is an accident.

And the Lord said to Moses: Take Joshua the son of Nun, a man in whom is the spirit, and lay your hand on him. And Moses had done it. He had brought Joshua before Eleazar the priest and before the whole congregation, and laid his hands on him, and given him his charge.

Now, on the plains of Moab, in front of all Israel, he does it again, in words. He says to Joshua: Be strong, and be brave. Because you will go with this people into the land that the Lord promised to their fathers, and you will give it to them. It is the Lord who goes in front of you. He will be with you. He will not fail you or leave you. Do not be afraid, and do not lose heart.

Do not be afraid. Atarah watches Joshua's face as Moses says it, from far back in the crowd, and she thinks that Joshua looks like a man who is trying very hard to be brave and not to cry, both at once. But she was a long way back, and it was a long time ago, and that is only what she thought.

Then Moses does something very practical. He writes it all down.

He writes the whole Law in a book, and gives it to the priests, who carry the ark of the covenant, and to all the elders of Israel. And he tells them: Every seventh year, at the Feast of Booths, when all Israel comes together before the Lord, you are to read this Law out loud, in front of everybody. Men and women and little ones, and the foreigner who lives among you. So that they can hear it, and learn it, and the children who have not known it can hear it and learn it too.

The little ones, he says. Ezer, sitting on his grandmother's knee, hears that, and sits up straighter.

And then Moses teaches them a song.

The Bible tells us the Lord told him to. Write down this song, the Lord says, and teach it to the people of Israel. Put it in their mouths. Because when they have forgotten everything else, the song will still be there, and their children will still be singing it.

Which is very true of songs, isn't it? You can forget almost everything you learned as a child, but the songs stay.

The Song of Moses is in the thirty-second chapter of Deuteronomy, and it is long, and in places it is stern, because Moses is warning them about what will happen if they forget the Lord. But it begins like this.

Listen, O heavens, and I will speak. Hear, O earth, the words of my mouth. Let my teaching fall like the rain. Let my words come down like the dew, like soft rain on the young grass, like showers on the green plants.

I love that. A hundred and twenty years old, and he wants his words to be like dew on young grass. Not a thunderstorm. Dew. The kind of thing that is there in the morning, quietly, and you didn't hear it come.

And then: He is the Rock. His work is perfect. All his ways are just. A God of faithfulness, with no wrong in him. Just and upright is he.

The Rock. Moses, of all people, calling God the Rock. The man who struck a rock and got water. The man who will not cross the river because of a rock. He sings: God is the Rock. And I wonder what Moses thought, when he sang that line.

And then the song remembers how God found Israel, as a young nation, alone in the desert. He found him in a desert land, Moses sings, in the howling waste of the wilderness. He surrounded him, he cared for him, he kept him as the apple of his eye. Like an eagle that stirs up its nest, and hovers over its young, and spreads out its wings, and catches them, and carries them on its feathers, so the Lord alone led him.

Ezer, the next day, sees a great bird circling over the hills to the east, very high up, on the warm air, and points at it and says, "Is that an eagle?"

"Maybe," says Atarah. "Or a vulture. They look much the same from down here."

"I'd rather it was an eagle," says Ezer, "for the song."

And so would I, frankly, though I should tell you that the Hebrew word in the song can mean either an eagle or a large vulture, and the scholars are not sure which Moses meant. A vulture, I am told, is an excellent parent. It just has a poor reputation.

And then, on the very same day that he sang the song, the Bible says, the Lord spoke to Moses.

Go up into these mountains of Abarim, the Lord said, to Mount Nebo, which is in the land of Moab, opposite Jericho. And look at the land of Canaan, which I am giving to the people of Israel to possess. And die on the mountain that you climb, and be gathered to your people, as Aaron your brother died on Mount Hor and was gathered to his people.

Gathered to your people. That is how the Bible talks about the deaths of the patriarchs. Abraham was gathered to his people. Isaac was. Jacob was. It is a very gentle way of saying it. Not lost. Not ended. Gathered. Like sheep at evening, or a family coming in for supper.

But before he goes, Moses does one last thing for them. He blesses them.

The thirty-third chapter of Deuteronomy begins: This is the blessing with which Moses the man of God blessed the people of Israel before his death. And then he goes through the tribes, one by one, the way Jacob had blessed his twelve sons long ago in Egypt, and he gives each one a blessing of its own.

I won't read you all of them, because some of them are rather complicated, and you may be getting sleepy. But here are a few.

To the tribe of Benjamin, the youngest, he says: The beloved of the Lord, he lives in safety beside him. The Lord shelters him all day long, and he rests between his shoulders.

Rests between his shoulders. Like a small child carried on a father's back. Atarah, hearing it, thinks of her father's shoulders, and the night by the sea, and holding on to his hair.

To the tribe of Zebulun, who would live near the sea, he says: Rejoice, Zebulun, in your going out.

And to the tribe of Asher, he says something that people have been saying to each other ever since, at weddings and birthdays and in hard times: As your days, so shall your strength be. Which means, more or less, you will have the strength for every day, when the day comes. Not all at once. Just enough. Like manna.

And at the end of the blessings, Moses says this, to all of them together. And if you only remember one line from tonight, I would like it to be this one.

The eternal God is your dwelling place. And underneath are the everlasting arms.

Underneath are the everlasting arms.

A people who have lived in tents for forty years, who have never had a house, who have moved on every time the cloud moved, are told: God is your home. And underneath you, wherever you are, whatever happens, there are arms that will not get tired.

Ezer falls asleep that night with his grandmother's arm under his head. And Atarah lies awake for a while, and listens to the camp going quiet, and thinks about it.

And then, one morning, Moses goes up the mountain.

The Bible says it in one sentence. Moses went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, which is opposite Jericho.

It does not say whether anyone went with him. It does not say whether he said goodbye. It does not say whether the people watched him go. I like to think that some of them did. I like to think that Atarah got up very early, before the manna was even on the ground, and took Ezer by the hand, and walked to the eastern edge of the camp, where you could see the slope of the mountain. And that, a long way off, they saw a small figure walking up it, steadily, with a staff, not looking back.

But that is only my invention, and the Bible does not say.

What the Bible does say is that when Moses got to the top, the Lord showed him all the land.

And then it gives the list, and I would like to read it to you slowly, the way Moses might have seen it, turning his head from one direction to the next.

The Lord showed him all the land. Gilead in the north, as far as Dan. All of Naphtali. The land of Ephraim and Manasseh. All the land of Judah, as far as the western sea. The Negeb in the south. And the plain, the valley of Jericho, the city of palm trees, as far as Zoar.

North, then west, then south, then the valley right below him. Just as the Lord had promised, back when Moses had pleaded to go over: lift up your eyes to the west and to the north and to the south and to the east.

I should be honest with you here, as Egeria was honest. If you stand on Mount Nebo today, even on a very clear day, you cannot actually see as far as Dan, which is a long way to the north, near the springs of the Jordan, or the western sea, which is the Mediterranean, on the far side of all those hills. The hills are in the way. Egeria herself wrote down only what she saw: the Jordan valley, the Dead Sea, Jericho, and a great part of the land of promise. And when the priests on the mountain showed her the place where Lot's wife had stood as a pillar of salt, she wrote to her sisters at home, we saw the place, but we saw no pillar, and I cannot deceive you about this.

But notice what the Bible says. It does not say, Moses looked out and could see as far as the sea. It says, the Lord showed him. What exactly that means, how much of it was the eyes of a hundred and twenty years and how much was the gift of God, the Bible does not explain, and I am not going to explain it either. It is enough to know that the Lord showed him. All of it.

And then the Lord said to him: This is the land that I swore to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob, saying, I will give it to your children. I have let you see it with your eyes. But you will not go over there.

Think how long that promise had been waiting. Abraham had walked through that land as a stranger, with his tents and his flocks, and been told, to your children I will give it. Isaac had dug his wells there. Jacob had slept on a stone at Bethel and dreamed of a ladder. And then the long years in Egypt, four hundred and thirty of them, the Book of Exodus says, and the slavery, and the bricks, and the crying out. And then the sea, and the mountain, and the forty years.

And here it was. All of it, laid out below Moses in the morning light. The promise, kept.

Not for him, not in the way he had hoped. But kept. He saw it kept.

And then the Bible says this, very simply, and I am going to say it as simply as it does.

So Moses, the servant of the Lord, died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the Lord.

And he buried him in a valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth-peor. And no one knows the place of his grave to this day.

He buried him. Who is he? The Hebrew sentence does not name anybody else. Most readers, Jewish and Christian, have understood it in the plainest way: that the Lord himself buried Moses. That Moses, who had spent his life taking care of other people, was taken care of at the very end by God, with his own hands, so to speak, in a quiet valley where nobody would ever make his tomb into a place to quarrel over. Some translations put it the other way, they buried him, and the Greek translation of the Old Testament does. But no one knows the place of his grave. That much is clear in all of them.

And the words according to the word of the Lord, in Hebrew, are al pi, which means, quite literally, at the mouth of the Lord. And the Jewish teachers of later centuries, in the Talmud, read those words in a way that I find very beautiful. They said that Moses did not die the way other people die. They said that God took his soul with a kiss.

That is a tradition, not something the Bible itself says, and I want to be clear about that. But it is a lovely way to read a small Hebrew phrase. At the mouth of the Lord. The way a parent kisses a sleeping child goodnight.

And then there is one more sentence, and it is my favourite in the whole chapter. Moses was a hundred and twenty years old when he died. His eye was not dim, and his strength had not gone.

His eye was not dim. Whatever the Lord showed him from the top of that mountain, he saw it with good eyes.

You may notice that it does not quite fit with what Moses said earlier, I can no longer go out and come in. And I think that is rather the point. It was not that Moses was worn out, or that he had nothing left to give. It was that it was time. His work was done. And the Bible makes sure we know that he went up that mountain on his own two feet, and saw everything clearly, right to the end.

Stephen, the first deacon, in the Acts of the Apostles, tells Moses's life in three parts. Forty years in Egypt, growing up in Pharaoh's house. Forty years in Midian, keeping sheep. And forty years in the wilderness, keeping a people. Three lots of forty. A hundred and twenty years. And at the end of it, a valley that nobody can find, and eyes that were not dim.

Down on the plain, the people of Israel waited, and he did not come back. And the Bible says that they wept for Moses in the plains of Moab for thirty days.

Thirty days. The same as for Aaron. A month of mourning in the camp, with the river in front of them and the mountain behind them.

Atarah wept, I think. She had never spoken to Moses in her life. He was just there, always, as far back as she could remember, at the front of everything. Like the cloud, and the manna, and the tent of meeting. And now he was not.

"Where is he now?" Ezer asks her, on one of those thirty evenings.

Atarah has to think about that. She is a grandmother of the people of Israel, a very long time before Christ. She does not know as much as we know about heaven. But she knows the words of the blessing.

"He's been gathered to his people," she says. "Like Aaron. Like Miriam. Like Abraham." She is quiet for a moment. "And God knows where he is. God buried him himself. God won't lose him."

It was spring, the time of the barley harvest, and the Jordan was in flood, overflowing its banks the way it did every year at harvest time. "Was it like this?" Ezer asks her, halfway across. "Before? At the sea?"

And Moses was not there to see it. And yet, in a way, he had already seen it. From the top of the mountain, with eyes that were not dim. The Lord had shown him.

And that is nearly the end. But there are three more things I would like to tell you, quietly, before you go to sleep.

The first is the very last thing the Book of Deuteronomy says about Moses. It says: And there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face.

Face to face. The Book of Exodus says it too. The Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend. Of all the things anyone ever said about Moses, that may be the best. Not that he was powerful, or wise, or that he did great signs in Egypt. But that God spoke to him as a friend.

And earlier in Deuteronomy, Moses had said, the Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me, from among you, and you shall listen to him. And Saint Peter, in the Acts of the Apostles, standing in the Temple in Jerusalem after Pentecost, told the crowd that those words had come true in Jesus.

The second thing is a very short line in one of the shortest books of the New Testament, the Letter of Jude. Jude mentions, almost in passing, that the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, argued about the body of Moses, and that even Michael did not dare to speak an insult against him, but only said, the Lord rebuke you. Jude does not tell the whole story. Some of the early Christian writers said that he was remembering an old Jewish book about the end of Moses, which now survives only in pieces. So we do not know much more than that one line. But it is a lovely thing to know, I think, that even in that quiet valley in Moab, Moses was not left alone. The archangel of God was looking after him.

And the third thing is this.

Many centuries later, Jesus of Nazareth took three of his friends, Peter and James and John, up a high mountain, by themselves. Christians have long believed it was Mount Tabor, in Galilee, though the Gospels only say it was a high mountain. And there, in front of them, he was transfigured. His face shone like the sun, and his clothes became white as light.

And two men appeared, talking with him. Elijah. And Moses.

Moses. Standing on a mountain inside the promised land. The land he had seen from the other side of the river, and had not been allowed to walk into. And there he was, at last, in the land, on a mountain, talking with the Lord face to face, as a man speaks with his friend.

And Saint Luke's Gospel tells us what they were talking about. They were speaking, Luke says, about his departure, which he was going to accomplish at Jerusalem. And the Greek word that Luke uses for departure is exodos. Exodus. They were talking about another exodus. A greater one.

The Letter to the Hebrews, speaking of Abraham and the patriarchs, says that they all died in faith, not having received what was promised, but having seen it and greeted it from far away. And then, at the end of that long chapter, after Moses and all the rest, it says that they did not receive what was promised, because God had something better planned, so that they would not be made perfect without us.

So Moses waits for us, I suppose. On the far side of the river. With eyes that are not dim.

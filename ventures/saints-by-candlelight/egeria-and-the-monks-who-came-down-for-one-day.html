Egeria and the Monks Who Came Down for One Day

From Edessa the pilgrim Egeria goes on to Harran, the city of Abraham, and arrives by pure good luck on the eve of the one day in the year when the hermits of all Mesopotamia come down from the hills; then the bishop takes her six miles out to the well where Jacob rolled away the stone for Rachel. Told from chapters twenty and twenty-one of her own travel letter, with Genesis told straight.

It is the middle of the night, outside a small city on a wide, flat plain in the north of Mesopotamia, and a woman is standing in the doorway of a church, watching the dark.

It is not quite dark. Out on the plain there are lights. Small ones, one here and one there, each of them moving, slowly, away from the city. They are lamps. Each lamp is being carried by an old man in a rough robe, walking home. Some of them are going north, and some east, and some towards the hills. None of them is in a hurry. None of them looks back.

Yesterday, all of them were here, in this church, in one great crowd. Tomorrow, none of them will be. They came down for one day, and the day is over.

The woman watches until the last of the lights goes out behind a fold in the ground. Then she goes inside, and finds her writing things, because she has a great deal to tell her sisters at home.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the travel letter of Egeria, a pilgrim who journeyed to the holy places about sixteen hundred and forty years ago, together with the Book of Genesis, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and an old hermit, a girl with some goats, and nearly all the small conversations are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

If you have been with us on the last few nights, you know Egeria very well by now.

If you haven't, here is all you need. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, very probably from the north-west corner of Spain, though we cannot be quite sure, because the beginning of her letter is lost. Some time around the year three hundred and eighty, she travelled east to see the places in the Bible with her own eyes. She lived in Jerusalem for about three years, and from there she went on journey after journey. Up Mount Sinai. Up Mount Nebo. Across the Jordan to look for the tomb of Job. And she wrote it all down, in a long, chatty, endlessly polite letter to a group of women at home, whom she calls her sisters, and her ladies, and sometimes, when she is very fond of them, her souls.

Only part of the letter survives, in a single old copy found in a library in Arezzo, in Italy, in eighteen eighty-four. But what is left is a treasure.

Last time, we left her in the city of Edessa, after three happy days. She had prayed at the tomb of the apostle Thomas. She had stood at a gate and heard a bishop read out a letter. She had watched a great many very large fish. And she was, she said, on her way home.

She was not, of course, on her way home.

"After three days there," she writes, "I had to go on further still, as far as Carrae, as it is called now. For in the holy Scriptures it is called Charra, where holy Abraham stayed."

I want you to notice that little word. "I had to." Necesse me fuit. It was necessary. She has said exactly the same thing before, about staying three days in Edessa. As if somebody were making her. As if she were being dragged, protesting, from one holy place to the next, by forces beyond her control. Nobody was making her. She simply could not be a day's ride from the city of Abraham and not go.

And then she gives her sisters a quotation, to remind them who Abraham was and why the place mattered. She says that it is written in Genesis that the Lord said to Abraham, "Go out from your land, and from your father's house, and go into Charra."

Now, if you have a Bible on your bedside table, and you are the kind of person who checks, you will find that Genesis does not say quite that. Egeria, I think, was quoting from memory, on a mule, a long way from her books, and she ran two verses together, the way we all do. What Genesis actually tells us is slightly different, and it is worth hearing properly, because it is the story of the place she was going to.

So here it is, told straight, from the end of chapter eleven and the beginning of chapter twelve.

There was a man called Terah. He lived in a city called Ur of the Chaldeans, far away in the south, in the land between the rivers. He had three sons, Abram, Nahor and Haran. Haran died young, in Ur, and left a son called Lot. Abram married a woman called Sarai, and the book tells us, very quietly, a sorrow that would shape their whole life. Sarai was barren. She had no child.

And Terah took his son Abram, and his grandson Lot, and his daughter-in-law Sarai, and set out from Ur, to go to the land of Canaan. But they did not get to Canaan. They came as far as Haran, and they settled there. And Terah died in Haran.

And then, at the start of chapter twelve, the Lord spoke to Abram. "Go from your country, and your kindred, and your father's house, to the land that I will show you. And I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you, and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing. And in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed."

To the land that I will show you. Not, here is a map. Not, this is where you are going. Just, go, and I will show you.

So Abram went, as the Lord had told him. He took Sarai his wife, and Lot his nephew, and everything they owned, and all the people who had joined their household in Haran, and they set out for the land of Canaan. And the book tells us how old he was, because it wants us to notice. Abram was seventy-five years old when he left Haran.

Seventy-five. Most of us, at seventy-five, are not setting off for a country we have never seen, on foot, on a promise. But that is the point of him. Abraham, as he was later called, is the one the Church calls our father in faith, because he went when he was called, without knowing where he was going.

Many centuries later, when the deacon Stephen told this story in Jerusalem, in the Acts of the Apostles, he said that God had first spoken to Abraham even before Haran, back in Mesopotamia, in Ur. And he said that after his father died, Abraham left Haran and came into the land where you now live. So between Genesis and Stephen, you can see where Egeria's half-remembered verse came from. Ur, and Haran, and the call, all tumbled together in the mind of a woman on a mule who loved the story and did not have the book in front of her.

And this, Haran, was the place. The place where Abraham stopped, and buried his father, and heard the call, and left.

Let me tell you a little about Harran, as it was when Egeria arrived.

It lies about a day's ride south of Edessa, on a wide, flat, open plain. If you go there today, it is in the south-east of Turkey, close to the border with Syria, and it is a small village, among great broken ruins. The village is famous for its houses, which are built of mud brick, with roofs shaped like tall pointed beehives, all clustered together, so that from a distance it looks rather as if a crowd of very large bees had decided to settle down and grow wheat.

In Egeria's time it was a Roman town, near the eastern frontier. The Romans called it Carrae. And the name Carrae was not a happy name for the Romans, because more than four hundred years before Egeria, a Roman general called Crassus, one of the richest men who ever lived, had led an army out on to that plain to fight the Parthians, and lost most of it, and his own life too. Roman historians remembered Carrae for a long time the way you remember the place where you once had a very bad day.

But the thing about Harran that would strike Egeria most was something else.

Harran was a city of the moon.

For as long as anyone could remember, long before Abraham, the people of Harran had worshipped the moon. There was a great temple to the moon god there, and it was famous across the whole East. Even as late as Egeria's own lifetime, when nearly the whole Roman world had become Christian, Harran had not. The Roman historian Ammianus Marcellinus tells us that when the emperor Julian, the last pagan emperor, marched east against the Persians, about twenty years before Egeria came, he stopped at Carrae, and there offered worship to the moon, according to the custom of the place.

So imagine coming to a city full of temples, where almost nobody shares your faith, and being told that somewhere in this city, Abraham once lived. Abraham, who left all that behind him. Who went out into the dark, under that same moon, because a voice that was not the moon's had called him.

I think Egeria felt that. I think it is why she says, so firmly, in a moment we will come to, that she found no Christians there at all.

She arrived, and she did what she always did. "As soon as I came to Charra," she writes, "I went straight to the church that is inside the city itself."

And she met the bishop. "A truly holy man of God," she says, "and himself both a monk and a confessor." That is almost exactly what she said about the bishop of Edessa, and you may remember what it means. A confessor was someone who had suffered for the faith and survived. Who had been threatened, or imprisoned, or sent into exile, and had not given in.

Egeria does not tell us his name. But historians think he was very probably a man called Protogenes. And if they are right, he has a lovely story of his own.

A few years before, under an emperor who had made life very hard for bishops who held to the faith of the Council of Nicaea, a priest of Edessa called Protogenes had been sent into exile together with Eulogius, who later became the bishop of Edessa, the kind old man we met last time. They were sent a very long way away, to a town called Antinoe, in Egypt, on the Nile. And according to the old Church historian Theodoret, who wrote it down some decades later, Protogenes found that most of the people in Antinoe were pagans. So, being a sensible man, he did not stand in the market place and shout at them. He opened a school. He taught the town's children to read and to write. And while he was teaching them their letters, he taught them the psalms as well.

When the hard times ended, the exiles came home, and Eulogius was given Edessa, and Protogenes, so Theodoret says, was given Carrae. Harran. Another city full of pagans. I think he must have felt he had been sent exactly where he belonged.

That is a historians' identification, and Egeria herself does not name him. But it fits. And it fits, too, with what she says about him, which is that, as soon as he met her, he kindly offered to show her every place she wanted to see.

"And he took us at once," she writes, "to the church that is outside the city, on the place where the house of holy Abraham stood. It is built, so the holy bishop said, on the very same foundations, and of the very same stone."

Now notice, once again, how honest she is. So the holy bishop said. She does not say, this was Abraham's house. She says, this is what I was told. It is the sort of small, careful phrase that makes me trust her about everything else.

And there, in the church on the stones of Abraham's house, they did what she always did. A prayer. A reading of the passage from Genesis. A psalm. Another prayer. And the bishop blessed them, and they went out again, into the sun.

Next, the bishop took them to a well.

"This," he said to her, "is the well from which holy Rebekah watered the camels of the servant of holy Abraham, that is, Eliezer."

Now, this is a different well from the one in the title of tonight's story, and a different story, though the two are cousins. It is the story of how Abraham, very old by now and living far away in Canaan, sent his most trusted servant back to his own people, back here, to the country around Harran, to find a wife for his son Isaac. And I should say that Genesis, in that chapter, never tells us the servant's name. He is only ever the servant, the oldest servant of the household. Earlier in Genesis, Abraham speaks of a man called Eliezer of Damascus who will be his heir if he never has a son, and the old readers, very reasonably, decided that this must be the same man. Perhaps it was. The bishop thought so. Genesis does not say.

We have told the whole of that story on another night, so I will only tell you the heart of it, straight.

The servant came, with ten camels, to the city of Nahor, Abraham's brother. And he stopped at the well outside the city, at evening, at the time when the women came out to draw water. And he prayed. "O Lord," he said, "God of my master Abraham, let it be like this. Let the girl to whom I say, please let down your jar so that I may drink, and who says, drink, and I will water your camels too, let her be the one you have chosen."

And before he had even finished speaking, out came Rebekah, the granddaughter of Nahor, with her water jar on her shoulder. And he ran to meet her, and said, please let me have a little water from your jar. And she said, "Drink, my lord." And when he had drunk, she said, "I will draw water for your camels too, until they have finished drinking."

Ten camels.

I should tell you, for the sake of the full picture, that a camel who has walked a long way across the desert is not a polite sipper of water. A thirsty camel can drink an astonishing amount, the best part of a bathful. Rebekah offered, without being asked, to fill ten of them, from a well, with a jar on her shoulder, for a stranger. That is not a girl being nice. That is a girl being heroic, in the quietest possible way. And the servant stood and watched her do it, in silence, the book says, wondering whether the Lord had made his journey prosper.

He had.

And so Egeria stood by the well where, so the bishop said, Rebekah had run back and forth with her jar, and I imagine her looking down into the dark water, and thinking of ten camels, and a girl who said, until they have finished drinking.

And then the bishop told her something that made her so happy that she wrote about it at length. Because he told her what day it was.

The church outside the walls, the one on Abraham's house, was not only a church of Abraham. It was also, Egeria says, a martyrium, the shrine of a martyr. "The shrine," she says, "of a certain holy monk named Helpidius."

I wish I could tell you who Helpidius was. I cannot. Egeria is the only one who tells us anything about him, and she tells us only his name, and that he was a monk, and that he was a martyr, and that his body lay in that church. I am not going to make anything up about a real martyr, so that is all we will say. He was a holy monk, and he gave his life for Christ, and the people of Harran remembered him.

And here is the thing. Egeria had arrived, she writes, on the day before his feast.

His day was the twenty-third of April. Egeria puts it in the old Roman way, the ninth day before the Kalends of May, which is the kind of date that makes you need a lie down. And on that day, she writes, all the monks from everywhere, from every corner of Mesopotamia, had to come down to Harran. Even, she says, the great ones, the ones who lived in solitude out in the wilderness, whom they call ascetics. They came down, she says, for the martyr, and also in memory of holy Abraham, because the church where the martyr lay was built on Abraham's house.

As it happens, the twenty-third of April is the day on which the Church now keeps the feast of Saint George, who had his own episode on this show, with a dragon in it. Egeria does not mention him. She had a great deal else on her mind.

Because now listen to how she describes her luck. It is one of the happiest sentences in the whole letter.

"And so," she writes, "beyond anything I had hoped for, it happened that I saw the holy monks of Mesopotamia, truly men of God, even those whose fame and whose lives were talked of far and wide, whom I had never thought I would be able to see at all. Not because it would have been impossible for God to grant me that too, God who has granted me everything. But because I had heard that they never come down from their places, except on the day of Easter, and on this day. They are such men, I was told, that they work many wonders. And I did not even know which month the martyr's day was in. And so, by God's will, it happened that I arrived on the very day, which I had not even hoped for."

You can hear her, can't you. You can hear her nearly falling over her own words.

It is a little like arriving in a town you have never visited, on the one day in the whole year when every famous person you have ever wanted to meet happens to be there, all at once, in the same room. And not having known. And not having planned it. And only realising when the bishop mentions it, casually, by a well.

So the monks came down.

Egeria does not describe them arriving, so let us imagine it, gently.

They came all through that day, in ones and twos, from every direction across the flat plain. Some walked from monasteries in the villages round about, in little groups, talking. And some came down from much further off, alone, from caves and huts in the hills, from places where they had lived in silence for years. These were the ascetics, the ones Egeria most wanted to see. They had long beards, and sun-dark faces, and robes that had been patched so many times that there was more patch than robe. Some of them looked as if a strong wind might blow them away. Some of them looked as if a strong wind would not dare.

And among them, we shall say, was an old man called Hanina.

He is our invention. There is no Hanina in Egeria's letter. But there were men like him, by her own account, and it seems a shame not to let one of them speak.

Hanina had lived for thirty years in a cave, two days' walk to the north, with a spring, and a little patch of ground where he grew lentils, and a very large number of lizards, whom he knew by sight. He came down to Harran twice a year, once for Easter and once for the martyr, and on both occasions he found the town too noisy, and the bread too soft, and other people's conversation too long.

He found Egeria sitting by the door of the church, writing.

He stood and looked at her for some time. A woman, from the far west, writing. The way you might look at a heron that had wandered into a kitchen.

"You are the lady from the end of the world," he said at last, in Greek, with a strong Syriac accent. "The bishop told us."

"I am," said Egeria, and stood up, and asked for his blessing, which he gave, and then she asked him the question she asked every holy man she ever met. "Father, will you tell me something? Something for my sisters at home?"

Hanina thought about it.

"What do your sisters want to know?"

"Everything," said Egeria, honestly.

That made him laugh, which surprised him, because he had not laughed in some time. And he sat down on the step beside her, and he told her about the cave, and the spring, and the lentils, and the year the spring dried up and he prayed, and went on praying, and after a long while it ran again. He did not say that God had answered him. He did not say that it was a miracle. He said only that he prayed, and that it ran again, and that he was grateful. And he told her about an old monk he had known as a young man, who had lived for forty years on a rock, and never once complained about the weather, which, said Hanina, was more than could be said for him.

And then he got up, rather suddenly, and said, "That is enough talking. It is bad for the soul." And went into the church.

I bring Hanina in for a reason, which is something Egeria herself says, at the end of her account of Harran. She tells her sisters that the bishop told her many other things, and so had all the other holy bishops and holy monks she had met, and then she adds this:

"But they were always things from the Scriptures of God, or deeds of holy men, that is, of monks. Either the wonders done by those who have already died, or the things that are done every day by those who are still in the body, that is, the ascetics. For I would not want you, my dear sisters, to think that monks ever tell any other kind of story, except about the Scriptures of God, or the deeds of the great monks."

I find this enormously funny. And very touching.

She is defending them. She is worried, you see, that her sisters at home will imagine that all these holy men sat around gossiping with her. And she wants them to know: no. Never. It was all Scripture, and holy lives. Nothing else.

Which may well have been true, of course. But I like to think of her, writing that sentence, very firmly, with a slightly guilty memory of an old man on a step, talking about his lizards.

Egeria stayed in Harran for two days, she says, because of the martyr's day, and because of the chance to see those holy men. They welcomed her, she writes, very gladly, and spoke with her, which she says she did not deserve.

And then they were gone.

"As soon as the martyr's day was over," she writes, "they were not to be seen there any more. But straight away, that very night, they set off back to the desert, each one of them to his own monastery, wherever he had one."

That very night. They did not stay for breakfast. They had come down for the feast, and the feast was finished, and the silence was waiting for them. And that is the moment we began with, a woman in a church doorway, watching little lamps go out one by one across the plain.

I find that a very beautiful picture of a certain kind of holiness. They came. They prayed. They blessed whoever asked. And then they went home, in the dark, without any fuss, to go on praying where no one could see them.

And after they had gone, Harran was suddenly very quiet. And Egeria noticed something.

"In the city itself," she writes, "apart from a few clergy, and the holy monks, if any of them live in the city, I found not a single Christian. They are all pagans."

And then she says this. "For just as we honour, with great reverence, the place where the house of holy Abraham first stood, in his memory, so those pagans, about a mile from the city, honour with great reverence the place where the tombs of Nahor and Bethuel are."

Nahor was Abraham's brother. Bethuel was Nahor's son, and the father of Rebekah. Egeria does not tell us why the people of Harran, who worshipped the moon, should honour the tombs of two of Abraham's relatives, and I am not going to guess. I will only say that I find it interesting that they did. The Christians had Abraham's house, and the pagans had Abraham's brother, and the two places stood a short walk apart, on the same plain, under the same sky.

And this made Egeria curious. And when Egeria was curious, she asked.

The bishop, she says, was very learned in the Scriptures. So she went to him. And here, for once, she gives us the whole conversation, word for word, or very nearly.

"I beg you, my lord," she said, "tell me something I want very much to hear."

And the bishop said, "Say what you want, daughter, and I will tell you, if I know."

I love that answer. If I know. A bishop, a confessor, a very learned man, and the first thing he says is that he might not know.

So Egeria said, "I know from the Scriptures that holy Abraham came to this place, with his father Terah, and Sarah his wife, and Lot his brother's son. But I have never read when Nahor and Bethuel came here. All I know is that, later on, the servant of Abraham came to Harran to ask for Rebekah, the daughter of Bethuel, the son of Nahor, for his master's son Isaac."

You can see what she has done. She has been to the tombs, or heard about them, and something does not add up. Genesis tells us that Terah and Abraham and Lot came to Haran. It does not mention Nahor coming with them. So how did Nahor's tomb get here?

And the bishop said, "Truly, daughter, it is written as you say, in Genesis, that holy Abraham passed through here with his family. And canonical Scripture does not say when Nahor and his family, or Bethuel, came here. But clearly they did come here, afterwards, because their tombs are here, about a mile from the city. And Scripture truly does bear witness that the servant of holy Abraham came here to receive holy Rebekah, and that holy Jacob came here again later, when he took the daughters of Laban the Syrian."

Which, if you think about it, is a very good answer. He agrees with her. He does not pretend Scripture says something it does not. He points out that Genesis does put Nahor's family here later on, because that is where the servant finds Rebekah. And then he gives her the most practical piece of evidence a man of the fourth century could offer. Well, the tombs are here, so they must have come.

And then Egeria asked him another question, and this is the one I think she had been waiting to ask all along.

"And where," she said, "is the well where holy Jacob watered the flocks that Rachel, the daughter of Laban the Syrian, was looking after?"

And the bishop said, "That place is six miles from here, beside a village that used to be the farm of Laban the Syrian. And whenever you want to go, we will go with you, and show you. There are many very holy monks there, and ascetics, and a holy church."

But before they went, Egeria asked one more question, and it is a slightly sad one.

She asked where Ur of the Chaldeans was. The place where Terah and his family had lived in the very beginning, before they ever came to Harran. The place Abraham's whole journey started from.

And the bishop said, "That place you are asking about, daughter, is at the tenth stage from here, inside Persia. From here to Nisibis is five stages, and from there to Ur, which was the city of the Chaldeans, another five. But now there is no way for Romans to go there. The Persians hold it all."

A stage, in those days, was about a day's travel. The places along the Roman roads where you could stop for the night and change your animals were called stages. So Ur was ten days' journey away. And Egeria, who had climbed Sinai, and crossed the Euphrates, and would have gone to Ur without a moment's hesitation, could not go.

The bishop's mention of Nisibis was not an accident. Nisibis was a great city, a little to the east, and for a long time it had been Roman, and the strong wall of the empire against Persia. But about twenty years before Egeria came, after the emperor Julian was killed fighting the Persians, his successor made a peace, and the price of the peace was Nisibis. The city was handed over. Its Christians had to leave. Among them, as it happens, was the great hymn-writer Saint Ephrem, who left Nisibis then and spent the rest of his life in Edessa, where Egeria had just been.

So when the bishop said, the Persians hold it all, he was talking about something that had happened in his own lifetime. A frontier that had moved. A road that had closed. And Egeria, for once, simply accepted it.

She tells us that this part of the world, on the border between the Romans and the Persians and the Chaldeans, is called, very specially, the East. And I think she wrote that down with a certain pride. She had come all the way from the end of the world in the West, and she had reached, very nearly, the place they called the East. You could not go further. Not that year.

So, after her two days, the bishop took her out to the well.

They went six miles across the plain. Six Roman miles, which is a little under nine kilometres, or about the distance most people can walk in two hours, if they are not stopping every few minutes to look at things. Egeria, I suspect, took longer.

It was spring. The plain round Harran is wide and open, and in April it would have been green with young wheat and barley, and full of larks going straight up into the sky singing, and coming down again, and going straight up again, as larks do, apparently without ever getting tired of it. The mule, I am sure, would have preferred to stay in Harran. Mules generally do.

And there, out on the plain, they came to the well. And beside the well, Egeria says, a church had been built in its honour. "Very big," she says, "and beautiful."

And there they did what they always did. The bishop prayed. And they read the passage from Genesis. And they sang a psalm suited to the place, and the bishop prayed again, and blessed them.

So let us read the passage with them. It is from chapter twenty-nine, and I will tell it to you straight.

Jacob had come a very long way. He was running away from his brother Esau, whom he had cheated, and who was very angry with him, and his mother Rebekah had sent him here, north, to her own brother, Laban, in Haran. On the way, at a place called Bethel, he had slept with his head on a stone, and dreamed of a ladder with angels going up and down it. But that is another night's story too.

And now he came, the book says, to the land of the people of the East. And he looked, and saw a well in the field, and three flocks of sheep lying beside it. And on the mouth of the well was a stone. A great stone. And when all the flocks were gathered there, the shepherds would roll the stone away together, and water the sheep, and put the stone back.

And Jacob said to the shepherds, "My brothers, where are you from?"

They said, "We are from Haran."

He said, "Do you know Laban, the son of Nahor?"

They said, "We know him."

He said, "Is he well?"

They said, "He is well. And look, here is his daughter Rachel, coming with the sheep."

And Jacob looked, and there she was.

And then Jacob, who has known Rachel for about four seconds, and who would quite like to talk to her without three crowds of shepherds listening, says to the shepherds, rather cunningly, "Look, it's still the middle of the day. It's not time to bring the flocks in yet. Why don't you water your sheep, and take them back out to graze?"

Go away, in other words.

And the shepherds said, no, we can't. Not until all the flocks are gathered here, and the stone is rolled away. Then we water the sheep.

And while he was still talking with them, Rachel came, with her father's sheep, because she was the one who looked after them.

And when Jacob saw Rachel, the daughter of his mother's brother, and the sheep of his mother's brother, he went up to the well, and he rolled the stone off the mouth of it. On his own. The stone that it took all the shepherds together to move. And he watered Laban's sheep. And then he kissed Rachel, and he wept, out loud.

And he told her who he was. That he was her father's kinsman. That he was Rebekah's son. And she ran, and told her father, and Laban came running out to meet him, and threw his arms round him, and kissed him, and took him home.

That is the story. And there is one more line about Jacob and Rachel, a little later in the chapter, that I think is one of the loveliest sentences in the whole of the Old Testament, so I will give it to you before we go on. Jacob agreed to work for Laban for seven years, so that he could marry Rachel. And the book says, Jacob served seven years for Rachel, and they seemed to him but a few days, because of the love he had for her.

And then Egeria saw the stone.

"We also saw," she writes, "lying near the well, that enormous stone which holy Jacob moved from the well. It is still shown today."

Enormous is not quite strong enough for her Latin. Her phrase is something like, a stone infinitely too big. A stone beyond all measuring. She does not tell us how big it was, but she clearly stood and looked at it, and thought, he moved that? On his own? Because of a girl?

Yes. Because of a girl. That is rather the point.

And here, let us imagine, a girl came to the well while Egeria was standing there. A real one, from the village nearby, about ten years old, with a stick, and a great many goats. She had come to water them, as girls from that village had been coming to that well, for all she knew, for ever.

She stopped, and looked at the bishop, and the clergy, and the lady in the travelling cloak who was staring at a stone.

"Is that the stone?" Egeria asked her, through the bishop, who spoke Syriac. "The one Jacob moved?"

The girl looked at the stone, which she had seen every day of her life, and which was mostly useful for sitting on.

"Yes," she said. "Everybody knows that."

"And could anyone move it now?"

The girl considered the stone, and then considered Egeria, and then considered the bishop, who was old, and thin, and had once been in exile.

"No," she said, kindly. "But you don't need to. We have a bucket."

And she let down the bucket, and began to water her goats, who were much less patient than Rachel's sheep, and much louder. And Egeria went and sat on the stone, which is, I suppose, what stones are for, and watched.

There was nobody living around the well, Egeria says, except the clergy of the church, and the monks who had their cells nearby. And the bishop told her about their lives, and their lives, she says, were truly unheard of.

And then, she says, after praying in the church, she went with the bishop to visit the holy monks, one after another, in their monasteries. And they received her, she says, gladly, wherever she went in, and spoke with her the kind of words that were fitting to come from their mouths. And they gave gifts, to her and to all who were with her, which, she says, is the custom of monks, for those they welcome gladly into their monasteries.

The word Egeria uses for these gifts is eulogiae, which means blessings. A monk's blessing, in this sense, was usually something small, and simple, and edible. A little bread. Some dried fruit. A handful of something from the garden. When she climbed down from Mount Sinai, years before, the monks there had given her fruit from the trees they grew on the mountain. Here, I imagine, it was bread, and perhaps a little cheese, and perhaps, it being spring, some young green things picked that morning. Small gifts, given by people who had almost nothing, to a stranger who had come a very long way. That is what a blessing is, really.

And then the bishop pointed across the plain.

The well, Egeria explains, stands in a great open plain, and opposite it, about five hundred paces away, there was a very large village, which they went through on their way. And this village, the bishop said, had once been the farm of Laban the Syrian. It was called Fadana.

Fadana. If you know your Genesis, that name may ring a faint bell. Genesis calls the country Jacob came to Paddan-aram. Fadana is the same name, worn smooth by many centuries of people saying it. So the village had kept, in its own name, the name of the country in the story.

And in the village, Egeria says, she was shown the tomb of Laban the Syrian, Jacob's father-in-law. And she was shown the place where Rachel stole her father's idols.

That last one needs a word of explanation, and it is a slightly comical story, so it is a good one to end with.

Jacob stayed with Laban for twenty years, all told, and he married Rachel, and her sister Leah too, which is a long story, and Laban did not always treat him fairly, which is a longer one. And at last, God told Jacob to go home. And Jacob gathered up his wives and his children and his flocks, and left, while Laban was away shearing his sheep. Without saying goodbye. Which was, admittedly, not very polite.

And as they were leaving, Rachel, without telling Jacob, took her father's household gods. Little idols, the sort of thing a household kept, in those days, in a corner of the house.

Laban was furious. He chased after them, for seven days, and caught them up in the hill country. And he said to Jacob, why did you run away? Why didn't you let me kiss my grandchildren goodbye? And why, he said, did you steal my gods?

Jacob, who did not know, said, whoever has your gods shall not live. Search everything.

So Laban searched. He searched Jacob's tent, and Leah's tent, and the tents of the two maids. And then he came to Rachel's tent.

And Rachel had hidden the idols in her camel's saddle, and she was sitting on it. And she said, very sweetly, "Please don't be angry, my lord, that I can't get up for you. I'm not feeling very well."

And Laban searched the whole tent, and did not find them. Because they were underneath his daughter.

I have always thought there is something wonderful about this. Here are these little gods that Laban is so frightened of losing, that he chases his family for a week across the hills to get them back. And where are they? Stuffed in a saddle, being sat on. The book does not say anything about it. It does not need to. It just tells you where the gods were, and lets you think about what kind of gods they must be.

And the God of Abraham, who had called him out of this very country, out of the land of the moon and the household gods, was not in a saddle. He was the one who had said to Jacob, go home, and I will be with you.

Egeria stood in the place where all that was said to have happened, and I think she smiled.

And then, she says, having seen everything, in the name of God, she said goodbye to the holy bishop, and to the holy monks who had come with her all that way, and she turned round, and went back. By the same road, and the same stages, that she had come by from Antioch.

In Antioch she stayed a week, while everything she needed for the journey was got ready. And then she went west. Into Cilicia, to Tarsus, the city of Saint Paul, and from there, because it was close, to the shrine of Saint Thecla, where, to her great joy, she found her dear friend Marthana. And that is a story we have told already.

After that, the letter goes on to Constantinople, and then it turns to something else entirely, a long and wonderful description of how the Christians of Jerusalem kept the feasts of the year, and then it breaks off, and we never hear the end. We do not know whether she reached home. I hope she did. I hope she sat down with her sisters, and handed round the copies of the letters from Edessa, and told them about the fish, and the stone, and the girl with the goats. And about the night the monks went home with their lamps.

And Harran?

Harran stayed true to the moon for a very long time. Long after Egeria, long after the Romans had gone, the city was still famous across the East as the last great city of the old pagan faith, and its scholars were known for their learning in the stars. Later still, it became a city of a different faith, with a great mosque and a tall square tower, and later still it was ruined, and the ruins are still there, out on the plain, with the beehive houses gathered round them.

Nobody now can tell you where the church on Abraham's house was. Nobody can show you, for certain, the well where Rebekah filled her jar ten times over, or the well where Jacob rolled away the stone, or the stone itself. Or the village called Fadana, or the place where Rachel sat very still on a saddle and said she was not feeling well.

But the plain is still there. And in April it still goes green. And the larks still go straight up into the sky and come down again.

And on a clear night, the moon still rises over Harran, round and bright, just as it did in Abraham's time, the moon the people of that city once thought was a god. And somewhere under that same moon, a very long time ago, a man of seventy-five heard a voice that was not the moon's, telling him to go. And he went.

I like to think of Egeria, on her last night in Harran, standing in the doorway of the church, looking up at it.

And of an old man called Hanina, who never existed, walking home across the plain in the dark, with his lamp, to his cave, and his spring, and his lizards.

And of all of them, the patriarchs and the pilgrims and the hermits, going out, one after another, into the dark, because they had been called. Not knowing quite where they were going. But knowing who had called them.

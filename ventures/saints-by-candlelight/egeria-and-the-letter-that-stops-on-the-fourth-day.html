It is night, on the Mount of Olives, and the whole city of Jerusalem is walking downhill.

Not quickly. Very, very slowly. Thousands of people, men and women and children and old monks and bishops and babies asleep on their mothers' shoulders, are coming down the dark hillside together, singing, at about the speed of a tortoise who has had a large lunch.

They have been up since before cockcrow. They have been praying in one holy place after another since before dawn, and they still have four to go. Somewhere near the back of the crowd, a small boy is asleep on the shoulders of a very large monk, and the monk is singing the psalm with his eyes half shut.

And at the bottom of the hill, at the gate of the city, a line of lights is coming out to meet them. Candles. A great many candles. Somebody has been counting them, and has decided there are about two hundred.

And a woman from the far west of the world, walking in the middle of it all, with sore feet and a happy heart, is thinking that she must write this down.

So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

If you have been with us for a while, you will know Egeria well by now. She is the pilgrim who climbed Mount Sinai, looked out from Mount Nebo, stood at the gate of Edessa, and kept the Great Week before Easter in Jerusalem. Last time, we watched with her as the lamp was carried out of the tomb at nightfall, and the little boys shouted Kyrie eleison.

If you have not met her, here is all you need to know. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, very probably from the north-west corner of Spain. Some time around the year three hundred and eighty, she travelled east to see the places of the Bible with her own eyes. She stayed in Jerusalem for about three years. And she wrote it all down, in a long, careful, and very polite letter to a group of women at home whom she calls her sisters, or, when she is being especially formal, "ladies, my sisters", or "your affection".

Tonight we come to the end of her letter. Or at least, to the end of what we have of it. Because the letter does not finish the way letters are supposed to finish. There is no "with love from Egeria". There is no "pray for me". It stops. In the middle of a sentence. On the fourth day of a feast.

We will come to that at the very end, when you are nearly asleep. It is not a sad thing, I promise. But it is a strange one, and I have been thinking about it all week.

First, though, there are fifty days to get through. And one of them is the longest day of the year.

Let me give you the map again, quickly, because tonight we are going to use all of it.

In the middle of Jerusalem stood the buildings round the place where Christ was crucified and buried. At the western end was a great round church over the tomb, which they called the Anastasis, the Greek word for resurrection. In the middle of it was the cave of the tomb, with railings round it. East of that was an open courtyard with a rock and a cross on it, which Egeria simply calls the Cross. And east of that was the great church, the basilica, with its marble and its columns, which they called the Martyrium. Its main doors opened onto the busy market street of the city, which matters for the end of the long day.

Then, on the southern hill of the city, there was a church called Sion. The Christians of Jerusalem believed it stood where the apostles had been gathered together in the days after the Resurrection, in the upstairs room, behind the locked door.

And outside the city, to the east, across a little valley, rose the Mount of Olives. On its slope was a church they called the Eleona, which is a sort of Greek for "the olive place". Inside it was a cave, where, the people of Jerusalem said, the Lord used to sit and teach his disciples. And further up, right at the top, was an open place called the Imbomon, which means something like "on the height". That, they said, was the place from which the Lord ascended into heaven.

So. The tomb, the courtyard, the great church. Sion on its hill. The cave on the Mount of Olives, and the hilltop above it. If you can hold those in your head, you can walk round Jerusalem with Egeria tonight. And if you can't, don't worry. Just follow the crowd. Everyone else is.

Now for some company.

If you were with us last time, you will remember that I gave Egeria a lodging house. It was kept by a widow called Theodora, who had strong arms and no patience for people who got up late. Theodora had a son called Zenon, who sang with the little boys at lamp-lighting, very loudly, and who knew exactly three words of Latin, which were "yes", "no" and "bread". And there was an old monk who came in sometimes for soup, called Brother Abraham, who walked more slowly than anyone in Jerusalem.

I made all of them up. Egeria never tells us where she stayed, or who she talked to over supper. But I have grown fond of them, and I don't think she would mind them coming along.

By the time of tonight's story, it is spring. Easter has come and gone. Zenon is a little older, and has added a fourth word of Latin, which is "Alleluia". Strictly speaking that is not Latin at all, it is Hebrew, but he uses it in every sentence, so nobody has the heart to tell him.

The first week after Easter, Egeria tells us, was kept exactly the way it was kept at home. "The eight days of Easter," she writes, "are kept just as they are with us." I like that very much. After all the strange and wonderful things she has described, all the processions and vigils and customs her sisters had never heard of, here is something she can simply say is the same. Somewhere in Spain, the sisters reading the letter must have nodded. Good. That bit we know.

But there were differences, of course. The churches were decorated all that week as they had been at Epiphany, with gold and jewels and silk and great candlesticks. And each day there was a different church for the main service. The great church on Sunday, Monday and Tuesday. The Eleona on the Mount of Olives on Wednesday. The Anastasis on Thursday. Sion on Friday. Before the Cross on Saturday. And back to the great church on the next Sunday, the eighth day.

And every afternoon of those eight days, after lunch, there was a walk.

The bishop went up the Mount of Olives, with all the clergy, and with all the newly baptised, the ones who had come up out of the water on Easter night. Egeria calls them the infantes, the infants, which is a lovely word for grown men and women in brand-new white clothes. And with them went the monks and the nuns, and as many of the ordinary people as wanted to. They sang hymns in the cave church. They prayed at the hilltop. And then they came down again, singing, in time for lamp-lighting at the tomb.

Every day for eight days. Up the hill, and down again.

"Brother Abraham," said Theodora, on about the third afternoon, "you are not going up that hill again."

"I am," said Brother Abraham.

"It took you until dark yesterday."

"It did," said Brother Abraham, very pleased. "And I saw every olive tree on the way."

On the evening of Easter Day itself, and again on the eighth day, the crowd went to Sion. And there, Egeria says, they read the Gospel that belonged to that very place, on that very day.

It is in the Gospel of Saint John, and it goes like this.

On the evening of that first day of the week, the disciples were together in a room, with the doors shut, because they were afraid. And Jesus came, and stood among them, and said, "Peace be with you." And he showed them his hands and his side. And the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord.

But one of them was not there. Thomas, called the Twin. And when the others told him, "We have seen the Lord", Thomas said, "Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger into the mark of the nails, and my hand into his side, I will not believe."

Egeria gives us his words in her own Latin, very short. Non credo, nisi videro. I will not believe, unless I see.

And then, on the eighth day, the following Sunday, they went back to Sion and read the rest. A week later, the disciples were in the room again, and this time Thomas was with them. And the doors were shut. And Jesus came and stood among them, and said, "Peace be with you." And he said to Thomas, "Put your finger here, and see my hands. Put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not be unbelieving, but believe." And Thomas answered him, "My Lord, and my God." And Jesus said, "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen, and yet have believed."

That last sentence, I think, must have landed rather strangely in Jerusalem. Here was a whole city full of pilgrims who had come thousands of miles precisely in order to see. To stand in the very places. To touch the rock. And here was the Lord himself, in the very place, saying that the ones who are really blessed are the ones who believe without seeing.

Which means, if you think about it, Egeria's sisters, sitting at home in Spain, reading her letter, who had never seen any of it, and who were never going to. Blessed are they.

I don't know whether Egeria noticed that. She doesn't say. But she wrote the letter for them, so that they could see a little, with her eyes. And I think she would have been glad to know that the Lord had already said a kind word about them, in the room on Sion, on the eighth day.

Then came the weeks between Easter and Pentecost. And about these, Egeria has one fact that she clearly finds remarkable, because she says it very firmly.

"From Easter to the fiftieth day, that is, Pentecost, absolutely nobody fasts here. Not even the monks."

Now, you have to understand how surprising that would have been. The monks of Jerusalem were famous fasters. In Lent, Egeria tells us elsewhere in her letter, some of them ate only twice a week, on the Saturday and the Sunday. They were the sort of people who regarded a second olive as an indulgence. And yet for fifty days, every single one of them stopped. No fasting. Not on Wednesdays, not on Fridays, not at all. Easter was a feast, and you do not fast at a feast. Even the Wednesday and Friday services, which were usually held in the afternoon after a morning without food, were moved to the morning at Sion, so that everyone could go home and have lunch.

"This is very difficult for Brother Abraham," Theodora said, putting a bowl of lentils in front of him.

"It is a discipline," said Brother Abraham, gravely, and ate the lentils. And then asked if there was any honey.

On the fortieth day after Easter, which was a Thursday, something happened that has puzzled scholars ever since.

Egeria says that on the day before, the Wednesday, straight after noon, everyone went to Bethlehem. That is a walk of about six miles, south, over the hills. They kept the vigil all night in the church at Bethlehem, the one with the cave where the Lord was born. And the next day there was the service there, with the priests and the bishop preaching, "in a way that fitted the day and the place". And then, late in the day, everyone walked back to Jerusalem.

Now, you might expect that on the fortieth day after Easter they would have been remembering the Ascension, because the Acts of the Apostles says the risen Lord appeared to the apostles for forty days. And today, in both East and West, the Ascension is kept on the fortieth day, on a Thursday, exactly as you would think. And you might expect them to keep it on the Mount of Olives, where the Ascension happened, not at Bethlehem, where the Lord was born.

But Egeria does not say what they were remembering at Bethlehem. She does not say it was the Ascension. She just says it was the fortieth day, and they went to Bethlehem, and that it was all very fitting. And scholars have been arguing about why ever since. Some think it was the Ascension, kept at Bethlehem for some reason we can no longer see. Some think it was another feast entirely, which happened to fall on that day in that year. Some think it may have been the anniversary of the church at Bethlehem.

I don't know. Nobody knows. And Egeria, who was there, and who could have told us in a single sentence, simply assumed that we would understand. Which is a thing all letter-writers do, and which is one of the reasons historians lie awake at night.

What I can tell you is that Zenon walked to Bethlehem and back again, all twelve miles, and slept for most of Friday. And that when he woke up, he said, "Alleluia. Bread."

And so we come to Pentecost. The fiftieth day.

Egeria begins her account of it with a sentence that every parent of small children will recognise, and every parish priest, and anyone who has ever organised anything at all. "On the fiftieth day," she writes, "which is a Sunday, the day of the greatest labour for the people."

The greatest labour. Maximus labor. She is not complaining. She sounds, if anything, rather proud. But she wants her sisters to understand that this was a very, very long day. So let me take you through it the way she does, one hour at a time. And if you fall asleep somewhere on the Mount of Olives, that is entirely appropriate. So did half of Jerusalem.

It began, as every Sunday began in Jerusalem, in the dark.

At the first cockcrow, the doors of the Anastasis were opened, and the people came in and kept the vigil, singing psalms and hymns, until the bishop came and read, at the door of the tomb, the Gospel of the Resurrection, which was read there every Sunday. Then the usual hymns and prayers, until daylight.

Theodora was there, with her basket and her blanket. Zenon was there, asleep in his mother's lap. Egeria was there, with her notebook, or so I like to imagine. And Brother Abraham was there, because he had set out the night before.

When morning came, everyone moved into the great church, the Martyrium, for the Sunday service. The priests preached, one after another, the way they always did in Jerusalem, and then the bishop preached, and the offering was made, the Eucharist, just as on every Sunday.

But today, Egeria says, the service in the great church was hurried. They hurried it so that it would be finished before the third hour, about nine in the morning. And as soon as it was over, the whole people, every last one of them, led the bishop, with hymns, across the city and up to Sion. They had to be at Sion by the third hour exactly.

And why the third hour? Because of what they were going to read when they got there.

It is in the second chapter of the Acts of the Apostles, and on the day of Pentecost, in the church on Sion, they read it in the place where the people of Jerusalem believed it had happened.

When the day of Pentecost had come, the apostles were all together in one place. And suddenly a sound came from heaven, like the rush of a mighty wind, and it filled the whole house where they were sitting. And there appeared to them tongues as of fire, resting on each one of them. And they were all filled with the Holy Spirit, and began to speak in other tongues, as the Spirit gave them the words.

Now there were staying in Jerusalem devout men from every nation under heaven. And at this sound the crowd came together, and they were amazed, because each one heard them speaking in his own language. Parthians and Medes and Elamites, people from Mesopotamia, Judea and Cappadocia, Pontus and Asia, Phrygia and Pamphylia, Egypt and the parts of Libya near Cyrene, visitors from Rome, Cretans and Arabians. "We hear them telling in our own tongues the mighty works of God." And some of them said, "What does this mean?" And others laughed, and said, "They are full of new wine."

To which, a few verses later, Saint Peter gave what I think is one of the great replies of the whole Bible. "These men are not drunk, as you suppose. It is only the third hour of the day."

Nine o'clock in the morning. Far too early, said Peter, for anyone to be drunk. And so the Christians of Jerusalem, three hundred and fifty years later, made very sure that they were standing in that very place at that very hour. If the Spirit came at the third hour, they would be there at the third hour. Not a minute late. Even if it meant hurrying the bishop's sermon.

Egeria puts it in her own way. "There is read that passage from the Acts of the Apostles where the Spirit came down, so that all languages understood what was being said." And then she explains, for her sisters, why they read it there. "Because this is the very place on Sion, though it is a different church now, where once, after the Lord's passion, the crowd was gathered with the apostles, when this happened."

It is a different church now. I love that she says that. She is not pretending that the room is still the room. She knows that the building has changed. But the place is the place. And the hour is the hour. And the reading is the reading. And that was enough.

I want to stop here for a moment, because there is something in Egeria's letter that belongs to this very morning, although she writes about it a few pages later.

All those languages. Parthians and Medes and Elamites. You will remember that Jerusalem in Egeria's day was a city of pilgrims, from everywhere. And Egeria tells us exactly how they managed.

The bishop, she says, knew Syriac, the language of the ordinary people of the countryside round about. But he always preached in Greek. Never in Syriac. Greek was the language of the church in Jerusalem, and of educated people, and of the whole eastern half of the Empire. So, Egeria says, there always stood beside the bishop a priest, who, as the bishop spoke in Greek, translated it into Syriac, so that everyone could understand.

And the readings, too, which had to be read in Greek. There was always someone standing by to translate them into Syriac, for the people, so that they should always be learning.

"And as for the Latins here," she writes, "that is, the ones who know neither Syriac nor Greek, so that they shouldn't be sad, it is explained to them as well. Because there are other brothers and sisters, who are Greek-Latins, and who explain it to them in Latin."

So that they shouldn't be sad. Ne contristentur. That is the reason she gives. Not so that they should be properly informed, or so that the liturgy should be correct. So that the people who couldn't understand should not feel left out, and sad.

I picture it like this. The bishop preaching in Greek. A priest beside him, saying the same thing again in Syriac. And somewhere in the crowd, a little knot of people from Gaul and Spain and Italy, with a bilingual brother or sister in the middle of them, whispering it all a third time, in Latin. Three languages at once. A low murmur, all over the church, like a beehive.

And it occurs to me that on the morning of Pentecost, of all mornings, in the church on Sion, of all places, they were reading about every nation hearing the mighty works of God in its own tongue, while, all round the church, people were doing their very best to make sure that everyone did.

It was not a miracle, the way the first Pentecost was a miracle. It was just people being kind, in three languages, every Sunday. But I don't think it was nothing, either.

Zenon, of course, spoke Greek to his mother and Syriac to his friends, and was very proud of it, and offered to translate for Egeria all day long. His translations were not always reliable. When she asked him what the bishop had just said, he usually told her it was "Alleluia".

After the reading of the Spirit, the service went on at Sion, and the offering was made there too, a second Eucharist that morning. And then, as the people were about to be sent away, the archdeacon stood up and raised his voice and made an announcement. Egeria gives us his exact words, as if she had heard him say it.

"Today, straight after the sixth hour, let us all be ready at the Eleona, at the Imbomon."

Straight after noon. On the Mount of Olives. At the top.

And so, Egeria says, everyone went home to rest a little. To have something to eat. And straight after lunch, they climbed the Mount of Olives, each person as best they could. And then she says something that I find wonderful. "So that no Christian remains in the city. They all go."

No Christian left in the city. Think about that. A whole city, empty, on a Sunday afternoon, its shops shut, its streets quiet, except for the dogs and the pigeons and perhaps a few puzzled visitors wondering where everybody had gone. And on the hill across the valley, the whole of Christian Jerusalem, climbing slowly through the olive trees in the afternoon sun.

Theodora went up with a basket on her arm. There was bread in it, and cheese, and a jar of water, and a second blanket, because she was a sensible woman and she knew how this day ended.

Zenon ran ahead, and came back, and ran ahead again, and came back again, so that by the top of the hill he had climbed it about four times.

And Brother Abraham walked. Very slowly. At the back. Singing.

Let us say that halfway up he was overtaken by a spice-seller's donkey, which had no religious opinions at all and was simply trying to get home. And that the donkey, too, was going slowly, so that for a good half mile the old monk and the donkey walked side by side, at exactly the same speed. Brother Abraham sang the psalms to it. The donkey, I am told, made no objection.

At the top of the hill, at the Imbomon, the place from which the Lord ascended, the bishop sat down, with the priests, and all the people sat down too, on the grass and the rocks and each other's cloaks.

And there, Egeria says, they read the lessons, with hymns between them, and antiphons, all fitting that day and that place. And they read the passage from the Gospel which tells of the Lord's ascension. And then they read, once more, from the Acts of the Apostles, the part that tells of the Lord ascending into heaven after the Resurrection.

It is in the first chapter of Acts. And on that hilltop, in the afternoon, it must have sounded very close indeed.

The risen Lord was with the apostles on the Mount of Olives. And he told them that they would receive power, when the Holy Spirit came upon them, and that they would be his witnesses, in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth. And when he had said this, as they were looking on, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight.

And while they were gazing into heaven as he went, two men stood by them in white robes, and said, "Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking into heaven? This Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven." Then they returned to Jerusalem, from the mount called Olivet, which is near Jerusalem, a Sabbath day's journey away.

So that is where they were. On the very hill. And on the morning of the same day, they had heard about the Spirit coming down, on Sion, at the third hour. And now, in the afternoon, they heard about the Lord going up, from here. Coming down, and going up, both on the same day. As if the whole story of those fifty days was being told all at once, from beginning to end, in the places where it happened, by people walking from one to the next.

Scholars have noticed that in Egeria's Jerusalem, the Ascension seems to have been remembered here, on the afternoon of Pentecost, rather than on a separate day. Later, the church in Jerusalem, like everyone else, kept it on the fortieth day, as we do now. But on the day Egeria saw, it was all one great day.

The sun began to go down. The catechumens were blessed, and then the faithful. And at the ninth hour, the middle of the afternoon, they all went down the hill a little way, singing, to the Eleona, the church with the cave where the Lord had sat and taught his apostles. By the time they got there, Egeria says, it was already after the tenth hour.

They were not hurrying, you see.

In the cave church on the Mount of Olives, they kept the lamp-lighting, the evening service, with its prayers and its lights. The catechumens were blessed, and then the faithful.

And then, as the dark came on, they began to go down the hill towards the city. All of them. Every last one, Egeria says, with the bishop, singing hymns and antiphons that fitted the day.

And now comes the sentence that I have been waiting all evening to tell you. Here it is, in Egeria's own words, as plainly as I can put them.

"And so they come slowly, slowly, all the way to the Martyrium."

Lente et lente. Slowly and slowly. And when they get to the gate, she explains why. "Because it is a long way from the gate to the great church, they get there at about the second hour of the night. Because they go slowly, slowly the whole way, for the people's sake, so that their feet should not be tired."

So that their feet should not be tired.

I have read a great many old descriptions of great religious processions. Kings and emperors, banners and trumpets. And I don't think I have ever read one that stops, in the middle of the glory, to say that they went slowly so that people's feet wouldn't hurt.

But that is what the bishop of Jerusalem did. On the longest day of the year, after a night vigil and two services and a climb and a hilltop and a cave, he walked down the Mount of Olives at the pace of the slowest pilgrim. At the pace of the grandmothers and the small children and the old monks. At the pace, I like to think, of Brother Abraham.

Brother Abraham, for once in his life, was not at the back.

"Do you see?" he said to Egeria, delighted, as they came down through the olive trees in the dusk. "Everyone is walking at my speed. I have been telling them for years."

And somewhere about halfway down, Zenon, who had climbed the hill four times, and sung at every station, and translated a great many things into "Alleluia", sat down quite suddenly in the middle of the road and announced that his legs had stopped.

His mother looked at him. Egeria looked at him. And a monk from somewhere far up the Nile, a very large and very quiet man with a beard like a bird's nest, who had been walking beside them, and whom none of them had ever met before, bent down without a word, and lifted Zenon up onto his shoulders, and walked on.

Zenon was asleep in about a minute.

When they came to the gate of the city, it was night. And there, Egeria says, the candles came out to meet them. The church candles. About two hundred of them, by her reckoning, though the words in the old manuscript are a little uncertain, and scholars argue over whether she said two hundred or something else. Brought out, she says, for the sake of the people.

So picture it. The dark hill behind them. The dark city in front. And at the gate, suddenly, a river of small flames, coming out to light the way home.

From the gate it was still a long way to the great church. They went slowly, slowly, through the dark streets, with the candles. And when they reached the Martyrium, Egeria says, the great doors were opened, the ones that face the market street, and the whole people came in, with hymns, with the bishop.

The shops were shut. The market was empty. And through the great doors of the great church went the whole of Christian Jerusalem, singing, carrying lights.

Inside, there were hymns, and a prayer, and the catechumens were blessed, and then the faithful. And then on again, with hymns, to the Anastasis, the round church over the tomb. Hymns, a prayer, the blessings. And then to the Cross, in the courtyard. Hymns, a prayer, the blessings.

And then, believe it or not, they were not finished. Because from the Cross, Egeria says, the whole Christian people, every last one, led the bishop with hymns all the way back up to Sion.

Sion, where they had been that morning. Where the Spirit had come down, at the third hour. They went back to it, in the dark, to end the day where the day's great story had begun.

At Sion they read the fitting lessons, and sang psalms and antiphons, and prayed, and the catechumens were blessed, and then the faithful. And the people were sent home. And as they left, every single person came up to the bishop's hand, as they always did in Jerusalem, for his blessing.

"And so," Egeria says, "each one goes back to their own house at about midnight."

And then she adds a sentence of her own, a summing-up, and I think there is a little sigh in it. "And so the greatest labour is borne on that day. Because they have kept vigil at the Anastasis from first cockcrow, and from then on, all through the day, they have never stopped."

From cockcrow to midnight. Church after church, a hilltop and a cave, two hills and one city. And all of it, every step, slowly, slowly, so that nobody's feet should be tired.

Theodora carried her basket home. The blanket had been used. The bread had been eaten. The large quiet monk from the Nile brought Zenon to the door of the lodging house and handed him over, still asleep, like a sack of flour, and bowed, and went away into the dark. And Egeria went up to her room, and took off her shoes, and sat on the edge of her bed for a long time without moving. And then she got out her pen.

The next morning, Egeria says, the fast began again.

"From the next day after the fiftieth day, everyone fasts, as is the custom throughout the year, each as they are able, except on the Sabbath and the Lord's day, on which there is never fasting in these places." And everything else went back to its ordinary pattern. The doors of the Anastasis opened at cockcrow, every day. The monks were there for the night hymns, all of them. The ordinary people came as they could. The clergy took it in turns. And the bishop came at first light for the morning prayer.

Brother Abraham was given a very small bowl of lentils, and no honey at all. He looked at the bowl for some time.

"It is a discipline," Theodora reminded him.

"It is," said Brother Abraham, sadly, and ate it.

Now there is one more thing from those fifty days that Egeria wants her sisters to know about, and it belongs to the first week after Easter. She puts it near the end of the letter, almost as an afterthought, but I don't think it was an afterthought to her.

You will remember, if you heard our story about Saint Cyril of Jerusalem, that before Easter the people preparing for baptism were taught for forty days, three hours every morning, in the great church. Egeria describes that too. But she says that, before Easter, there were certain things they were not yet told. The mysteries themselves. What baptism is, what the anointing means, what happens at the altar. Those had to wait until after they were baptised.

So in the eight days after Easter, every day, after the morning service, everybody went with hymns to the Anastasis. And there, she says, the bishop stood in the cave of the tomb itself, leaning on the inner railing, and explained to the newly baptised everything that had been done to them on Easter night.

The doors were shut. No catechumens were allowed in, only the newly baptised, and the faithful who wished to hear. And so the explaining happened behind closed doors, in the round church, over the tomb.

But the doors did not keep everything in. Because, Egeria says, as the bishop explained each thing, and told the story of it, there was such a noise of people praising, of the crowd calling out in approval, that their voices could be heard far outside the church.

"He unfolds all the mysteries in such a way," she writes, "that nobody could fail to be moved by what they hear explained like this."

I like to imagine Zenon sitting on the step outside with the other boys, because at that hour the doors were shut. And hearing, through the thick walls of the round church, a sudden great roar of voices. And then quiet. And then another roar.

"What are they doing in there?" one of the boys asks.

And Zenon, who did not know either, but who never in his life admitted to not knowing anything, says, with great confidence, "Alleluia."

And then Egeria says something, a little paragraph on its own, which I think may be the heart of her whole letter. She has been describing it all for pages and pages, the churches and the hours and the processions. And then she stops, and tells her sisters what she loved most.

"This, above all, is very pleasing here, and very wonderful. That the hymns and the antiphons, and the readings, and the prayers the bishop says, always have words that fit the day that is being kept, and the place where it is being done. Always fitting. Always suitable."

That's it. That's what moved her most. Not the gold, though she has told us about the gold. Not the crowds, though she has told us about the crowds. But that in Jerusalem, everything fitted. The Resurrection read at the tomb. Thomas read in the room with the shut doors. The Spirit read on Sion at the third hour. The Ascension read on the hilltop. Every word in its own place, at its own time.

And I wonder if she was thinking, as she wrote it, about her own sisters at home. Who could not go to the tomb, or to Sion, or to the hilltop. And who would read the same words, the same Gospels, on the same days, in a little church somewhere in Spain, a long way from anywhere in particular. The words did not belong only to Jerusalem. That was the whole point. They had come from there, and gone out to the ends of the earth, exactly as the Lord had said they would, on the Mount of Olives. All Egeria could do was to tell her sisters what it was like to hear them at home.

And so we come to the autumn. And the last thing in Egeria's letter.

It is a feast called the Encaenia. That is a Greek word that means "the dedication", or more exactly, "the making new". It was the anniversary of the day when the two great churches, the Martyrium and the Anastasis, had been consecrated to God. And it was kept, Egeria says, with the highest honour of all. Because, she says, the Cross of the Lord had been found on that very same day.

They had arranged it on purpose, she explains. When the churches were first consecrated, they chose the day on which the Cross had been found, so that both could be celebrated together, with all joy, on the same day. That was the tradition of the Jerusalem church, and she tells it as she heard it.

And then she adds something that shows you what kind of reader of Scripture she was. "And it is found in the holy Scriptures," she writes, "that this was the day of dedication on which holy Solomon, when he had finished the house of God that he had built, stood before the altar of God and prayed, as it is written in the books of Paralipomenon."

Paralipomenon is the old Greek and Latin name for the books we call Chronicles. It means something like "the things left out", because the books of Chronicles tell again some of the stories from the books of Kings, with extra details that Kings had left out. And in the Second Book of Chronicles, King Solomon, when the Temple is finished, stands before the altar, in front of all the people, and spreads out his hands towards heaven, and prays. "But will God indeed dwell with man on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you. How much less this house that I have built." And when he has finished praying, the glory of the Lord fills the house. And the dedication is kept for seven days, and then the feast for seven days more, in the seventh month of the year, in the autumn.

So Egeria, standing in the great church in Jerusalem in the autumn, was thinking of Solomon in the Temple in the autumn. Heaven cannot contain you. How much less this house. And still, the house was dedicated, and still, God came.

Today, both Catholics and Orthodox keep the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross on the fourteenth of September. And the Orthodox Church still keeps, the day before, the dedication of the Church of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. That is this feast. Egeria's feast. Still going, sixteen hundred and forty years later.

The feast lasted eight days, Egeria says. And for many days before it began, people started to gather in Jerusalem from everywhere.

Monks, she says. Crowds of monks, from many provinces. From Mesopotamia. From Syria. From Egypt. And from the Thebaid, which is the far south of Egypt, up the Nile, where, she adds, there are a very great many monks. And not just monks, but people from every place and every province. "For there is no one," she writes, "who does not make their way to Jerusalem on those days, for such great joy, and for such honourable days." And ordinary men and women too, lay people, with faithful hearts, came from every province for the holy days.

And bishops. "Even when there are few of them," she says, "there are more than forty or fifty bishops in Jerusalem on those days. And with them come many of their clergy."

Even when there are few. Forty or fifty is a thin year. I find that very funny, and I think she meant it to be a little bit funny, too.

And then she says a thing that tells you how much this feast mattered to people. "And what more need I say? Anyone who has not been present at so great a feast in these days thinks they have committed a very great sin. Unless, of course, some necessity has kept them back from their good intention."

That last bit is very Egeria. She is a careful person. She doesn't want to frighten anybody. So she adds the exception. Unless you couldn't help it. Unless you had a good reason. Then, it's all right.

Theodora's lodging house was full. Every room was full, and the roof, and the yard. There were monks from Mesopotamia sleeping on the floor of the kitchen, and two deacons from somewhere in Syria in Egeria's room, very apologetic about it, and in the yard, under the fig tree, wrapped in his cloak, a very large and very quiet monk from the Thebaid with a beard like a bird's nest.

Zenon recognised him at once. He went out, in the morning, and sat beside him under the fig tree, and offered him some bread.

The monk took it, and smiled, and said nothing at all. And Zenon, who usually talked to everybody, said nothing either. They just sat there, the two of them, under the fig tree, eating bread, in the early morning, while the bells of Jerusalem began.

And so Egeria begins to describe the feast for her sisters. The churches were decorated, she says, just as they were at Easter and at Epiphany. Gold, and jewels, and silk, and candles. And each day there was a different church for the service, just as at Easter and Epiphany.

On the first day and the second, the service was in the great church, the Martyrium.

On the third day, it was at the Eleona, the church on the Mount of Olives, on the very hill from which the Lord ascended into heaven after his passion. And inside that church, she reminds her sisters, is the cave where the Lord used to teach his apostles, on the Mount of Olives.

And then she writes, "On the fourth day..."

And that is all.

That is where the letter stops. Not at the end of a page, or a chapter, or even a sentence. On the fourth day. Quarta autem die. And then nothing.

Here is why.

Egeria's letter was not kept in her own handwriting. The pages she sent home to Spain are long gone. What we have is a copy, made by a monk, by hand, about seven hundred years after she wrote it, in a monastery in Italy. One copy. Just one. It sat in a library for hundreds of years, and somewhere along the way, the first pages fell out of it, and so did the last pages, and so did a few from the middle. Nobody knows when. Nobody knows where they went.

And then, in eighteen eighty-four, a scholar called Gian Francesco Gamurrini, working in a library in the town of Arezzo, opened an old book and found it. A letter from a woman pilgrim, with no beginning and no end, describing Sinai and Edessa and Jerusalem, in a Latin that was not quite the Latin of the schoolbooks, but the everyday Latin that people actually spoke. He did not know who had written it. It was years before scholars matched it to a seventh-century letter by a Spanish monk called Valerius, who praised a pilgrim called Egeria, and realised who she was.

So the beginning of her journey is lost. And the end of her letter is lost. And what we have is the middle. Mountains and churches and vigils and lamps and little boys shouting Kyrie eleison, and a whole city walking slowly down a hill in the dark, and a feast with forty or fifty bishops in a thin year. And then, the fourth day.

We don't know what happened on the fourth day. I could guess. I could tell you which church they probably went to, and what they probably read, and I would probably be right. But I am not going to. Because she didn't tell us, or if she did, it is lost, and it would not be honest of me to put words in the gap. The fourth day belongs to her.

We don't know what happened to Egeria, either. Whether she went home to Spain. Whether she ever saw her sisters again. Earlier in her letter, she says that she was thinking of going on to Ephesus, to pray at the tomb of Saint John, and that if she was still alive after that, she would tell them about it, either by letter, or face to face, or, if God allowed, in some other place. And then she asks them to remember her.

That is the last we hear of her own plans. Not here, at the end, but in the middle of the letter, where the story of her travels finishes, at Constantinople, on her way home. Ephesus, and then, God willing, home. And after that, as a second part, the long description of Jerusalem's year, written down from memory and from her notes for her sisters. And then, the fourth day.

I said at the start that this was not a sad thing. Let me tell you why I think so.

Most of our lives, if you think about it, end on the fourth day. Not on the last day of anything. In the middle. With things still being decorated, and processions still to go, and churches still to visit, and letters still half written. Very few people get to put a neat full stop at the end of their story and say, "There. That's all of it."

Egeria didn't. But what she left us is so full of life, and so full of good things, that the stopping hardly matters. The fifty days were kept. The longest day was walked. The feast went on, with or without her pen. On the fifth day, and the sixth, and the seventh and the eighth, they went to the churches, and sang the hymns, and read the readings that fitted the day and the place. And the next year, they did it again. And the year after that.

And somewhere, if you like, there was a lodging house where a widow called Theodora swept out the kitchen after the monks had gone home, and an old monk walked slowly to the morning prayer, and a boy who knew four words of Latin, one of which was not Latin, sat under a fig tree and wondered where the big quiet man from the Nile had gone.

And in a church somewhere in Spain, a long way from Jerusalem, a group of women, reading a letter that had been read aloud so many times it was falling apart, came to the words, "On the fourth day", and looked at each other, and smiled, and knew exactly what came next. Because whatever it was, it was the same feast, and the same Lord, and the same words, that they were keeping too.

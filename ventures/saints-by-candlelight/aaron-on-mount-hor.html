Aaron on Mount Hor

In the fortieth year, at the edge of the land of Edom, the Lord tells Moses to take his brother Aaron and Aaron's son Eleazar up Mount Hor; three old and young men climb in the sight of the whole camp, the priest's robes are put on the son, Aaron dies on the top, two come down, and all Israel weeps for him thirty days. Told from the Book of Numbers, chapters twenty and thirty-three.

What's true in this story

Who
Aaron the priest, Moses, Eleazar son of Aaron, and the people of Israel camped at Mount Hor, with an invented woman who spun blue yarn for the priest's robe as a girl at Sinai
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Book of Numbers as Scripture; Moses is honoured as a saint and prophet in both traditions, and Aaron the priest is honoured among the holy forefathers
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty, verses twenty-two to twenty-nine, with chapter thirty-three, verses thirty-seven to thirty-nine, told straight in a plain rendering; the book gives Aaron no last words, says nothing of how he died or where he was buried on the mountain, and Deuteronomy chapter ten names the place of his death as Moserah, so none of that is settled; where Mount Hor was is uncertain and told as uncertain
Legend
none told as fact; the rabbinic saying that Aaron died by the kiss of the Lord is told as a saying of the rabbis in the Talmud, the identification of Mount Hor with the mountain near Petra is told as an old tradition that scholars still debate, and the Christian reading of Aaron's death in the Letter to the Hebrews is told as that letter's reading
What we invented
the woman Meira, who spun blue yarn for the robe at Sinai and watches the climb from the camp with her grandson, is our own invention, and so is her name; so are the words between Moses and Aaron on the last evening, the order in which the garments were taken off, and what the people said to one another while they watched; nothing is added to what the Lord, Moses, Aaron or Eleazar say or do in the book
Feast day
Moses the prophet is remembered on the fourth of September in the Orthodox Church and in the Roman Martyrology; the Orthodox Church remembers Aaron among the holy forefathers in the weeks before Christmas, and we give no calendar date for Aaron or Eleazar that we cannot vouch for

There is one very old tradition, as old as the first century at least, because the Jewish historian Josephus writes about it, that places it in the mountains near the city that the Greeks called Petra, the rose-red city carved into the rock, in what is now the country of Jordan. There is a tall mountain there that the people of the region have called, for a long time, Jabal Harun. The mountain of Aaron.

On the very top of it there is a small white shrine with a dome, a holy place for Muslims, who also honour Aaron, whom they call Harun, as a prophet and the brother of Moses. And on a high shoulder of the mountain, just below the top, there are the ruins of an old Christian monastery from the Byzantine centuries, dedicated to Saint Aaron, where monks lived and prayed and welcomed pilgrims who had climbed all the way up. Archaeologists from Finland have spent years digging it out of the rubble, very carefully, with little brushes.

Many scholars think that Jabal Harun is too far from Kadesh to be the Mount Hor of the book, and they look for it elsewhere, nearer the wilderness of Zin. And the book of Deuteronomy, in a short list of places, says that Aaron died and was buried at a place called Moserah, which may be a camp near the foot of the mountain, or may be another tradition altogether. We do not know. We have not settled it, and I don't think we need to.

But I like to think of those monks, high up on the mountain of Aaron, ringing their bell for prayers in the morning. Bells on a mountain again.

There is one more thing.

Christians have always read the Book of Numbers alongside the New Testament. And there is a letter in the New Testament, the Letter to the Hebrews, which spends a long time thinking about Aaron and the priesthood. It says that nobody takes the honour of being a priest for himself. He is called by God, just as Aaron was.

And then it says something that I think the people at the foot of Mount Hor would have understood very well. It says: the former priests were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing in office. But Christ holds his priesthood for ever, because he continues for ever. And so he is always able to save those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them.

Many priests, because each one had to take off the robe in the end, and pass it on, and go up the mountain. And one priest who never has to take it off.

The bells, you might say, never stop.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty, verses twenty-two to twenty-nine, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The journey from Kadesh, the Lord's words to Moses and Aaron at Mount Hor on the border of Edom, the climb in the sight of all the congregation, the garments taken from Aaron and put on Eleazar, Aaron's death on the top, the two coming down, and the thirty days of weeping are all the book's own, told plainly. Chapter thirty-three gives the date, the first day of the fifth month of the fortieth year, and Aaron's age, a hundred and twenty-three. The refusal of the king of Edom is in chapter twenty, verses fourteen to twenty-one.

The robes are described in Exodus, chapters twenty-eight and thirty-nine, and the women spinning blue yarn are in chapter thirty-five. That the garments pass to the son is Exodus, chapter twenty-nine. Moses's words about his brother's death are in Deuteronomy, chapter thirty-two, and Deuteronomy, chapter ten, names the place Moserah; we have not settled that. Eleazar's later life is in Numbers, chapter twenty-seven, and Joshua, chapters fourteen and twenty-four.

The saying about a kiss is from the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Bava Batra, and Hillel's words are in the Mishnah, tractate Avot. Both are later Jewish tradition, told as tradition. Josephus wrote of the place near Petra in his Jewish Antiquities. The words from Hebrews are chapters five and seven.

And the woman Meira, her blue thread, her grandson, the order in which the robes were taken off, and every word between Moses and Aaron on that last evening, are our own invention.

That's the story of Aaron on Mount Hor for tonight. Aaron the priest is remembered in the Orthodox Church among the holy forefathers in the weeks before Christmas, and his brother Moses on the fourth of September.

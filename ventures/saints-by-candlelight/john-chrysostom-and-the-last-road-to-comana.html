John Chrysostom and the Last Road to Comana

The greatest preacher of his age, a small, bald, stubborn man with a weak stomach, is made archbishop of the capital of the world, tells the rich to sell their silver, shelters the man who appointed him, is sent into exile twice, writes letters of comfort from the mountains, and dies on the road at a martyr's chapel saying Glory to God for all things. Saint John Chrysostom, told gently.

Full script

Imagine a mountain road, a very long time ago, at the end of summer.

It has been raining all afternoon. Not a gentle rain. A heavy, cold, mountain rain that comes sideways off the hills and runs down the road in little brown rivers.

Along the road walk three men. Two of them are soldiers, with cloaks and helmets and the bored, damp faces of soldiers everywhere who have been given a job they did not ask for. And between them walks an old man. He is small. He is very thin. He is quite bald, and the rain is running straight off the top of his head and into his eyes, because nobody has given him a hat.

He is sixty or so. He is ill, and has been ill for a long time. He walks slowly, and one of the soldiers keeps telling him to walk faster.

A few years before this, this little wet man stood in the greatest church in the greatest city in the world, and spoke, and thousands of people held their breath to listen. Some of them clapped so loudly that he had to ask them to stop.

Now he is walking to the end of the world, in the rain, with no hat.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the life and last journey of Saint John Chrysostom, told mostly from the book his friend Palladius wrote about him and from John's own letters, and a boy who keeps the lamps, the talk on the road, and most of the words people say are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Over the last few weeks on this show we have met three people from Cappadocia, in the middle of what is now Turkey. Saint Basil the Great, who ran a soup kitchen. His big sister Saint Macrina, who baked the bread and kept him humble. And Basil's best friend, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, who only ever wanted a quiet room with his books, and kept being put in charge of things.

The Orthodox Church keeps a feast at the end of January for three great teachers together. They call them the Three Holy Hierarchs. Two of them are Basil and Gregory. Tonight we meet the third.

He was not from Cappadocia. He came from further south, from the great city of Antioch, in what is now the very south of Turkey, near the border with Syria. And in his own lifetime he was simply called John. John the priest. Then John the bishop. It was only later, a good while after he died, that people began to call him by the name we know him by now. Chrysostom. It is Greek, and it means Golden Mouth.

He was given that name because of the way he preached. And I should say, at the start, that "golden mouth" does not mean he only said nice things. He said a great many things that people did not want to hear. He said them beautifully, which in some ways made it worse.

Antioch, when John was born there about the year three hundred and forty-nine, was one of the three or four biggest cities in the Roman Empire. It sat on a river, the Orontes, with a mountain behind it, and it was rich, and noisy, and enormously proud of itself. It had a main street that ran for miles, lined on both sides with covered colonnades, so that you could walk the length of the city in the shade, past shops and fountains and food stalls. At night, unusually for the ancient world, the main streets were lit with lamps. People from Antioch would tell you that their city never really slept. People from other cities would tell you that this was because nobody in Antioch had anything sensible to do in the morning.

It was also the city where, the Book of Acts tells us, the followers of Jesus were first called Christians. The people of Antioch never let anyone forget that either.

They loved chariot races. They loved the theatre. They loved a good argument, a good joke at someone else's expense, and a good speech. Above all, a good speech. Going to hear a famous speaker in Antioch was a little like going to a concert now. People went for the performance. They clapped the clever bits. They talked about it afterwards over dinner. And sometimes, it has to be said, they went to church for the same reason.

John's father was a senior officer in the army, a man called Secundus. And he died when John was a baby. So John was brought up by his mother, Anthusa, who had been widowed very young, at about twenty.

In those days a young, well-off widow was expected to marry again, and quickly. It was the sensible thing. It was what families arranged. Anthusa did not. She stayed a widow, and gave her whole life to bringing up her son and his older sister, and to running the family property, which she did very well, with stewards and servants and lawyers who all, one imagines, tried to take advantage of a young woman on her own and then rather wished they had not.

Many years later, John wrote that he had once had a teacher, a famous pagan teacher, who asked him about his mother. How old was she? How long had she been a widow? And when the teacher heard that she had been widowed at twenty, and had stayed faithful to her husband's memory for twenty years, he turned to the people around him and said, "Good heavens. What women these Christians have."

John does not name the teacher, but most people think it was Libanius. Libanius was the most famous teacher of public speaking in the whole eastern empire. He was not a Christian, and he was not shy about it. And John went to his school, and learned everything he had to teach. How to build an argument. How to hold a crowd. How to make people laugh, and then make them think, and then make them feel ashamed of themselves, all in the space of five minutes.

There is a story, told by the historian Sozomen a generation or so later, that when Libanius was dying, his friends asked him who should take over his school. And the old pagan said, "It ought to have been John. If only the Christians had not stolen him."

We cannot be sure he really said it. But it is the kind of thing people wanted to remember, and I think it tells us something true. The young man could have had a glittering career. Lawyer. Official. Speaker at the imperial court. He had the gift for all of it.

Instead, while he was still a young man, he was baptised by the bishop of Antioch, a gentle old man called Meletius. And he began to think, very seriously, about giving his whole life to God.

Now, in those days, if you were a young man in Antioch who wanted to give your whole life to God, there was a place you went. You went up into the hills.

The mountains behind Antioch were full of hermits and monks. Some lived in little communities. Some lived alone in caves. A few, later on, would even live on top of pillars, though the most famous of those had not been born yet.

John wanted to go. His mother did not want him to. In a book he wrote years later, he gives her a speech, a long, sad, loving speech, in which she takes him into her room, sits him on the bed where he was born, and begs him not to make her a widow a second time. "Wait," she says, more or less. "Wait until I am buried. Then go wherever you like."

We do not know how much of that speech is exactly what she said, and how much is John, the trained speaker, writing it up. But he waited. He lived at home, and prayed, and studied the Scriptures, and kept a kind of monastery in his own bedroom, which cannot have been easy for anyone else in the house.

Then, when he was in his middle twenties, he went up into the hills at last. He lived for about four years in a community, under an old monk from Syria. And then, Palladius tells us, he went off on his own, to a cave, for two more years.

And there he did something that I would ask you, very kindly, not to try at home. He hardly slept. He hardly ate. He stood up for much of the night, praying, so that he would not fall asleep. He learned great stretches of the Bible by heart. And in the cold and the damp of the cave, with no proper food, his body simply gave way. His stomach was ruined. His kidneys were damaged. He was ill, really ill, in ways that never quite went away for the rest of his life.

So he came down from the mountain, and went home to Antioch. Rather embarrassed, I imagine. He had gone up to be a hero of the desert, and come back with a bad stomach.

And this, I think, is where God's sense of humour comes in. Because if John had stayed in his cave, he would have been one more holy hermit in the hills, and very few people would ever have heard him. Instead, the bad stomach sent him back to the city. And in the city, there were people. Thousands and thousands of people. And they needed someone to talk to them.

He was made a deacon by Meletius, in the year three hundred and eighty-one, and then a priest, five years later, by the next bishop, Flavian. And Flavian, who was a wise man, looked at this thin, intense young priest with the ruined stomach and the extraordinary voice, and said, in effect, "You preach."

So John preached. For twelve years, in Antioch, he preached. Two or three times a week, sometimes every day. Long sermons, an hour or more, going through whole books of the Bible verse by verse. Matthew. John. Genesis. The letters of Saint Paul, which he loved more than anything. Somebody took the sermons down in shorthand as he spoke, and so hundreds of them still survive. If you put them all together on a shelf, you would need quite a long shelf.

People came in crowds. They pushed and shoved to get near the front, and pickpockets came too, because a crowd standing very still with its mouth open is a pickpocket's dream. John noticed this, and told them so from the pulpit. And people clapped. They clapped his sermons like a performance. And John, who was very human, liked it, and also knew it was a problem. He told them once that he wished they would stop clapping, and do what he said instead. And they clapped that too.

He was funny. People forget that about him. He would describe the people in his congregation so exactly that everyone would turn and look at someone. The man who spends the whole sermon thinking about his dinner. The lady who comes to church to show off her earrings. The people who will stand for hours in the sun to watch a chariot race and then complain that church is too long.

And then, in the year three hundred and eighty-seven, something happened that made him famous.

The emperor raised taxes. The people of Antioch, who did not like being told what to do, rioted. And in the middle of the riot, a crowd went to the public square and pulled down the statues of the emperor and his family, and dragged them through the streets.

This was a very, very bad idea. In the Roman Empire, the statues of the emperor were the emperor, as far as the law was concerned. To insult them was treason. And the emperor at the time was Theodosius, who had a temper. Everyone in Antioch woke up the next morning, looked at the broken statues, and realised that the whole city might be punished. Soldiers came. Arrests began. People fled into the hills. Nobody knew what would happen.

Old Bishop Flavian set off at once, in the middle of winter, on the long journey to Constantinople, to throw himself at the emperor's feet and beg for mercy. And while he was gone, it was Lent. And John preached. Every day, to a frightened city.

Those sermons still survive. They are called the Homilies on the Statues. And they are not what you might expect. He did not only say, "Don't worry." He said, "Look. You have been frightened by an earthly king. You have spent weeks terrified of what one man might do. Now think how little you have feared God. Think how much you have spent on parties, and how little on the poor." And then he would comfort them. He told them about the monks who had come down from the mountains to plead with the officials for the city. He told them to stop swearing oaths, which was a great habit of Antioch, and he set them homework. Literally. He told them to give up swearing for the week, and come back and tell him how it went.

And in the end, the emperor forgave the city. Flavian came home at Easter with the news. And John preached a sermon of thanksgiving, and I imagine there was quite a lot of clapping.

Now. You might think a man like that would spend the rest of his life in Antioch, preaching, until he was old and white-haired and everyone's favourite grandfather. And he might have. But in the autumn of the year three hundred and ninety-seven, the bishop of Constantinople died.

Constantinople was the new capital of the eastern empire. The emperor lived there, and the whole government, and a great many people with a great deal of money and influence and opinions. Choosing a bishop there was not a religious question only. It was politics. And the man who was really running the government at that moment, for a young and rather weak emperor called Arcadius, was a palace official called Eutropius, the chamberlain. Eutropius had heard John preach. And he decided that John would make an excellent bishop for the capital, and would make him, Eutropius, look very good indeed.

The difficulty was that the people of Antioch would never let John go. There would be a riot. And Antioch had already shown what it could do with a riot.

So, the historians tell us, the governor of Antioch sent a message to John, asking him to come and meet him at a chapel of the martyrs just outside the city gates. John went. And when he got there, they put him in a carriage and drove away with him, fast, all the way to Constantinople, changing horses as they went.

He was, in other words, kidnapped. Kindly, and for an excellent job, but kidnapped. I have always wondered whether he managed to pack.

And in February of the year three hundred and ninety-eight, he was consecrated bishop of Constantinople. The bishop who did the consecrating was Theophilus, the bishop of Alexandria in Egypt, a powerful, clever and very determined man, who had wanted someone else to get the job. Remember him. He comes back.

The new archbishop moved into the bishop's palace, looked around, and was horrified.

It was full of fine things. Marble, silk, silver, expensive furniture. John sold most of it, and gave the money to the hospitals. He started new hospitals, too, for the sick and for strangers who had nowhere to go. He looked at the accounts of the church, and cut out the waste. And he stopped giving grand dinners.

This was a scandal. The bishop of the capital was expected to entertain. Important people expected to be invited to dinner and to talk about important things over very good food. John ate alone, in his rooms, very plainly. Partly this was because he thought the money would be better spent on the poor. And partly, I suspect, it was the stomach. A man who has ruined his digestion in a cave in the hills does not want to sit through a twelve-course banquet with senators. But people took it badly. They said he was proud. They said he was unfriendly. They said he did not know how things were done in the capital.

And then he started preaching.

He preached exactly as he had preached in Antioch, only now the people in front of him were the richest people in the world. And he told them what he thought of their riches. He told them that every silk robe in their wardrobes could have clothed a poor family for a year. He told them, in one famous sermon, that he had heard that some of them had chamber pots made of silver. That is, the pots you kept under the bed for use in the night. Made of silver. While Christ, he said, was sitting outside the door of the church in rags, hungry.

He also told them to stop talking in church. He told the young men to stop going to the races during Holy Week. He told the clergy to stop living with housekeepers who were not their mothers or sisters, and to stop going to dinner parties to be fussed over by rich ladies. He told some of the monks of the city, who wandered about being admired, to go back to their monasteries and stay there.

The poor loved him. They crowded into the great church to hear him. The rich, some of them, loved him too, and were changed, and gave their money away. And a good many other people began, quietly, to make a list of their grievances.

Among the people who loved him most was a woman called Olympias.

She came from one of the noblest families in the capital. She had been married young, and widowed within two years, and she was enormously rich, and very beautiful, and everyone, including the emperor himself, had a plan for who she should marry next. She refused them all. She had been made a deaconess of the church, one of the women who served at the baptisms of women, cared for the sick and the poor, and looked after the women of the church. And she had founded a community of women right next door to the great church, and she was spending her fortune, as fast as she could, on the poor.

John found that she was giving money away so fast and so freely that people were taking advantage of her. And so he told her, which must have been strange for him, to slow down a little. To give more carefully. To give to those who really needed it. Which shows you that he was not simply someone who said "give everything away" to everyone. He thought about it.

And Olympias, for her part, looked at the thin archbishop with his bad stomach, eating his lonely suppers, and quietly took over his food. She sent him meals. She made sure they were the kind of plain, gentle food he could manage. She became one of his closest friends. And later, when everything went wrong, it was to her that he wrote most of his letters.

And then there is the strange story of Eutropius.

You remember Eutropius. The chamberlain. The man who had had John kidnapped and made archbishop. In the year three hundred and ninety-nine, Eutropius fell from power. He had made too many enemies, and in a single day it all collapsed. The emperor turned against him. Soldiers were sent to arrest him. And the most powerful man in the empire, the day before, ran in terror through the streets of the city, into the great church, and grabbed hold of the altar.

In those days the church was a place of sanctuary. If you held on to the altar, nobody was supposed to drag you away. And Eutropius, ironically, had tried to pass a law limiting exactly that right of sanctuary, only a little while before.

The soldiers came. John stood in front of the altar and would not let them in. And soon afterwards, with the great church packed and Eutropius still cowering under the altar, John preached.

The sermon still survives. It begins with the words of the book of Ecclesiastes. "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity." And he pointed, quite literally, at the man who had been so powerful the week before, now hiding under the holy table, shaking. Where are your fine friends now? Where are the feasts, the crowds who cheered you, the flatterers? All gone, like a dream when you wake. Like flowers when the summer ends.

Which is hard to listen to, if you are Eutropius. But then John turned to the crowd, who were angry, who wanted Eutropius punished, and he told them to have mercy. To forgive him. That the altar was there precisely for people like this. That the church was the mother who takes in even the son who has hurt her.

And the church kept him safe. Eutropius, sadly, left his sanctuary some days later, and came to a bad end afterwards, which is a story for grown-ups and not for tonight. But for as long as he held on to that altar, the little archbishop he had once kidnapped stood between him and the soldiers.

I think that is one of the best things anyone ever did in that church.

I have to tell you about the troubles now. I will tell them gently, because this is a bedtime show, and because they are sad, and because there were faults on more than one side.

The Empress Eudoxia, the wife of the emperor Arcadius, was young, and strong-willed, and very much in charge of the palace. At first she and John got on well. Then they did not. Some of his sermons against luxury and against rich women who wore too much jewellery seemed, to her, and to the people around her, to be about her. Whether John meant them to be is something historians still argue about. But it does not really matter what he meant. It was how they sounded.

And then Theophilus came back. The bishop of Alexandria. He had his own quarrels with John, over some monks from Egypt who had fled to Constantinople and asked John for help. And Theophilus came to the capital, with a great many bishops from Egypt, and found that a good many people in the palace and in the church were very happy to help him.

In the summer of the year four hundred and three, Theophilus held a meeting of bishops, at a country house across the water from the city, a place called the Oak. And the Synod of the Oak, as it is called, summoned John to answer a list of complaints. Some of the complaints were serious, and some were very silly indeed. One of them was that he ate alone. Another was that he had sold the marble that had been set aside for a church. John refused to go, because he said, quite fairly, that his enemies could not be his judges. And so the synod deposed him, and the emperor sent him into exile.

The people of the city were furious. Crowds surrounded the great church to stop him being taken. And John, who did not want a riot, slipped out quietly to the soldiers on the third day and let them take him across the water.

And then, that very night, something happened in the palace. The old writers disagree about exactly what. Some say there was an earthquake. Palladius speaks of a misfortune in the empress's own rooms. Whatever it was, the empress was frightened, and she sent word, at once, begging John to come back.

He came back. The whole city went out to meet him. They carried torches. They sang. They filled the sea between the city and the shore with boats. It was, for a little while, a very happy ending.

It did not last.

A few months later, a silver statue of the empress was put up on a tall column of red stone, right outside the great church. And it was dedicated with music and dancing and shouting, just outside, while the services were going on inside. And John complained about it, loudly, from the pulpit. And somebody took the news to the empress.

Things went from bad to worse. That Easter, in the year four hundred and four, soldiers were sent to break up the great Easter night service in the church, where hundreds of people were being baptised, and the newly baptised ran out into the dark streets in their white robes, frightened. And a few weeks later, in June, the order came. John was to go into exile again. This time, for good.

He knew what would happen if he went out by the front door. The crowds would fight to stop him, and people would be hurt. So he went to say goodbye, quietly, in the baptistery of the church. Palladius tells us who was there. Olympias, and a few other deaconesses with her. Women who had worked beside him, and who were crying. And he told them, more or less, "Don't be afraid. Don't stop your work. Look after the church. And be kind to whoever is made bishop after me, if he is made bishop properly, and not by force. Pray for me. You will not see my face again."

Then he slipped out by a side door, and gave himself up to the soldiers, and was taken down to a boat.

And that night, the great church caught fire. The flames spread to the senate house next door. By morning, both were ruins. Nobody knows how the fire started. His enemies blamed his friends. Olympias herself was brought to court and accused of starting it, which was ridiculous, and she was fined a great deal of money, and later sent away from the city too. She bore it all with great courage, and she, too, is remembered today as a saint.

John never saw Constantinople again.

They sent him to a place called Cucusus.

Cucusus was a small town high in the Taurus mountains, in what is now south-eastern Turkey, about as far from the capital as you could send someone without falling off the edge of the empire. It took him about seventy days to get there, in the summer heat, with a fever for much of the way. He wrote to Olympias about the journey. He told her about the fever, the bad roads, the nights without sleep, and about one dreadful night when he had to leave a town in a hurry, sick, on a dark mountain road, because of threats against him. He did not dress it up. And then, typically, he told her to stop worrying about him.

And then he arrived. And Cucusus, it turned out, was rather kind to him. People in the town took him in, and gave him a house, and a fire. The winters there were savage, with deep snow, and he spent them wrapped in blankets beside the fire, coughing, and writing letters. And in the summers, bandits from the mountains raided the countryside, and everyone had to run to the fortresses for safety.

And here is the remarkable thing. This sick, exiled old man, in a mountain town at the end of the world, became, if anything, more important than he had been in the capital. People came to see him. From Antioch, which was not so very far away. From all over. Friends sent money, and he spent it on the poor of the district, and on churches, and on missionaries he sent out to places that had never heard the Gospel. The bishop of Rome, Innocent, and the Western emperor, wrote letters on his behalf and demanded a fair hearing for him. Monks and bishops came to visit him. And John wrote letters. More than two hundred of his letters survive, and most of them come from these years.

The best of those letters are the ones he wrote to Olympias.

Back in the capital, Olympias was in a bad way. She had lost her friend. She had been put on trial, and fined, and her community was being broken up. And she had fallen into a kind of darkness. A great, grey heaviness of the heart, where nothing seems worth doing and every day is long. The Greek word John used for it means something like despondency, or deep discouragement. We might call it depression.

And John, from his mountain town, wrote to her. Again and again. Long letters, gentle letters, sometimes a little bossy letters. Seventeen of them survive.

He told her that this heaviness was the worst of all the troubles she was carrying. Worse than the fine. Worse than the trial. Worse than his own exile. And that she must fight it, the way you would fight an illness. Not by pretending she was not sad. But by remembering.

Remember, he told her, all the good you have done. Every hungry person you have fed. Every sick person you have cared for. None of it is lost. God has seen all of it. It is stored up, and nobody can take it from you.

He told her that God allows storms, but is the one who steers the ship.

He told her, again and again, that he was well. Which was not quite true, but he told her so anyway, and then he told her about the snow, and his cough, and how he never left his room in winter, and how he was looking after himself, because he knew she would ask.

And he wrote, at about this time, a little book with a title that I think is one of the best titles of any book ever written. It is called That No One Can Harm the Person Who Does Not Harm Himself. And the idea of it is this. People can take your money. They can take your home. They can take your good name. They can send you to the mountains with no hat. But they cannot take away your goodness. Nobody can make you bitter, or cruel, or unfaithful, unless you let them. The only real harm is the harm we do to ourselves, by choosing to do wrong. And everything else, even the worst of it, God can use for good.

When a man writes that from his own bed, in exile, coughing, with the snow outside, it is worth listening to.

His enemies in the capital did not like any of this. They had hoped that sending him to the mountains would make people forget him. Instead, the mountains had become a kind of pulpit.

So, in the summer of the year four hundred and seven, a new order came. John was to be moved again. Further. To a place called Pityus, a tiny settlement on the far eastern shore of the Black Sea, about as remote a place as the Roman Empire possessed. And he was to be taken there on foot, by two soldiers, as quickly as possible.

Palladius, who knew people who had been there, tells us about the journey. The soldiers marched him for three months. Through the heat of the day, and the storms. One of the two soldiers was hard, and impatient, and seemed almost to want the old man to die on the road. The other, Palladius says, was kinder, and would quietly do him small kindnesses when his companion was not looking.

I like to imagine that kinder soldier. We do not know his name. But I imagine him, at the end of a long wet day, finding a dry corner of a stable, and a little bread, and some watered wine, and putting them down beside the old man without a word.

"You should eat, Father," he might have said.

And John, who could hardly eat anything by now, might have said, "Thank you, my son. God bless you." And meant it.

"You are not angry?" the soldier might have asked. "With them? With us?"

"Angry," the old man might have said, thinking about it. "No. I am tired. That is a different thing. Glory to God for all things."

That last sentence is not our invention. It was the thing John said, all his life. Palladius calls it his usual saying. Glory to God for all things. When something good happened. When something bad happened. When he was clapped in the great church, and when he was marched through the rain. Glory to God for all things.

Near the end of the summer, they came to the town of Comana, in the hills of Pontus. And the soldiers marched him straight through the town without stopping, and out the other side, and some miles further on, to a little chapel by the road.

It was a chapel of the martyrs. A small church built over the grave of a holy man who had died for Christ, a hundred years before, in the last great persecution. His name was Basiliscus, and he had been the bishop of Comana. And there was a priest who lived beside the chapel, and looked after it.

Here, now, I am going to add someone, and I want to be clear that we have invented him. In our story, the priest has a boy who helps him. A boy of about ten, who sweeps the floor of the chapel and keeps the lamps lit by the martyr's tomb. We do not know of any such boy. But there are always boys like him, in little chapels by the road, and I like to think of one being there.

The boy watched the soldiers arrive that evening. The impatient one, the kind one. And between them, the old man. Wet, bald, shuffling, so thin that the boy could have counted his ribs. The boy had never seen anyone look so tired.

"Who is he?" the boy whispered to the priest.

And the priest, who knew, said, "He was the archbishop of Constantinople."

The boy looked at the little old man again, and did not believe it. Archbishops, in his experience, had gold on their clothes and servants to carry things. This one had nothing but a damp cloak and a cough.

They gave the travellers somewhere to sleep, in the little rooms beside the chapel. And the boy filled the lamps, as he did every night, and the small flames flickered in the dark beside the tomb of Basiliscus. And everyone went to sleep.

And in the night, Palladius tells us, John had a visitor.

He saw, in the dark, the martyr Basiliscus himself standing beside him. And Basiliscus said to him, "Courage, brother John. Tomorrow we shall be together."

That is all. Not a long speech. Seven or eight words. The kind of thing one old friend says to another at the end of a long journey. Courage. Tomorrow we shall be together.

And Palladius adds that the people there said that the martyr had appeared to the priest of the chapel too, and said to him, "Make ready a place for our brother John. He is coming."

We tell you this as Palladius's report. He was not there himself, but he collected the story from people who were, and he wrote it down within a very few years. We do not know exactly what happened in that little room. But we know that John woke up the next morning knowing that he was going to die that day. And he was not frightened.

In the morning, he asked the soldiers if they might stay a little longer. Just until late in the morning. Just a few hours. The impatient soldier said no. They had their orders. And so, very slowly, they set off.

They walked for perhaps three or four miles. And then it was clear, even to the impatient soldier, that the old man could not go on. He was burning with fever. He could not stand. And so, at last, they turned round, and brought him back to the little chapel of Basiliscus.

The boy, in our story, saw them coming up the road again, and ran to fetch the priest.

What happened next, Palladius tells very simply, and I will tell it simply too.

John asked for white clothes. Clean white clothes, the kind that people wore when they were baptised. And he took off everything he had been wearing on the long road, all of it, even his shoes, and put on the white. And the old clothes he gave away to the people who were there.

Then he received Holy Communion, the Body and Blood of the Lord, there in the chapel, beside the martyr's tomb. And he prayed. He prayed for the people around him.

And then he said the thing he always said. Glory to God for all things.

And he said "Amen." And he stretched out his feet, and he went home to God.

It was the fourteenth of September, in the year four hundred and seven. In the churches it was the feast of the Holy Cross. He was about sixty years old.

In our story, the boy stood by the door, and did not quite understand what had happened, and then did. And the priest put his arm round him. And then the boy went, because it was what he did, and because he did not know what else to do, and filled the lamp by the martyr's tomb, and lit it.

The kind soldier, I like to think, stood outside in the road for a long time, with his helmet in his hands.

They buried John there, beside Basiliscus, in the little chapel by the road. And Palladius tells us that a great crowd came for the burial, monks and holy women and ordinary people, from all over the country round about, from as far as Syria and Cilicia and Armenia. They had heard he was coming down the road. They came to meet him, and they were too late, and so they came to say goodbye.

The news reached Olympias in the capital, in time. She lived only a little while longer, about a year, and then she too went home to God.

And the story of John had one more chapter.

Thirty years went by. The emperor Arcadius died. The empress Eudoxia had died long before, not long after the exile, still young. Their son became emperor, Theodosius the Second, a gentle, bookish, rather pious man, who grew up hearing stories of the archbishop his parents had sent away.

And in the year four hundred and thirty-eight, Theodosius sent to the little chapel near Comana, and had John's body brought home. It came back across the hills and down to the sea, and across the water to Constantinople, on a winter's day at the end of January. And the whole city went out to meet it, the way they had gone out to meet him when he came back from his first exile, all those years before. The sea was covered with boats again. The people carried lamps again.

And the young emperor, the historian Theodoret tells us, laid his face on the coffin, and begged forgiveness for his father and his mother, who had wronged this man.

They laid John in the church of the Holy Apostles, in the place where the emperors and the archbishops were buried. Centuries later, his relics were taken to Rome, and rested there for a very long time, in Saint Peter's. And then, in the year two thousand and four, as we told you in Saint Gregory's episode, Pope John Paul the Second gave a large part of them back to the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople, Bartholomew, together with the relics of Saint Gregory. And they were carried home again, to the city on the Bosphorus, sixteen hundred years after John had left it by a side door.

People began to call him Golden Mouth some generations after he died. And they have never stopped.

His sermons are still read. Every Easter night in Orthodox churches all over the world, a short sermon is read aloud that has come down under his name. It is traditionally his, though scholars are not quite sure. It invites everyone to the feast. Those who came early and those who came late. Those who kept the fast and those who did not. Everyone. "The table is full," it says. "Let no one go away hungry." I cannot think of a better summary of what John spent his life saying.

And the Divine Liturgy that most Orthodox and Eastern Catholic Christians celebrate on most Sundays of the year is called the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom. How much of it he wrote himself is debated. But his name is on it, and his voice is in it, every week.

He is one of the Three Holy Hierarchs of the Orthodox Church, with Basil and Gregory. There is an old story about how that feast began. Hundreds of years later, so the story goes, Christians in the city of Constantinople were arguing about which of the three was the greatest. Some said Basil, some said Gregory, some said John. And the three appeared in a dream to a bishop called John of Euchaita, and told him that they were one before God, and that there was no first and no last among them, and that people should stop arguing and keep a feast for all three together. And so, the story says, they did. We tell you that as the traditional story, not as history. But I think it is the kind of thing all three of them would have said. Basil, sternly. Gregory, with a poem. And John, with a sermon, an hour long, with jokes.

In the Catholic Church he is a Doctor of the Church, one of the great teachers, and the patron of preachers. His feast is on the thirteenth of September, the day before the day he died. In the Orthodox Church his feast is on the thirteenth of November, with another day at the end of January for the return of his relics, and the feast of the Three Hierarchs a few days later.

In icons, he is shown as a man with a high bald head, a thin, sharp, rather tired face, and a short, thin beard. He is very often holding a book. Sometimes he is shown with Saint Paul whispering in his ear, because he loved Saint Paul's letters so much, and because there is an old story that a friend of his once saw Paul, at night, helping him to understand them. That is a legend, and a lovely one.

So if you have had a long day, and things have not gone the way you hoped, and people have been unkind, or unfair, or simply tired and impatient, as people are, you could think of him. The small, bald man with the bad stomach and the golden mouth, who told the rich the truth and sheltered the man who had wronged him, who was sent away twice, and wrote letters of comfort from the snow, and walked to the end of the world in the rain. And who said, at every turn of the road, and at the very end of it, Glory to God for all things.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Most of tonight comes from Palladius, a bishop and friend of John, who wrote a Dialogue about his life a few years after his death. Palladius was on John's side, and says so. The church historians Socrates and Sozomen, a generation later, give a fuller and sometimes less flattering picture. Theodoret describes the return of the relics. John's own letters, especially the seventeen to Olympias, and his sermons, fill in the rest. The best modern life is J. N. D. Kelly's Golden Mouth.

What is history. His birth and family, his teacher Libanius, his monastic years and broken health, his preaching at Antioch and the riot of the statues, his removal to Constantinople, his reforms, Eutropius at the altar, Olympias, the Synod of the Oak, his recall, the second exile, the fire, Cucusus and the letters, the march toward Pityus, his death near Comana in four hundred and seven, and the return of his body in four hundred and thirty-eight.

What is told as someone's report. The teacher's remark about his mother is John's own account. Libanius's dying words are Sozomen's. The martyr Basiliscus appearing in the night, and his message to the priest, are Palladius's report. The story of the Three Hierarchs' feast, and Saint Paul at his ear, are traditional stories.

We have told the quarrels from John's side, as Palladius does, and kept them short. Theophilus of Alexandria is remembered as a saint by the Coptic Church, and we have tried not to make anyone a villain.

What we invented. The boy with the lamps, the kind soldier's words, the talk on the road, the weather, and the wording of nearly everything. John's own words are given in our plain paraphrase.

That's the story of Saint John Chrysostom for tonight.

The Father Who Ran Down the Road

A younger son takes his share and leaves, and an older son stays and works. Luke, chapter fifteen, told straight from the Gospel: the far country, the pigs, the long road home, the father running, the ring and the robe, and the elder brother standing outside the music.

Full script

Picture a road at the end of a long day. It is dusty and white, and it runs down between two stone walls toward a village on a hill. And on that road, a long way off, is a figure walking very slowly, the way a person walks who has been walking for weeks and is no longer sure he wants to arrive.

And at the top of the hill, on a flat roof, an old man is standing with his hand over his eyes. He has stood there, if we are honest, on a great many evenings. He sees the figure. And the old man, who is much too old for it, and who is very important in that village, and who has never in his whole life been seen to do such a thing, picks up the hem of his robe and begins to run.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves. Let us begin a long time before, at the breakfast table.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is a parable from the Gospel according to Luke, chapter fifteen, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the village, the weather, the farm work, and the talk of the servants, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

First, a word about who is listening to the story, because in the Gospel it matters.

Luke tells us that the Lord had been eating with people the neighbours did not approve of. Tax collectors, and others whom the respectable people called sinners, had crowded in to hear him. And the Pharisees and the scribes, who took the law very seriously, stood at the edge of the crowd and muttered. This man welcomes sinners and eats with them, they said. You can almost hear the tone. It is the tone of someone who has seen the guest list and has Concerns.

So the Lord told them a story. Actually, he told them three. The first was about a shepherd with a hundred sheep who loses one, and leaves the ninety-nine to look for it. The second was about a woman with ten silver coins who loses one, and lights a lamp and sweeps the whole house until she finds it. And in both of those, when the lost thing is found, somebody calls the neighbours and says, rejoice with me.

The third story is the one we are telling tonight. It is the longest of the three, and the one that people have loved for two thousand years. And it is the only one of the three in which the lost thing can walk, and talk, and decide, and come home on its own two feet. And, as we will see, the only one that has a person standing outside at the end.

Now, to understand it, we need to know a little about how a family worked in that country, in those days.

A family like this one was a farm, and a farm was a family. There was a house, and fields, and animals, and servants who were hired by the day or the season, and a man at the head of it who was called the father, and who held all of it together. When the father died, the land was divided among the sons. Under the old law, which we find in the Book of Deuteronomy, the eldest son received a double share, because he would carry the house on afterwards. So if there were two sons, the elder would get two parts out of three, and the younger one part out of three.

And here is the important thing. The land was divided when the father died. Not before. The son of a family like this could expect to wait for his share, and nobody would say a word about it, because the waiting was part of the arrangement. You lived in the house, you worked the fields, you were looked after, and one day, a long time from now, it would all be yours.

Which brings us to the younger son.

The Gospel does not give him a name. It says only that there was a man who had two sons. So we will call him what Luke does, the younger one. And we will give him some of the things a younger son might have had. A good pair of sandals. A voice that carried across a field. A great many friends. And an idea in his head, which had been growing there for some time, the way an idea does, that his life was a very large thing and the village was a very small one.

The Gospel says this, and it says it plainly. The younger son said to his father, Father, give me the share of the property that will belong to me.

Let us stop and listen to how that sounds. In that country, in that time, it was not a polite thing to say. It was the sort of thing you might say about a man who was already gone. Give me what I would have had when you died. It was not quite a curse, but it was not far from one. And any father who heard it would have had every right to be angry, and to send the boy out of the room, and to hold the dinner in cold silence for a month.

We can imagine the kitchen. It is early. There is bread on the table. A servant is somewhere in the back, sweeping, and has become very interested in a single corner of the floor. The elder son is outside already, because he is always outside already. And the younger son stands there, with his hands behind his back, having rehearsed the sentence the whole way down the stairs, and having said it rather faster than he meant to.

And the father, in the Gospel, does not shout. He does not forbid it. He does not say a single word that Luke tells us about. The Gospel says, quite simply, so he divided his property between them.

He gave it. That is all. We are told nothing about what it cost him to give it. The parable leaves that to us, the way it leaves a great many things to us. But a person who has ever let somebody go, knowing it was a mistake, and knowing that holding on would make it a worse one, may have some idea.

There was some selling to do. Land is not easy to carry. So in the days that followed, the younger son turned his portion into coin, and the coin into a purse, and the purse into a belt. And the Gospel says that not many days later he gathered all he had and travelled to a distant country.

Notice the words. A distant country. Luke does not say which. A traveller of that day might have gone to a seaport, or to a great city across the water, or a place where nobody knew his father's name. That, we suspect, was the point of it. The farther the better. A person who has made up his mind to be a different person always wants a good distance between himself and the people who knew the first one.

We will not stay long in the distant country, because the Gospel does not either. Luke tells us in one sentence what it did to him. There he squandered his property in dissolute living.

That is all. We are not told the details. And, since this is a bedtime story, we are going to be grateful for that and leave it just as short.

But let us say something kind about the young man, because the parable is kind to him, and because it is true. For a while it must have been wonderful. He was young. He had money in his belt. He had never in his life had so many people glad to see him. And the thing about a purse full of coin is that everyone is your friend while it is full, and nobody can quite remember your name when it is empty. He bought dinners for people. He lent to people. He was, for a season, the most popular man in a place where nobody knew who his father was.

Then, the Gospel says, he had spent everything.

It is a sentence that comes down like a closed door. Everything. We can imagine him, one morning, turning out the purse onto a table, and shaking it, and looking inside it, and then shaking it again, as though a second shake might find something the first one had missed.

And, because trouble likes company, the Gospel adds the next part at once. A severe famine took place throughout that country, and he began to be in need.

So here is the picture. No money. No friends, because the friends went where the money went. A country where everybody is hungry, and nobody is feeling generous. And a young man a very long way from home, with a good pair of sandals that are now rather less good.

What do you do? He did what a person does. He looked for work. And the Gospel says that he went and hired himself out to one of the citizens of that country, who sent him to his fields to feed the pigs.

Now we have to be careful here, because modern listeners may not feel what the first listeners felt. For a Jewish family in that time, the pig was an unclean animal. The law said so. You did not eat it. You did not touch it. And a young man who had grown up in a house that kept the law, and who now stood knee-deep in a pig yard, in a foreign country, being paid to feed them, had come down a very long way. He had gone as low as a man of his people could go. And the Gospel is not gentle about it. It says that he would gladly have filled his stomach with the pods that the pigs were eating, and no one gave him anything.

Pods. The Gospel says pods, and the old readers have guessed that they were carob pods, the hard brown beans that grow on a tree in that part of the world. They were fodder. They were what you gave to animals, and to the very poor in a bad year. And he would have eaten them, and nobody gave him any.

Let us leave him there a moment, in the pig yard, at the end of a long day. There is a wind, and it smells exactly the way you would think. The sun is going down. The animals are making the noises that animals make. And a young man, who has not had a proper meal in some time, is sitting on a fence rail with his chin in his hands.

And then comes the sentence that the whole parable turns on. It is a very small sentence. The Gospel says, but when he came to himself.

When he came to himself. Not, when he was rescued. Not, when someone found him. Not even, when he had a plan. But when he came to himself, as a person does who has been somewhere very far away inside his own head, and has just noticed the way back. It is a beautiful, plain, human phrase, and it is exactly the kind of thing the Lord says that makes you sit up. Because it means that somewhere under all of it, underneath the sandals and the squandering and the pigs, there was still a person who knew what was true.

And what did that person think? The Gospel tells us. He said, How many of my father's hired hands have bread enough and to spare, but here I am dying of hunger!

Notice what he thinks about. He does not think about his father's love, not yet. He thinks about the hired hands. The day labourers. The people who came for the season and went home in the evening. Even they, he thinks, have bread enough and to spare. And here he is, a son of the house, starving in a pig yard.

It is not a very noble thought. It is a hungry one. And that is rather the point of the story, for the Lord does not wait for the young man to have noble thoughts before he starts walking home. He lets a hungry stomach start the journey, and trusts the rest to the road.

So the young man makes a plan. The Gospel gives us the words he rehearses, and it is worth hearing them, because he practises them the whole way. I will get up and go to my father, he says, and I will say to him, Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; I am no longer worthy to be called your son; treat me like one of your hired hands.

It is a good speech. It is honest, as far as it goes. It does not ask for the money back. It does not ask for the house, or the robe, or the place at the table. It asks to be hired. A bed in the servants' shed, bread at the end of the day, and a chance to earn his keep. And, we may suspect, a young man who had said it forty times already, to the pigs, who did not mind.

And the Gospel says: So he got up and went to his father.

Now we go back up the hill, to the house. And here we have to use our own imagination, because the parable is quiet about the years in between. But we can guess a little.

The elder brother, we may suppose, went on with the work. The Gospel says he was out in the field when the story comes to its end, and that is the kind of person he was. Up before it was light. Out with the animals. Back at dusk with dirt under his nails and a quiet sense, which nobody had asked him to carry, that someone in this family had to be the one who stayed. He was reliable. He was steady. He kept the accounts, and he kept the fences, and he never once forgot to latch the gate. And the Gospel is not unkind to him. It is simply that nobody threw him a party for it.

And the father. We have nothing but one line about the father in all those months, and it is the most surprising line in the whole story. The Gospel says that while he was still far off, his father saw him.

While he was still far off.

Think about what that means. A man cannot see his son from a long way off unless he is looking. Not looking once, on one evening, but looking again and again. Standing at the edge of the roof. Going out to the gate when the light is going. Shading his eyes against the sun, on a road that stayed empty, evening after evening, for months. A person does not recognise a figure by its walk, from half a mile away, in the dusk, unless the whole of him has been waiting for that walk.

We are not told how many evenings. We are only told that when the day came, he saw him first.

And what does the father do? The Gospel gives him a string of verbs, one after another, like a man who has stopped thinking at all. He was filled with compassion. He ran. He put his arms around him. He kissed him.

He ran.

It is hard to say how strange that is. In that country, a man of standing did not run. A respected elder walked, slowly, with his robes about him, and let others come to him. To run in public, with your legs showing and your dignity in the dust, was something a child did, or a servant. And here is the head of a household, an old man, hitching up his robe and pounding down the road in front of the whole village, because he cannot bear to wait the last two hundred steps.

We can imagine the neighbours coming to their doors. Is that not so-and-so? What is he doing? And behind them, a servant, who had been up on the roof, saying quietly, I think the boy is back.

The young man is still a little way off when the father reaches him. He has been walking and rehearsing for weeks, and he is thin and dusty and smells of the pig yard, and he has his speech ready. And before he can get a single word out, he is in his father's arms, and his father is kissing him, over and over, and the speech is somewhere in the road behind them.

But he is an honest young man, and he has come a long way to say it, so he begins anyway. The Gospel gives us his words. Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; I am no longer worthy to be called your son.

And then he stops.

Listen carefully, because this is one of the quiet things in the story. He has the second half of his speech ready. Treat me like one of your hired hands. It was in the plan. It is in the rehearsal. But in Luke's telling he never gets to it. The father does not let him. The Gospel does not say that the father interrupts him. It simply does not give us the sentence. It goes straight on to the father speaking, to the servants, as though the son's request had been a small thing that dissolved in the middle of the road.

Because the father says, Quickly, bring out a robe, the best one, and put it on him; put a ring on his finger and sandals on his feet.

The best robe. Not an ordinary one, not a servant's robe, but the best in the house, the one that was kept for a guest of honour. A ring, which was a sign of belonging to the household, of having a voice in it. And sandals. A servant went barefoot. A son wore sandals. And a boy who had come home with his sandals worn through was being told, with an armful of cloth and leather, who he still was.

And there is more. Bring the fatted calf and kill it, and let us eat and celebrate, for this son of mine was dead and has come to life again; he was lost and has been found.

The fatted calf. In a household like this, there was one. It was fed up and kept, the way a family keeps a good bottle, for the day when something very great had to be marked. A wedding. A return from war. It was not for a Tuesday. It was not for any ordinary good news. And the father, without a moment's discussion, says: that one. Today.

And listen to his reason. He was dead. He has come to life. He was lost. He has been found. It is the very same word, found, that the shepherd and the woman used in the two other stories, and the very same thing happens after it. Somebody calls everyone in and says, rejoice with me.

And the Gospel says: and they began to celebrate.

So we have a house that is full of music. We can see it from the road: the lamps lit in every window, the smell of roasting meat, the sound of a pipe and a drum and a great many people who have no idea how to dance and are doing it anyway. There is a robe on a young man's shoulders, and he is a little unsteady and a little stunned and still looking at the door every few minutes, as though somebody is about to tell him he is in the wrong house.

And now the Gospel turns, quite quietly, to another person. The Lord has been telling the story to the Pharisees and the scribes, and here, with a very small phrase, he turns to look at them. Now his elder son was in the field.

The elder son. The one who stayed. The one who latched the gate.

And here is what happens to him. He is coming home at the end of the day. He is tired. His hands are dirty, and his back hurts the way it always hurts, and he is thinking, perhaps, of a bowl of lentils and an early night. And as he comes up the road, he hears something. Music, and dancing.

We have to be fair to him. He did not know. Nobody had come out to tell him. He had been in the field all day, and the house had filled with people and lamps and meat, and not one person had thought to send a message to the one who was working. It is a small, sharp, human thing, and every family has had a day like it.

So he does the sensible thing. The Gospel says he called one of the servants and asked what was going on.

And the servant, who is just delighted, says: Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fatted calf, because he has got him back safe and sound.

Safe and sound. It is a lovely phrase, and a servant's phrase, and entirely innocent. The servant thinks he is bringing good news. And the elder brother stands there in the road, with his tools in his hand and the music pouring out of the doors of his own house, and something in him goes very still.

The Gospel says, Then he became angry and refused to go in.

We should not rush past that. Because it is, for a great many listeners, the place where the story becomes theirs. A person who has been the younger son has a certain kind of relief when they hear this parable. But a person who has been the elder one knows exactly what he is feeling, and knows it right down in the stomach. Years of faithful work. Years of not asking for anything. And now, in a single evening, a party on a scale that nobody has ever given him, for the one who walked out and spent the money.

The Lord does not say that the elder brother is wicked. He does not say that his anger is stupid. He lets it stand there, in the road, in the dark, plainly and honestly, the way it stands in a great many hearts.

And then comes the other great moment of the parable, and a very quiet one. Because the father, who has already run down one road tonight, does something else the Gospel is careful to report. His father came out and began to plead with him.

He came out. He left the lamps, and the music, and the guest of honour, and the best robe, and he went outside to the dark road where the other son was standing. The father who ran to the one who was far off now walks out to the one who is near. And not to command him. Not to scold him. The Gospel uses a word that means he begged him, or urged him, or pleaded.

It is hard to say how much is in that sentence. In the house is one son who was lost. Outside is another who has been standing next to him all along. And the father, who could very easily have stayed with the party and sent a servant, goes out into the dust himself.

And the elder son answers. The Gospel gives his words, and they are some of the most honest words in all of Scripture. Listen, he says. For all these years I have been working like a slave for you, and I never disobeyed your command; yet you have never given me even a young goat so that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours came back, who has devoured your property with prostitutes, you killed the fatted calf for him!

Let us go slowly through it.

For all these years. He has counted them. He has kept them, the way a person keeps a ledger.

Working like a slave. It is a hard word. He does not say, working as a son. He says, as a slave. In his own mind he has been a hired man in his father's house all this time, and he has not noticed, until tonight, that he has been the son all along.

I never disobeyed. It is true. And it is also the sound of a person who thought that obedience was a thing you paid in, and that the father owed him change.

You never gave me even a young goat. Not a calf. A goat. A small one. And here is the hurt that has been sitting underneath the whole speech, and which he has never once said aloud until now. He has not wanted much. He has only wanted to be told, once, that he was seen.

And then, this son of yours. Not my brother. This son of yours. He has taken the relationship apart and handed half of it back.

And what does the father say? The Gospel gives us his answer, and we will give it as it is, because it is the heart of the whole story. He said to him, Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. But we had to celebrate and rejoice, because this brother of yours was dead and has come to life; he was lost and has been found.

Son. The first word. Before anything else, before the answer, before the explaining. Child, or son, the Gospel's word is the tender one, the one you use for someone you love very much. He does not say, how dare you. He does not say, that is enough. He says, Son.

You are always with me. It is the thing the elder brother had forgotten. He had thought that he was a worker, waiting for his wages. And his father tells him, quietly, that he has never been one. That he has been at the table every night. That all that is mine is yours, not because you earned it, but because it is already yours. The goat, and the calf, and the house, and the hill. It was never being kept from him. He had only never asked for it.

And then the last sentence, which gently puts the brother back into the family. This brother of yours. Not, this son of mine, the way the elder son had said it. This brother of yours. The father hands him back, as a person hands a child its mother's hand.

We had to celebrate. Not, we chose to. Not, it would be nice to. We had to. Because a lost thing was found, and there is nothing else to do. A person who has found the sheep, or the coin, or the child, does not keep it quiet. It would be like finding a lamp lit in a dark house and putting a cloth over it.

And then the Gospel stops.

This is the most remarkable thing about it, and we are going to stop exactly where Luke stops. We are not told whether the elder brother goes in. We are not told whether he puts down the tools, and takes the old man's arm, and walks into the music. We are not told whether the younger one comes out into the road, with the best robe on his back, and says something to him. We are not told what happens next at all.

It is the Lord's last word to the Pharisees and the scribes. It is the place where he leaves a door open, and a lamp on, and a father standing in the road, and lets the listeners decide what they will do. And whatever happened next, we may guess, was not told, because the people hearing it were standing in the road too.

So we will leave them there. A father, an elder son, and a house full of light. Out on the dark road, two people who love each other, one of them with his hand held out. And the sound of the pipe, through the open door, a little way off, still going.

Let us come down now, slowly, as we do at the end of a story.

This parable has been told, and painted, and prayed over for twenty centuries. In the Orthodox Church it is read on a Sunday in the weeks before Great Lent, when Christians begin turning their faces toward Easter, and the hymns of that Sunday are full of the road home. In the Catholic Church it is read on a Sunday in Lent, in the year of the cycle that uses Luke. And in both churches, people come back to it year after year, the way you come back to a house you grew up in.

It is sometimes called the parable of the prodigal son. The word prodigal means wasteful, and it was a fine name for the younger one. But a great many readers have said that the story is really about the father. About the old man who is watching the road, and the one who runs, and the one who goes out into the dark to plead. It is, in a way, a portrait of how the Lord says that God looks at us. Not as a judge with a ledger, but as a parent at the edge of a roof, with a hand over his eyes.

And there is room in it for both sons. The one who has wandered a long way off and does not think he can be taken back. And the one who has stayed, and kept the gate, and has begun to wonder whether it was seen. The Lord does not tell either of them that their feelings are foolish. He tells them both the same thing. You are my child. Come in.

Think, for a moment, of the long road in your own life. A place you walked away from, once, or were afraid to go back to. A person you have been meaning to speak to for years. A door you were certain was shut. It may be that somebody has been watching the road. It may be that somebody has never stopped.

The lamps are still lit at the top of the hill. The best robe is still hanging on the peg. And the old man on the roof is, even now, with his hand over his eyes, looking down the road.

A few things for the curious. The story we have told tonight comes from the Gospel according to Luke, chapter fifteen, verses eleven to thirty-two, and it is a parable. That means it is a story the Lord told to teach something, and not a report of one family. We do not know, and the Gospel does not say, who the father and the sons were, where the far country was, or what the village was called. Later readers have given them names and maps. We have not.

What follows the Gospel closely is what the father, the younger son, the elder son and the servant say, the order of events, and the open ending. The words of the younger son's rehearsed speech, the robe, the ring, the sandals and the fatted calf are all in the text. The pods the pigs ate were probably carob pods, which is a reading rather than a certainty. The old law about a double share for the eldest son is in the Book of Deuteronomy, and we have used it only as background, because Luke does not say how the property was divided.

What we invented is the colour. The roof, the evenings of watching, the pipe and the drum, the servant who was interested in the corner of the floor, the lamps in the windows and the elder brother's tired walk home are ours, told in the spirit of the original. So are the narrator's guesses about what the characters felt. Where the Gospel is silent, for example about the months between the leaving and the return, we have said so, and kept the guessing small. And we ended the story exactly where Luke ends it.

That's the parable of the father and his two sons for tonight.

Aaron's Staff That Budded in the Tent of Meeting

After a bitter quarrel over who may serve as priest, and a terrible day that Aaron ends by running into the crowd with a censer to stand between the dead and the living, the Lord tells Moses to lay twelve staffs with twelve names in the tent overnight; in the morning one of them has budded, blossomed and borne ripe almonds. Told from the Book of Numbers, chapters sixteen to eighteen.

What's true in this story

Who
Aaron the priest, Moses, the leaders of the twelve tribes of Israel, Korah and those who rose up with him, and the people of Israel in the wilderness, with an invented leader's granddaughter
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Book of Numbers as Scripture; Moses is honoured as a saint and prophet in both traditions, and Aaron the priest is honoured among the holy forefathers
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Book of Numbers, chapters sixteen to eighteen, told straight in a plain rendering; the book does not say where in the wilderness this happened, in which year of the forty, what wood the staffs were made of, or whether this was the same staff Aaron threw down before Pharaoh, so none of that is settled
Legend
none told as fact; the reading of the blossoming staff as a picture of the Virgin Mary is told as how Christian writers and hymn-writers have read it, and the Latin play on rod and virgin as a medieval wordplay, not as part of the book
What we invented
the leader of the tribe of Zebulun and his granddaughter, who carves his name on his staff and waits for the morning, are our own invention; so are Aaron's words to Moses on the night of the staffs, the look of the camp, and what the people said to one another while they waited; nothing is added to what the Lord, Moses, Aaron or Korah say or do in the book, and the staffs, the names, the night and the almonds are the book's own
Feast day
Moses the prophet is remembered on the fourth of September in the Orthodox Church and in the Roman Martyrology; the Orthodox Church remembers Aaron among the holy forefathers in the weeks before Christmas, and we give no calendar date for him that we cannot vouch for

Each branch with its cups like almonds, each with a bud and a flower. So every night, in the holy place, there was light shining out of golden almond flowers. Buds and blossoms, the same as on Aaron's staff, keeping watch through the dark.

And Christians, when they read this story, kept noticing things.

They noticed the dry wood, cut off from any root, that brought forth life without being planted. And many Christian writers and hymn-writers, in the East and in the West, came to see in it a picture of the Virgin Mary, who brought forth a son without a human father. In the hymns of the Eastern Church especially, she is sometimes greeted as Aaron's staff that blossomed. And in the Middle Ages, writers in Latin enjoyed the fact that their word for a rod, virga, sounds almost exactly like their word for a virgin, virgo, and they put the two together with Isaiah's prophecy that there shall come forth a rod out of the root of Jesse, and a flower shall rise up out of his root.

They noticed, too, that dead wood came to life overnight in a dark place before the Lord. And they thought of another morning, and another dark place, and another stone rolled away.

All of that is how Christians have read the story, not something the Book of Numbers says itself. But I think it is a very natural way to read it.

And the sons of Korah went on singing.

And in my imagination, somewhere in the wilderness, years later, an old man from Zebulun is sitting at the door of his tent in the evening with his plain staff across his knees. His granddaughter is not eight any more. She is grown, with children of her own, and one of them, a small boy, is sitting next to his great-grandfather, running his finger along some crooked letters in the wood.

"Who did this?" the boy asks. "It's all wobbly."

"Your grandmother did it," says the old man. "When she was a little girl."

"Why?"

And the old man tells him.

He tells him about the twelve staffs. About the long night when nobody slept. About walking home without his stick, and having to lean on a little girl instead. About Moses coming out of the tent in the morning with an armful of wood, and the one in the middle that was all flowers.

"Was yours the one?" asks the boy.

"No," says the old man. "Mine was one of the other eleven."

"Oh," says the boy, a little disappointed.

"It's all right," says the old man. "Most of us are one of the other eleven. That's a good thing to be. Somebody has to poke the fire."

And he gives the boy the staff to hold, and the boy holds it very seriously, and the sun goes down behind the dunes, and in the middle of the camp the fire comes on over the great tent, as it does every night. And somewhere inside, in the dark, in front of the golden chest, there is a stick that is covered in flowers, keeping watch.

And everyone else can sleep.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapters sixteen, seventeen and eighteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. In some Bibles, including the Hebrew text, the verses about the staffs are numbered a little differently, near the end of chapter seventeen. The quarrel of Korah, Dathan and Abiram, the censers, Aaron running into the crowd and standing between the dead and the living, the twelve staffs with their names, the night in the tent, the buds, blossoms and ripe almonds, the staff kept before the testimony, the people's fear, and the Lord's words to Aaron about being his portion are all the book's own, told plainly. The sons of Korah who did not die are in chapter twenty-six, and Aaron's death on Mount Hor is in chapter twenty.

The leader of the tribe of Zebulun and his granddaughter are our invention. There really was a staff for every tribe, but the book gives none of the leaders' names in this chapter, and nothing they said or felt. The crooked letters, the night in the tent, and the conversation between Moses and Aaron are ours, told in the spirit of the original.

The book does not say where in the wilderness this happened, or what wood the staffs were made of, and it does not say whether this was the same staff that Aaron threw down before Pharaoh, so we have not settled any of that. Hebrews says the staff was kept in the ark and First Kings says that by Solomon's day only the tablets were inside it; we have told both and not tried to reconcile them. The reading of the staff as a picture of the Virgin Mary is how Christian writers and hymn-writers have read it, and the play on virga and virgo is a medieval Latin one.

That's the story of Aaron's staff that budded for tonight.

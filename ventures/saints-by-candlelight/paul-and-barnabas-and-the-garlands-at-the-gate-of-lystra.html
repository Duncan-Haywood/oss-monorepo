Paul and Barnabas and the Garlands at the Gate of Lystra

Two tired travellers leave Iconium in a hurry, a man who has never walked stands up in the square at Lystra, and a whole town decides that its visitors are Zeus and Hermes and comes to the gate with oxen and garlands. Then the stones, a circle of friends, a quiet road back through every town that threw them out, and a door opened for the nations. Told straight from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter fourteen.

Full script

It is a bright morning in a small town on a high, wide plain, and something is coming up the road towards the gate.

First you hear it. Pipes, a drum, and a great many people talking at once, in a language that sounds a little like Greek and is not Greek at all. Then you see it. A priest in a white robe, walking slowly and grandly, the way priests walk when they know everybody is watching. Behind him, two oxen, big and patient and very puzzled, with wreaths of wool and flowers hung round their horns and draped over their shoulders, like cattle on their way to a wedding. And behind the oxen, it seems, the whole town.

At the side of the road, two travellers are standing with their mouths a little open. One of them is tall and calm and has a kind face. The other is shorter, and has been talking for most of the morning, and for once has stopped.

"Barnabas," says the shorter one. "What are they saying?"

"I was going to ask you," says Barnabas. "I think," he adds slowly, as the oxen draw nearer and the crowd turns, all together, to look straight at the two of them, "I think they are saying it about us."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter fourteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and the weather, the roads, the houses and a few of the small conversations are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Tonight we are going to the middle of what is now Turkey, to a high, flat country of wheat and sheep and wide skies, about a thousand metres above the sea, with mountains standing round the edges of it like the rim of a very large bowl. In the old days this country was called Lycaonia. The winters there are cold and the summers are hot and dry, and the wind comes across the plain with nothing to stop it, and the people who lived there were farmers and shepherds and weavers, and spoke their own language at home, which hardly anybody else in the world could understand.

And we are going there about fifteen years after the first Easter, give or take, with two travellers on the road.

The first is Barnabas. You may have met him before, because he turns up all through the early chapters of Acts, usually helping somebody. He was a Jew from the island of Cyprus, of the tribe of Levi, and his real name was Joseph, but the apostles in Jerusalem gave him a nickname, Barnabas, which Luke tells us means son of encouragement. I like to think he earned it. He sold a field and gave the money away. When a new believer called Saul turned up in Jerusalem, and everybody was frightened of him, because until very recently Saul had been arresting Christians, it was Barnabas who took him by the arm and brought him to the apostles and said, more or less, it's all right, he's one of us now. And years later, when the church at Antioch in Syria was growing faster than anyone could teach it, it was Barnabas who walked all the way to Tarsus to fetch Saul back to help.

Saul, by the time of our story, is mostly called Paul. He was a Jew too, from Tarsus, a tentmaker by trade, very clever, very learned in the Scriptures, very determined, and, if we are honest, not always the easiest man to travel with. He talked a great deal. He walked fast. He did not much like changing his plans.

The two of them had been sent out together by the church at Antioch, after a time of prayer and fasting, on what we now call the first missionary journey, though nobody at the time called it anything at all. They had sailed to Cyprus, Barnabas's home, and walked the length of the island. Then they had sailed north to the mainland, and their young helper, John Mark, who was Barnabas's cousin, had left them there and gone home to Jerusalem. Acts does not say why. Paul, we learn later, was not pleased about it. Barnabas, being Barnabas, was probably sorry for everyone.

And then the two of them had climbed up from the coast into the mountains, to a town called Antioch of Pisidia, which is not the same Antioch they had started from. The ancient world had a great many towns called Antioch, much as the modern world has a great many called Springfield, and it causes about the same amount of confusion.

At Pisidian Antioch, Paul had preached in the synagogue on the Sabbath, and a great many people had listened, Jews and Greeks together, and a great many had believed. But some of the leading people of the town did not like it, and in the end the two travellers were driven out. And as they left, Acts says, they shook the dust off their feet, which is what the Lord had told his disciples to do when a town would not listen. And then, and I love this line, the disciples they left behind were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit. Which is not how you would expect a chapter to end when the preachers have just been thrown out of town.

So they set off east, along the road across the plain, to the next town. And that is where tonight begins.

The next town was called Iconium. It is still there. It is a big modern city now, called Konya, but in those days it was a busy market town on the edge of the plain, with water running down to it from the hills, and orchards and gardens round it, and a Jewish community with its own synagogue.

Paul and Barnabas did what they always did when they came to a new town. They found somewhere to stay, and they found work, because Paul liked to earn his own bread, and on the Sabbath they went to the synagogue. And the same thing happened at Iconium that had happened at Antioch. Acts says they spoke in such a way that a great number of people believed, both Jews and Greeks.

I want to stop on that word, both, for a moment. It is easy, when you tell these stories, to make them sound like a contest between two sides. But Paul and Barnabas were Jews. The first believers in every town they came to were mostly Jews, and Greeks who already went to the synagogue and prayed to the God of Israel. The arguments that followed were arguments inside a family, about the family's own Scriptures and the family's own hopes, and families, as you may have noticed, argue loudest of all.

And there were arguments. Some of the people who did not believe stirred up the townspeople against the travellers, and soured their minds, as Acts puts it. You can imagine the conversations in the market. Who are these two? Where did they come from? Didn't I hear there was trouble at Antioch?

But Paul and Barnabas did not leave. Acts says they stayed a long time, speaking boldly, and the Lord bore witness to what they said by letting signs and wonders be done by their hands. Notice who is doing what in that sentence. Paul and Barnabas speak. The Lord bears witness. The two travellers are not wizards. They are messengers, and the One who sent them backs up the message.

I picture them in a small rented room above a workshop, with Paul stitching leather at a bench in the daylight and talking to whoever came up the stairs, and Barnabas going round the town in the evenings to visit the people who had believed, with bread under his arm, sitting in kitchens, encouraging. That is my picture, not Luke's. But it seems to me the kind of thing a son of encouragement would do.

And slowly, Acts says, the whole town divided. Some sided with the Jews who did not believe, and some sided with the apostles.

That word, apostles, is worth noticing too, because it is one of only two places in the whole Book of Acts where Luke calls Paul and Barnabas by that name. Both of them are in tonight's chapter. It is as if Luke wants us to know that what is happening on this dusty plain is the same work that Peter and John were doing in Jerusalem.

At last, some of the people of the town and some of their leaders made a plan. They would set on the two travellers, treat them shamefully, and stone them.

Somebody warned them. Acts does not say who. It says only that they learned of it. I like to think it was one of the people Barnabas had sat with in a kitchen, running up the stairs in the dark, out of breath.

"They're coming in the morning," she says, or he says. "You have to go tonight."

And Paul, who did not like changing his plans, looks at Barnabas, and Barnabas looks at Paul, and Barnabas says, "I think we have to go tonight."

So they went. They slipped out of Iconium and went south, to the cities of Lycaonia, to Lystra and Derbe and the country round about. And there, Acts says, they went on preaching the good news.

Which tells you something about these two. Being chased out of a town did not make them stop. It only made them move.

Lystra was a long day's walk from Iconium, south and a little west, across open farmland, with the mountains getting closer all the time. It was a smaller town than Iconium, sitting on a low hill by a stream, with fields all round it.

It was also, as it happens, a Roman colony. About fifty years before our story, the emperor Augustus had settled old soldiers there, veterans of his legions, and given them land, and built a road to join the town to the other colonies round about. So in Lystra you had a few Roman families who spoke Latin and thought of themselves as rather important, and Greek-speaking traders, and a great many local people who had farmed that land since long before any Roman arrived, and who spoke Lycaonian at home. It was the kind of town where, if you stood in the market square, you could hear three languages in five minutes, and the local one was the one you could not understand.

Acts does not mention a synagogue at Lystra. Perhaps there was none, or only a small gathering in somebody's house. So the travellers did what preachers do in a town with no synagogue. They went to where the people were, and they talked.

And in that town, Acts says, there was a man who could not use his feet. He had been lame from his mother's womb, and he had never walked.

Acts does not tell us his name. It does not tell us where he sat, or how old he was, or who looked after him. It tells us only those three things, and then a fourth, which is the important one. He listened to Paul speaking.

So let me give him a place to sit, and I'll tell you plainly that the place is my invention. Let's say he sat every morning by the edge of the square, near the fountain, on a folded blanket, where people passing would see him and sometimes give him a coin or a piece of bread. Next to him, let's say, there was a woman who sold dried figs from a basket, and they had been neighbours for so many years that they had long since run out of things to say to each other, and mostly sat in a friendly silence and watched the town go by.

And one morning, into the square, comes a short man with a travelling cloak and a tall friend, and the short man stands by the fountain and begins to talk. In Greek, which most people in Lystra understood well enough for buying and selling, even if they did not dream in it.

He talks about a God who made the sky and the earth. He talks about a man called Jesus, from a little town in Galilee, who healed people and taught them and was put to death, and who rose again on the third day. He talks about forgiveness, and new life, and a kingdom that is not like the kingdoms of this world.

Most people in the square stop for a minute or two, and listen with half an ear, and then remember the bread they were going to buy and move on.

The man on the blanket does not move on. He cannot. But that is not why he stays. He stays because he is listening. He is leaning forward, with both hands flat on the blanket beside him, and he is listening to every word, the way you listen when somebody is telling you a thing you have been waiting your whole life to hear.

And Paul, Acts says, looked at him intently.

I love that. In the middle of his sermon, with a crowd drifting past, Paul notices one man. And he looks at him, the way you look at a face you are trying to read, and he sees, Acts says, that the man had faith to be made well.

Then Paul said, in a loud voice, so that everybody in the square turned round,

"Stand up straight on your feet!"

And the man jumped up. That is the word Luke uses. He did not get up slowly, carefully, holding on to the wall, the way you might expect a man to get up who had never stood in his life. He leapt up. And he walked.

I imagine the fig-seller dropping her basket. I imagine figs rolling all over the square, and children chasing them, and the man who had never walked standing in the middle of it all, looking down at his own feet as if they belonged to somebody else, and then taking a step, and then another, and then laughing.

That part is mine. The leaping is Luke's.

Now, you have to understand what the people of Lystra thought had just happened.

They had seen a stranger speak a word, and a man who had been lame since birth, whom everybody in the town had walked past every morning for years, jump up and walk. And they knew exactly what that meant. Or they thought they did.

The crowd began to shout, Acts says, in Lycaonian.

"The gods have come down to us in the likeness of men!"

And they decided which gods. The tall, calm, dignified one, who had stood quietly at the side the whole time, they called Zeus, the king of the gods. And the short, talkative one, who did all the speaking, they called Hermes, because Hermes was the messenger of the gods, and the god of speech, and Paul, Acts says, was the chief speaker. Which, if you have read any of Paul's letters, will not surprise you at all.

Why would they think that? Well, there is an old story that may help. A Roman poet called Ovid, who died a few decades before our story, wrote down a tale set in the hills of Phrygia, which is the next country over from Lycaonia. In it, Jupiter and Mercury, which is what the Romans called Zeus and Hermes, come down to earth disguised as poor travellers, and knock at a thousand doors asking for a bed for the night, and a thousand doors are shut in their faces. At last they come to a tiny cottage with a thatched roof, where an old couple called Baucis and Philemon live, and the old couple take them in, and give them the best of their little food, and even try to catch their one goose to cook for them. The goose, I am pleased to say, runs straight to the visitors for protection, and gets away. And the gods reveal themselves, and reward the old couple, and punish the neighbours who shut their doors.

Nobody knows whether the people of Lystra had heard that very story. But scholars often point to it, because it shows that a tale like it was told in that part of the world. And archaeologists have found inscriptions in the countryside round Lystra showing local people honouring Zeus and Hermes together. So put yourself in the shoes of a farmer from Lystra. Two strangers walk into town. One of them speaks, and a miracle happens. You have heard, all your life, that the gods sometimes come down in disguise to see how people treat travellers, and that the towns that shut their doors are sorry afterwards. You are not going to make that mistake.

So the people of Lystra did what seemed to them the right and generous thing. They went to the priest.

Outside the town, Acts says, there stood a temple of Zeus. And the priest of Zeus, hearing the news, brought oxen and garlands to the gates, and wanted to offer sacrifice, along with all the people.

Now here is the funny thing, and it is a reading, not something Luke says outright, but a great many people have noticed it. All this shouting was in Lycaonian. Paul and Barnabas did not speak Lycaonian. And Acts says that the apostles only found out what was going on when they heard of it, which sounds as though somebody had to explain it to them.

So picture it. There they are, Paul and Barnabas, perhaps back at the house where they were staying, perhaps still in the square, very pleased about the man who has been healed, and thinking that the town seems to be taking it rather well. A good deal of cheering. Some running about. Somebody playing a pipe.

And then up the road come the priest, and the oxen in their garlands, and the whole town behind them, and everyone is looking at the two of them with enormous respect, and one of the local people who speaks Greek comes up, bowing, to explain, very politely, what is about to happen.

And then, at last, they understood.

Acts says they tore their clothes. That was what a Jew did in those days when he heard something terrible, something that dishonoured God. It was not a tantrum. It was the strongest way they had of saying, no, no, this is wrong, stop.

And they ran in among the crowd, shouting.

Here is what they said, in my own plain words, but keeping everything Luke gives.

"Friends! Why are you doing this? We are only men, human beings like you, with the same nature as you. We have come to bring you good news, that you should turn away from these empty things to the living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them. In the generations that have gone by, he let all the nations go their own ways. And yet he never left himself without a witness. He has been good to you. He has given you rain from heaven, and harvests in their seasons, and filled your hearts with food and with gladness."

I want to stay with that little speech for a minute, because it is one of my favourite speeches in the whole Bible.

When Paul preached in the synagogue at Antioch, he started with Abraham and Moses and King David, and quoted the psalms and the prophets, because he was talking to people who knew them. But the farmers of Lystra had never read the psalms. So he did not start there. He started with the weather.

You know the rain, he says. You know the harvest. You know what it is like to sit down at the end of a long day in the fields to a full table, and to feel glad. That gladness did not come from nowhere. Somebody has been sending you the rain all along. Somebody has been filling your barns. You did not know his name, but he knew yours, and he has been kind to you all your lives.

It is a very gentle way to tell somebody that they have been worshipping the wrong thing. He does not call them fools. He tells them that they were right to be grateful, and that they have simply been saying thank you at the wrong door.

And it did not work very well, at least not straight away. Acts says, with what I think is a little dry humour, that even with these words they scarcely restrained the people from sacrificing to them. You can see it. The priest, very disappointed, with all his preparations made. The oxen, who had no opinion about any of it, chewing quietly in their garlands. And the crowd going slowly home, muttering, not entirely convinced that they had not just insulted two gods by taking no for an answer.

I like to think the oxen went back to their field that afternoon still wearing their flowers, and that somebody's children took the garlands off them in the evening and hung them over a gate. That is not in Acts. But I am very fond of those oxen, and I think they deserved a quiet evening.

Now comes the hard part of the story, and I will tell it gently, because it is bedtime.

Some days or weeks later, Acts does not say how long, some of the men who had opposed the travellers at Antioch and at Iconium came to Lystra. They had walked a long way. They must have been very sure they were right. And they talked to the people of the town, and persuaded them.

It does not take much, it seems, to turn a crowd. The same people who had wanted to call Paul a god now picked up stones. And they stoned him, and dragged him out of the town, and left him outside the gate, thinking he was dead.

That is all Luke says, and that is all I will say. Years later, writing to the Christians at Corinth, Paul listed all the things he had suffered, and one of them is just four words. Once I was stoned. He did not dwell on it, and neither will we.

Because what happens next is the part I want you to remember.

The disciples came. Acts says that the disciples gathered round him. Not many, probably. A few people from Lystra who had believed in the few weeks the travellers had been there. Perhaps the man who had been healed, walking out through the gate on his own two feet to stand beside the man who had told him to stand. Perhaps the fig-seller. I do not know. Luke does not name them. He only says that they came, and stood round him in a circle, outside the town, on the ground where he had been left.

And Paul got up.

He got up, and walked back into the town. Not away from it. Into it. Through the same gate where, not long before, the priest had brought the oxen.

And the next day, he and Barnabas set out together for Derbe.

There is something else I should tell you here, and it is a guess, so I will say it is a guess. A few years later, when Paul came back through Lystra on another journey, he found a young believer there called Timothy, whose mother, Eunice, was a Jewish woman who believed, and whose father was a Greek. Timothy became Paul's dearest helper, and Paul called him his beloved child in the Lord. And when Paul was an old man, he wrote to Timothy and reminded him, you know all about my sufferings, what happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium and at Lystra.

You know all about it. Which makes some people wonder whether a boy called Timothy, with his mother and his grandmother Lois, was standing in that circle outside the gate. Acts does not say so. But I like to think about it. A boy, watching a battered man get up off the ground and walk back into the town that had thrown him out, and deciding, perhaps that very day, that whatever this man believed, he wanted to believe it too.

Derbe was further on, out on the edge of the plain, towards the mountains in the south-east. It was a small, quiet town, so quiet that for centuries afterwards nobody was quite sure where it had been, and scholars still argue about the exact spot. Acts says very little about it, only that they preached the good news there and made a good many disciples. For once, nobody seems to have thrown anything at them. I imagine Barnabas was very relieved.

And now Paul and Barnabas had a choice to make.

People who know the roads point out that from Derbe the way home was not far. A few days to the south-east, a great pass runs through the mountains, the Cilician Gates, and on the other side of it lies Tarsus, Paul's home town, and beyond that, the road to Antioch in Syria, where they had started. They could have gone that way. Nobody would have blamed them. They had been chased out of one town, plotted against in another, and stoned in a third.

They went back.

They turned round and walked back the way they had come. Back to Lystra, where the stones had been. Back to Iconium, where the plot had been. Back to Antioch of Pisidia, where they had shaken the dust off their feet. Every one of the towns that had thrown them out.

Why? Acts tells us. They went back to strengthen the souls of the disciples, and to encourage them to keep going in the faith. They had left little groups of new believers behind them in each town, with no Scriptures of their own, no buildings, no teachers, and a great many angry neighbours. And they were not going to leave them like that.

And what did they say to them? Acts gives us one line of it, and it is not what you might expect. It is not, don't worry, everything will be easy now. It is this.

"We must go through many hardships to enter the kingdom of God."

Which is a very honest thing to say. It was not a threat, and it was not a complaint. It was the kind of thing a person says when they have just come back to your town with the bruises still on them. I am not going to pretend to you that this road is smooth. I have walked it. Look at me. And it is still the road to the kingdom, and it is worth it, and you will not walk it alone.

And in each town, they did one more thing. Acts says they appointed elders for them in every church. Presbyters, in the Greek, which is where the English word priest comes from. People to look after the little flock when the travellers had gone. And they did it, Acts says, with prayer and fasting, the same way the church at Antioch had sent them out at the start. And then they commended them, all those small, new, frightened, joyful communities on the cold high plain, to the Lord in whom they had believed.

I picture it at night, in somebody's house in Lystra, with the lamps lit. A small room, crowded. A man who used to sit on a blanket by the fountain, standing at the back, because he likes standing now and does it whenever he can. Barnabas with his hand on somebody's shoulder. Paul, still a little stiff, praying in a low voice. And then the goodbyes at the door, and the two travellers going off down the dark street, and everyone in the room knowing that they might not see them again for years, or at all.

Then the long road home.

They walked back down out of the mountains, through the country of Pisidia, to the coast of Pamphylia. They preached at Perga, which they had hurried through on their way up. Then they went down to the port of Attalia, which is still a port today, and found a ship, and sailed back to Antioch in Syria, to the very church that had laid hands on them and sent them out, it might have been two years before. Nobody knows exactly how long they had been gone.

And when they arrived, Acts says, they gathered the church together, and told them everything that God had done with them.

I want you to notice that. Not everything that they had done. Everything that God had done with them. They did not come home and say, we preached brilliantly, and we were very brave, and we were nearly made into gods, and then we were nearly killed. They came home and said, look what God did. And most of all, Acts says, they told how he had opened a door of faith for the nations. For the people who were not Jews. For the farmers of Lystra who had never read a psalm, and the Greeks of Iconium, and the quiet folk of Derbe.

A door of faith. Opened by somebody else. All they had done was walk through it, and hold it open, and keep holding it open, even when people threw things at them.

And then, Acts says, they stayed there with the disciples for no little time. Which is Luke's way of saying a good long while. And I hope they rested. I hope somebody cooked for them. I hope Paul let himself sit down.

That is nearly the end of the story.

Paul and Barnabas did not travel together again after that. On the next journey they disagreed about whether to take young John Mark with them a second time, and they parted, Barnabas taking Mark to Cyprus, and Paul taking a new companion, Silas, back up through the mountains to Derbe and Lystra. And it was on that journey, at Lystra, that he met Timothy. So the story of tonight's chapter did not stop. It went on.

Barnabas, by an old tradition, went home to Cyprus and gave his life for the faith there, and he is honoured as the founder of the church of Cyprus. Mark, the cousin who had gone home early, became a great helper of Paul in the end, and Paul asked for him by name in one of his last letters. Which tells you that Barnabas was right to give him a second chance. Sons of encouragement usually are.

Lystra fell slowly quiet. Over the centuries, people forgot exactly where it had been. Then, in the eighteen-eighties, an American scholar walking over a low hill near a village on the plain found an old stone with a Latin inscription on it, and the inscription had the town's name. So now we know where it stood. The hill is fields again. Sheep graze on it. In the spring there are flowers, and you could, if you were patient, make a garland from them.

And the plain is still there, wide and high, under the same big sky. The rain still comes, in its season. The wheat still grows. And at the end of a long day, the farmers still sit down to their tables, and feel glad, and if they are very wise, they say thank you, and say it at the right door.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is told in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter fourteen, with the last verses of chapter thirteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. That is our only account of these events, and everything Paul, Barnabas, the crowd and the priest do and say is kept as Acts gives it, in our own plain rendering. Paul mentions his sufferings at Antioch, Iconium and Lystra in the Second Letter to Timothy, and says once I was stoned in the Second Letter to the Corinthians.

We invented the weather, the look of the towns, where the healed man sat, the fig-seller, the rented room, the thoughts and small talk of the travellers, and the scenes in the houses. The idea that the apostles did not understand the Lycaonian shouting at first is a common reading of the words they heard of it, not something Luke states. The tale of Baucis and Philemon is in Ovid's Metamorphoses; whether the people of Lystra knew such a tale is a suggestion scholars make. That Timothy saw any of it is a guess, and we have said so. Barnabas's death on Cyprus is tradition. A later book, the Acts of Paul and Thecla, also tells of Paul at Iconium; it is not Scripture, and we told its story as legend in episode one hundred and ten. Paul's letter to the Galatians remembers being received like an angel of God when he was ill; whether it was written to these same towns is debated, and we leave it there.

That's the story of Paul and Barnabas and the garlands at the gate of Lystra for tonight. Saint Barnabas is remembered on the eleventh of June, and Saint Paul, with Saint Peter, on the twenty-ninth of June, in both the Catholic and Orthodox calendars. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

And here is a blessing. May the Lord who sends the rain and fills your heart with food and gladness be thanked at your table tonight. May you have a Barnabas beside you on the road, and be one to somebody else. If you have been knocked down, may friends stand round you in a circle until you can get up. And may you rest, as two tired travellers rested at the end of a long journey, in a house full of people who were glad to see them home.

Goodnight, and God bless.

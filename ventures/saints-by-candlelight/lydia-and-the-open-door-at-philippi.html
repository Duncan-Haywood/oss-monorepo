Lydia and the Open Door at Philippi

A dealer in purple cloth meets a few travellers by a river outside a Roman town and opens her house to them. A few days later, at midnight, a prison door opens as well, and a jailer asks the best question in the book. Told straight from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter sixteen.

Full script

Picture a river at the edge of a town, early in the morning, and a handful of women sitting on the bank with their baskets beside them. Nothing is happening. Somebody is passing round a flask of water. Somebody has brought a bit of bread, and is breaking it in a businesslike way. The birds are noisy and the mist is going.

Then four tired men come down the path, dusty to the knees, and ask whether they might sit down.

And the woman nearest the water, who is used to people asking her for things, looks them over and says, quite kindly, "You look as though you have walked a long way."

It will be a long week. It will have a river in it, and a market, and a riot, and an earthquake, and a very unhappy man with a sword. But it begins with a seat on a riverbank, and a woman who moves her basket over.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter sixteen, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the weather, the talk among the women, the look of the river and the prison, and the quiet moments between the verses, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let us start with the road.

Some years after the Lord went home to the Father, a man called Paul is walking around the northern edge of the Mediterranean, telling people about Jesus. You may remember him from other nights. He is small, we are told, or at any rate he is not impressive to look at, and he is the sort of person who can be knocked down by a stone and then get up and go into town for dinner. He has been walking for a very long time. He has two companions with him, Silas and Timothy. Silas is an older man from the church in Jerusalem, with a good singing voice, which will matter later. Timothy is young, and his mother and grandmother raised him on the Scriptures, which will not matter tonight but is a nice thing to know about a person.

They have a plan. Paul would like to go east, into the province of Asia, which is the part we now call the western edge of Turkey. And Luke, who is writing the book, says very simply that the Holy Spirit stopped them. He says it twice. They tried to go one way, and the way was shut. They tried another, and that was shut as well. He does not tell us how. A dream, a closed road, a sick traveller, a strong feeling in the middle of the night, we are not told. We are only told that Paul and his friends found themselves walking not where they had planned to go but down, down, down to the sea, to a port called Troas, with nowhere else to go.

Every traveller knows that feeling. You set out for one place, and a series of small, annoying events pushes you toward another, and only years later do you understand why.

And at Troas, in the night, Paul has a dream.

He sees a man of Macedonia, standing and begging him, and saying, "Come over to Macedonia and help us."

That is the whole dream. It is short. There is no angel in it, no thunder. Only a man, a stranger, from the other side of a stretch of sea, asking for help, and it is enough. In the morning Paul tells the others. And Luke, who has been telling the story up to now as "they" and "he," suddenly begins to say "we." We immediately tried to cross over to Macedonia, he writes, concluding that God had called us to preach the good news to them.

We. Nobody explains it. The writer simply steps into his own story, the way a person quietly sits down at a table where he has been invited. From here, for much of the chapter, Luke is there. If he is telling it, then what he tells us about that river and that prison was seen by a man who was standing in the room.

So they sail. The wind is good, which is rare. They stop for a night at an island called Samothrace, then land at a harbour called Neapolis, which simply means New Town, and which is the Greek equivalent of calling a place Newtown. And then they walk inland, about ten miles, up and over a ridge, and down onto a wide green plain with mountains behind it. And on the plain is a town named Philippi.

Now, we should spend a few minutes on Philippi, because it is not what you might expect.

It is in Macedonia, yes, in Greece. But it is not very Greek. It is Roman. About ninety years before this story, there was a great battle on that plain, where the men who had killed Julius Caesar were beaten by the men who wanted to avenge him. And afterwards, the winners did what Roman winners did. They took their old soldiers, who were tired and wanted somewhere to settle, and they gave them farms there, and made the whole town a Roman colony. That meant something quite particular. A colony was a little piece of Rome set down in a foreign country. The people were Roman citizens. The laws were Roman laws. The language of the courts was Latin. The coins had Latin on them. The magistrates wore Roman robes, and had officers who walked in front of them, carrying bundles of rods, to show that they were in charge.

If you want a modern comparison, imagine a small town in the middle of the countryside which for some reason has its own flag, its own anthem, its own uniforms and its own opinions about how things are done, and is rather proud of all of them. That was Philippi. A little Rome, with a good road running through it.

It was a good road, too. It was called the Via Egnatia, and it ran across the whole of the north of Greece, from the Adriatic coast to the Aegean, and the Roman armies marched along it and the traders came and went along it with their donkeys. Philippi sat right on it, and there were always people coming through. Soldiers, farmers, merchants, travellers, and every kind of gossip.

The one thing that Philippi did not seem to have, if you were looking for it, was a synagogue.

Now, in those days, you needed ten Jewish men to make a synagogue, and perhaps Philippi did not have ten who wanted to be Jewish in a town that was so very, very Roman. Luke does not say. He only says that on the Sabbath, the travellers went outside the gate, beside the river, where they supposed there was a place of prayer.

Supposed. We like that word. It is the word of people who have come a very long way and are not quite sure of the address.

There was a river, outside the town, a small clear one, and the people who wanted to pray met beside it. The water was handy for washing before prayer, as devout Jews did. It was quiet. It was outside the city walls, away from the temples of the Roman gods, and it was a good place to be, if you wanted to speak to the Lord God of Israel without anybody watching.

And when they arrived, Luke says, they sat down and spoke to the women who had gathered there.

The women. Not the men. We are not told why there were only women. Perhaps the men were at work, or at the barracks. Perhaps it was simply the custom of the place. What we know is that on the first Sabbath that Paul and his friends spent in Philippi, the whole of the congregation they found was a handful of women by a river. It was not a crowd. It was not a hall with a platform. There was not a single person in it who could have been called important.

Paul sat down with them anyway.

And that is a rather good thing to remember, when you are tired, and have come a long way, and do not seem to have been given much of an audience.

Let us meet the woman at the water.

Her name is Lydia. And Luke tells us three things about her, in one tidy sentence, which is how Luke likes to do things.

First, that she is a dealer in purple cloth. Second, that she is from the city of Thyatira. And third, that she is a worshipper of God.

Let us take those one at a time.

Purple first. In the ancient world, purple was not a colour you picked up at a shop for a few coins. It was a luxury. The dye was made from a certain kind of sea snail, which lives in the Mediterranean, and you needed thousands of them to dye a single robe. A writer called Pliny, who lived not long after this story, wrote a whole section about how it was done, and it is a smelly, patient, expensive business. The shells were crushed, and the juice was boiled in lead vats for days, and the result was a deep, rich, red-purple that stayed bright for years. Emperors wore it. Senators wore a stripe of it. Wealthy people liked to have a touch of it somewhere, if they could afford it.

So Lydia sells something precious. She is not a market stall woman with a basket of onions. She is a merchant in a high-end trade, with customers who have money, and probably with a couple of staff and a storeroom. She is a person of some substance.

Second, Thyatira. That is a city in Asia, in the same country that Paul was told not to enter a few days earlier, which is a rather nice detail, if you think about it. He was stopped from going to Lydia's home town, and so Lydia came to him. Thyatira was famous for dyeing. We have inscriptions from the ancient city that name a guild of dyers, and the word comes up again and again, which is a handy sign that the place was proud of its trade. So she may have grown up with vats and stained fingers, in a town that smelled of mordant and boiling water, and then moved across the sea to sell what she knew. We are not told. We are only told she is from there.

And here is a small thing for the curious. The name Lydia may simply mean a woman from Lydia, which is the region in which Thyatira stood. So it is possible that "Lydia" is what the people of Philippi called her, the way you might call a person from Paris "Parisienne," and that she had another name, which we do not know. That is only a guess. It may be her name outright. We will call her Lydia, as Luke does.

Third, she is a worshipper of God. In the language of the time, that is a particular sort of person. She is not a Jew by birth, and she is not exactly a convert. She is one of those many Gentiles, men and women, who had heard about the one God of Israel, and felt something in them leaning towards him, and who attended the prayers and read the Scriptures and kept the Sabbath as best they could, without taking the whole step of joining. They were God-fearers. There were a great many of them, and the early Church found more of them than anywhere else.

So here is Lydia. A Greek-speaking woman, a long way from home, in a Roman town where the gods are everywhere, and she goes out every Sabbath to a river to pray to a God that the neighbours would think very peculiar indeed.

We do not know why. We know only that she is there, and she is listening.

And Luke says the best sentence of the scene, in a very few words.

The Lord opened her heart to give heed to what was said by Paul.

Not Paul opened it. Not a clever argument. Not a stirring speech. The Lord opened it. Paul speaks, and the words fall into the air, and she hears them, and somewhere inside her a door that has been closed for a long while swings quietly open.

We can imagine it was not dramatic. We think it may have been a little like the moment when you are listening to a friend, and suddenly realise that what they are saying is true, and has been true for a long time, and that you have been waiting for someone to say it. A small click. A deep breath. An "oh."

And then, Luke says, she was baptised, with her household.

Her household. That is a plain word that covers a lot of people. The household of a merchant like Lydia would have included family, servants, workers, perhaps freed people and their children. All of them, it seems, were part of it. Luke does not tell us how, or what they said, or what the water was like, or whether it was cold. He only says that it was done, and that there was a river nearby, and it was a quiet, plain, wet little beginning for the Church in Europe.

This is, as far as we know from the record, the first Christian community on that side of the sea. It begins with a businesswoman, her household, and a river.

And then she does the thing that makes her a saint.

She says, "If you have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come to my house and stay."

Let us hear that again. If you have judged me faithful, she says. If you have decided I am real. If you think I'm sincere, then come in.

Luke adds a short sentence. And she prevailed upon us.

She prevailed. The Greek is a strong word. It means something like she insisted, and would not take no for an answer, and very possibly dragged them by their sleeves. We can imagine the four of them, travellers, a little shy, a little embarrassed, perhaps saying, "Oh, we couldn't," and Lydia, who has run a business and managed a household and is used to getting people to do what is good for them, simply saying, "Nonsense. It is settled."

Anyone who has ever been fed by someone who would not let them refuse will know this woman.

So they go. We are not told what the house was like. Let us give it a guess, since we have said we will invent a few things. A good-sized house in the town, with an inner courtyard, and a storeroom at the back with bolts of cloth in it, folded and wrapped against the dust. A faint smell of dye clinging to everything, sharp and salty, like the sea in a hot week. Some rooms upstairs. A kitchen with a hearth. A woman in a corner of it, who has been told that there will be four more to dinner, and has quietly started to count the lentils.

And that evening, or that week, or that month, because Acts does not tell us how long they stayed, a house in Philippi becomes something new. It becomes the meeting place. Later in the chapter, when Paul and Silas finally leave town, Luke says they went to Lydia's house and saw the brothers and encouraged them. Which means that by then, there is a group. There are other believers. They know where to go. And where they go is her door.

That is what we mean when we say that she is a saint of hospitality. She did not preach in the marketplace. She did not write a book, so far as we know. She had a house, and a table, and she opened both. It is hard to overstate how much that mattered in a world where there were no church buildings, and wouldn't be for another two hundred years. The Church lived in people's houses. It sat on their floors. It ate their bread. It held its prayers in their upstairs rooms, with their children's toys pushed out of the way.

Without the houses, there is no Church. And the houses were opened, one by one, by people like Lydia, who said, "Come, stay."

Now. We said there would be a riot. We had better get on to it.

For a number of days, Luke says, the travellers keep going to the place of prayer. And on one of those days, they meet a girl.

Let us be gentle about her. She is a slave. That is the first and the saddest thing. She belongs to some owners, in the plural, who have found a clever way to make money from her. Luke says that she has a spirit of divination, and that she makes a lot of profit for her masters by telling fortunes.

We do not need to say very much about what that means. In the ancient world, there were people who were thought to be able to say what was coming, and the town paid them for it, and the owners of this girl were doing very nicely out of the arrangement. But whatever was in her, it was not hers, and it was not good for her. She was being used, from the outside and from the inside, and nobody in the whole of Philippi seems to have noticed.

And she begins to follow Paul and his friends around the streets.

She walks behind them, and she shouts. "These men are servants of the Most High God," she cries, "who proclaim to you the way of salvation."

Now, that is true. Every word of it is perfectly true. If a person said it to you on the street, you might think it a rather nice endorsement. But Paul does not welcome it. And Luke tells us that she did this for many days, so we can imagine the scene. A girl shouting the same sentence, day after day, the whole way up the street, past the baker and the water seller and the man with the olives, and every head turning to see who these strangers are. It is not helpful. It is something a little like having a loudspeaker van following you around, announcing your business. And behind the voice, what is really speaking is not a girl who has understood a word of it, but something that is using her to make a noise.

Paul gets annoyed. Luke's word is that he was greatly troubled. He is a patient man, but there is a limit. And one day he turns around, and speaks, not to the girl, but to what is in her.

"I order you in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her."

And Luke says, quite simply: And it came out that very hour.

That is all. No fire, no thunder. A girl, a street, a few words, and then a different kind of quiet. We can imagine her standing a moment, looking about as if she had just woken from a nap, and finding the street very large and strange. We are not told what she did next. We are not told her name, or where she went, or whether anyone looked after her. We would very much like to know. But Luke does not say, and so we leave her where he leaves her, free, standing in the sun.

And that is the end of her part in the story, and the beginning of a much more difficult part for the men.

Because the owners come out, and they look at her, and they look at Paul, and they realise that something has gone wrong with their business. Luke puts it plainly: when her owners saw that their hope of profit was gone, they seized Paul and Silas and dragged them into the marketplace before the authorities.

Hope of profit. That is the phrase. Not the girl's health. Not a miracle. Their hope of profit.

The marketplace of a Roman colony was a grand place. There would have been a paved square, a platform for the magistrates, a row of shops, statues of emperors, and an excited crowd. The magistrates are the two chief officers of the town, and Luke says that they are brought before them, and the charge goes like this:

"These men are Jews, and they are disturbing our city. They advocate customs that it is not lawful for us as Romans to accept or practise."

Look carefully at that. The owners do not say, "They took away our income." That would sound rather small. They say, "They are Jews, and they are un-Roman." They reach for what they know will make a Roman crowd angry. A foreigner, with strange customs, upsetting the order of a very proud town. And it works. The crowd joins in. The magistrates tear the clothes off the men and order them to be beaten with rods.

We will say only that it was a hard beating, and not tell you more than Luke does. He says that after they had inflicted many blows, they threw them into prison and ordered the jailer to keep them safely.

The jailer is a careful man. He has been told to keep these two safely, and he takes the instruction seriously. He puts them in the inner cell, the one furthest from the door, the darkest and the coldest, and he fastens their feet in the stocks.

Let us sit there for a moment, in the dark.

It is a small room. It is damp. It smells of stone and old straw and the people who have been there before. Their backs are sore, and their feet are in wood, and they cannot lie down comfortably or sit up comfortably, and there is no light at all except a thin line under the door. They have walked a long way, preached in a strange town, helped a girl, and now they are here. Anyone could be forgiven for being low in spirits.

Now, if you were Paul or Silas, what would you do?

Luke tells us.

About midnight, he says, Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them.

Praying, and singing. In the middle of the night, in the stocks, with their backs sore. And the other prisoners were listening.

That is a remarkable thing. It was not a performance, because no one had asked for one. It was not a trick. It was only that two tired men, who had nothing else to hold on to, held on to the Lord. And they did it out loud, in a place where the whole cellblock could hear them.

We like to imagine that Silas was the one with the better voice, and that he began, and that Paul came in on the second line, a little flat. We do not know. What we know is that in the deepest, darkest part of the prison, somewhere around midnight, there was a psalm. And other men, who had been sitting in their own misery, stopped what they were doing, and listened.

And now the earthquake.

Luke says: Suddenly there was a great earthquake, so that the foundations of the prison were shaken, and immediately all the doors were opened, and everyone's chains were unfastened.

Everyone's chains. Not just Paul's and Silas's. All of them. Every man in the prison finds himself, in the dark, with his shackles hanging open and his door swinging free.

Now this is a strange thing, and we want to notice what does not happen. Nobody runs. Nobody pushes past anybody. Nobody seizes the chance. We are told, in a moment, that every one of them is still there. We do not know why. Perhaps they were too startled. Perhaps they were listening to the song, and the song had held them in place. Perhaps they simply did not know what to do. But whatever it was, a room full of people who had been given every excuse to flee stood still, and waited, and watched.

The jailer wakes up.

We should say something about this man. He is a Roman official of a modest kind, probably an old soldier, which is who tended to get such jobs. He is responsible for the prisoners, and in Roman law, if a prisoner escaped, the person in charge could be given the same punishment the prisoner would have received. In some cases, that meant death. He knows that. Every jailer knew that.

So when he sees the doors open, he assumes the worst. All of his prisoners are gone. His career is over, and so is his life, and so is the safety of his family. He cannot face it. He draws his sword, and he is about to kill himself.

In the dark, a voice calls out.

It is Paul. He is still in the inner cell, which means he can see the jailer's silhouette against the open door, and he can see what he is about to do.

"Do not harm yourself!" he shouts. "We are all here!"

All of them. Every one. The men who could have run, did not. And the man who was about to die on his own blade is stopped by the one voice in the prison that has nothing to fear from him.

That is the second great sentence of the chapter. We are all here.

The jailer calls for lights. He rushes in. And Luke tells us that he fell down trembling before Paul and Silas. Not because of the earthquake. Not because of the sword. But, we think, because of what he has just heard.

Then he brings them out, and he asks them the best question in the whole book.

"Sirs, what must I do to be saved?"

We do not know exactly what he meant. Perhaps he only meant, how do I get out of this trouble? Perhaps he had heard the girl shouting in the street, "the way of salvation," and the words had stayed with him. Perhaps he had listened to the singing through the wall, that night and other nights, and it had worked on him in a way he could not explain. Perhaps he meant it in the biggest possible sense. We are not told. Luke only gives us the question, and we may hear it as we like. It is a plain, human, honest question, and it is asked by a frightened man in the middle of the night.

And Paul and Silas answer, in a few quiet words.

"Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household."

That is the whole of the answer. Believe. Not do a hundred things. Not pass a test. Believe in the Lord Jesus. And again, that word, household. The same word that was used of Lydia. For this Church, the Lord does not save people as tiny, separate, lonely units. He saves them with their families, their servants, and the whole noisy crowd that lives under one roof.

And then they speak the word of the Lord to him, and to everyone in his house. They tell him the whole story, we imagine, starting from the beginning, in the middle of the night, in the jailer's own living quarters, with the children woken up and sitting on the floor in their nightclothes.

And now comes the part of the story that we love the most, because it is the smallest.

The jailer takes them, in that same hour of the night, and washes their wounds.

Let us see it. A man who, a few hours ago, had locked them in the stocks, and put them in the darkest cell, and who was following orders, now kneels on the floor with a basin of water and a cloth. He takes the cloth, and he gently wipes their backs. He is an old soldier. He has seen a great many wounds. He knows how to clean them, and how to bind them, and he does it carefully, and probably does not say much.

And then, Luke says, immediately he was baptised, he and all his family.

In the middle of the night, in the same hour. We do not know where. Possibly a courtyard well, or a cistern, or a basin in the house. And the washing of the wounds and the washing of baptism come together in one night, in one house, with one family. We think it is the loveliest thing in the chapter. A man who washed the wounds of the apostles was himself washed in the Lord.

And then, because he is a decent man, he brings them up into his house, and sets a meal before them. Hot food, at the dead of night. Bread, and something to go with it. We are told that he rejoiced, he and his entire household, that he had become a believer in God.

He rejoiced. Imagine it. This same man, a few hours before, had been about to end his life. And now the whole family is awake, around a table, full of bread and wonder, and the dawn is not far off. The prisoners are still in the prison. The doors are open. The chains lie where they dropped. And in the house next door, there is a feast.

When it is day, the magistrates send their officers with a message to the jailer.

"Let those men go."

That is all. It may be that the magistrates, who have had a night to think about it, have decided that they perhaps went too far. Or it may be that they heard about the earthquake, which would have been noticed in a small town. We do not know. But the order is clear, and the jailer, who is pleased, goes to Paul and says, "The magistrates have sent word to let you go. Now therefore come out and go in peace."

Which is a perfectly kind thing to say.

But Paul does not go.

He says something that surprises us a little. He says, "They have beaten us in public, uncondemned, men who are Roman citizens, and have thrown us into prison. And now do they cast us out secretly? No! Let them come themselves and take us out."

Now. That requires a bit of explaining.

Paul is a Roman citizen. So, it seems, is Silas. That meant something quite specific. A Roman citizen could not lawfully be beaten without a trial, and he could not be put in prison without a hearing. It was one of the most basic rights in the empire, the sort of thing that would get a magistrate into serious trouble if he ignored it. And the magistrates of Philippi had ignored it. They had done so in public, in front of everyone, without so much as asking a question.

Why did Paul not say so before? Luke does not tell us. Perhaps there was too much noise. Perhaps no one would listen. Perhaps he thought it was not the moment. Whatever the reason, he says it now, and he says it for a particular reason. He does not want to slip out of the back door like a criminal who has been quietly let off. The young Church in Philippi is going to have to live in this town, long after he has gone. If it is known that the two leaders of the new faith were thrown out in the night like thieves, then every believer will carry that shame. So he asks for an apology in public.

It is a small, dignified request. And notice what he does not ask for. He does not ask for revenge. He does not demand that the magistrates be punished. He asks only that they come themselves, and bring them out by the front door, so that the Church is not made ashamed.

The officers go back and tell the magistrates. And Luke says, with one of his dry little touches, that when the magistrates heard that they were Roman citizens, they were afraid.

Of course they were. They had just done something that could have cost them their jobs.

So they come. They come themselves, to the prison, and they apologise. They bring Paul and Silas out, and they ask, very politely, if the two of them would be so kind as to leave the city.

And so we come to the end.

Paul and Silas leave the prison. They do not leave the city, not straight away. Luke says that they went to Lydia's house. There they saw the brothers and encouraged them. And then they departed.

Of course they did. Where else would they go? There is a door, and behind it a table, and behind that a woman who is probably already counting the lentils.

We should like to have been there. The room would have been full. Lydia, her household, the other believers, and perhaps by now the jailer, and his family, and the people from the river. Two men who had been beaten, sitting among them. Everyone talking at once. The story of the earthquake being told, and told again, and being corrected on the details. Someone would have said, "And then the chains fell off!" and someone else, "No, they didn't fall, they just came open." And someone would have laughed. And someone would have brought more bread.

That is how the Church in Philippi began. A river. A house. A prison. A second house. And a great many people sitting down together to eat.

Let us slow down now.

Paul went on. He went west, along the great road, to other towns, and other prisons, and other houses. But he never forgot Philippi. Years later, he wrote them a letter, from a prison of his own. We still have it. It is the shortest and the warmest of his letters, and if you read it, you can hear how fond he is of them. I thank my God, he writes, in all my remembrance of you. I hold you in my heart. And at the end, he thanks them for a gift they had sent him, again and again, when no other church had thought to. They had remembered him. They had kept sending help.

We cannot prove it, but we think, when he says that, that he is thinking of a woman with purple on her hands, and a man with a basin.

The sun is going down now, over the plain. The river has gone quiet. The women are packing their baskets, and the mist that was there in the morning has long since gone, and the first stars are coming out over the mountains.

In a house on a quiet street, a lamp is lit in an upstairs window. Nobody is shouting. Nobody is running. A guest is asleep in the best room, and a cloth is folded over a chair, and the whole household is settling down for the night.

And in a small house, on the other side of the town, a man who used to guard the doors of the prison is lying awake, listening to his children breathe. He has a basin by the bed. He is not afraid of anything at all.

There are doors in the world that close, and doors that open, and the story of Philippi seems to say that the Lord is very good at the second kind. He opens hearts. He opens prisons. He opens the doors of kitchens and storerooms and upstairs rooms, and sometimes, if we are very lucky, he opens ours.

And if there is something in your life tonight that feels shut, a door you cannot find the key to, a worry that has locked you in, a long road with no sign at the end of it, you might do what Paul and Silas did. You might say a psalm, even a bad one. You might sing it, even flat. You might not know whether anyone is listening. But the other prisoners are, and so is someone else.

And then you might sleep.

Here are the notes for the curious.

The story of Lydia and the jailer is told in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter sixteen, verses nine to forty, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. That is our only full account of them, and everything that Paul, Silas, Lydia, the girl, the jailer and the magistrates do and say in the story is kept as Acts gives it. The dream of the man of Macedonia, the places the travellers passed through, the women at the river, the girl and her owners, the earthquake, and the demand for a public apology are all in the chapter. The Letter to the Philippians, in which Paul writes warmly to the same community, is a real letter, and we have drawn on it only for a few of its words.

We invented the weather, the mist on the river, the talk among the women, the look of Lydia's house, the thoughts of the characters, the sounds and smells of the prison, and the scene around the table at the end. The idea that Lydia may be a name that simply means a woman of Lydia is a suggestion that scholars make, and we have offered it as a guess. Her trade in purple, and the dye made from sea snails, are well attested. The guild of dyers at Thyatira is known from inscriptions. The beating, and the right of a Roman citizen to a trial, are attested in the law and in other parts of Acts. We do not know how many believers there were in Philippi, or how long Paul stayed. The girl and the jailer are not named in Acts, and we have left them unnamed. Later writers say a good deal about the later lives of Lydia and the jailer, and we have named that and left it out, because we cannot stand behind it.

That's the story of Lydia and the open door at Philippi for tonight. Lydia is remembered in both the Catholic and Orthodox calendars, though not on the same day, and calendars differ, so you will want to check your own.

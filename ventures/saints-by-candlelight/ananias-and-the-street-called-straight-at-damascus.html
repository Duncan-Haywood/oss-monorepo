Ananias and the Street Called Straight at Damascus

A quiet disciple in Damascus is told in a vision to go and visit the most frightening man in the city, and he says what any of us would say. Then he goes. Told straight from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter nine.

Full script

Picture a man sitting in a dark room with his eyes open, and seeing nothing at all.

He has been sitting there for three days. He has not eaten. He has not had so much as a cup of water. Somebody, at some point, has led him in by the hand, and sat him down on a bench, and he has not moved since. His friends, if you can call them that, are standing in the courtyard, whispering. They have never seen him like this. This is a man who, a week ago, could walk into any room in Jerusalem and make everyone in it look at their shoes.

And he is praying. That is the strange thing. If you put your ear to the door, you would hear it. He is saying words that he has known since he was a boy, the old psalms, and between the psalms he is not saying anything. He is only waiting. For something. For someone.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter nine, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the weather, the look of the street and the house, the talk of the neighbours, and the quiet moments between the verses, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let us start with the man in the dark room, because you will have met him before, and he has a different name now than the one he is using tonight.

Tonight, he is called Saul. He comes from a city called Tarsus, on the south coast of what is now Turkey, a busy harbour town full of merchants and philosophers and people who made tents. He is a Jew, a Pharisee, and a very serious young man. He studied in Jerusalem, we are told, under a famous teacher called Gamaliel. He knows the Scriptures the way some people know the stations on their favourite railway line, every stop and every connection. And he is certain, quite certain, that a new movement growing up around a teacher called Jesus of Nazareth is a mistake. A dangerous one.

Remember that he is not a villain in his own mind. Very few people are. He thinks he is protecting something he loves. He thinks the new teaching is going to break his people's faith in pieces, and so he does what a serious young man with a letter of authority does. He goes after the followers of Jesus, in their houses and their meeting places, and has them arrested. Luke, who wrote the Acts, is plain about it. He says Saul was breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord. That is not a pleasant phrase, and we will not stay on it. It is enough to say that the people of the new Way had good reason to be afraid of him, and they were.

So when the news goes round, in Jerusalem and then farther and farther out, that the high priests have given Saul letters to the synagogues of Damascus, authorising him to find any followers of the Way there, men or women, and bring them back in chains, a great many people stop what they are doing, and sit quietly for a moment.

Damascus is a long way north. Roughly a hundred and thirty miles from Jerusalem, if you walk it, and walking it would take the better part of a week. We do not know the exact route and Acts does not tell us, and so we will not pretend to.

What we do know about Damascus is that it is one of the oldest cities in the world still lived in. It has been a city for thousands of years. It sits in a green oasis at the edge of the desert, fed by a river that runs down from the mountains, and from the desert side it looks like a miracle. After days of dust and rock, you come over a rise, and there it is, a great patch of orchards and gardens and rooftops, the colour of a lettuce in a field of sand. There were Jews living in Damascus in this period, a good many of them, with several synagogues. There were Greeks, and Arabs, and Romans, and traders from every road. It was the sort of place where a person could be one thing in the morning and another by the evening, and nobody would be surprised.

And at the heart of the city, running from one gate right through to the other, there was a long straight street. Romans liked a straight street. Most of the old cities of the east were a tangle of little lanes, and the Romans came along with their rulers and their surveyors and drew a line down the middle. This one still runs through the old city of Damascus today, lined with shops, and it is still known by a name that goes back to Luke's own book. It is called, in Greek, the Street Called Straight. In Latin, the Via Recta. In every language, it means exactly what you think.

Why does that matter? It matters because the whole of tonight's story takes place in about a dozen houses within a quick walk of it, and the street is the thread. It is almost the only street in the story that has a name.

Now, we need to say a little about the people who lived there, the followers of Jesus, because they were not what you might expect.

They were not a church, not yet, not in the sense of a building with a bell. They were a handful of families, here and there, who had heard about the Lord from travellers or from relatives, and who met in each other's houses to pray and to break bread. Some of them had fled from Jerusalem after the trouble there. Some had been in the city from the start. They kept their heads down, because they had to. They did not put up signs.

And among them there was a man called Ananias.

That is nearly all we are told, and you should know that Luke is not stingy here, by his own standards. He tells us that Ananias was a disciple. He gives him a name. He tells us that, many years later, in a speech, Paul remembered him as a devout man by the standards of the law, well spoken of by all the Jews who lived there. That is a lovely phrase. A man whom everyone in the neighbourhood, of every opinion, would say was a decent sort. The kind of person who is asked to witness a will, or to settle a quarrel over a fence.

We do not know how old he was. We do not know his trade. We do not know if he was married, or had children, or kept a dog. The Acts of the Apostles does not say, and so the way we have pictured him tonight is our own. We imagine a man in middle life. We imagine him tidy, careful, a bit slow to speak, one of those who listens to the end of a sentence before replying. We imagine that he is the person in his little group who is always sent to talk to the landlord. A quiet man, with a quiet life.

Which makes it all the more surprising what happens next.

It is an ordinary evening in Damascus. The heat has gone out of the day. In the narrow lanes off the main street, people are carrying water, and shutting up their shops, and calling their children in. Somewhere somebody is frying onions, and the smell goes on drifting, the way it always does, from one rooftop to the next.

And word is going round the little group of believers that something has happened on the road.

It is not clear who brought the news. Perhaps a merchant, perhaps a traveller who had been on the road behind Saul's company. The story, as it reaches Damascus, is confusing. A man had been coming up the road with an escort, with letters, with every intention of making trouble. And something had stopped him. A light, they said. A great light from the sky, brighter than the noon, at midday. He had fallen to the ground. There had been a voice. The men who were with him had heard the sound of it, but had seen no one, and they had stood there with their mouths open, not knowing what to do. And then Saul had got up, and found he could not see, and the men had taken him by the hand, one on each side, like a child, and led him on into the city.

Nobody in the little group knows what to make of it. Some of them think it is a trick. Some of them think it is a punishment, and are not sorry. A few of them, the more careful ones, simply say nothing and go home and lock the door. And one of them, Ananias, sits in his own house that night, a little troubled, saying his prayers, with a lamp lit and a cup of something warm going cold beside him.

And then the Lord speaks to him in a vision.

Luke says it very simply. The Lord said to him in a vision, "Ananias."

And he answers. He answers the way people answer in the Bible, the way Samuel answered, and Abraham, and Moses at the bush. He says, "Here I am, Lord."

Now this is the moment the whole story hangs on, so we will go slowly.

The Lord says to him, "Get up and go to the street called Straight. Ask at the house of Judas for a man of Tarsus named Saul. He is there now, praying. He has seen a man named Ananias come in and lay hands on him, so that he may see again."

That is the instruction. It is short, and clear, and it has an address in it. Straight Street, the house of Judas. This is not the Judas of the other story. It is a common name, as common as John or Peter would be today, and the Judas who owns this house is a man we hear of nowhere else. He has simply lent a room, or let one, to the most dangerous guest in the city, and has presumably been wondering ever since whether that was wise.

Notice, too, the little detail the Lord gives. He is praying. Of all the things Ananias might have been told about Saul, the Lord begins with that. Not that he is blind. Not that he is afraid. That he is praying. As if to say, you may have heard many things about this man, and most of them are true, but here is another thing, and it is true as well.

And Ananias, who is a polite man, and a reverent one, who has said "Here I am, Lord" with perfect sincerity, answers in a way that is so human, so very much what any of us would say, that you can almost hear him clearing his throat.

"Lord," he says, "I have heard from many people about this man. I have heard how much harm he has done to your holy ones in Jerusalem. And he has come here with authority from the chief priests to arrest everyone who calls on your name."

Do you see what he is doing? He is not refusing. He is not saying no. He is saying, in the politest possible way, are you sure? Have you got the right address? Because the person you have just described is the person whose name makes grown men go quiet. Everyone here has heard of him. We have been talking about nothing else. He has come to put us in chains. And you want me to walk up to his front door and knock?

It is a very good objection. If you sat in the room with Ananias and listened to it, you would not say he was lacking in faith. You would say he was thinking, which is a thing the Lord seems not to mind. We think of how Moses argued with God at the bush, and Gideon asked for a sign, and Mary asked, how can this be? The Lord, in Scripture, has a remarkable patience with people who ask the question honestly.

And the Lord answers him. Not with a rebuke. Not with thunder. With an explanation, a short one, and a gentle one.

"Go," he says, "for this man is my chosen instrument to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the people of Israel. I myself will show him how much he must suffer for my name."

That is the whole of it. Go, because I have chosen him. Go, because I have a plan for him. And it will not be an easy plan, and he will pay for it, and you may tell him so.

Notice that the Lord does not say, he has changed. He does not say, he is sorry. He does not say anything about Saul's heart at all. He says only that he is chosen, and that he will suffer. And perhaps that is the answer that Ananias needs, because it tells him that the Lord is not asking him to forget what the man has done. He is only asking him to go.

Luke does not describe what Ananias feels. We would guess that he sat there for a little while. A long enough while for the lamp to burn down a finger's width. It is the kind of silence you get when somebody has finally been told the thing they most hoped they would not be told.

And then he gets up.

Let us follow him through the city.

It is early the next morning, or perhaps later that same night. Acts does not say, and we have chosen the morning, because it seems the kind of errand a man would want to do in daylight. The sky over Damascus is the pale colour of the inside of a shell. The shopkeepers are putting out their awnings. A boy is leading a donkey with a pair of baskets across the end of a lane, and Ananias steps aside for him and says good morning, as he has said it to that boy and that donkey every day for years.

He walks along the Street Called Straight. It is not long before he sees the house. It is a good house, solid, with a heavy door and a lintel over it, the kind of place a person of some standing would own. There is a man in the doorway, sweeping. We think that this is Judas, though Acts does not say so. We think that Judas has been sweeping that same bit of step for a very long time, because he does not quite know what else to do.

And now Ananias has a choice to make. He can still turn back. He could say that he has the wrong house. He could walk on to the end of the street, and sit by the gate for an hour, and then go home, and tell himself that he had tried. No one would know.

Instead he does the plain, courteous thing, the thing the Lord asked him to do. He says to the man at the door, "Good morning. I am looking for a guest of yours. A man of Tarsus, called Saul."

And Judas looks at him for a long moment, and then he steps aside. We cannot say why. Perhaps he is so relieved that somebody has come that he does not stop to ask who. Perhaps there is something in the face of Ananias, an ordinary, tired, honest kind of face, that tells him there is nothing to be afraid of.

The room is small and dim. The shutters are drawn. There is a mat on the floor, and a bench by the wall, and on the bench, with his hands in his lap, sits a man who looks, to Ananias, much smaller than he had expected.

He is not tall. He is not especially fierce to look at. He has not shaved, or eaten. His eyes are open, and they are cloudy, as if a thin pale skin had been drawn across them. He has turned his head toward the door, the way a blind person does, at the sound of a step. And he is not saying anything.

Think of it from Ananias's side. Here, for days, in the minds of every believer in Damascus, has stood a monster, a man with a letter and a long reach. And here he is, and he is hungry, and he is blind, and he is waiting to be told what to do.

Ananias stands in the doorway. He has a speech prepared. He has been preparing it, in his head, all the way down the street. Something sober, something careful, something suitable to the moment.

He does not use it.

He walks across the room, and he puts his hands on Saul's shoulders, and he says the first words that the Lord gives him. He says, "Brother Saul."

Brother.

We should stop there for a moment, because it is the most surprising word in the story.

A day before, this man had been, to Ananias, the enemy. The one to be feared. The one whose name was whispered, and then not said. And now the first word that comes out of him is not "you," or "sir," or "Saul of Tarsus." It is "brother." It comes out as simply as a man passing the salt.

We do not know whether Ananias had decided to say it, or whether it simply came. Luke gives no hint. We only know that this is the first thing Saul hears from a follower of Jesus. Not a curse, or a cry for mercy. Not a bargain. A name, with a family word in front of it.

And then Ananias says the rest.

"Brother Saul, the Lord Jesus, who appeared to you on the road by which you came, has sent me so that you may regain your sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit."

That is all. He does not say, I forgive you, because it is not his to forgive. He does not say, I understand, because he cannot. He delivers the message exactly as he was given it, and he gets to the point, and he puts his hands on the man's head.

And Luke says, "Immediately something like scales fell from his eyes, and he regained his sight."

Something like scales. We do not need to say more than Luke does. Luke is a doctor, we are told, in another letter, and he is careful about words. He does not say that scales fell. He says it was like scales. The Greek word he uses means something that flakes, or peels. It is a small phrase, and it is exactly the sort of thing a man says who has actually watched it happen and does not want to claim more than he saw.

And Saul looks up.

What does he see? Acts does not say. But we may be allowed to guess, and our guess is that he sees a plain-looking man, a little out of breath, with his hands still raised, in a small room that is suddenly very bright. He sees a rough wall, and a bench, and a shaft of daylight coming through a gap in the shutter, full of dust. And he sees a face looking at him without any fear. He has not seen a face like that in a long time, and certainly not on one of the people he has been hunting.

And what does Ananias see? He sees a man, now, who is just a man. Who has been crying, perhaps, or is about to. Who is looking at him, as if he does not know what to do with the fact that someone has come.

The next part is quiet, and it is the part we like best, because it is so ordinary.

Luke says, "He got up and was baptised."

That is all he says. He got up, and he was baptised. He does not describe where, or with what, or who stood by. We do not know if there was a river, or a pool, or a jar in a courtyard. Years later, when Paul tells the story himself, he remembers that Ananias said to him, "Why do you delay? Get up and be baptised, and wash away your sins, calling on his name." And then Luke goes on, in the same breath.

"And after taking some food, he regained his strength."

We find that wonderful. Here is a man who has had the light of heaven fall on him, who has been struck blind, who has been called by name by the Lord himself, and whose entire life has just turned upside down. And the very next thing in the account, almost as if it were the most natural thing in the world, is that he is given something to eat.

Think of it. He has not eaten for three days. And so, after the great, enormous thing, comes the small, necessary one. Somebody goes into the kitchen. Perhaps Judas, perhaps a servant. Somebody brings bread and some olives and a bowl of something warm. And the man who a week ago had a warrant in his bag sits down and eats, like a man who is going to be all right.

We do not know what the meal was. Luke does not say. Our guess, because it is Damascus, is that there were figs, or apricots, which Damascus is famous for, and flat bread, and perhaps some yogurt. We don't claim to know. But we are sure of this much, because Luke tells us: he ate, and he was strengthened.

And what happens to Ananias?

This is the odd and the lovely thing. Nothing, as far as we are told. After this scene, he disappears from the book. We never hear of him again in Acts. In the other tellings, in the later speeches, he is mentioned in a line or two, as a devout man, well spoken of. And that is the end of his appearances.

He has done the one thing the Lord asked, and he has done it well, and then he goes home. He goes back to the quiet street, and the quiet life, and whatever it was he did before. Perhaps he never saw Saul again. Perhaps he did, on a few days, in the weeks that followed. Luke says that Saul remained for several days with the disciples at Damascus, so there is every chance that the two of them sat in the same room more than once. But we do not know.

What we do know is that without a man called Ananias, there is a gap in the story. The Lord could have healed Saul himself, from heaven, in the dark. He did not need a messenger. He chose to send one. He chose to send a nervous, polite, careful man who had said "are you sure?" and then had gone anyway. And that choice, we think, says something kind about how the Lord prefers to work. It is usually through someone's front door.

There is a thing that goes round in the old commentaries, that the greatest thing Ananias did was not the laying on of hands. It was the walk down the street. Anyone can lay on hands once they are in the room. But getting to the room, past one's own fear, and one's own good sense, and one's own very understandable wish to be somewhere else, is the part that costs.

We should tell you what happened next, though only briefly, because it belongs to other nights.

Luke says that Saul spent some days with the disciples in Damascus. And almost at once, he began going into the synagogues, the very synagogues that he had come to raid, and proclaiming that Jesus is the Son of God. You can imagine the faces. People who had been told to expect a prosecutor found themselves listening to a preacher. They said to each other, in Luke's words, "Is this not the man who made havoc in Jerusalem of those who called on this name? And has he not come here for the purpose of taking them bound before the chief priests?"

It is a very reasonable question. There is something almost comic about it. If you can picture a policeman arriving in a town with a list of names and then spending his first week handing out leaflets to the people on the list, you will have an idea of the mood.

Paul himself, in a letter, says that after this he went away to Arabia, which in those days meant the desert country to the east and south of Damascus, and then came back to the city. And in time, Luke tells us, some of the people in Damascus decided that this new preacher was too much trouble, and they began to plot against him. They watched the city gates, day and night. And one night, his friends took him, and let him down over the wall in a basket. That is a story for another evening.

But for tonight, we will go back to the room on Straight Street, and the morning, and the man called Ananias, and the thing he said.

Let us slow down now.

It is late. The light is going out of the room on Straight Street, and the shutter is open to the evening air. In the courtyard, somebody is drawing water. The sound of a bucket on a rope, the little knock of it against the stones, goes on and on. Out in the main street there is the clop of a donkey, and the call of a seller, and a child being told to come in.

And in the room, two men are sitting side by side on a bench.

One of them is a man we know, or think we know, who will become the greatest traveller of his age, who will cross the sea a dozen times and be shipwrecked and beaten and run out of town, and write letters that we are still reading two thousand years later. But tonight he is only a tired man with his hands around a cup.

The other is a man who will never be famous. Whose name will turn up in a handful of verses. Who is going to leave in a few minutes, and say goodnight, and walk home along the long straight street in the dusk.

They are not saying much. There is not much to say. It has been, for both of them, a very long day.

And the man called Ananias gets up, and puts his hand for a moment on Saul's shoulder, and says something quiet. Perhaps it is only "Goodnight." Perhaps it is "I will come tomorrow." We cannot know. Luke does not tell us. But the door is open, and the evening air is coming in, and somewhere in the street a lamp is being lit.

Here is a thought to take to bed with you.

Most of us will never be asked to do anything large. We will not be struck down on a road, or called by name in a blaze of light. But a good many of us, at some time or another, will be asked to do one small thing that frightens us. To go and knock at a door. To make a phone call. To say the first word to a person we have fallen out with. To sit down next to somebody that everyone else has avoided.

And if that is you, then you might remember Ananias. He did not feel brave. He said "are you sure?" And the Lord, who is not at all offended by that question, gave him an address, and a name, and one word to start with. And the word was brother.

There is a lot of the Gospel in that one word.

And now you can let it go. You can let the day go. The street is quiet, and the shutters are closing, and the night over Damascus is full of stars.

Here are the notes for the curious.

The story of Ananias and Saul is told in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter nine, verses one to nineteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Everything that Ananias, Saul and the Lord say and do in the story is kept as Acts gives it, including the vision, the address on Straight Street, the objection of Ananias, the words of the Lord about a chosen instrument, the greeting of Brother Saul, the falling of something like scales, the baptism and the meal. The detail that Ananias was a devout man by the standards of the law and well spoken of by the Jews of Damascus comes from Paul's own retelling in chapter twenty-two. Paul tells of the same meeting on the road a third time in chapter twenty-six, and he says in his Letter to the Galatians that he once persecuted the church and then was called. Acts does not say that Saul was on a horse, and we have not put him on one.

We invented the evening and the morning, the smell of onions, the boy with the donkey, the look of the house, the sweeping man at the door, the meal of figs and bread, the thoughts of Ananias, and the quiet scene at the end. We named Judas as the one at the door, and we said that this was a guess. We do not know the age, the trade or the family of Ananias. The distance from Jerusalem is an estimate, and the exact route is not known. Acts does not say when in the day Ananias went, and we chose the morning. The Street Called Straight is a real and ancient street in Damascus, though the line of the modern street is not exactly that of the old one. A house and a chapel are shown in Damascus today as the house of Ananias, and that is a later tradition, which we do not rely on. Later writers say a good deal about what became of Ananias, and we have named that and left it out, because we cannot stand behind it. The Orthodox Church counts him among the Seventy, a tradition we have noted and not argued.

That's the story of Ananias and the Street Called Straight for tonight. Ananias is remembered in both the Catholic and Orthodox calendars, though not on the same day, and calendars differ, so you will want to check your own. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

And here is a blessing. May the Lord who spoke to Ananias in the evening speak to you in yours. May you be given the courage to knock at the hard door, and the grace to say the first word kindly. May whatever you fear tonight be smaller in the morning. May there be bread on the table and a friend in the room. And may you sleep like a man who has done what he was asked, and is going home along a quiet street.

Goodnight, and God bless.

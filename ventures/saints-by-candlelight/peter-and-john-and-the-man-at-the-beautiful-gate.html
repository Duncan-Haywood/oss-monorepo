Peter and John and the Man at the Beautiful Gate

At three in the afternoon, two fishermen going up to the Temple to pray stop for a man who has been carried to the same gate every day of his life. He asks for coins. He is given something else. A story of an outstretched hand, a night in a cell, and two men who cannot stop talking about what they have seen, from Acts chapters three and four.

What's true in this story

Who

Tradition

Scripture and history
Acts chapters three and four are Scripture; the Temple in Jerusalem, the afternoon hour of prayer, beggars at its gates, the Sadducean priesthood, Annas and Caiaphas, and the arrest and release of Peter and John are all part of the real world of the book

Full script

Picture a man sitting on the ground beside a gate, with a little cloth spread out in front of him, and nothing whatever on the cloth.

It is early in the morning, and he has already been there a while. The stones under him are cool. The sun is just beginning to come over the wall. Somebody has set him down, and arranged his legs so that they do not look uncomfortable, and put the cloth where the people can see it, and gone off to do whatever it is that people do on a morning. And he has settled himself in, the way you settle into a chair you have sat in ten thousand times, and he is looking at the feet that go by.

That is what he sees, mostly. Feet. Sandals, and bare feet, and the shoes of rich men, and a donkey's feet, now and then, which he rather likes. He has never been able to stand up and look anyone in the face at their own height. He has got very good at feet. And he is about to be given a great surprise by two particular pairs of them.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapters three and four, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is about Saint Peter and Saint John, and a man at a gate. A few of the small things around the edges, the man's morning, the friends who carry him, the night in the cell and the walk home, are our own invention, told in the spirit of Acts, and the notes at the end say which. What is said at the gate, what is said to the crowd, and what is said before the council, we have kept as close to Luke as we can. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let me tell you first where we are, and when.

We are in Jerusalem, a few weeks after the first Pentecost. The friends of Jesus, the ones who followed him and saw him alive after the cross, have not scattered. They are still here, in the city where everything happened. They meet in houses. They share their bread and what they own. They pray together, and they listen to the apostles, and a good many new people have joined them. And, like every pious Jewish family in Jerusalem, they have not stopped going to the Temple.

That is worth stopping on for a moment. Peter and John were Jews. Jesus was a Jew. His mother, his cousins, his friends, all of them kept the Sabbath and the feasts and the hours of prayer, and they did not stop doing so when they came to believe that Jesus was risen. The Temple was the house of God, and they went up to it to pray, as they always had. So when Luke tells us that Peter and John were going up to the Temple at the hour of prayer, he is not telling us something remarkable. He is telling us that it is an ordinary afternoon.

The hour in question is the ninth hour, which in the way they counted the day was about three o'clock in the afternoon. That was the time of the afternoon sacrifice, and a great many people came to pray with it. So the courts were busy, and the gates were busy, and the steps going up to the gates were busy, like a station at five o'clock.

And Peter and John are walking up together.

Now, a word about the two of them, because they are an odd pair, and you will like them.

Peter we know. He is a fisherman from the shore of the lake in Galilee, a big, warm, quick man, who speaks before he thinks, and who loves the Lord with all of his heart and has also, as you may remember, once let him down very badly, and been forgiven. He is a man who has learned what it is to be forgiven, and it has made him gentler than he used to be.

John is his friend, and a fisherman too. The two of them worked the same boats, long ago, with John's brother and with their fathers, and they have known each other since they were boys. John is quieter than Peter. He is the sort of man who notices things, and remembers them, and thinks about them for years. In this story, in fact, he speaks only once, right at the end, and then together with Peter. He walks beside Peter, and he looks, and when Peter looks at the man, Luke says, John looks too. That is mostly all. But I think you will find that it is quite enough.

They have no money to speak of. I want you to remember that. They have left their boats and nets, and what they have they share with the others, and Peter in particular is not a man with a full purse. He will say so, in a minute, quite cheerfully.

Now let us go back to the man at the gate.

Luke tells us three things about him, and I will tell you them in Luke's order.

First, that he was lame from his mother's womb. He was born that way. His legs and his feet had never held him, not for one step, not in all his life.

Second, that he was carried. Every day, Luke says, people carried him and put him at the gate of the Temple which is called Beautiful, to ask alms of those who were going in. Every day. Somebody picked him up in the morning, and somebody set him down, and somebody, at the end of the day, came and carried him home. He did not choose the gate. He did not choose anything. It was a long way, I think, from being a free man.

And third, in a place we will come to, when the whole thing is over, Luke tells us how old he was. And that is a lovely thing, and I will save it.

Now, it is a good thing to ask for alms at the gate of the Temple. It was a respectable way to ask. The law of Moses had told the people of Israel again and again to care for the poor, and to give to the one in need, and it was thought a holy thing to give a coin to a man at a holy gate, on your way in to pray. So the man's friends, or his family, or whoever they were, had put him in the best place they could think of. The gate called Beautiful. Where all the good people passed by, thinking good thoughts.

I will tell you honestly that we do not know exactly which gate this was. The Temple had a number of gates, and the old writers describe them in several ways, and scholars have been arguing for a very long time about which one Luke means. Some say the great bronze gate on the east side. Some say another. I would rather tell you that than guess. All we are told is that it was called Beautiful, and that the man was there. And for tonight, I think that is all we need.

Let us imagine, for a little, what his morning was like. This part is our own, and not in Acts.

A boy comes for him at sunrise. Not a boy, perhaps, a young man. It does not matter. Let us say it is a cousin, who has done it so often that he does it half asleep. He gets his arms under the man's shoulders, and his knees, and lifts him like a rolled carpet. And they set off. The cousin grumbles a bit about the stairs. The man says that he is a very heavy weight to be carrying so early, and the cousin says the weight is not the trouble, the trouble is the conversation. And they both laugh, because they have said it before, and that is how they are with each other.

I think it is important to say this. People who are in need are not always sad. This man had a cousin he joked with. He knew the names of half the people who passed. He knew who always gave a coin, and who never did, and who would say something kind and give nothing, which was, he thought, the worst of all. He had a whole life at that gate. It was a small life. But it was a life.

And every day, when the afternoon came, and the great crowds went up to pray, he held out his hand.

So here come Peter and John, at three in the afternoon, up the steps, in a crowd of people.

And the man at the gate sees them coming. Of course he does. He sees everyone coming. He sees two pairs of sandals, rather worn ones, and he looks up to see the faces, and he says what he has said ten thousand times.

He asks them for something.

Luke says nothing about what he said. Only that he asked. I think it was probably very short. A word or two. "A coin, for God's sake." Or whatever the phrase was in his language, that went along with it, a phrase worn smooth with use, like a stair.

And what happens next is that Peter stops.

Think how unusual that is. A hundred people had gone past him already that day, in a hurry, with their eyes on the gate and their minds on their prayers. And here are two men who have stopped. Luke says that Peter fixed his eyes on him, with John, and said, "Look at us."

Look at us.

I find that a very moving thing to say. Not "here is a coin." Not "God bless you, friend," and a quick step on. But "look at us." A man who has spent his whole life looking at feet is being asked to lift his face and look at someone's eyes, because someone wants to look back at his.

And he does. Luke says he fixed his attention on them, expecting to receive something from them.

Of course he did. He was expecting a coin. He had every reason to.

And now Peter says the thing that this story is remembered for. I will say it as plainly as Luke does.

"Silver and gold I have none," says Peter. "But what I do have, I give to you. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, rise up and walk."

I want you to notice what Peter does not say. He does not say, "I am sorry, I have nothing." He does not say it as an apology, or a regret. He says it as a plain fact, quite cheerfully, and then he says, "but what I have." Peter is a man who knows that he has something. It is not his, and he did not make it. But he has been given it, and it is a great deal more than a purse of coins, and he is not ashamed to hand it over.

And what he has is a name. The name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth. Not a charm, and not a spell. A name, spoken in prayer, of a person who is alive. Peter does not say, "I heal you." He says, "In the name of Jesus." Whatever is about to happen, it is not going to be Peter's doing. He is being very careful about that, you will notice, all the way through the story.

And then he does something very simple. Luke says he took him by the right hand and raised him up.

He took him by the hand.

Not by a rope, or a rod. By the hand. A big, rough fisherman's hand, and a thin hand, held out for a coin for years and years. And Peter pulled.

Now. I am going to read you what Luke says next, and I will read it slowly, because it is one of the most cheerful sentences in the whole of the New Testament.

"And immediately his feet and ankles were made strong. And leaping up, he stood and began to walk, and he entered the Temple with them, walking and leaping and praising God."

Immediately. His feet and ankles. I have always noticed that Luke is exact about that. Feet and ankles. It is the writing of a man who wants you to know it was real, and how it was real, and what part of the body was involved. Luke was a physician, the old tradition says, and some think that you can hear it here, in the care he takes about the feet and the ankles. It is a small thing, but it is a nice thing to notice.

And then the verbs. He leaped up. He stood. He began to walk. He went in with them. Walking, and leaping, and praising God. Some readers have heard behind these words an old promise from the prophet Isaiah, about a day when the lame would leap like a deer. That is a reading, and not something Luke says out loud, and I offer it only as a thing that many people have noticed. But I do think it is easy to understand why.

Think of it. A man who has never put one foot in front of another. He does not know how to walk. He has no habit of it, no balance, no knowledge of where his feet are. And he leaps. He cannot stop. He goes into the Temple at a run, I imagine, and then stops, and looks down at his feet, and does it again.

And all the people see him.

Luke tells us that all the people saw him walking and praising God. And they knew him. They knew him, Luke says, as the one who used to sit for alms at the Beautiful Gate of the Temple. And they were filled with wonder and amazement at what had happened to him.

I think it is lovely that they knew him. They had passed him every day. They had walked past him for years, perhaps, in a hurry, with their thoughts on other things. And now here he is, going past them, on his own two feet, and they know exactly who he is. That is how you know it is true. Everyone had seen him. Nobody could say it was someone else.

And the man himself. What does he do? Luke says he held on to Peter and John. He clung to them. He would not let go. Of course he would not. They were the only two people in the whole of Jerusalem who had looked at him, and he did not want to let them out of his sight.

And the whole crowd, Luke says, ran together to them in the portico called Solomon's, utterly astonished.

Solomon's Portico is a long covered walk, with pillars, on the east side of the Temple court. It is a good place to gather, because it is shady, and because there is a lot of room. And there the whole crowd comes, running, and stands around them, and stares. And Peter, who has never in his life been able to resist a crowd, begins to speak.

I will not give you the whole of Peter's speech, because it is long, and it is a speech, and you are meant to be falling asleep. But I will give you the outline, in the order he gives it, because it is simple and it is very Peter.

First he says: why are you looking at us as though we had done this by our own power, or our own holiness? We did not. It is the God of Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, the God of our fathers, who has glorified his servant Jesus.

Notice that again. He turns it away from himself at once. Not us. Him.

Then he says something hard. He says that this Jesus is the one you handed over, and denied before Pilate, when Pilate had decided to let him go. You asked for a murderer to be released to you, and you killed the author of life. But God raised him from the dead, and we are witnesses of it.

Now, that is a hard thing to hear, and I do not want to slide past it. In later centuries, some people took sentences like these and used them to hate their Jewish neighbours, and that was a grievous wrong. Peter is a Jew, speaking to Jews, in the Temple of his own people, about events that happened in his own city a few weeks before. He is not speaking about a race or about all Jews for all time. He is speaking to the very people who were in Jerusalem that spring, and some of them had been in the crowd. And then, almost at once, he softens it, and says something very tender. He says, "And now, brothers, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did your rulers."

I know that you acted in ignorance.

He does not say, you are damned. He says, I know you did not understand. And then he says what he has been getting to all along: repent, and turn again, so that your sins may be wiped out, and times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord.

Times of refreshing. I love that phrase. It is what the Church has always hoped to be, for the people who come to it, tired. A time of refreshing.

And that is the heart of the speech. The man is walking, because Jesus is alive, and it is not too late for any of you to turn round.

Now, while Peter is still speaking, something happens.

The priests come. And with them the captain of the Temple, who was the officer in charge of keeping order in the courts, and some of the Sadducees. Luke says they were greatly annoyed. They were annoyed because the apostles were teaching the people, and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection of the dead.

This needs a word of explanation. The Sadducees were one of the groups in Jerusalem in those days, and among the things they did not believe in was the resurrection of the dead. They held that it was not taught in the first five books of Moses, and so they would not accept it. And here were two Galileans, in the Temple, on a busy afternoon, with a crowd round them, saying that a man had risen from the dead, and the proof of it was standing beside them, with perfectly good feet.

It was a great annoyance. It was also, if you were responsible for the peace of the Temple, a problem. A crowd is a crowd. And the authorities in Jerusalem, as you can imagine, had not much liked what had happened to the last Galilean teacher who gathered crowds.

So they arrest Peter and John. And because it is already evening, they put them in custody until the next day.

And here is a sentence I should like to put alongside the arrest. Luke tells us, in the same breath, that many of those who had heard the word believed, and that the number of the men came to about five thousand.

Five thousand. In the same afternoon that two of the apostles were put in prison. That is how Luke writes. In the same sentence, the door shuts on two men, and opens for five thousand. I think he does that on purpose.

So here is Peter, and here is John, for a night in a cell. That is the part I invented.

I do not know what they said to each other that night. Luke does not tell us. But I think I can guess what sort of night it was.

It was not a frightening one, I think. Not for John, at any rate. John was not a man who was easily frightened, or he would not have been standing at the cross. I imagine him sitting with his back against the wall, with his knees drawn up, quite calm, watching the little square of sky go dark through the bars. And Peter beside him, who is a man who cannot stay still, pacing a little, and sitting, and getting up again.

"It was a good afternoon," says Peter, at last.

"It was," says John.

"I did not think we would be here by the evening."

"No."

A long pause. The sound of someone snoring in the next cell, a gentle, steady sound, like a boat at its moorings.

"Did you see his face?" says Peter. "When he stood up?"

"I saw it," says John.

And that is all they say. They do not need to say any more. They sit there in the dark with the thing they have seen, and they are, I think, very nearly happy. Because they have seen a man stand up who had never stood. And a night in a cell is a small price for that. They have been in worse. They have, either of them, been much more frightened, on a lake, in a boat, in a storm. And the Lord has come to them there.

And somewhere across the city, in a house with a lamp in it, a man is lying on a bed, quite unable to sleep, because he has too much to think about, and his feet keep wanting to get up and go somewhere. He has, I think, found his way home on his own. His cousin was very surprised.

The next morning, they bring Peter and John before the council.

This is the same body that had tried Jesus, and Luke names the leading people in it. There is Annas, the high priest, or the one who is still called so, and Caiaphas, the one who had been high priest at the time of Jesus's trial, and John and Alexander, and the rest of the high priest's family. They sit in a half circle. And they stand the two fishermen in the middle of them, and they ask them a question.

"By what power or by what name did you do this?"

Now think of that, because Peter must have. It is not so many weeks since he stood in the courtyard of the high priest, on a cold night, by a fire, and a servant girl said to him, "You also were with him," and he said he did not know the man. He denied it three times. That was in this same city, within sight of the same house.

And Luke says, very quietly, "Then Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, said to them."

Filled with the Holy Spirit. It is the answer to the whole thing. This is not the same Peter. It is the same man, with the same voice, and the same big hands. But the fear is gone out of him. He is not trying to be brave. He is just telling the truth, because it has been given to him to do it.

And what he says is this, and it is quite short.

"Rulers of the people and elders, if we are being examined today concerning a good deed done to a crippled man, by what means this man has been healed, let it be known to all of you, and to all the people of Israel, that by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead, by him this man is standing before you well."

He is standing before you well. And I should like you to see this next part, because Luke wants us to. The man is there. They have brought him in, to be questioned, or he has come of his own accord, and Luke tells us that he is standing beside them. In the council chamber. On his own feet. And Luke says, with a little dry humour that I like very much, that "seeing the man who had been healed standing beside them, they had nothing to say in opposition."

They had nothing to say.

There is a whole tradition of arguing in this room, and a great many learned men, and nobody can think of an answer. Because there, between two Galilean fishermen, is a man with strong ankles, and he has been a beggar at the gate for as long as any of them can remember.

Then Peter says one more thing, and it is the one that people have remembered the longest. He quotes the psalm.

"This Jesus is the stone that was rejected by you, the builders, which has become the cornerstone."

That is from Psalm one hundred and eighteen, one of the great psalms of the Temple, which these very men have sung at the feasts every year of their lives. The builders threw the stone away, and it became the stone that holds the corner. Peter takes it, and says, here is the verse you know by heart, and it is about this.

And he finishes with the words: "And there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved."

No other name. And that was the name he had said at the gate, on the afternoon before. The name that sent a man away on his own feet.

I will say this about that last sentence. It is a strong one, and the Church has always heard it as strong. But I notice that Peter does not say it to frighten anyone, or to shut anyone out. He says it in answer to a question, "by what name?" And he says it because it is the truth he has been given and cannot keep to himself. That is the spirit in which I hope it is always said.

And now we come to a sentence I love.

Luke says that when the council saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were uneducated, ordinary men, they were amazed. And they recognised that they had been with Jesus.

Uneducated, ordinary men. The Greek is a plain, blunt phrase. It means men with no schooling in the law, men who had not studied under any of the great teachers, and who were what we might call laymen. Fishermen, in other words. And the council cannot understand how they speak so well.

They can find only one explanation. They recognised that they had been with Jesus.

I think that is the best thing anyone says about Peter and John in the whole of the Acts. They had no learning, and no money, and no position. But there was something about them that reminded the council of a man they had already met, and perhaps hoped never to meet again. They had been with Jesus. You could tell.

I have thought about that a good deal. It is what I should like to be said of me, if anything is to be said. Not that I was clever, or strong, or good at my work, though I would take any of those. But that someone, somewhere, looked at me for a moment and thought, he has been with Jesus.

And the council did what councils do. They sent the two men out of the room, and they talked it over among themselves.

And what they said is quite honest. I like them for it, a little. They said, "What shall we do with these men? For that a notable sign has been done through them is plain to all the inhabitants of Jerusalem, and we cannot deny it."

We cannot deny it.

They did not say it was false. They could see it was not. The man was there, and everyone in Jerusalem knew him. All they could do was try to limit the damage. So they called Peter and John back in, and they ordered them not to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus.

And here is Peter's answer, and John's. It is the one time in the story that Luke gives John a voice, and he gives it to him together with Peter, in one breath.

"Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge. For we cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard."

We cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard.

It is such a simple reason. It is not defiance, quite. It is not even courage, perhaps. It is just that they cannot help it. If you had seen a man like that stand up, you would not be able to keep quiet, either. It would burst out of you at the dinner table. It would come out in the street. You would be telling the baker.

The council threatened them a little more, and let them go. Luke says they could find no way to punish them, because of the people, for all were praising God for what had happened. And he adds, at the very end, the thing I promised to tell you.

The man on whom this sign of healing had been performed, he says, was more than forty years old.

More than forty. Forty years, at that gate, or at one like it, or in a house with his legs folded under him. And one afternoon, in about the time it takes to say a prayer, it was all over. I find I cannot hear that number without feeling something move in my chest. It is not a young man's rescue. It is a rescue that took a long time to come, and came all at once.

I do not know whether he ever thought about that, in the years after. I do not know whether he had a family, or a trade, or what he did with all those new years. Luke does not say. Later stories have given him a name, and a life, and I have kept them out of this one, because they are later, and because I think the better thing is to leave him as Luke does. Unnamed. A man at a gate.

But I like to think that he learned, in the end, to walk without looking at his feet. That one day, in the middle of a crowd, he looked up. And saw faces.

When Peter and John were released, Luke says, they went to their friends, and reported what the chief priests and the elders had said. And when the friends heard it, they lifted their voices together to God in prayer. They prayed a psalm. And when they had prayed, the place in which they were gathered was shaken, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit, and spoke the word of God with boldness.

That is how this part of the story ends. Not with a trial, and not with a threat. With a house full of people, praying, and a floor that trembles a little, and nobody afraid.

Now. Before we go, I should like to say a word about the two saints themselves, and about the gate.

Peter and John are honoured in both the Catholic Church and the Orthodox Church. Peter is kept, along with Paul, on the twenty-ninth of June, by both churches, on their own calendars. John the Apostle is kept in the West on the twenty-seventh of December, and in the East the Church keeps John the Theologian in the spring and again in the autumn. They are among the best loved of all the saints, and you will meet them again in other stories.

And the gate? The Temple that Peter and John went up to was destroyed by the Romans in the year seventy, not many years after this. There is no Beautiful Gate there now. There is a wall, and a great platform where the Temple stood, and the sound of pilgrims. But the story has outlasted the gate. Wherever there is someone sitting at the edge of things, asking for a little help, and someone going past in a hurry, Peter's words are still there to be heard. Look at us. I have no silver and gold. But what I have, I give to you.

And I think that is a good prayer for tonight, for all of us. That we may have our eyes open, and our hands free, and that when we find that we have nothing in our purse, we may find that we have something else.

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter three, verses one to twenty-six, and chapter four, verses one to thirty-one. It is Scripture, and in both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches it is read in the liturgy, especially in the weeks after Easter.

What we have kept from the text: the hour of prayer at three in the afternoon; the man lame from his mother's womb, carried each day to the gate called Beautiful; his asking for alms; Peter's "Look at us"; the words "Silver and gold I have none"; the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth; the taking of the right hand; the feet and ankles made strong; the walking, leaping and praising God; the crowd's recognition of him; Solomon's Portico; Peter's turning of the wonder away from himself; the call to repent, and the words "I know that you acted in ignorance"; the arrival of the priests, the captain of the Temple and the Sadducees; the arrest and the night in custody; the five thousand who believed; the council the next day, with Annas and Caiaphas; the question "By what power or by what name"; Peter filled with the Holy Spirit; the man standing beside them; the quotation of the psalm; the words "no other name"; the council's amazement that these were uneducated, ordinary men who had been with Jesus; their admission that they could not deny the sign; the order to be silent; the answer "we cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard"; the release; the prayer of the friends; and the man's age, more than forty.

What we flagged as not in the text, or uncertain: which gate was called Beautiful is not known, and we said so. The hearing of Isaiah's leaping lame behind Luke's words is a reading some people share, and not something Luke states. The tradition that Luke was a physician comes from Paul's letter to the Colossians and from early writers, and the idea that his care for the feet and ankles shows it is a suggestion only. The later names and lives given to the healed man are tradition, and we left them out. And in the words Peter speaks to the crowd about those who handed Jesus over, we have said plainly that they are spoken by a Jew to Jews about events of that spring, and not about a people for all time, because they have too often been misused.

What we invented: the man's morning, his cousin who carries him, their joking on the stairs, the feet he watches go by, the night in the cell and the little talk of Peter and John in it, and the walk home. None of them is in Acts. None changes what happens, or what it means. And we have told the arrest and the council as gently as Luke does, because a bedtime story should not frighten anyone.

For further reading, the third and fourth chapters of the Acts of the Apostles are not long, and they are very much worth reading slowly, in a quiet room, with a chair beside you that you have no need to be carried to.

That's the story of Saint Peter and Saint John and the man at the Beautiful Gate for tonight. The Catholic and Orthodox Churches keep Saint Peter, with Saint Paul, on the twenty-ninth of June.

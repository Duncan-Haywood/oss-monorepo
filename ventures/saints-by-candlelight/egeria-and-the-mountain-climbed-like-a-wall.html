Saints by Candlelight · Episode 127

Egeria and the Mountain Climbed Like a Wall

A pilgrim from the far west of the Roman world reaches the mountain of God in the desert of Sinai, climbs it straight up from the dark on a Sunday morning, hears the Book of Exodus read at the top, and comes down in the evening to a garden where the bush of Moses is still growing. Told from the opening chapters of Egeria's own travel letter, written in the early three hundred and eighties.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Egeria the pilgrim, the monks and priests of Sinai and the old priest of the church on the summit, and remembered there Moses, Aaron and the seventy elders, and the prophet Elijah
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; Moses and Elijah are honoured as saints and prophets in both; Egeria is called blessed by a seventh-century monk but has never been canonised in either tradition, so we claim no feast for her
Scripture and history
the arrival through the great valley, the prayer at the first sight of the mountain, the monastery at the foot on Saturday evening, the climb on Sunday straight up and down the outer peaks, the small church on the summit and its old priest, the reading, the offering and the gifts of fruit, the view, the cave of Moses, Horeb and the cave of Elijah, the rock of Aaron and the elders, the bush still alive in a garden with water at the tenth hour, and the walk out past the holy places of the valley are all in Egeria's Travels, chapters one to five; Justinian's walled monastery is in Procopius; the church and the bush still at the monastery of Saint Catherine are there today
Legend
that this mountain is the biblical Sinai, and that this bush is the very bush, are traditions already old when Egeria came and are told as traditions; the stories of the places come from Exodus, Numbers and the First Book of Kings, told straight
What we invented
the young monk we call Theodore, the mule, every word anyone says, the weather, Egeria's tired legs and her thoughts are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; what Egeria says happened is kept, and her own words are given in our plain rendering
Feast day
Moses the prophet on the fourth of September and the prophet Elijah on the twentieth of July, in the Orthodox calendar and in the Roman Martyrology; no feast for Egeria

Full script

It is still dark, and a woman in a travelling cloak is standing at the bottom of a mountain, looking up.

At least, she thinks she is looking up. It is hard to tell. Above her there is only a great black shape against a slightly less black sky, and a few stars, and the cold. The desert is very cold before dawn. Nobody tells you that about deserts.

Beside her an old priest is tying his sandals. Around her a little crowd of monks are pulling their hoods up and murmuring their morning prayers. Somebody has a lamp. Somebody else has a basket.

The woman says, politely, because she is a very polite person, "Which way does the path go round?"

And the young monk next to her, who has been looking forward to this moment all night, says, "It doesn't go round. It goes up."

He points. Not along. Up. Straight up, into the dark, as if the path were a ladder leaned against a wall.

The woman looks at the mountain. The mountain does not look back. It has been there a very long time, and it is not impressed by anybody.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is a true one, from the travel letter of a pilgrim called Egeria, written in the early three hundred and eighties, which tells of the day she climbed the mountain of Moses, and the young monk who guides her, and the small conversations on the way, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

If you heard our episode about Egeria and her friend at the shrine of Saint Thecla, you have met tonight's pilgrim already. If you didn't, here is all you need.

About sixteen hundred and forty years ago, a Christian woman from the far west of the Roman world, from the north-west of Spain, perhaps, or the south of France, nobody is quite sure, set out to see the places in the Bible with her own eyes. She spent about three years in and around Jerusalem, and she travelled from there to Egypt, to the desert of Sinai, to the mountain where Moses looked out over the promised land, to the city of Edessa in what is now Turkey, and in the end to Constantinople. And all the way, she wrote letters home to a group of women she calls her sisters. "Ladies, my sisters," she says, "venerable ladies," and sometimes, which is very sweet, "your affection," as if her sisters were one big warm feeling waiting for news.

Those letters were lost for about eight hundred years. Then, in the year eighteen eighty-four, a scholar found a copy of part of them in a library in the Italian town of Arezzo. The beginning was missing. The end was missing. And this is where tonight's story starts, because the very first words we have of Egeria's letter are the end of a sentence. Whatever she wrote before them, the pages are gone. The first thing she says to us, across sixteen centuries, is, roughly, "were shown, according to the Scriptures."

Which is very Egeria. She has been shown something. It was according to the Scriptures. And she is about to tell you all about it.

So we join her in the middle of a walk, in the middle of a desert, in the middle of a sentence.

Now, where is she?

She is in the south of the Sinai peninsula. If you have a map of Egypt in your head, it's the triangle of land on the right-hand side, pointing down into the Red Sea, with Egypt to the west and the Arabian desert to the east. It is a land of mountains. Not gentle green mountains with cows on them. Red and grey and pink granite mountains, bare as bones, with deep dry valleys between them, and here and there a spring, and round the spring a few palm trees, and round the palm trees, in Egeria's day, a few monks.

Because by the time Egeria came, the desert of Sinai was full of hermits. Christians had been going out into the deserts of Egypt for about a hundred years, to live alone with God, to pray and work with their hands and eat very little. Some of them had come to Sinai, because Sinai was not just any desert. It was the desert. It was where God had spoken to Moses out of a burning bush. It was where the whole people of Israel had camped at the foot of a mountain wrapped in smoke and fire, and had heard the voice of God, and had been given the law. It was where Elijah the prophet had run to, much later, when he was tired and frightened and wanted to give up, and where God had come to him, not in the wind, and not in the earthquake, and not in the fire, but in a still, small voice.

So the monks came, and they built little cells of stone near the springs, and they planted small gardens, and they looked after the holy places. And they kept the old memory of which mountain was the mountain, and which rock was which rock, and where the bush had been.

I should say here, honestly, that nobody can prove which mountain in the desert was the mountain of Moses. Scholars have suggested several. The one the monks showed Egeria is the one Christians have called Mount Sinai since at least her day, and probably a little before, and it is the one pilgrims still climb. We will follow the monks, because Egeria did, and because they were the ones who lived there. But it is a tradition, a very old and much-loved one, and I'd rather tell you so than pretend.

And Egeria herself? She is not a young woman in a hurry. She is, most scholars think, a lady of some standing, perhaps a consecrated woman, a nun of the kind there were in those days, with enough money and enough friends to travel safely. She has an escort of soldiers on the dangerous stretches. She has local guides, holy men from the monasteries, whom she calls her holy guides. She has a mule, almost certainly, because nobody walks across the Sinai carrying their own luggage if they can help it. We are going to give her one. It doesn't have a name in the letter. Very little does. But it has opinions, and we will hear from it.

And she has her Bible. Not a little pocket Bible. Probably not a whole Bible at all; whole Bibles were huge, heavy, and very expensive. But she, or one of her party, carried the books they needed. Because Egeria had a custom, and she tells her sisters about it again and again. Whenever she came to a holy place, she wanted three things to happen there. A prayer. A reading of the passage from Scripture about that place. And a psalm, if there was a psalm that fitted. Prayer, reading, psalm. In that order, in every single place.

She is, in other words, the most organised pilgrim in history. Keep that in mind. It is going to matter.

So, back to the walk.

Egeria and her party are coming along a path between mountains. On both sides of them the rock goes up, close and high, and the path winds along the bottom. They have been walking like this for a long while. And then, all at once, the mountains on either side step back.

"Walking along," Egeria writes, "we came to a place where the mountains we had been travelling between opened out, and made a great valley, endless, very flat and extremely beautiful. And across the valley appeared the holy mountain of God, Sinai."

You can see it, can't you? A narrow, rocky path, and then suddenly space, and light, and a flat plain stretching away, and at the far end of it, a great red wall of mountains.

The holy guides stopped. And one of them said, the way a guide says something he has said many times and still means, "It is the custom here. Those who come say a prayer at this spot, when they first see the mountain of God."

So they did. Egeria knelt down on the gravel, and the guides knelt, and the soldiers, I expect, stood at a respectful distance and looked at their feet, the way soldiers do. And the mule took the chance to look for something to eat, and found nothing, because this is the Sinai, and nothing is the main crop.

And then Egeria got up and asked a question. She always asked questions. "How far is it to the mountain?"

"About four miles," said the guides. "Across the valley."

"And how big is the valley?"

They told her. About sixteen miles long, they said, and four miles across. Egeria wrote it down. She loved a measurement. She doesn't always get them right, nobody had a tape measure that long, but she always asks.

And the guides told her something else, something that made her look at the gravel under her feet in a new way.

"This place where the mountains open out," they said, "is joined to the place they call the Graves of Lust."

If you know the Book of Numbers, you know what that means. The people of Israel, wandering in the desert, got very tired of eating manna. Manna every morning. Manna every evening. Manna baked, manna boiled. And they complained, and wept in the doorways of their tents, and said, "Who will give us meat to eat? We remember the fish we ate in Egypt, and the cucumbers, and the melons, and the leeks, and the onions, and the garlic." Which I have always thought is a very human list. Nobody, when they are homesick, longs for something grand. They long for onions.

And God sent them quails, more quails than they could possibly eat, and the people gathered them for two whole days and a night. And there was a plague, and the people who had been greedy were buried there. And so the place was called, in Hebrew, Kibroth-hattaavah, which means the graves of craving. The Graves of Lust, in Egeria's old Latin Bible.

It was a sobering thing to hear, at the very first sight of the holy mountain. A sort of signpost: here is where people forgot to be grateful. Mind your step.

Egeria minded her step. And they set off across the valley.

It was late on a Saturday when they reached the foot of the mountain.

The valley had been long and flat and hot, and then the light went gold and then pink and then the cold came down, the way it does in the desert, all at once, like somebody opening a door. And there, tucked against the bottom of the mountains, were a few small stone buildings, and a church, and a garden, and some monks coming out to see who it was.

"The monks who lived there received us very kindly," Egeria writes, "and showed us every kindness. There is a church there too, and a priest."

That's all she says. She is a guest. She doesn't describe the supper, which is a shame, because I would like to know. But we can guess. Bread. Probably some lentils, or beans. Olives, if they had them, which they may not have. Dates, very likely. Water from the spring, which, after a day crossing that valley, would have tasted better than any wine.

Let us say that one of the monks who looked after her was young. The letter gives no names, so we will call him Theodore. Let us say he was about nineteen, and had come to the mountain from a village in Egypt two years before, and had climbed the mountain dozens of times, and was very, very proud of it.

"You'll go up in the morning?" said Theodore, sitting by the fire.

"If the holy fathers will take us," said Egeria.

"Oh, we'll take you. We all go. Every Sunday, as many as can, up to the church at the top. The priest up there will have the offering ready for us." He paused, with the expression of a young man about to say something he has been saving up. "Have you climbed many mountains, lady?"

Egeria thought about it. She had climbed several. She had grown up, very possibly, among the mountains of the far west, which are not small. She had been travelling for years. She had legs like a mule's.

"A few," she said.

"Not like this one," said Theodore, with great satisfaction.

"Is it very high?"

"It is very high. But that's not the thing. The thing is, you can't see it."

Egeria looked out of the doorway at the great dark shape of mountains behind the monastery, blotting out half the stars.

"I can see it now," she said.

"No," said Theodore. "You can see the others. The mountain of God is in the middle of them. They stand round it like, like," he searched for a picture, "like big brothers round a little sister. Except she's the tallest. You can't see her from here at all. Not until you're right at her feet."

Now, this part is true. Egeria writes it in her letter, and she says the brothers had told her before she came. The mountain looks from the valley like one mountain. But when you go into it, it is several, a whole crowd of peaks. And the peak where the glory of God came down is the one in the middle, and it is higher than all the rest, and yet you cannot see it until you are standing right underneath it. And then, she says, when you have climbed it, and come down again the other side, you can see it from there, which you couldn't do before.

I think Egeria liked that. A holy mountain that hides itself until you've made the effort. It's rather like a lot of holy things.

"Get some sleep, lady," said Theodore. "We go up in the dark."

And she slept, and the mule slept, and the stars went round over the mountain of God, the same stars Moses saw, and the same ones you can see tonight if you go outside.

Which brings us back to where we started. Sunday morning, very early, very dark, very cold, and a pilgrim at the bottom of a path that goes straight up.

Egeria is very clear about this part. I'll give it to you in her own words, as closely as I can turn them into English. "These mountains," she writes, "are climbed with enormous effort. You don't go up gently, round and round, in a spiral, like a snail's shell, as we say. You go straight up, the whole way, as if you were climbing a wall. And then you have to go straight down each of those mountains, until you come to the very foot of the middle one, which is the one properly called Sinai."

Up, and down. Up, and down. Up the first big brother. Down the other side of him. Up the next. Down again. And only then, at the bottom of the last dip, the foot of the little sister who is actually the tallest, and up her.

The mule, by the way, did not come. The mule took one look and stayed with the monks at the bottom, which is the first sensible decision anyone has made in this episode.

So they climbed. The old priest went first, because he knew every stone. Then the other priests, then the monks, those who were young enough or strong enough, then Egeria and her party, and Theodore somewhere in the middle, darting forward and back like a sheepdog.

The lamp swung. The breath came out white. Hands went down to the rock as often as feet. Every so often somebody in front would stop, and everybody would stop behind them, and stand, breathing, holding on, and looking at the next bit, which was steeper.

"Is this the middle one yet?" asked Egeria, at the top of the first.

"No," said Theodore cheerfully. "That's down there. Behind the next one."

They went down. They went up. The sky behind them went from black to grey to the colour of a pigeon's wing. Somewhere a bird started singing, one small brave voice on a whole mountain.

"Is this the middle one?"

"Not yet."

Egeria's legs, which had crossed a good part of the Roman world, began to have views. Her knees in particular had a great deal to say. She did not say any of it out loud. She was a lady and a pilgrim, and she was climbing to the place where God gave Moses the law. You don't complain on your way up to the law. You do, however, think about sitting down, very hard, at every step.

And she says something in her letter here that I love. She says they got up the mountain "at the bidding of Christ our God, and helped by the prayers of the holy men who went with us." Not by her own strength. She is quite sure of that. Her own strength, I suspect, ran out somewhere on the second big brother. The rest was prayer.

The sun came up while they were climbing. It came up over the mountains to the east and turned every bare red peak to fire, one after another, as if somebody were lighting candles. And the shadows ran down into the valleys and filled them with blue. And the old priest, without stopping, started to sing a psalm, and the monks sang with him, and their voices went out thin and clear into all that enormous morning.

"Now," said Theodore, at the bottom of the last dip. "Now look up."

And there she was. The mountain of God. A great red-grey peak going up above them, higher than anything around, which they had not been able to see all the way up, and which now filled the whole sky.

Egeria looked up at it, and her knees said, "Oh no," and her heart said, "Oh yes," and her heart, as usual, won.

They reached the summit at the fourth hour of the day.

The Romans counted the hours of the day from sunrise, so the fourth hour is about the middle of the morning. Say ten o'clock. They had been climbing for hours.

And on the top there was a church.

"Not great in size," Egeria writes. "Because the place itself, the summit of the mountain, is not very big. But the church itself is great in grace."

Small in size, great in grace. I think that is one of the loveliest descriptions of a little church anyone ever wrote. Most of us know a church like that.

And as they came up to the door, out of a cell nearby came the priest who looked after it. Egeria describes him with real affection. He was, she says, a healthy old man, a monk from his youth, what they called there an ascetic, and, as she puts it, "worthy to be in that place." Which is the highest praise she knows. And the other priests met them there too, and all the monks who lived near the mountain, all of them, she says, who weren't kept back by age or weakness.

Nobody lived right on the top. There was nothing up there but the little church, and a cave. The holy men told her that this cave was where Moses had been, when he went up the mountain the second time.

Here is that story, very quickly. The first time Moses went up the mountain, he was there forty days and forty nights, and God gave him the law written on two tablets of stone. And while he was gone, the people down in the valley got bored and frightened, and made themselves a golden calf, and danced round it. And when Moses came down and saw it, he was so angry that he threw down the tablets and broke them at the foot of the mountain.

And then, after a great deal of sorrow and prayer, God told Moses to cut two new tablets, and come up again. And Moses asked to see God's glory, and God said, "You cannot see my face. But I will put you in a cleft of the rock, and cover you with my hand while I pass by, and then you shall see my back." And so Moses went up a second time, and the Lord passed by, and proclaimed his name: "The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness."

That is what they remembered in that cave. Not the anger. The second chance. The mercy. The God who writes the law again after we have broken it.

And then they went into the church, and Egeria did what Egeria always did.

They read the passage. She says, "the whole passage from the book of Moses was read in that place." Picture it. A little stone church on the top of a mountain, early on a Sunday, crowded with monks and priests and pilgrims, all of them out of breath, and the old priest reading, in the place where it happened, most likely about the mountain wrapped in smoke, and the trumpet growing louder and louder, and the voice of God. "I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. You shall have no other gods before me."

And then the offering was made, which is Egeria's word for the Eucharist, the Mass, the Divine Liturgy, and they received communion. On the top of Mount Sinai. On a Sunday. I imagine she never forgot it.

Now, when they came out of the church, the priests gave the pilgrims a present.

Egeria calls it by a Greek word, eulogiae, which means blessings. In those days, when you visited a holy man or a holy place, it was the custom for them to send you away with a little gift, a blessing to carry. And the blessings at Sinai were fruit.

Which surprised Egeria very much, because the summit of Sinai is bare rock. Not a tree. Not a bush. Not a blade of grass. She says so: the mountain is so rocky that it hasn't even got a shrub on it.

But down below, she explains to her sisters, at the feet of the mountains, there are little patches of soil, and there the holy monks very carefully plant little trees, and orchards, and build little chapels and cells beside them, and grow fruit, as she puts it, "with their own hands, from the soil of the very mountain."

So the old monks had carried fruit up that terrible path, up and down the big brothers, all the way to the top, so that they could give it away to visitors. I find that very moving. You climb all that way, and at the top somebody hands you an apple.

We don't know what kind of fruit. Egeria doesn't say. Apples, perhaps, or pears, or figs. In the monastery gardens at Sinai today they grow almonds and olives and apricots and grapes, but that is today. Let us say a fig, and leave it at that.

"Here, lady," said Theodore, holding one out. "From the garden at the bottom. I watered that tree myself."

"You carried this all the way up?"

"Somebody has to," said Theodore. "It wouldn't be much of a blessing if we only gave it to people at the bottom."

And then Egeria, the fig in one hand and her breath slowly coming back, did what she always did next. She asked to be shown things.

"I began to ask them," she writes, "to show us each of the places." And the holy men did, all of them, the ones she asked for and the ones they knew about that she hadn't thought to ask.

And then she turned round and looked out from the walls of the church. And she forgot about her knees altogether.

Because all those mountains, those enormous big brothers she had climbed up and slithered down in the dark, which, she says, were so huge she thought she had never seen higher, were far below her now. "They looked like little hills," she writes. Little hills. All the peaks of the Sinai, spread out round her like a rumpled red blanket, and she was standing on top of the whole world.

And the holy men pointed. There, they said, is Egypt. There is Palestine. There is the Red Sea. There is the sea that goes to Alexandria. And there, and there, and there, as far as you can see, are the endless lands of the Saracens, which was what the Romans called the desert peoples of Arabia.

"All of it so far below us," she writes, "it was hardly believable. But the holy men pointed out each one."

Now, I must be honest with you. Modern travellers who have stood up there say that you cannot really see the Mediterranean from the top of that mountain, or Palestine either. It's much too far. You can see a great many mountains, and on a clear day the gulfs of the Red Sea glittering on either side. The monks were pointing in the right directions, and Egeria believed them, because they lived there and she was a guest, and because when you are standing on the top of Sinai with a fig in your hand, the whole world does seem to be in sight.

I don't think anyone was lying. I think they were pointing at where those places were, the way you might stand on a hill and say, "London's over there." And Egeria wrote down what she was told, and her sisters back home, thousands of miles away, were thrilled. And I am inclined to forgive everybody involved.

Now, they didn't go back down the same way. Remember what Theodore said: from the far side, you can see the mountain. Egeria's guides had planned it like a good walking tour. Go up the side where the climb is best. Come down the side where the descent is best. And that way, they would come down at the head of the valley, where the bush was. And that's where they would spend the night.

So, after the fig and the view, they started down, and before long they came to another mountain joined onto Sinai. It was called Horeb. And there was a church there too.

"This is the place Horeb," Egeria writes, "where the holy prophet Elijah was, when he fled from the face of Ahab the king."

You may remember the story. Elijah had been very brave, braver than any one man should have to be. And then the queen, Jezebel, swore she would kill him, and all his bravery ran out at once, like water out of a cracked jar. He ran away into the desert, and lay down under a bush, and said to God, "It is enough. Take my life. I am no better than my fathers." Which is what a great many tired people have said, in one way or another.

And God didn't scold him. God sent an angel, who woke him up and said, "Get up and eat." And there was a cake baked on hot stones, and a jar of water. And Elijah ate, and lay down again, and the angel woke him a second time, and said, very kindly, "Get up and eat, or the journey will be too much for you." Which is a sentence I think about a great deal.

And Elijah went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mountain of God, and he went into a cave and stayed there. And God said to him, "What are you doing here, Elijah?"

And then there was a great wind, that broke the rocks to pieces. But the Lord was not in the wind. And after the wind an earthquake. But the Lord was not in the earthquake. And after the earthquake, a fire. But the Lord was not in the fire. And after the fire, a still, small voice. A low whisper. A sound, one translation says, of thin silence.

And when Elijah heard it, he wrapped his face in his cloak, and went out, and stood at the mouth of the cave.

That was the cave they showed Egeria. "The cave where holy Elijah hid is shown there to this day," she writes, "in front of the door of the church that is there." And they went in, and made the offering again, and prayed very earnestly, she says, and read the passage from the Book of Kings.

"For this," she tells her sisters, as if they might have forgotten, "was always very much my custom. Whenever we came to any place, I wanted the passage from the Bible to be read there."

Yes, Egeria. We know. We love you for it.

I like to think she stood for a moment in the mouth of that cave, after the reading, and listened. The wind had dropped, the way it often does up there in the middle of the day. And there was nothing to hear. Nothing at all. Just the great quiet of the mountains, and her own heart, still thumping from the climb, and slowing down, and slowing down.

They went on down. Not far from Horeb, the holy men stopped again, by a great round rock with a flat top. There was no building there. Just the rock, and in the middle of it, a sort of altar made of stones.

"This," the holy men said, "is where Aaron stood, with the seventy elders, when Moses went up to receive the law."

It is in the Book of Exodus. Moses went up, with Aaron his brother, and Aaron's sons, and seventy of the elders of Israel. And the Bible says something astonishing about them. It says they saw the God of Israel, and under his feet there was something like a pavement of sapphire stone, as clear as the sky itself. And they ate and drank. And then Moses went on up the mountain, into the cloud, and the elders waited below.

So they read the passage there too, and said a psalm, and prayed. And then it was afternoon, and getting on for evening, and they came down at last to the head of the valley. And there, among a great many little stone cells, was a church. And in front of the church, a garden.

"A very pleasant garden," Egeria writes, "with plenty of very good water." After a whole day on that bare rock, after the cold dark and the climbing and the glare of the sun on the summit, you can feel her relief in that sentence. Green leaves. Running water. Shade.

And in the garden there was a bush.

They had arrived at the tenth hour, which is about four in the afternoon. And the holy men brought her to the bush, and said, "This is the bush out of which the Lord spoke to Moses in the fire."

And Egeria wrote one of the most famous sentences in her whole letter. The bush, she says, "is alive to this day, and puts out new shoots."

Still alive. Still growing.

The story, of course, is from the third chapter of Exodus. Moses, long before he was the leader of anybody, was a shepherd, looking after the sheep of his father-in-law Jethro, the priest of Midian. And he led the flock to the far side of the wilderness, and came to Horeb, the mountain of God. And there he saw a bush on fire. But the bush was not burning up. It burned and burned, and it was not consumed.

And Moses said to himself, "I will turn aside and see this great sight, why the bush is not burnt."

And when God saw that he turned aside to look, God called to him out of the bush. "Moses, Moses." And he said, "Here I am." And God said, "Do not come near. Take off your sandals, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground."

They showed Egeria that too, the place where Moses was standing when he was told to take off his sandals. I hope she took hers off. After the day she'd had, I think she would have been glad to.

And it was there, at that bush, that God told Moses his name. Moses asked, "If the people ask me, what is the name of the one who sent me, what shall I tell them?" And God said, "I am who I am. Say to them, I am has sent me to you."

It was too late in the day to celebrate the offering again; it was evening. So they said a prayer in the church, and another at the bush in the garden, and read the passage from the Book of Moses, as always. And then, she says, because it was late, they had supper with the holy men there, in the garden, in front of the bush. And they stayed the night.

Imagine that supper. Bread and fruit, perhaps, and water from the spring, eaten outside, in the cool of the evening, with the last of the light going pink on the mountains above, the mountain she had climbed that morning now standing up over the valley where she could finally see it. Monks and pilgrims together, tired and happy, talking quietly. And in the middle of it all, a bush. An ordinary-looking bush. Green leaves. A few new shoots. Not on fire, not tonight. Just growing, the way it had always grown, quietly, in the presence of God.

"Are you tired, lady?" said Theodore.

"I have never been so tired in my life," said Egeria.

"Was it worth it?"

She looked at the bush. She looked up at the mountain. She looked down at the fig stone in her hand, which she had kept all day without noticing.

"Ask me again in the morning," she said. "When I can feel my legs."

And the next morning, very early, they asked the priests to make the offering there, by the bush. And they did.

On the walk out, the holy men took them down the whole length of that great valley, and showed them everything.

The place where the people of Israel had camped, all those weeks, while Moses was up the mountain. The great stone, standing fixed in the ground, where they said the golden calf had been made. The place where Moses, coming down with the tablets, broke them in his anger. The stream where he threw the powdered gold of the calf into the water. And more places, and more places, every one with its story, every one with its reading, every one with its psalm.

Egeria tells her sisters, at the end of this part, that she can't possibly write it all down. It would take too long. She saw every single place, she says, that the books of Moses tell of in that valley. And then they came back out through the gap between the mountains, the very place where she had first knelt to pray, where the Graves of Lust are. And from there, on along the road through the desert towards the oasis of Pharan, and on, and on, in the end, back to Jerusalem.

And the mountain of God went back to hiding behind its big brothers, the way it always had.

That was, as far as we know, about sixteen hundred and forty years ago.

About a hundred and seventy years after Egeria, the emperor Justinian, who liked to build things, built a strong wall round the monks at the foot of the mountain, by the bush, to protect them from raiders. The historian Procopius wrote about it at the time. Inside that wall, a great church was built, and it is still there, and still in use. In time, the monastery came to be named after Saint Catherine of Alexandria, and it is one of the oldest Christian monasteries in the world where monks have never stopped praying. It belongs to the Orthodox Church. Catholics love it too. The monks there still keep a library full of ancient books, and still welcome pilgrims.

And behind the church, in the corner of a courtyard, there is still a bush. A big, tangled, green bush, a kind of wild bramble, with its roots under a little chapel that is called the Chapel of the Burning Bush. And when pilgrims go into that chapel, they take off their shoes.

Is it the very bush? Nobody can say. It is the bush the monks have kept and watered for as long as anyone remembers, in the place they have always pointed to. When Egeria saw it, it was already old, and already loved. And it is still putting out new shoots.

And people still climb the mountain. Nowadays most of them go up at night, by lamplight and torchlight, so as to be on the top for the sunrise, the way the sun came up over Egeria and her monks while they were climbing. Some go up by the long winding path that was made later, and some by a great stone staircase of thousands of steps that the monks laid down the mountain, which they call the Steps of Repentance, and which goes, I'm told, more or less straight up. Like a wall.

There is still a little chapel on the top. Small in size. Great in grace.

And Egeria? She went home, or towards home, eventually. The last part of her letter that we have finds her in Constantinople, planning another trip, because of course she is. We don't know what happened to her after that. A monk in the mountains of Spain, three hundred years later, remembered her and called her blessed, and held her up to his brothers as an example of courage: a woman who had crossed the world, and climbed the holy mountains, for love of God. That's all. Her name. Her letter. Her custom.

Prayer, reading, psalm. In every place.

It's not a bad custom for the end of a day, wherever you happen to be. A little prayer. A few words of Scripture. A psalm, if you have one in you. And then rest.

The mountain will still be there in the morning. And the bush will still be growing.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the first five chapters of what is left of Egeria's Travels, a long letter in Latin written by a pilgrim woman to her sisters at home, probably in the early three hundred and eighties. The arrival through the opening in the mountains, the prayer at the first sight of the mountain, the size of the valley, the monastery at the foot on Saturday evening, the climb on Sunday straight up and down, the arrival at the fourth hour, the small church great in grace, the old priest, the reading and the offering, the gifts of fruit grown by the monks, the view and the mountains that looked like little hills, the cave of Moses, Horeb and the cave of Elijah, the rock of Aaron and the elders, the bush that was still alive and putting out shoots, the supper in the garden, the offering the next morning, and the walk out down the valley are all in the letter. Her own words are given in our plain rendering, shortened in places. The beginning of the letter is lost, so we don't know exactly how she came to Sinai.

The stories of the places come from the Books of Exodus and Numbers and the First Book of Kings, told straight. That this mountain is the biblical Sinai, and this bush the bush of Moses, are traditions already old in Egeria's day; we have told them as traditions. Valerius of Bierzo's praise of Egeria is a real seventh-century letter. Justinian's walls are in Procopius.

The young monk Theodore, the mule, the fig, every word anyone says, and everyone's thoughts and knees, are our own invention.

That's the story of Egeria, and the mountain climbed like a wall, for tonight. Egeria has never been made a saint and has no feast day, but Moses the prophet, who met God on that mountain, is remembered on the fourth of September, and the prophet Elijah, who heard the still small voice there, on the twentieth of July.

The Woman at the Well of Sychar

At noon, in the heat, a woman of Samaria comes to Jacob's well with her jar and finds a tired stranger asking her for a drink. The longest conversation in the Gospels, told from John chapter four, and the town that asked him to stay.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Jesus, the woman of Samaria, the disciples, the people of Sychar
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; the Gospel is Scripture in both churches, and the Orthodox Church venerates the woman as Saint Photini, the Samaritan woman, equal to the apostles
Scripture and history
John chapter four is Scripture; Jacob's well near the ancient Shechem, Mount Gerizim and the old quarrel between Jews and Samaritans are real places and real history, and the well can still be visited today
Legend
the name Photini, meaning the enlightened one, and the later accounts of her life and her witness after Pentecost come from Orthodox tradition and are told here only as tradition, never as the Gospel
What we invented
the woman's walk to the well, the heat, the weight of the jar, her thoughts, the small remarks of the disciples on their return, and the evening in the town are our own invention, told in the spirit of the Gospel; everything Jesus and the woman say to each other is kept very close to John's account
Feast day
the Gospel is read in the Catholic Church on the Third Sunday of Lent in Year A, and in the Orthodox Church on the Sunday of the Samaritan Woman, the fifth Sunday after Pascha, with Saint Photini also remembered on her own day in the Orthodox calendar

Sources

  • The Gospel according to John, chapter four, verses one to forty-two, Catholic and Orthodox New Testament
  • The Gospel according to John, chapter eight, verse fifty-eight, and chapter eleven, for the other places where John records the divine name spoken of Jesus
  • Genesis, chapter thirty-three, verse nineteen, and chapter forty-eight, verse twenty-two, and Joshua, chapter twenty-four, verse thirty-two, for Jacob's field at Shechem
  • Second Kings, chapter seventeen, and Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews, Books Nine and Eleven, for the history of the Samaritans, with the caution that Josephus is a hostile witness
  • John Chrysostom, Homilies on the Gospel of John, Homily Thirty-One to Thirty-Four, fourth century, for the early Christian reading of the woman as a willing and quick learner
  • Raymond Brown, The Gospel according to John, Anchor Bible, 1966, for the commentary on chapter four

Full script

Picture a well, in the middle of a hot afternoon, and a man sitting on the edge of it with his eyes half closed.

It is not a pretty well. It is a deep, round hole in a field, with a low stone wall around it, and a flat rock to sit on, and not one single tree. Behind the man there is a dusty road, and in front of him there is a long slope that goes up to a mountain, and nobody has put up a sign that says where he is. He has walked a very long way. He has no bucket and no rope. And he is, quite plainly, thirsty.

And now, down the path from the town, comes a woman with a jar on her shoulder, walking alone at the one hour of the day when nobody else walks to the well. She sees the man. She stops. She does the sensible thing and decides not to look at him.

And then the man says a thing to her, a very small thing, only four words in the old language, and it is the beginning of the longest conversation that anyone has with Jesus in all four Gospels.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Gospel according to John, chapter four, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is about a woman of Samaria whom the Orthodox Church venerates as Saint Photini. A few of the small things around the edges, the walk, the heat, the thoughts, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the Gospel, and the notes at the end say which. What Jesus and the woman say to each other we have kept as close to John as we can. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

We begin with a map, and I promise it is a short one.

If you are in the land of Israel, in the time of Jesus, and you wish to walk from Judea in the south, where Jerusalem is, to Galilee in the north, where Nazareth and the lake are, you have a choice of roads. There is a road down the valley of the Jordan, which is hot and flat and a bit long. And there is a road straight through the hills, which is shorter, and cooler, and goes right through the middle of a country called Samaria.

Now, a great many people in those days took the long road. They would cross over the river, go all the way round, and come back across it again, which is a lot of extra walking, for a very particular reason. And the reason is that the people of Judea and the people of Samaria did not get on.

I want to be careful here, because this is the kind of thing that is easy to say badly. The Samaritans were not strangers from far away. They were neighbours, and cousins in a way. They read the first five books of Moses, as the people of Judea did. They kept the Sabbath. They waited for a promised one who would come and explain everything. They said they were the children of Jacob, and they were very proud of it. But long before, in the years after a war and an exile, the two peoples had drifted apart, and then they had quarrelled, and then the quarrel had become a habit, and then the habit had become a rule. The Samaritans said that the place to worship was on their own mountain, which is called Gerizim. The people of Jerusalem said that the place to worship was Jerusalem. Each side was quite sure about it, and each side had been quite sure for four hundred years.

It is a bit like two branches of a family who have not spoken since a wedding in which somebody said something about somebody's cooking. Nobody now remembers exactly what was said, but everybody remembers who said it.

And John, who is not a man to waste a sentence, puts one in the middle of our story to tell us how things stood. He says, in so many words, that Jews do not share things with Samaritans. And I think that this is exactly what he wants us to hold in our heads, right at the start. This is a story where somebody is going to do something that is simply not done.

So, with the map in your head, let us go and sit down at the well.

It is a few days into a long journey. Jesus and his disciples have been in Judea, where a great many people had been coming to be baptised, and word had gone round, and, as John mentions in a mild sort of way, some people who disliked all the attention had started to take an interest. So Jesus decides to go home to Galilee for a while. And John says, and I love this, that he had to go through Samaria.

Now, he did not have to, strictly. There was the long road. But John says he must, and a good many readers over the centuries have wondered what sort of must that is. I will not tell you what to think. I will only say that the road went through Samaria, and so did he.

They walk all morning. And by the middle of the day they come to a little town called Sychar, which sits at the foot of a mountain. And just outside it there is a field, and in the field there is a well.

This is no ordinary well. It is the well of Jacob. The old story says that Jacob, who is also called Israel, bought a field here, long ago, when he came home from his years with his uncle Laban, and that he gave it to his son Joseph. And somebody dug a well in it, and people have been drawing water from it ever since, for the whole of the time between Jacob and the day we are telling about. If you go to that place today, you can still find a well, a very deep one, under a church, and the people who look after it will let down a bucket and you can taste the water, and it is cold, and it is good. I mention that because I think you ought to know that this is a story with an address.

And it is noon. John says it is the sixth hour, which in his way of counting the day is the middle of it, the hottest part, when the sun is straight above and your shadow shrinks to a little puddle under your feet.

Jesus is tired. I want you to notice this. John, who writes more than any of the others about who Jesus is, and about the glory, and about the Word that was with God at the very beginning, here simply says: Jesus, tired out by the journey, sat down by the well. He does not say he rested like a person who has all the time in the world. He says he was worn out. It is one of the plainest sentences in the whole Gospel, and every time I hear it, I think, well, there it is. The Word became flesh, and the flesh got tired and sat down on a rock.

And the disciples, all of them, have gone into the town to buy food. So there is nobody with him. He is on his own at the well, at noon, in the heat, with no bucket.

Now let us walk back up the path, into the town, for a little while, and see who is coming.

The Gospel does not tell us her name. It does not tell us how old she was, or what she looked like, or what her house was like, and so I am going to have to tell you a few things that are not in the text, and I will flag them as we go. She is a woman of Sychar. She has a jar. And she is going to the well, in the heat of the day, when the other women of the town are not.

Why is she there at noon? John does not say. A good many people over the years have said it was because she was ashamed, and wanted to avoid the others, and perhaps she was. Others have pointed out that nobody can tell from the text, that people go for water at all sorts of hours for all sorts of reasons, and that a household which has run out of water at midday has to send someone. I do not know. So let us just say that she goes at noon, and that she is alone, and that she is carrying a jar that is not heavy yet, because it is empty, and will be heavy in a quarter of an hour. And let us say that she is thinking, as she walks, about the sort of things people think about on their way to the well. The washing. The bread. What somebody said yesterday. Whether the mule has been fed.

And she comes round the low wall of the well, and there is a man sitting on it.

She sees at once that he is a Jew. You could tell, in those days, by the way a man dressed, and by the way he spoke, and she would have known it by the time she had put her jar down. And she sees that he is alone. And she has the same thought that any sensible person has when she finds a strange man at a well: she will fill her jar, and she will not say a word, and she will go home.

She lowers the jar on its rope. It goes down, and down, a long way. And the man says:

"Give me a drink."

That is the whole sentence. In the old Greek, it is four words, or a little more, depending on how you count. Give me a drink. And it is not at all what she expected, and she is so surprised that she forgets her plan about not saying a word.

"How is it," she says, "that you, a Jew, ask for a drink from me, a woman of Samaria?"

I want you to hear what she is actually saying. She is not rude. There is a bit of an edge to it, perhaps, but mostly she is just astonished. She is saying: do you know who I am? Do you know who you are? Do you know what it is that you are doing? Because in her whole life, she has never seen it done.

And the answer he gives her is the strangest answer you could imagine.

"If you knew the gift of God," he says, "and who it is that is saying to you, give me a drink, you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water."

Now, I want you to think about this from the woman's side of the well. She has just been asked for a drink by a man who is, clearly, thirsty. And now the same man is telling her that if she knew who he was, she would be the one asking, and he would give her water. She looks at him. She looks at the rope in her hands. She looks at the well, which is very deep. And she says, quite reasonably:

"Sir, you have no bucket, and the well is deep. Where do you get this living water?"

And then, with a flash of what I can only call local pride, she adds:

"Are you greater than our father Jacob, who gave us the well, and drank from it himself, and his sons, and his flocks?"

Now, that is a very good question, and I think she says it with a straight face, and I think a little smile at the corner of her mouth. Because here is a tired man with no bucket, sitting on the wall of Jacob's well, and he is talking as though he has something better than Jacob's well. In her place, you would ask exactly that.

And he does not answer it directly. He does not say, yes, I am greater than Jacob. He says:

"Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again. But whoever drinks of the water that I will give will never be thirsty. The water that I will give will become in him a spring of water, welling up to eternal life."

Let us stop for a moment, and have a quiet look at that.

The word "living", in the old languages, was what people said about running water, the kind that comes from a spring or a stream, as opposed to the kind that sits in a cistern and goes a bit green. So when he says living water, she understands him, quite naturally, to mean a spring. A freshwater spring. A better source than a hole in the ground a hundred feet deep. And a spring would be a very good thing to have, if you are a woman who goes to the well every day.

And so she says the most sensible and the most human thing in the whole conversation. She says:

"Sir, give me this water, so that I may not be thirsty, nor have to come here to draw."

I like her very much for that. She does not pretend to understand more than she does. She does not say, ah yes, the spiritual meaning. She says, in effect: that sounds wonderful, and it would save me a lot of walking. And I think there is something in it that is just right. She does not understand yet. She is asking, all the same. And what Jesus has told us, in other places, is that asking is where it begins.

And now the conversation turns, and it turns on a single sentence.

Jesus says to her: "Go, call your husband, and come here."

Now, we come to a part of the story that people have told in a great many ways, and I am going to tell it carefully, because I think it deserves it.

She says, "I have no husband."

And Jesus says, "You are right to say, I have no husband. For you have had five husbands, and the one you have now is not your husband. What you have said is true."

That is what John gives us. It is quite short. And it has been the cause of an enormous amount of preaching, a good deal of it a bit unkind to her. Over the centuries, a great many preachers have decided that she must have been a woman of bad character, and that this is the reason she came to the well at noon, and that Jesus is exposing her. You might have heard it told that way.

But if you read it slowly, you will notice a few things. First, he does not scold her. Not once. He does not tell her to repent, or that what she has done is a sin, or that she is in danger. He says, what you have said is true. He says it, if anything, like a man who is paying her a compliment for being honest. Second, notice that nobody in the story, not even the woman, uses the word sin. And third, notice what she does next. She does not blush and run. She does not get angry. She says, and I think she says it very quietly:

"Sir, I see that you are a prophet."

That is a woman who has just had her life read out to her by a stranger at a well, and her whole response is, I see that you are a prophet. There is no defensiveness in it at all. And I think that is part of what the old writers meant when they praised her. Saint John Chrysostom, in the fourth century, who preached on this passage at great length, speaks of her as quick to learn, patient, and not at all proud. He says that the things that would have made another person angry did not make her angry, and that she stayed in the conversation.

Now, what do we actually know about her five husbands? Nothing. John does not tell us. They could have died. She could have been widowed, again and again, which in those days was not unusual. She could have been put away by men who had the right to do it, without her say in the matter. She could have been passed from one brother to the next in the old way of the law, if there had been no children. Some of those would be her grief, and some of them might be her fault, and the text does not say which. It is entirely possible that she was a woman who had had a very hard time. And so the thing that I think we can say, without going beyond what is written, is that Jesus knows her whole life, and that she stays, and that he stays.

And now, I think, a little relieved to be off that subject, and a little, perhaps, relieved to have something more comfortable to argue about, she asks a question about religion.

I really do think this is a human moment. When a conversation gets close to something tender, there is nothing like a theological dispute to change the subject. And her question is the great question of the whole region, the old one, the four-hundred-year-old one.

"Our fathers worshipped on this mountain," she says, and she very likely points up the slope at Gerizim, which is standing right there, enormous and green, "and you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people must worship."

So there it is. The quarrel. It is not abstract. She is looking at the mountain as she says it. Her people and his, and a thousand years of argument, and the question of where God is to be found.

And Jesus does not take a side. I think that is the most striking thing about his answer. He does not say, yes, you are quite right, and he does not say, no, you are quite wrong. He says:

"Woman, believe me, the hour is coming when you will worship the Father neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem."

And then he says, quite plainly, and without any malice, that her people worship what they do not know, and that his people worship what they know, because salvation is from the Jews. That is Scripture, and we shall let it stand as it is, and we shall note that he says it kindly, to a woman who has just asked him a straight question. And then he says the sentence that I think the whole conversation has been walking towards:

"But the hour is coming, and is now here, when the true worshippers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth, for the Father seeks such people as these to worship him. God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and in truth."

I have heard that passage read at a great many services, and I always find myself noticing a small thing, which is this. He says, the Father seeks such people. Not that such people must seek the Father, though of course that is true too. He says the Father is the one who is seeking. And there is a tired man at a well, at noon, who has walked all morning, and who has been waiting for a woman who is not going to make it easy for him. And I do not think that is an accident.

And she says, a bit wistfully, I think, because she has been given a great deal to think about:

"I know that the Messiah is coming. He who is called the Christ. When he comes, he will tell us all things."

It is a good thing to say. It is a gentle sort of hope. She is saying: well, I do not understand all this, and I cannot settle the argument, but there is one coming who will. And she says it as a woman who has waited for a long time.

And then Jesus says something that is, in the Greek, very short, and very strong.

"I am he, the one who is speaking to you."

Now, there is a little thing in the old Greek that I would like to tell you, because it is lovely, and then we will go on. The words he uses are "I am". Just that. In some other places in this Gospel, those same two words are used by Jesus in such a way that the people listening understand he is speaking in the very language of the divine name, the name that God gave to Moses at the burning bush. Here, the sentence can be read quite simply, as, I am he, the Messiah, the one you are waiting for. It can also be read as something deeper. Both readings are very old, and the churches have held both. The thing I want you to notice is that the first person in all of John's Gospel to whom Jesus says plainly, I am the Messiah, is not a priest, or a scholar, or one of the twelve. It is a woman, from a people nobody respected, at a well, at noon.

I think that is worth sitting with, for a minute. I shall be quiet while you do.

And just at that moment, the disciples come back.

You can imagine it. They come over the little rise, with their arms full of bread and olives and whatever else a group of hungry men might have been able to buy in a small town at midday, and they see their teacher at the well. And they see, with him, a woman. A Samaritan woman. Talking to him, as if she had every right to.

John says they were amazed. Of course they were amazed. It is not the way things are done. He does not tell us what they were thinking. He tells us only this, which is one of my favourite lines in any Gospel. He says: no one said, what do you want, or, why are you talking with her.

Nobody said a word. Twelve grown men, all of them with a perfectly good opinion, all of them standing there with their bread, and not a single one of them has the nerve to ask the question. And I think, if we are honest, that we have all been one of those men. We have all stood by with an opinion in our mouths, and kept it there, because the person we are with looks as though they know something we do not.

And the woman, at this moment, does something that, in all the many times I have read this passage, has never stopped surprising me.

She leaves her jar.

That is what John says. The woman left her water jar and went away into the town. And I think that is the best single sentence in the story.

Think of it. She came for water. She came with a jar, she lowered it down, she was going to carry it home, because it is the one thing she came for and the thing she needed. And she leaves it there, on the wall of Jacob's well, in the sun, and goes off, almost running, I think, up the path, with her hands empty. She has gone off and forgotten the one thing she came to get. Or she has not forgotten it at all, and has found, perhaps, that she was after something else. John does not explain. He simply tells us, and he trusts us to see it.

And she goes into the town, to the very people she might have been avoiding, and she says to them:

"Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did. Can this be the Christ?"

Now, do you hear what she does there? She does not say, I am sure that he is the Messiah. She says, can this be? She puts it as a question, and she gives them a reason, and she invites them to come and look for themselves. It is not a sermon. It is the gentlest, cleverest sort of invitation. Come and see. And it is, I think, one of the loveliest examples of how people actually pass on good news. Not by telling other people what to think, but by saying, will you come with me and look?

And the people of Sychar come. John says that they went out of the town and were coming to him. A whole crowd of them, trickling down the path in the heat of the afternoon, because of one woman's question.

While they are coming, there is a small scene at the well that I should not leave out.

The disciples have put down their bread. And they say to Jesus, as friends do, "Rabbi, eat."

And Jesus says, "I have food to eat that you do not know about."

And they look at each other, as you would, and say, in an undertone, did someone bring him something? Had he found a stall we missed? And I do rather like that, the way the twelve of them go straight to the practical, the way I would, the way we all would. They have spent a morning thinking about lunch, and he has spent the same morning on something else.

And he says to them: "My food is to do the will of him who sent me, and to finish his work."

And then he turns, as people do, and points at the fields. It is the time of year, we do not know exactly which, but it is the sort of weather in which the fields are green, and in the old way of counting there are four more months before the harvest. And he says:

"Do you not say, there are yet four months, and then comes the harvest? Look. I tell you, lift up your eyes and see how the fields are white for harvest."

And I think, at this moment, if the disciples looked up, they would have seen something. Because coming over the green fields, down the path from the town, in their white and cream and brown clothes, there are the people of Sychar, in a long, uneven line, coming to see. They are the harvest. It was a wonderful way of saying it, and I do not think it was lost on them. The fields were white, and the whole slope was moving.

And then he says, in his lovely, oblique way, that one sows and another reaps, and that they have been sent to reap what they did not work for, and that others have done the work and they have entered into it. And, as I read it, I think he is saying: you are not the first to be here. There have been others, and you do not know all their names, and one of them is a woman who has just left her jar.

And so the Samaritans arrive. And what happens next is simple, and quiet, and I think the best ending that a story could have.

John says that many of the Samaritans from that town believed in him because of the woman's word, because she said, he told me all that I ever did. And then, when they come to him, they ask him to stay with them.

Think of that. A whole town, who have been told for hundreds of years that they are on the wrong side of everything, asks a Jewish teacher, to stay. And he does.

He stays two days.

I love the two days. John does not tell us a single thing that happened in them. He does not give us a sermon, or a miracle, or a list. He says only, he stayed there two days. Two days in a town where, on the usual maps, nobody would go. Two days of meals, I imagine, in houses that a good Jewish traveller should not, by the usual rules, have entered. Two days of sitting by the fire in the evening. And whatever was said, and whatever was eaten, I think we can say that it was the kind of thing that nobody on either side would have thought possible a week before.

And then John gives us a last line, and I think it is very fine. Many more believed, because of his word. And they said to the woman:

"It is no longer because of what you said that we believe, for we have heard for ourselves, and we know that this is indeed the Saviour of the world."

I want to say a word about that sentence, because it is easy to hear it as a bit hard on her. As though they said, thanks very much, but now we have the real thing. I do not hear it that way at all. I hear it as exactly what she wanted. She said, come and see. They came, and they saw. And now they are saying, we know it ourselves. That is not a rebuke. That is what she was hoping for. It is what a good guide wants, at the end of the trail: not to be needed any more, because the other person can see the view.

And notice what they call him. Not the Messiah of the Jews, not the Christ, which is a word with a good deal of Jewish history in it. They call him the Saviour of the world. It is a bigger name. And it is Samaritans who say it, first, in this Gospel, a whole town of people who are, by the standards of the day, outsiders, in a field at the foot of a mountain.

It is evening now, and I would like us to go slowly.

The sun has dropped behind the mountain. It is cooler. The light on the slope of Gerizim has gone from white to gold, and now it is going from gold to a dim sort of rose. In the town of Sychar, the lamps are being lit. And somewhere in one of the houses, I think, there is a woman sitting quietly at a table, with her hands in her lap.

She is not saying very much. She has said a great deal today. She has told a town about a man at a well. And she has been told, by that man, a few things she is going to be turning over for a long time. About a spring that does not run dry. About a mountain, and a city, and a worship that is neither here nor there. About a Father who is looking for people. And she is thinking, perhaps, about her five husbands, and the one who is not, and that she was spoken to with such gentleness, about all of it, that she has hardly known what to do with herself.

And I think that out by the well, in the dusk, her jar is still sitting on the wall. Somebody might have found it the next morning, and carried it down to her, and said, you left this. And she might have said, so I did. And then she would have smiled, and it would have been quite plain that she did not mind a bit.

And out in the field, in the dark, there is a quiet sound, just the sound of a few people talking. The man is sitting at a table in the house of someone who has asked him to stay. His disciples, who have had a very long day and learned a great deal, and said almost nothing, are asleep on the floor. And a child, who was told to go to bed an hour ago, is peering round the door, to look at the guest.

The Gospel does not tell us what became of her. John does not say her name again. After this one passage, she goes quietly out of the Gospels, and does not come back.

But the churches, the Eastern ones especially, have never forgotten her. The Orthodox tradition has given her a name, Photini, which in Greek means the enlightened one, the woman with light in her, and it counts her among the saints, and it calls her equal to the apostles. A very old tradition says that after the resurrection, and after Pentecost, she went out and told the story to a great many people, a long way from Sychar, and that she told it with the same gentleness with which she had told it to her neighbours, and that it was said she was a tireless witness until the end of her life. Those accounts were written down long after, in the lives of the saints, and we have no way of knowing the details, so we will hold them lightly, as the tradition itself does. We do not need them. What we have is the one line in John, and I think the line is enough. She went and told. She left the jar and she went and told.

Every year, in the Orthodox Church, there is a Sunday, in the springtime after Pascha, that is called the Sunday of the Samaritan Woman, and the whole of John chapter four is read aloud. And in the Catholic Church, in the third week of Lent, in one year out of three, the same passage is read to the catechumens, to the people who are getting ready, in the darkness of the winter, to be baptised at Easter. And I think that is right. That the people who are about to go down into the water should hear the story of a woman who came to a well for water and was offered something else. It is one of the oldest ways in which the churches have understood what happens in baptism, and it is the one that the whole story seems to lean towards.

As for the well, it is still there. The town of Sychar is gone, or nearly so, and a modern city of Nablus has grown up in the valley, and the mountain is still Gerizim, and the people who call themselves Samaritans, a very small community indeed now, still live on it and keep their old ways. And down in the valley, at the foot of the slope, in a church that has been built, and destroyed, and rebuilt again, many times, there is a deep, round well. If you go there, they will let down a bucket. And you can lean over the rim, and look down into the dark, a very long way, and listen, and you can hear the water.

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Gospel according to John, chapter four, verses one to forty-two. It is Scripture, and in both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches it is read in the liturgy every year. The place, Jacob's well near Sychar, the old Shechem, is a real place, and the history of the quarrel between Jews and Samaritans is real history, though the details of how it began are argued about, and Josephus, who tells us a good deal about it, was not a friendly witness.

What we have kept from the text: everything Jesus and the woman say to each other, as near as a retelling can; the noon hour; the disciples gone for food and their astonishment on their return; the woman leaving her jar; her question to the town, can this be the Christ; the talk of food and harvest; the two days; and the last confession of the people of Sychar, that he is the Saviour of the world.

What we flagged as not in the text: John does not tell us why she came at noon, and we offered two readings and chose neither. He does not say that her five husbands were her fault, or that she was in any kind of disgrace, and neither do we. John does not say what Jesus did during his two days in the town. And the name we have used for her, Photini, is the Orthodox tradition's name, not John's, and the stories about her later life are tradition, not Gospel.

What we invented: her walk to the well and the things she is thinking, the weight of the jar, the heat, the way the disciples whisper about lunch, the evening in the town, and the child at the door. None of it changes what happens or what it means.

For further reading, the Gospel of John, chapter four, is not long, and it is well worth reading aloud, slowly, perhaps with a glass of water beside you.

That's the story of the woman at the well for tonight. The Gospel is read in the Catholic Church on the Third Sunday of Lent in the first year of the cycle, and the Orthodox Church keeps the Sunday of the Samaritan Woman in the weeks after Pascha, and remembers Saint Photini on her own day as well.

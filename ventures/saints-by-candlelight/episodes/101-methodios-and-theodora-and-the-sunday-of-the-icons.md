It is night, and it is cold, and there are a great many candles.

A long line of people is walking through the streets of a very old city. Bishops in white and gold. Monks in black. Soldiers without their swords. Ladies of the palace with their hoods up. Fishermen, bakers, a man who sells onions, two very tired donkeys who have come by mistake. And in the middle of it all, a small woman in a plain dark cloak, holding the hand of a little boy who keeps yawning. The woman is the empress. The little boy is the emperor. He is three.

Further back, a girl of about ten is walking beside her grandmother. The girl is holding a candle in one hand. The grandmother is holding something wrapped in an old linen cloth, the way you might carry a loaf that has just come out of the oven, close against her chest.

"Can I see it yet?" the girl whispers.

"Not yet," says her grandmother. "When we get there."

"Where are we going?"

The grandmother smiles, and nods up the hill, to where a dome as big as the sky is waiting in the dark.

For the last two nights we have been following one long story, and tonight we come to the end of it.

Two nights ago we met Saint John of Damascus, who sold baskets in the market and wrote, from a monastery in the desert, the great defence of the holy icons, when an emperor in Constantinople had ordered them taken down. Last time we met Saint Theodore the Studite, the abbot with ink on his sleeves, who walked round his garden with his monks on Palm Sunday, carrying the icons a second emperor had just forbidden all over again.

If you missed those, here is the short version.

An icon is a holy picture. A face, usually, painted on a wooden board, with gold behind it. The face of Christ, or of his Mother, or of a saint. Christians in the East had been praying in front of them, and kissing them, and carrying them in processions, for a very long time.

Then, in the seven hundreds, some of the emperors, and some of the bishops with them, became worried. They were not wicked men, most of them. They were afraid that people were worshipping wood and paint, the way the old pagans had worshipped statues. So they said: take them down. Paint over them. Put a plain cross instead.

And other Christians said: no. You have misunderstood. We do not worship the wood. When I kiss a picture of my mother, I am not in love with the paint. The honour goes through the picture, to the person. That is what Saint Basil the Great said, long before any of this, and that is what Saint John of Damascus said, and that is what a great council of bishops said at Nicaea, in the year seven hundred and eighty-seven. Worship belongs to God alone. Honour, the ordinary loving honour you give to a picture of someone you love, may be given to the icons. And the Son of God, who became a man with a real human face, may be painted, because he was seen.

That council settled it. For about thirty years.

Then, in eight hundred and fifteen, a new emperor brought the old rule back. That was the trouble Theodore the Studite walked round his garden about. And for nearly thirty years more, in the great city, the icons were down again.

Which is a very long time. Long enough for a whole generation of children to grow up and never once see a holy picture in a church.

Tonight's story is about one of those children. Or rather, it is about two saints who are not children at all, and one child who is watching them.

* * *

The city is Constantinople, which we call Istanbul. The year is eight hundred and forty-three.

I want you to imagine it in winter. The wind comes down off the Black Sea, sharp as a knife, and goes right through the streets and out the other side into the Sea of Marmara, taking people's hats with it. The harbours are full of ships that have decided not to go anywhere until spring. Smoke goes up from a hundred thousand chimneys. Bread is being baked. Fish is being shouted about. Somewhere, in a narrow street near the great church, a man is arguing with a goat.

And at the top of the hill stands Hagia Sophia, the church of Holy Wisdom, which had been standing there for three hundred years already, and which is still standing there now. Its dome is so wide that people said it was not resting on anything at all, but hanging from heaven on a golden chain. Inside, it was all light. Marble of every colour, green and red and white and purple, gold on the ceiling, and hundreds and hundreds of lamps.

Somebody had to look after those lamps.

So let me introduce our family. They are invented, I should say at once. But there were families like them, and somebody had to do the job.

The father is called Stephanos. He is a lamp-lighter at the Great Church. Every evening he goes up and down ladders with a long pole and a jar of oil, filling and trimming and lighting, and every morning he does it all again the other way round. He smells permanently of olive oil, which is not the worst thing a man can smell of. He is cheerful and not very tall, and he knows every lamp in the building by name. He has named them himself. Nobody else uses the names.

His daughter is called Zoe, which means life. She is ten. She is allowed to come to work with her father on the condition that she does not climb anything, a condition which she has interpreted, over the years, more and more generously.

And then there is the grandmother, Stephanos's mother, whose name is Anna. She is old. Older than anybody Zoe knows. She has a face like a walnut and very quick hands and opinions about everything, especially fish. And she remembers.

That is the important thing about Grandmother Anna. She remembers before.

* * *

It begins, as far as Zoe is concerned, with a question at bedtime.

They live in two rooms over a cooper's shop, not far from the Great Church, so close that you can hear the semantron in the morning, which is a long wooden board that a monk hits with a mallet to call people to prayer. Zoe sleeps on a mattress in the corner of the back room, and Grandmother Anna sleeps on the other side of a curtain, and at night, when the lamp is out, they talk.

"Grandmother," says Zoe, one night in the winter. "What was it like? Before?"

"Before what?"

"You know. Before. When the pictures were up."

There is a pause on the other side of the curtain.

"Who's been talking about the pictures?"

"Everybody," says Zoe, which is true. That winter, everybody in the city was talking about the pictures, in low voices, in doorways, in the bread queue. Because the emperor was dead.

I should explain that.

The emperor who had kept the icons down for the past dozen years or so was called Theophilos. He was a clever man, and a builder, and very fond of things that were made of gold and moved by themselves. He had a golden tree in his palace with golden birds in it that sang, and golden lions beside the throne that roared and swished their tails, worked by some hidden arrangement of pipes and bellows. Ambassadors were said to find this very alarming. I find it rather wonderful, and I would like a golden lion, but nobody has offered.

Theophilos was also very firmly against the icons. And in January of eight hundred and forty-two he had died, quite young, of an illness. His son, who was also emperor now, was called Michael. Michael was two.

So the person who was really running the empire was his mother. The widowed empress, Theodora.

And everybody in the city knew one thing about Theodora, or thought they did. They whispered it in the bread queue. The empress, they said, loved the icons.

"So is it true?" says Zoe, in the dark.

"Is what true?"

"About the empress. And the dolls."

There is another pause. And then Grandmother Anna begins to laugh, very quietly, on the other side of the curtain.

"Oh," she says. "The dolls. Well. If you're going to hear it, you had better hear it properly."

* * *

Now. What follows is a story. It comes from the chronicles written in Constantinople in the century after, and from a Life of Theodora written by people who loved her. Some of it may have happened exactly so. Some of it has very probably been polished on the way. I will tell it the way Grandmother Anna would have heard it, in the bread queue, and you may decide for yourself how much to believe. That is what I do.

Theodora came from Paphlagonia, a province along the coast of the Black Sea, a place of forests and fishing towns, a long way from any palace. Her family were respectable. Her father was an officer. Her mother was called Theoktiste. And her mother, quietly, all her life, had kept icons.

When the young emperor Theophilos was looking for a wife, the old story says, he did it the way Byzantine emperors were supposed to do it in the stories. A bride-show. The most beautiful and well-brought-up young women in the empire were invited to the palace, and lined up in a great hall, and the emperor walked along the line holding a golden apple. Whoever he gave the apple to would be empress.

I want to be honest with you. Historians have some doubts about bride-shows. They may have happened, or something like them may have happened, or they may be the kind of thing that later writers liked to say happened, because it makes a better story. But this one is famous, so here it is.

Theophilos walked along the line. And he stopped in front of a young woman who was very beautiful and very clever, whose name was Kassia. And to make conversation, or perhaps to test her, he said something rather pompous. He said: well, it was through a woman that all the bad things came into the world. He meant Eve, and the apple, and the serpent, and all that.

And Kassia looked at him, and said: yes. And it was through a woman that all the better things came too.

She meant Mary. The Mother of God.

Which was an excellent answer. Theologically it was perfect. As a way of getting an emperor to marry you, it was a disaster. Theophilos, the story says, did not like being answered back, and he walked on, and gave the golden apple to the next young woman along. Who was Theodora.

And Kassia? Kassia went off and became a nun, and founded a convent, and wrote hymns. Wonderful hymns. One of them is still sung every year in Holy Week in Orthodox churches all over the world, and people go to church specially to hear it. She is Saint Kassia now. I think she did all right.

But back to Theodora. Theodora became empress. And Theodora, like her mother, quietly kept icons.

Now here is the bit about the dolls.

Theodora's mother, old Theoktiste, lived in a house of her own in the city. And the emperor's little daughters, Theodora's girls, used to go and visit their grandmother. And when they came, Grandmother Theoktiste would bring out a box. And in the box were icons. And she would show them to the little girls, and hold each one up to their faces for them to kiss, and tell them who it was.

Well. You know what happens next. You have been a child.

The girls came home to the palace, and their father the emperor asked them what they had been doing at Grandmother's. And the smallest one said: Grandmother has a box of beautiful dolls, and she puts them on our heads and we kiss them.

Theophilos was not pleased. The girls were not allowed to visit Grandmother quite so often after that.

"And that's the dolls?" says Zoe.

"That's half the dolls," says Grandmother Anna. "There's more."

There was a man at the palace called Denderis. He was the court jester. He was a funny little man, who did tricks and said silly things and was allowed to wander more or less anywhere, the way a cat is. And one day he wandered into the empress's private room, and found Theodora there, alone, holding a little icon and kissing it.

"What's that?" said Denderis.

And Theodora, who was quick, said: "These are my beautiful dolls, and I love them very much."

And Denderis went straight off to the emperor, who was at dinner, and said in front of everybody: "Mama was kissing the beautiful dolls!" Except he did not say Mama, he said something sillier.

The emperor got up from the table, the story says, very red in the face, and went to find his wife. And Theodora, who was even quicker the second time, said: dolls? Oh, that fool. I was standing with my maids in front of the mirror. He saw our faces in the glass, and thought they were dolls. And she gave the emperor a look that said, really, you listen to a jester? And he went back to dinner.

And afterwards, the story says, Theodora had a quiet word with Denderis. A very quiet word, about what happens to jesters who carry tales.

So the next time, a few weeks later, when the emperor was at dinner and thought he would have a bit of fun, and called out across the table, "Denderis! Has Mama been kissing her dolls again?", the little jester put one finger to his lips, and the other hand on his bottom, as if remembering something unpleasant, and said: "Hush! Not a word, Majesty. Not a word about the dolls."

Zoe laughs so much at this, in the dark, that her father calls through from the other room, "Go to sleep!"

"Did that really happen?" she whispers, when she can.

"Who knows," says Grandmother Anna. "But I'll tell you what I do know. The empress loves the pictures. Everybody who knows anything knows that. And the emperor is dead. And something," she says, and she sounds suddenly not old at all, "something is going to happen."

* * *

It did not happen at once. That is one of the interesting things about this story.

You might expect that the moment Theophilos died, Theodora would have run through the palace flinging icons up on every wall. She did not. For about a year, nothing very obvious happened at all.

She had good reasons. She had a small son to keep safe, and an empire to run, and an army that was still partly full of officers who had served her husband and agreed with him. The patriarch of Constantinople, the chief bishop of the city, was a man called John, who was the cleverest iconoclast of them all. People called him John the Grammarian, which in those days meant something like a professor. Some of his enemies called him worse things, including a wizard, which I am sure was unfair. If you are going to undo something that has been the law for nearly thirty years, you have to do it carefully. Or people get hurt.

So Theodora waited, and talked, and listened. She had a minister she trusted, a quiet and very capable official called Theoktistos. Not to be confused with her mother Theoktiste, though I admit the Byzantines did not make this easy. Half the people in this story seem to be called Theo-something. It means God, so you can see why it was popular.

And she sent for a man who had been waiting a very long time for this moment.

His name was Methodios.

* * *

Stephanos the lamp-lighter is the one who tells Zoe about Methodios. He hears about him at work. Everybody at the Great Church is talking about him that winter, in corners, in the dark at the top of ladders.

"He's from Sicily," says Stephanos, at supper, which is bean soup again. "From Syracuse. Came here as a young man to make his fortune. Had a bag of money with him, they say, and meant to buy himself a job at court."

"What happened?"

"He met a monk," says Stephanos, "and never got to court. The monk talked to him for an afternoon, and the next thing anybody knew, he'd given the money away and gone off to a monastery."

"That happens a lot in these stories," says Zoe.

"It does," says Stephanos. "You'd think people would learn to stay away from monks."

Methodios, like Theodore the Studite, held firmly to the icons. And when the old rule against them was brought back in eight hundred and fifteen, he did not stay to argue. He went to Rome.

That was a long way to go in those days. Weeks, by sea and road. But Rome was where the pope was, and the popes had always stood by the icons, and Methodios thought, quite reasonably, that a letter from the pope might help. He stayed there some years. And in eight hundred and twenty-one, when a new emperor came to the throne in Constantinople, a man called Michael, Methodios came back, carrying a letter from Pope Paschal the First, asking the new emperor, very politely, to put the holy pictures back.

"And did he?" says Zoe.

"No," says Stephanos. "He did not."

Michael the Second did not want to be told by anybody, least of all by a monk from Sicily with a letter from Rome. He had Methodios beaten, and then he sent him to prison, on a little island off the coast. And there Methodios stayed for about nine years.

The old Life of Methodios says his prison was more like a tomb than a room. Small and dark, with hardly any light, and hardly room to stretch. I will not tell you more than that, because it is bedtime and because it was not nice. But I want you to know the number. Nine years. Nine summers and nine winters. Most of you listening to this have not been alive for much longer than that, and some of you rather less.

And he came out. That is the other thing I want you to know. He came out, thin and grey and not at all well, and he was not bitter. Or if he was, he got over it. Because the next emperor, Theophilos, the one with the golden lions, did something rather odd. He argued with Methodios about the icons, and did not change his mind. And then, the chroniclers say, he kept Methodios near him at the palace anyway, and even took him along on journeys, because Methodios was so clever and so learned, and Theophilos liked clever people around him, even ones who disagreed with him.

"That's a strange thing to do," says Zoe.

"It is," says her father. "But I've known stranger. There's a deacon at the Great Church who can't stand the precentor, and they eat lunch together every single day."

So Methodios, by the time Theophilos died, was an old man who had spent years on both sides of the palace walls. He had been in a prison that was nearly a grave. He had dined with the emperor who kept him there. He knew exactly how strongly people felt, on both sides. And he knew that what was coming needed not just courage, but gentleness.

Which is why Theodora sent for him.

* * *

In the first weeks of eight hundred and forty-three, early in the spring, a meeting of bishops and monks and officials was called in Constantinople. A synod, it is called. They talked for a long time, and here is what they decided.

First. The council of Nicaea of seven hundred and eighty-seven was right. The holy icons may be honoured, as that council said. Worship to God alone, honour to the images, and the honour passes to the person shown.

Second. John the Grammarian could no longer be patriarch. He was sent off to live in a monastery outside the city. Nobody hurt him. I want to say that clearly. It would have been easy, in that moment, for the side that had been pushed down for thirty years to take its revenge. They mostly did not. That was Theodora's doing, and Methodios's.

Third. The new patriarch would be Methodios.

And there was one more thing. Theodora asked for something.

She had loved her husband. He had been against everything she believed about the icons, and he had made life very hard for people she admired. And he had been her husband, and the father of her children, and she had loved him. And she did not want him remembered, forever, as an enemy of the Church. She did not want his name read out with the names of the heretics.

So she told the bishops something. She said that in his last days, when he was very ill, Theophilos had been sorry. That he had changed his mind, at the end. One version of the story says that she put an icon into his hands when he was dying, and he kissed it.

Now, did that happen? We do not know. Historians are, let us say, cautious. A loving wife, a dying husband, nobody else in the room. It is the kind of thing that is very hard to check.

But the bishops did a merciful thing. They believed her, or chose to. They agreed that Theophilos would not be condemned. And the old story goes on.

The old story says that Methodios, the new patriarch, took a book, and wrote in it the names of all the emperors who had fought against the icons. And he laid the book on the altar of the Great Church. And the bishops and the people prayed, all through a week, for the soul of the emperor Theophilos. And at the end of the week, when Methodios opened the book, the name of Theophilos had gone. Wiped clean off the page, as if it had never been written there.

That is a legend. It is written down in the old accounts, but it is a legend, and I am telling it as one. If it happened, it was God's doing, not the patriarch's and not the empress's. But I think it is a lovely legend all the same. Because what it is really saying is something that is true whether or not the ink moved. God is more merciful than we are. And a wife's prayers for her husband are not wasted.

* * *

Now it is Lent.

Lent in Constantinople was serious. No meat, no cheese, no eggs, no fish with a backbone, no wine, no oil on most days. Long services, with a great many prostrations, which means kneeling down and touching your forehead to the floor, and getting up again, and doing it again, until your knees have opinions. Zoe's family eats a great deal of bread and olives and beans, and Stephanos the lamp-lighter, who is not allowed his oil on the plate, has to spend all day carrying it up ladders, which he says is the hardest part of Lent and nobody appreciates it.

And word goes round the city, in the bread queue, in the doorways, that something is going to happen at the end of the first week of Lent. On the Sunday.

"What's going to happen?" says Zoe.

"Wait and see," says her father, who does not know either.

But Grandmother Anna knows. Or guesses. Because on the Saturday evening, the last day of the first week, she gets up off her stool in the corner, very slowly, because of her knees, and goes to the big wooden chest at the foot of her bed. The one where the winter blankets live. And she opens it, and reaches down, under the blankets, under a folded tablecloth nobody has used in years, under a bag of dried lavender, all the way to the bottom.

And she takes out something flat, wrapped in an old linen cloth.

Zoe has never seen it. She did not know it was there.

"What is it?" she says.

"Put your cloak on," says Grandmother Anna. "And find your good candle. We're going out."

* * *

It is dark when they get to the church at Blachernae.

Blachernae is right at the other end of the city, up in the far corner, where the land walls come down to the Golden Horn. It is a long walk for an old woman with bad knees. Stephanos takes one arm and Zoe takes the other, and Grandmother Anna holds the wrapped thing against her chest with both hands and refuses to let either of them carry it.

The church at Blachernae was very old and very special. It was the church of the Mother of God, and it kept something precious: a robe that, by long tradition, had belonged to the Virgin Mary herself. The people of Constantinople believed that she watched over their city, and Blachernae was where they went to ask her to.

And tonight it is full. Fuller than Zoe has ever seen any church. People are standing in the doorway and down the steps and out into the courtyard and halfway down the street. And they are all, or nearly all, carrying candles. And a great many of them, Zoe notices, are carrying something else. Flat things. Wrapped in cloths. Held close.

"Grandmother," Zoe says. "Everybody's got one."

"Of course they have," says Grandmother Anna. "Did you think I was the only one?"

The later accounts say that the empress and the bishops and the people kept a vigil at Blachernae that night. A vigil is a night of prayer: psalms and hymns and readings, one after another, while the candles burn down and get replaced. Zoe has been to vigils before, at the Great Church, and she usually falls asleep somewhere around the middle and wakes up with her face on her father's cloak. Tonight she does not fall asleep once.

Because somewhere, at the front, near the altar, is the empress. Zoe cannot see her. There are too many tall people in the way. But a woman near them, who has a good view because she is standing on a step and also on her husband's foot, reports in a whisper. The empress is wearing black, because she is still in mourning. She has the little emperor with her. He has fallen asleep in a monk's arms. The new patriarch is there. He is very old and very thin and his beard is quite white, and his voice, when he sings, is not loud. But everybody can hear it.

And at the front, by the altar, where for nearly thirty years there have been only plain crosses, somebody has set up the holy icons. The Saviour. His Mother. Lit with lamps. And people are going up, one at a time, as the night goes on, and kissing them.

Grandmother Anna does not go up. Zoe wonders why. And then she sees that her grandmother is crying, quite quietly, without making any fuss about it, and she understands that her grandmother is not quite ready yet.

* * *

When it begins to get light, they set off.

Morning. The first Sunday of Lent, in the year eight hundred and forty-three. Which, as near as anybody can work out, was the eleventh of March.

Out of the church at Blachernae comes the procession. First the cross. Then the deacons with censers, swinging, so that the cold morning air smells of incense. Then the clergy, and the monks, and the bishops, and the new patriarch Methodios, walking slowly, because of the nine years. And the empress Theodora, with her small son, who is awake now and holding his mother's hand and looking at everything. And then the icons, carried high. And then everybody else. All of Constantinople, it seems to Zoe. All of it, walking.

It is a long way. From Blachernae, in the far corner of the city, across the hills to the Great Church. Down narrow streets and wide ones. Past the bakeries, which are open, and the fish market, which is closed because it is Lent and also Sunday, and past the cooper's shop with the two rooms over it where Zoe lives, and past the man who argues with the goat. The goat is there too. It does not join in.

And people come out of their houses as the procession passes, and stand in their doorways, and some of them start to cry, and some of them start to sing, and some of them go inside and come back out a moment later with something flat and wrapped, and join the end of the line.

They are singing. Zoe does not know the words at first. Then she picks them up. It is a hymn to the icon of Christ. It asks forgiveness, and it gives thanks, and it says that he came to save the world, and that he has filled everything with joy. She sings it over and over, all down the hill, until she knows it by heart, which she still will, when she is as old as Grandmother Anna.

And somewhere around the middle of the city, Grandmother Anna stops.

"Grandmother? Are your knees bad?"

"My knees are always bad," says Grandmother Anna. "Hold this." And she gives Zoe her stick. And with both hands, there in the middle of the street, with the procession going on around her like a river round a rock, she begins to unwrap the linen cloth.

It is a small icon. Not much bigger than Zoe's two hands put together. The paint is dark with age, and with candle smoke, and with years at the bottom of a blanket chest. It is the Mother of God, holding her child, her cheek against his cheek. And in the bottom corner, the paint has worn away almost to the wood, in a little smooth patch, about the size of a thumb.

"That's where I held it," says Grandmother Anna. "When I prayed. All those years. In the dark."

"Was it yours before?"

"It was my mother's. She had it in the window. Everybody did, on our street. Then they told us to take them down. So I took it down. And I put it in the chest. And every night I took it out again, after your grandfather was asleep, and said my prayers, and put it back." She smiles. "Your grandfather knew. He pretended not to. He was a good man."

Zoe looks at the little worn patch, about the size of a thumb.

"Do you want to carry it?" says Grandmother Anna.

And Zoe gives her grandmother back her stick, and takes the icon, very carefully, in both hands, and holds it up against her chest, the way you might carry a loaf that has just come out of the oven. And they walk on.

* * *

The Great Church is full to the doors and beyond.

Stephanos the lamp-lighter has been up since long before dawn, which is to say he did not go to bed, lighting every single lamp in the building. Every one. Even the one up in the far corner that he calls Old Grumpy, because it always smokes, and which he usually leaves till last and then forgets. Tonight, he says afterwards, Old Grumpy burned as clear as a star. He takes this as a sign. Of what, he is not sure.

And the procession comes in through the great doors, under the dome that hangs from heaven on a golden chain, into all that light.

Zoe has been inside Hagia Sophia a hundred times. More. She has played hide and seek behind its columns, been told off by its deacons, fallen asleep in its galleries. She thought she knew it.

But she has never seen it like this. Because now there are faces in it.

The icons that were carried in the procession are being set up, near the front, where everyone can see them. Lamps are being lit in front of them. And people are going up to them. Hundreds of people. Thousands. Old men who remember. Young women who do not. A soldier with a scar across his chin, who stands in front of the face of Christ for a long time, without moving, and then kneels down on the marble and touches his forehead to the floor.

And Zoe, holding her grandmother's icon, understands something she had not understood before. She had thought, all her life, that the church was beautiful. It was. But it had been beautiful the way a mountain is beautiful, or the sea. Huge and bright and grand, and not looking at you.

Now there are faces in it. And the faces are looking back.

There is a Liturgy, a long and glorious one, with the patriarch Methodios at the altar. Zoe does not remember much of it afterwards, except the singing, and the light, and her grandmother's hand on her shoulder. And at the end of it, something new.

A deacon stands up and reads out a long declaration. A list, really, of what the Church believes about the holy icons, and of the people who had stood up for them. And after each name of somebody who had defended the icons, the whole church sings, all together, memory eternal. Memory eternal. Memory eternal. Which means: God remember them, forever.

Zoe listens to the names. Patriarchs who had stood firm and been sent away. And names she does not know, of bishops and monks and nuns and ordinary people who had been sent away, or locked up, or hurt, for the sake of the faces in the pictures.

And every time, the whole of the Great Church sings, memory eternal. Memory eternal. Memory eternal.

And her grandmother sings it too, in a cracked old voice, with tears running down into the walnut lines of her face, and does not wipe them away.

[pause]

That was the first Sunday of Orthodoxy.

It has been kept every year since, for nearly twelve hundred years, on the first Sunday of Great Lent. In Orthodox churches all over the world, and in the Byzantine Catholic churches too, on that Sunday, the people bring icons from home, and after the Liturgy they walk round the church in procession, carrying them, and singing. Old people and young people. And always, always, a great many children, holding small icons up against their chests, very carefully, in both hands.

* * *

After that, things slowly went back to something like normal. Which is to say, there were arguments.

Methodios was patriarch for four years. They were not easy years. He had to decide what to do about all the priests and bishops who had been on the other side, which was a great many of them. Some people wanted them all thrown out. Methodios was firmer than some people liked and gentler than others liked, which is usually a sign that you are trying to be fair. He even had a long and rather tiring quarrel with the monks of Stoudios, Theodore's own monks, about old arguments from Theodore's day and about who should be made a bishop. Holy people do not always agree. They argue, and they are still holy. I find this a great comfort.

But he did one thing that I think would have made Theodore the Studite very happy.

You may remember from last time that Theodore had died in exile, at a little monastery on a headland across the water from the city, in eight hundred and twenty-six, and was buried there, far from his monastery. In January of eight hundred and forty-four, Methodios and the monks of Stoudios had Theodore's body brought home. Carried across the water and through the city, with candles, and laid to rest at Stoudios, beside his brother and his uncle, in his own monastery, among his own monks, near the garden where he had walked on Palm Sunday with his arms full of icons.

So the old abbot came home in the end. Even if his monks did spend the next few years arguing with the patriarch who brought him. I think Theodore would have found that quite funny. Or at least, I hope he would.

[pause]

Methodios died in the summer of eight hundred and forty-seven, on the fourteenth of June. He was old, and he had been ill for a long time, ever since the island. The Orthodox Church remembers him on the day he died, as Saint Methodios the Confessor. That word again, which we met last time. A confessor is somebody who suffered for the faith, and did not die for it, and simply kept on saying the true thing until it was true for everyone again.

Theodora ruled for her son for another nine years or so, and by every account she ruled well. She kept the borders safe, and the treasury full, and when her son grew up and wanted to run things his own way, as sons do, she stepped aside. Not very happily, it must be said. In the end she went to live in a convent, with some of her daughters, and spent her last years in prayer. She died some time after eight hundred and sixty-seven. The Orthodox Church keeps her feast on the eleventh of February, as Saint Theodora the empress, the one who brought back the holy icons.

The church at Blachernae is gone now. A spring is still there, in the same spot, in a quiet corner of Istanbul, and a small church built over it, and people still go there to pray. Hagia Sophia is still there, of course. It has had a long and complicated life since that Sunday, and it has been many things. But the dome is still up, hanging from heaven on its golden chain. And if you go inside, and look up, you can still see, high on the walls and the vaults, gold mosaics of faces. The first of them were made about twenty years after our story, in the time of Theodora's son, by people who could very well have walked in that procession, or whose parents had. And they are still looking back.

[pause]

Zoe and her grandmother are our own invention. But if there had been a Zoe, I like to think that she went home that Sunday, very tired, and that her grandmother did not put the icon back in the chest.

I like to think that she put it in the window, where her own mother had kept it. With a little lamp in front of it, one that her son the lamp-lighter filled for her every evening without being asked.

And I like to think that, years later, when Grandmother Anna had gone home to God, and Zoe was grown up, and had children of her own, she kept it in the window too. And that on the first Sunday of Lent, every year, she took it down, and gave it to her smallest child to carry round the church, very carefully, in both hands. And she showed them the little worn patch in the corner, about the size of a thumb, and told them how it got there.

That is what the icons are, really. Not magic. Not idols. A face of someone you love, held in your hands, in the dark, for years, until it wears smooth where you held it. And then one day you carry it out into the light. And you find that everybody else was holding one too.

So if tonight there is something you have been holding onto in the dark, quietly, by yourself, and wondering whether anybody else believes in it, or whether it will ever come out into the light, you can rest. You are not the only one. You never were. Somewhere, at the bottom of a great many blanket chests, there are a great many faces, waiting.

And the morning comes. It does not come quickly. Sometimes it takes nearly thirty years. But it comes.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

Now the notes for the curious.

Our sources tonight are mainly two kinds. First, the chronicles written in Constantinople in the tenth century, especially the collection known as Theophanes Continuatus, which means the continuation of Theophanes, which tells the story of Theophilos and Theodora in some detail, and which you can read in English and Greek in the edition by Michael Featherstone and Juan Signes Codoner. Second, the Lives of the two saints: the Life of Theodora, translated by Martha Vinson in the Dumbarton Oaks collection Byzantine Defenders of Images, and the old Greek Life of Methodios. For the background, the best modern history is Leslie Brubaker and John Haldon's Byzantium in the Iconoclast Era. The declaration read that Sunday, the Synodikon of Orthodoxy, grew longer over the centuries, and nobody is quite sure which names were in it on the very first morning; Jean Gouillard's edition sorts out the layers. The service of the Sunday of Orthodoxy is in the Lenten Triodion.

What is history. Methodios's Sicilian birth, his journey to Rome and his return with a letter from Pope Paschal the First, his imprisonment under Michael the Second and his later years near the court of Theophilos. The death of Theophilos in January eight hundred and forty-two. Theodora's regency for her small son. The synod of early eight hundred and forty-three, the removal of John the Grammarian, Methodios as patriarch. The first celebration of the restoration of the icons on the first Sunday of Lent that year. The return of Theodore the Studite's body in eight hundred and forty-four. Methodios's death in eight hundred and forty-seven, Theodora's later years in a convent. The golden lions and the singing birds of the palace are described by a visiting ambassador in the next century, though Theophilos is usually given the credit.

What is the report of the old stories. The bride-show, the golden apple and Kassia's answer. The dolls of Theoktiste and Theodora, and Denderis the jester. The details of Methodios's prison. Theodora's account of her husband's last days, and the name wiped from the book on the altar, which is plainly a legend and told as one. The route of the procession, from Blachernae to the Great Church, is how the later accounts give it.

What we invented. Zoe, Stephanos the lamp-lighter, Grandmother Anna and her icon, the two rooms over the cooper's shop, the goat, Old Grumpy the lamp, the woman standing on her husband's foot, and nearly all the words anybody says. The hymn Zoe learns on the walk is the old troparion of the Sunday of Orthodoxy, but we do not know that it was sung on that very morning, and the words at the end are our own plain rendering of the Greek. We have left out a great deal of the politics, and almost all of the cruelty of the iconoclast years, which was real, and which falls hardest on painters and monks. And we have left out most of the long quarrel between Methodios and the Studites, which is complicated, and was settled after both sides had gone home to God.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Methodios and Saint Theodora for tonight. Saint Methodios is remembered on the fourteenth of June and Saint Theodora on the eleventh of February, in the Orthodox Church, and the Sunday of Orthodoxy is kept on the first Sunday of Great Lent by the Orthodox and by the Byzantine Catholic churches.

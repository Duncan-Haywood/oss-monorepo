Picture a woman sitting on the ground in the desert, a long bowshot away from her son, with her back to him, and her face in her hands.

She is not angry. She was angry once, a long time ago, and it has all run out of her, the way water runs out of a skin with a hole in it. She is only tired, and thirsty, and she has done everything a mother can do, and it has not been enough. So she has done the last thing that she can bear to do. She has put the boy down in the shade of a bush, and she has walked away, because she cannot watch.

And she says, out loud, to nobody, Let me not see the child die.

And then she begins to cry.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the book of Genesis, chapters sixteen and twenty-one, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the nights on the road, the thoughts of a tired woman, and the little things said at a well, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

We begin with a name, and the name is Hagar.

Now Hagar is not one of the people that children usually hear about first. If you grew up on stories of Abraham, you probably heard about the stars, and the tent, and the three visitors, and the ram caught in the thicket. You may not have heard very much about the Egyptian woman who lived in the next tent along. And that is a pity, because Genesis takes her very seriously indeed. She is given two whole scenes, and in each of them something happens that happens almost nowhere else in the Bible. We will come to that. First let us find out who she is.

Hagar is a servant in the household of Abraham and Sarah. Or rather, to use the names they have at the start of our story, Abram and Sarai. They have not yet been given their longer, grander names, and we shall call them Abraham and Sarah most of the time, because that is how we know them, though you must remember that in the first scene they are still Abram and Sarai.

Genesis tells us that Hagar is an Egyptian. It does not tell us how she came to be there. Perhaps she was given to the household as a gift. Genesis does tell us, earlier on, that when Abraham went down into Egypt in a famine, Pharaoh made him presents, of sheep and oxen and donkeys and servants, and some of the old readers have guessed that she came with them. That is a guess, and it is only a guess, and Genesis does not say. What it does say is that she is a slave. The word it uses means a female servant, belonging to a household, who does what she is told. Nobody asked her what she thought, and nobody asks her until an angel does.

So here is a young woman, far from the river she was born beside, in a camp of tents in the hill country of Canaan, with a master she did not choose and a mistress she cannot please. I should like you to hold on to that. Because when we say that this story is about God seeing her, you will want to know how much there was to see.

[pause]

Now to the trouble.

Abraham and Sarah have been promised a son. God told Abraham, years before, that he would be the father of a great nation, and that his descendants would be as many as the stars. And Abraham believed it. Genesis says so in so many words: he believed the Lord, and it was counted to him as righteousness. But the years have gone by. Abraham is seventy-five when he leaves his home to follow the promise, and by the time of our story he has been in Canaan for ten years. Sarah is growing old. There is no child. And there is a great deal of waiting, which is very hard on a household, in any age.

So Sarah has an idea. It is an idea that people had in those days, and Genesis does not say that it is a good one. She says to her husband: The Lord has kept me from bearing children. Go in to my servant. It may be that I shall obtain children by her.

That is a very hard sentence to read, and I do not think we are meant to read it quickly. In the world of that time, it was a known custom. A wife who could have no children might give her servant to her husband, and the child born would be counted as hers. But a custom is not the same as a kindness, and the woman it was done to had no say in it. Hagar is not asked. Genesis is quite plain about that. Sarah gives her, and Abraham listens to the voice of Sarah, and Hagar is, as the old translators put it, taken.

And the thing that comes of it is a child on the way, and a household in which two women now live in the same few tents, and neither of them is happy.

Genesis tells us what happens next with great economy. When Hagar sees that she has conceived, her mistress becomes small in her eyes. That is the phrase. Hagar looks at Sarah differently. She has done what Sarah could not do, and she knows it, and she lets it show.

Now we ought not to be too hard on either of them. I think that is the first lesson of this story. Sarah is a woman who has waited and wept for years, and who has done a risky thing out of desperation, and who now has to watch it come true in somebody else's body. And Hagar is a woman who has been used, and who has, at last, one thing in the whole world that is hers. Of course they quarrel. They were set up to.

Sarah goes to Abraham and says, in effect: This is your fault. May the Lord judge between you and me. And Abraham, who is not at his best in this scene, says: She is your servant. Do to her as you please.

And Sarah does. Genesis says she dealt harshly with her. We do not know how, and we need not guess. It is enough to know that it was harsh enough that a pregnant woman, alone, in a country she did not know, decided that the desert was better.

So Hagar runs away.

* * *

Let us go with her.

She goes south. Genesis says she is found by a spring of water in the wilderness, on the way to a place called Shur, and Shur is the direction of Egypt. So I think we can be fairly sure of where she was going. She was going home. She was walking back toward the river she was born beside, a young woman with a child inside her, and not very much else.

Now I want you to think about what that walk is like, because I think we are inclined to hurry over it. She has no cart. She has, perhaps, a skin of water and a little bread, and whatever she was carrying when she left. She is walking through country with very little shade and very few wells, and every one of those wells belongs to somebody, and she is a stranger and a runaway. If anyone finds her, she can be taken back. If nobody does, she can die out there. The old people of that country knew this about the desert. It does not hate you. It simply does not notice you.

And she stops at a spring. Of course she does. Everybody stops at a spring. She sits down by it, I imagine, and drinks, and puts her hands in the water, and perhaps, for a moment, feels the first ease she has had in months.

And then somebody speaks to her.

Genesis says it was the angel of the Lord. Now this is a phrase we will meet a number of times in the old books, and the old readers of both our traditions have thought a great deal about it. What we need to know tonight is simple. Someone from God has found her. It is the first time in Genesis that an angel appears to anyone, and it is to a servant, and a foreigner, and a runaway. We shall let that sit.

And the first thing the angel says is her name.

Hagar.

Not, Girl. Not, You there. Not, Whose servant are you. Hagar. The name that she has not heard, I should think, said gently for a very long time.

And then, a thing that I think is worth remembering: the angel does what a good friend does, and asks two questions. Hagar, servant of Sarai, where have you come from and where are you going?

Now, the angel knows. The angel of the Lord, who knows everything about her, does not say, I know where you have come from. The angel asks, and waits, and lets her say it. It is a question that somebody asks you when they want you to say the true thing out loud. And Hagar answers. She does not make a speech. She says, I am fleeing from my mistress Sarai.

That is all. And in those six words, I think, there is more truth than in many a long sermon.

* * *

Now the angel gives her an instruction, and it is a hard one. Return to your mistress, and submit to her.

I will not pretend this is easy to hear, and nor does Genesis pretend. The old readers have worried about it for as long as there have been readers. What we can say is what the text says: she is told to go back, and she is told why. She is not told to go back and be quiet. She is told to go back because of what she is carrying. And the angel goes on, and gives her a promise.

I will so greatly multiply your offspring that they cannot be numbered for multitude.

Stop on that a moment. It is the same promise that was made to Abraham. The same, nearly word for word. The great promise of Genesis, the one about the stars and the sand, has just been made to a runaway slave girl, sitting beside a spring, and it has been made on her own account. She is not a means to someone else's blessing. She is told that she is going to have a son of her own, and that her own descendants will fill the earth.

And then the angel tells her his name, before he is born. You shall call him Ishmael. Because the Lord has given heed to your affliction.

Ishmael. It is a name that means God hears. God hears. In the one name, the whole of the angel's message is folded up and put into her hands. Whenever she says her son's name, for the rest of her life, she will be saying: God has heard.

The angel says a few more things about the boy, and they are not all comfortable. He will be a wild man, Genesis says, with his hand against everyone and everyone's hand against him, and he shall live at odds with his kin. It is not a prophecy of a smooth life. But it is a prophecy of a life, and of a long one, and a free one, which is more than she had a moment ago.

And then Hagar does something that I think is the most remarkable thing in the chapter. She gives God a name.

Nobody else in the whole of Genesis does this. People name places, and they name children, and they name altars. But Hagar, the Egyptian servant, standing by a spring in the desert, says of the Lord who spoke to her: You are El Roi. You are a God of seeing. And then she adds a line that is a little hard to translate, and the old translators have wrestled with it, but it comes to something like: Have I really seen him who sees me? Have I stayed alive after looking at the one who looks at me?

I am quite sure that she was astonished. And I think she should have been. She has been invisible for most of her life. She has been a servant, which is, in many households, a kind of furniture. And now she knows that there is One who sees her, who saw her when nobody else did, who followed her into the desert to say her name.

And the well is named for it. Genesis calls the spring Beer-lahai-roi. The well of the Living One who sees me. And it says, rather charmingly, that it is still there, between Kadesh and Bered. Whoever wrote that line is telling us that if we go and look, we will find it. This is a story about a real well in real country, and it matters.

And Hagar goes back.

[pause]

Now I should like to tell you what the old text says next, because it is so short that you can miss it. Hagar bore Abram a son. And Abram called the name of his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael. Abram was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to Abram.

That is all that Genesis says. But notice what happened. The angel told her the name. And Abraham, who had not been in the desert, who had never heard the angel, gives the child the name that the angel gave. Somehow, the word got home. We are not told how. But I think we may guess that Hagar told him. That she came back from the desert, and stood in front of the man who had said, Do to her as you please, and said, God has spoken to me. And he listened.

We will say it was a small thing. I think it was a very great one.

* * *

Years go by.

We are given one more glimpse of the child, thirteen years on, when God makes a covenant with Abraham, and gives him the longer name, and gives Sarah hers. And Abraham, who is ninety-nine, laughs a little, and falls on his face, and says to God, Oh that Ishmael might live in your sight. And God says, As for Ishmael, I have heard you. I will bless him. I will make him fruitful and multiply him greatly. He shall be the father of twelve princes. And I will make him a great nation.

But the covenant, God says, shall be with Isaac. And Isaac is the son that Sarah will bear, a year from then, in her old age. That is a story we have told on other nights, and the laughter in it is very sweet. When the baby is born, they name him Isaac, which means, he laughs. And Sarah says, God has made laughter for me. Everyone who hears will laugh with me.

And she means it. I do not think she is anything but happy.

And so we come to the second scene. It is a feast day. Isaac has been weaned, which in those days happened later than it does now, perhaps at two or three years old, and Abraham gives a great feast to celebrate. Everyone is there. The tents are full of noise. There is music, and there is meat, and there are children running between the legs of the grown-ups. And somewhere in the middle of it is Ishmael, who is by now a good deal older than the baby, and who, Genesis says, is laughing.

Now there is a small puzzle here. The word that Genesis uses for what Ishmael was doing is a play on the word Isaac, the word for laughing. It can mean playing. It can mean laughing. It can mean, in some contexts, laughing at someone, mocking. The old translators have gone different ways. Some say he was playing with the child. Some say he was making fun. And we do not know. Genesis does not say. We will only say that Sarah looked at the two boys together, and what she saw frightened her.

Because she was thinking about who would be the heir.

* * *

Let us stay in that tent for a moment, because it is a hard scene, and the text does not hurry past it, and nor should we.

Sarah goes to Abraham. And she says: Cast out this slave woman with her son. For the son of this slave woman shall not be heir with my son Isaac.

Now we should notice a few things. The first is that she does not use Hagar's name. She says, this slave woman. She says, her son. She does not say, my husband's son. She is cutting every thread. And the second is that she does not say, I am afraid. She says, he shall not be heir. She states it as a matter of law and inheritance, as if it were a business problem. I think underneath it is the oldest fear there is: that there will not be enough, and that her child will go without.

And the text says that the thing was very displeasing to Abraham on account of his son.

On account of his son. I want you to hear that. Abraham is not indifferent. Whatever else he is, he is a father, and it is his son who is being sent away. He does not say, Of course. He is troubled. He does not sleep, I imagine, that night.

And God speaks to him. And this is the part where I think we have to be most careful, because it is a hard saying, and it is Scripture. God says to Abraham: Be not displeased because of the boy and because of your slave woman. Whatever Sarah says to you, do as she tells you, for it is through Isaac that your offspring shall be named.

And then he says the thing that is the reason Abraham can do it at all. And I will make a nation of the son of the slave woman also, because he is your offspring.

Because he is your offspring. God has not forgotten the boy. He is not being thrown away. He is being sent out under a promise, and God has said, in so many words, that he will look after him.

I do not think this makes the scene easy. It is not easy. I think it is meant to be hard, and meant to be felt. What it tells us is this. People do hard and unjust things to each other, even good people, even in households that God has chosen. And God does not leave the people on the other side of it. He will go on seeing them. He will go on hearing.

Abraham gets up early in the morning.

[pause]

There is a simplicity about what follows that I find very moving. Genesis does not give us a speech. It says: So Abraham rose early in the morning, and took bread and a skin of water, and gave it to Hagar, putting it on her shoulder, along with the child, and sent her away.

Bread and a skin of water. Look at how little. A man who owns flocks and herds, and servants by the hundred, and silver and gold, and he gives her a loaf and a skin. I do not think this is meanness, exactly. I think it is a man who has been told to send her away and has been told that God will care for her, and who is trusting God to do the rest. But it is a great deal of trust, and it is a very small amount of bread. I should like to say a word for Abraham here. He believed that God would look after them. And he was right. But it must have felt, as he stood in the doorway of his tent in the cold morning, like the longest walk of his life, to watch the two of them go.

Also, a small difficulty. Genesis says that he put the child on her shoulder. Now by our reckoning, Ishmael is by this time a good deal more than a baby. If we add up the years, as the text itself does, he is a young person, perhaps sixteen. It is hard to see a mother carrying a boy that age on her shoulder. The old readers have noticed this, and have said different things about it. Some say the line is about the bread and the skin, and the child is simply mentioned along with them. Some say the text is not to be pressed so hard. We do not know. We will say only that the boy is young enough to be her child, and to need her, and that she is the one who leads the way.

And so they go out of the camp, through the gap in the tents, into the light.

* * *

They wander in the wilderness of Beersheba.

Beersheba is a name that you will meet again in this book, because it is a place of wells. Abraham will dig one there. Isaac will dig it again. It is a name that means, well of the oath, or well of seven. But on this morning, it is not yet a place where anybody lives. It is empty country, scrub and stone and thorn, and the light is very white.

I think that they walk for a day, and then another. I do not know. Genesis does not tell us how long. It says only that she wandered, and that the water in the skin ran out.

And you will have noticed that she does not turn back. She is a woman who has been here once before, who ran away by the spring on the road to Shur, and who was told by an angel to return. And she did return. And now she has been sent out, not by her own choice, and she cannot return, because there is nothing to return to.

She is on her own with her son, and the water is gone.

Now, I should like to say a word about what thirst is like, because you may never have felt it, and we should not skip it. It begins as a small thing. A dryness. You think, I should like some water. Then the dryness spreads to your lips, and your tongue, and it grows heavy in your mouth. You stop being able to think of anything else. The light hurts. Your feet feel a long way off. And if you are a mother, and your son is beside you, and you can see that he is going through the same thing, only faster, because he is younger, you stop thinking about yourself altogether.

The boy lies down. He cannot go on. And Hagar, who has done everything she can, who has walked and carried and sung to him, and tried, I should think, to remember every story she knew about water, looks about her for shade.

There is a bush. A scrubby bush, one of the low desert shrubs, not much taller than a man. She puts the boy down under it.

And then she walks away.

She goes, Genesis says, about the distance of a bowshot, and she sits down opposite him. And she says, Let me not look on the death of the child.

Do you see why she goes? She cannot bear to watch. Not because she does not love him. It is the opposite. She is a mother, and it is her duty to sit with her child, and she cannot. She is far enough away that she cannot hear him, and near enough that she could run to him in a moment. It is the most painful distance in the world.

And she sits there, and she lifts up her voice, and she weeps.

[pause]

Now here is the part of the story that I should like you to listen to very carefully, because I think it is the heart of it. It is a small line, and it is easy to miss.

Genesis says: And God heard the voice of the boy.

Not the voice of the mother. The voice of the boy.

Hagar is the one who is weeping. She is the one who has spoken. But the text says that God heard the boy. And I think that is because it is written, as you will remember, in his name. Ishmael. God hears. The angel told his mother, years ago, by a spring, what the child would be called. And now, in the same way, the name comes true. The boy does not have to say anything. His name says it for him. God hears.

And the angel of God calls to Hagar from heaven.

I want you to notice that it is not the desert that speaks. The angel comes from above. And the first thing the angel says, once again, is her name.

What troubles you, Hagar?

What troubles you. Again he asks. He knows perfectly well what troubles her. But he asks, because it is the way that God speaks to people who are in despair. He asks, and he waits, and he lets them say it.

Fear not, the angel says. God has heard the voice of the boy where he is.

Where he is. Not where he ought to be. Not in the safe house in the camp. Under a bush, in the heat, at the end of his strength. God has heard him there.

And then the angel gives her three things to do, and I think they are three very good things.

Arise.

Lift up the boy.

Hold him fast with your hand.

Get up. Pick him up. Hold him. She has been sitting, a bowshot away, with her face in her hands. She is told to stand. She is told to go back, and put her arms around her son, and not let go. It is a very ordinary instruction, and it is exactly what she wants to do, and has been afraid to.

And the angel says again the thing that was said to Abraham. I will make him a great nation.

And then, a line I love, because it is so quiet. Genesis says: Then God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water.

Notice what it does not say. It does not say that God made a well. It does not say that a spring burst from the sand. It says God opened her eyes, and she saw it. It was already there. It might have been there all the time. It might have been a little way off, behind a stand of thorn, a few steps from where she was sitting. She had been so busy weeping that she did not see it.

I do not want to make too much of this. It is a miracle, and it is God's, and Genesis tells it in a very plain way. But I think there is a lesson in the plainness. Sometimes, when we are in despair, the help is nearer than we think. Not because we are foolish, but because our eyes are full of tears. And sometimes what we need is not for something to be made, but for our eyes to be opened.

She goes to the well. She fills the skin. She gives the boy a drink.

I would give a great deal to have seen his face.

Let us slow down now.

God was with the boy, Genesis says. He grew up. He lived in the wilderness. And he became an expert with the bow.

An archer. The boy who nearly died of thirst under a bush grows up to be a man who can feed himself in the desert, and feed others, and who can take care of his own. He lives in the wilderness of Paran, which is south and east of where they were. And his mother does for him one last thing that a mother does. She finds him a wife. And, Genesis says, she takes her from the land of Egypt.

From Egypt. The land that she was born in, and that she was walking toward when the angel first found her. So she does not get to go back herself. But she sends, I should think, a letter, or a message, or whatever one sent in those days, and a young woman comes from the river to the desert, and marries her son. And a little of home is brought to her.

We are told, later in Genesis, in chapter twenty-five, the names of Ishmael's twelve sons, the twelve princes God promised. And we are told that he lived a hundred and thirty-seven years. And that when Abraham died, it was Isaac and Ishmael together who buried him, in the cave at Machpelah. Genesis says it very simply: His sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him.

His sons. Both of them. In the end, the two boys who were set against each other at a feast stand side by side at their father's grave. It does not say how they came to be there, or what they said to each other. But I think it is a good place to leave them.

[pause]

Now, before the night gets any later, let us stop and be quiet for a moment.

I have been telling you a story about a woman who ran away, twice. Once she was alone, and once she was with her child. And both times, she was found. And what I should like you to take to bed with you is not a clever idea, but a very simple one.

There are people who feel that nobody sees them. People who do the work of a house and are not thanked. People who are sent away from places where they thought they belonged. People who have done their best for a child and it has not been enough. If that is you, tonight, then I should like you to think of Hagar. Because the Bible does not leave her unseen. The Lord has a name for himself, in her mouth. He is the God who sees.

And if you are the other sort, the sort who has the house and the household and the place at the table, and who has, sometimes, without quite meaning to, pushed someone out of it, then I think you may want to think of Sarah and Abraham, and not too harshly. They were afraid. They did a hard thing. And God did not abandon the person they hurt, and did not abandon them. He is large enough to hold both. I think most of us need that to be true.

The sun has gone down on the wilderness of Beersheba. The white light has turned gold and then grey, and the thorn bushes have gone dark, and there is a smell of cool water in the air. At the edge of the well, a woman has laid out a cloak on the sand, and a boy is lying on it, with a full belly, fast asleep. She is sitting beside him with her hand on his hair. She is looking at the first stars, which are very large and very close, as they are in the desert. And she is thinking, perhaps, of the promise she was given by a spring, years ago, about how many there would be.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is told from the book of Genesis, chapter sixteen, and chapter twenty-one, verses eight to twenty-one, with short glances at chapter seventeen and chapter twenty-five. It is Scripture for both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches, and we told it straight.

What is Scripture: Hagar the Egyptian, servant of Sarai; Sarai's giving her to Abram; the conception and the change between the two women; Sarai's harsh dealing and Hagar's flight; the angel of the Lord at the spring on the way to Shur; the two questions; the command to return; the promise of offspring too many to count; the name Ishmael and its meaning; the words about his life; Hagar's naming of the Lord as the God who sees; the well called Beer-lahai-roi, between Kadesh and Bered; the birth of Ishmael when Abram was eighty-six; the promise about Ishmael in chapter seventeen; the feast at the weaning of Isaac; Sarah's demand; Abraham's distress and God's word to him; the bread and the skin of water; the wandering in the wilderness of Beersheba; the child under the bush; the bowshot; the weeping; God hearing the boy; the angel's call and its three commands; the eyes opened and the well; God with the boy; the archer in the wilderness of Paran; the wife from Egypt; and, in chapter twenty-five, the sons of Ishmael and the burial of Abraham by both his sons.

What we flagged as not in the text: Genesis does not say how Hagar came to the household, and the guess that she was one of Pharaoh's gifts is only a guess. It does not say what Sarai's harshness was. It does not say what Ishmael was doing at the feast, and the word can mean playing or mocking, so we offered both. It does not say how long they wandered, or where exactly the well was. The picture of an older boy carried on the shoulder is a real difficulty in the text, and we named it and did not settle it. And it does not say how the news of the angel came to Abraham, so our remark that Hagar must have told him is a guess.

What we invented: the walk to the spring and the thoughts of Hagar by the water, the talk between the two women, the morning at the door of the tent, the feeling of thirst, and the evening at the well. None of it changes what happens or what it means.

What is tradition: there are many later stories about what became of Hagar and Ishmael, in the Jewish, Christian and Muslim traditions, and they do not all agree. We have left them out. The letters of Paul use Hagar as a figure, in a different way, and that is a story for another night.

For further reading, the book of Genesis itself is the best place, and the verses we have told are short enough to read aloud in a few minutes.

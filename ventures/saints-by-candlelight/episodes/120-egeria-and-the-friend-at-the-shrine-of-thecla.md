It is a bright morning in the south of what is now Turkey, and a woman in a dusty travelling cloak is walking up a low hill.

She has been travelling for a very long time. Years, in fact. Her sandals have been resoled more times than she can count. Behind her, a mule is carrying everything she owns in the world, which is not much, and a great many notes, which are.

At the top of the hill there is a wall, a big, plain, serious wall, and behind the wall there is a church, and around the church there are little houses, dozens and dozens of them, scattered over the hillside like sheep.

And out of one of those little houses comes another woman, walking fast, and then walking faster, and then, which is not at all what you expect from a respectable lady in charge of a great many other respectable ladies, running.

The woman on the path stops. She puts her hand over her mouth. She knows that face.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Tonight's story begins, as some of the best stories do, with a library.

In the year eighteen eighty-four, an Italian scholar called Gian Francesco Gamurrini was looking through old books in a library in the town of Arezzo, in Tuscany. Arezzo is a lovely old hill town, all stone and terracotta, and the library he was in belonged to a charitable brotherhood that had been collecting books for a very long time. And among those books Gamurrini found a manuscript, a little over eight hundred years old, copied, most scholars now think, at the great monastery of Monte Cassino. It contained some writings of Saint Hilary of Poitiers, which were interesting. And after them, it contained something nobody alive had read.

It was a letter. A very long letter, in Latin, written by a woman, to a group of other women back home. The beginning was missing. The end was missing. Several pages in the middle were missing. But what was left was a travel diary, of a journey round the holy places of the Bible, made by a Christian woman some time near the end of the fourth century, about three hundred and eighty years after the birth of Christ.

Nobody knew who she was. The pages with her name on them, if there ever were any, were gone.

And then somebody remembered another letter. It was written in the seventh century, about three hundred years after the journey, by a monk called Valerius, who lived in the mountains of the far north-west of Spain, in a region called Bierzo. Valerius had written to his fellow monks to encourage them in their hard life. And to do it, he had told them about a woman. A blessed woman, he said, who came from the farthest shore of the western ocean, and who crossed the whole known world to see the holy places, and who climbed mountains that would make strong men sit down. And he gave her name. In the different copies of his letter it is spelled in different ways, but most scholars now think it was Egeria.

So that is who our pilgrim is. Probably. Egeria, from the far west of the Roman world, from the north-west corner of Spain, perhaps, or the south of France. Nobody is quite sure.

What we can say about her comes almost entirely from the letter itself. And the letter tells us a great deal, because Egeria is a wonderful writer to travel with.

She is not a polished writer. Her Latin is the Latin people actually spoke, not the Latin of the schoolroom, which is why scholars of language love her. She repeats herself. She says "as I said above" a lot. She uses the same few words over and over. When she likes something, it is very beautiful. When something is big, it is very big. When she is pleased with a bishop, he is a truly holy man. She is not trying to be clever. She is trying to tell her sisters back home exactly what it was like.

And she is curious. Oh, she is curious. At one point, near the River Jordan, she sees a pleasant valley and asks what it is called, and then she writes, and I am giving you her own words as plainly as I can, "because I am rather curious, I began to ask." I love her for that. If there is one sentence that sums up Egeria, it is that one. Because I am rather curious, I began to ask.

She had a habit, wherever she went, which she describes several times. When she and her party arrived at a holy place, a place where something in the Bible had happened, first they said a prayer. Then they read the passage from the Bible about that place, out loud, right there. Then they sang a psalm that fitted. And then they prayed again. At the burning bush. On the top of Mount Sinai. At the place where Moses saw the promised land. Prayer, reading, psalm, prayer. Again and again and again.

And she wrote it all down, for the women at home, whom she calls her ladies, her venerable sisters, and once, very tenderly, her light. We do not know exactly who they were. Probably a community of women who had given their lives to God, as Egeria had, in some form or other. Some people think she was a nun. Some people think she was a wealthy widow, or a lady of a good family living a consecrated life at home. She clearly had money, or friends with money, because travelling for years cost a great deal. And she clearly had good introductions, because bishops met her, monks guided her, and once, in the desert near Egypt, Roman soldiers escorted her from fort to fort, which is not something that happens to everybody.

But mostly what she had was this. She wanted to see. She wanted to stand in the places, and read the words in the places, and pray. And then she wanted to tell the people she loved all about it.

* * *

By the time of our story, Egeria had been living in Jerusalem for three years.

Three whole years. She says so herself. And Jerusalem, in the three hundred and eighties, was a very good place to be a curious pilgrim.

The great churches that the emperor Constantine had built over the place of the Crucifixion and the tomb of Christ were only about fifty years old. Pilgrims were coming from everywhere. From Egypt and Syria, from Asia Minor and Greece, from Italy and Gaul and Spain. And so the church in Jerusalem had to manage something that we still struggle with today. Everybody was speaking a different language.

Egeria describes it, and it is one of my favourite things in her whole letter. The bishop, she says, always preached in Greek. And beside him there stood a priest, who translated what he said into Syriac, so the people from the east could understand. And for the people who spoke only Latin, like Egeria, there were brothers and sisters in the crowd who knew Greek and Latin both, and who explained it to them quietly, so that, as she puts it, nobody missed out.

Imagine it. A great stone church, full of lamps, full of people. A bishop speaking. A priest speaking after him. And all around the edges, a soft murmur, like water, of people leaning over to whisper in their neighbours' ears. So, what he's saying is.

I think that is a rather beautiful picture of the Church. Everybody helping everybody else to hear.

Now, Egeria tells us that she met her friend Marthana in Jerusalem. Marthana had come there to pray, as so many people did. Egeria does not tell us where or how they met. So I am going to make it up, and I am telling you now that I am making it up, and you can imagine it some other way if you prefer.

Let us say it was on Good Friday.

On Good Friday in Jerusalem, Egeria tells us, the bishop sat on a chair on the hill of Calvary, with a table in front of him covered with a linen cloth. And the deacons stood around the table. And a silver-gilt box was brought, and opened, and out of it came what the church of Jerusalem kept as the wood of the Cross, with its inscription. And the people came forward, one by one, all morning, to bow down, and to touch the wood with their foreheads and their eyes, and to kiss it.

And the deacons stood guard the whole time. Egeria explains why, and I promise you I am not making this up. Some years before, she says, somebody had come up to kiss the wood, and had bitten off a piece of it, and run away with it in his mouth. And so now the deacons watch. Very carefully. Every single person.

So let us imagine that Egeria was standing in that long, slow line, on a grey spring morning, with her hands folded and her head bowed, trying very hard to be recollected and holy and not to look at anybody. And let us imagine that the woman in front of her, a tall woman from Asia Minor with a calm face and a plain dark veil, turned round as the line shuffled forward, and caught her eye, and nodded very slightly towards the deacons, who were standing round the table with their arms folded, looking at everybody's teeth.

And let us imagine that Egeria, who had been very solemn all morning, very nearly laughed out loud. And had to bite her lip. And did not dare look at the tall woman again until it was all over, and they were outside, in the cold air, and the tall woman said, quietly, in Greek, "I don't think they trust us."

And Egeria said, in her careful, school-taught Greek, "I don't think they trust anybody."

And that was the beginning of a friendship.

* * *

Marthana, Egeria tells us, was a deaconess.

In the churches of the East at that time, there were women called deaconesses. They helped at the baptism of women, and they cared for the women of the Christian community, visiting the sick, teaching, guiding, keeping order. And Marthana, Egeria says, was in charge of communities of women who had given their lives to God. Egeria uses two words for them. One is the plain one, virgins, meaning women who had chosen not to marry so as to belong entirely to Christ. The other is a long Greek word, apotactites, which means, roughly, people who have said goodbye. People who have said goodbye to property, to the ordinary life, to everything that was not God.

And Egeria tells us one more thing about Marthana. She says that her way of life was praised by everyone in the East. Everyone. That is quite something, from a woman who had met a great many holy people in three years, and who did not hand out praise she did not mean.

So let us imagine that, during the weeks Marthana was in Jerusalem, the two women saw a good deal of each other. That they went to the services together, and stood at the back, and Marthana whispered translations of the Greek when the Latin helpers were busy elsewhere. That they walked out to the Mount of Olives together, and sat on a wall in the sun, and talked about the places they came from.

Let us imagine Marthana telling Egeria about where she lived. A hill near the sea, in the south of Asia Minor, just outside a city called Seleucia. A shrine, very old and very famous, built over the place where Saint Thecla had lived and prayed at the end of her life. Little houses all over the hillside, for the men and women who had come to live near the shrine and pray. Pilgrims arriving every day. And the sea, a little way off, blue as anything.

"You should come," Marthana might have said.

And Egeria might have said, "Seleucia is a very long way from anywhere I am going."

And Marthana might have smiled, and said, "Everywhere is a long way from somewhere."

And then the weeks were over, and Marthana went home, back over the sea, back to her hill. And the two women said goodbye, in the way people said goodbye in those days, when the person going away might as well be going to the moon. No letters you could count on. No way of knowing. Probably for ever.

Egeria stayed. She went on looking, and reading, and praying, and writing it all down.

And the years went by.

* * *

At the end of three years, Egeria decided it was time to go home.

But not, of course, directly. Egeria did nothing directly. If there was something worth seeing within a few weeks of the road, Egeria was going to see it.

First, she decided, she would go to Mesopotamia. She had heard that there were holy monks there, living in the desert, monks so holy that people talked about them all over the East. And there was the city of Edessa, where, so the tradition went, the King Abgar had once written a letter to Christ himself, and received an answer. And there was the town of Harran, where Abraham had lived with his family before God called him to go to a land he did not know.

So she went. She went north from Jerusalem to Antioch, the great city of Syria, and then east, across the river Euphrates, which she describes as huge and terrifying and flowing as fast as the Rhone, and I would love to know how she knew what the Rhone was like, because that is one of the little clues that makes people wonder whether she came from the south of France.

In Edessa the bishop showed her everything, very kindly. He showed her the city gate through which, the story said, the messenger had brought the letter from Christ. He told her stories of how the letter had protected the city. And he gave her copies of the letters of Abgar and of Christ, even though, she admits, she already had copies at home. But these ones, she says, seemed longer. Which is so exactly what a curious person would notice that I can hear her saying it.

Then she went on to Harran, and she was lucky, because she arrived the day before the feast of a local martyr, and all the holy monks had come in from the desert for it. Which, for Egeria, was like arriving in a town on the one day of the year when all the famous people are there at once. She met them. She talked with them. The bishop of Harran, who had been a monk himself, took her round. A few miles out of the town she was shown a well, and told that this was the well where Jacob had rolled the stone away and watered the flocks of Rachel, which you can read about in the book of Genesis, chapter twenty-nine. And she stood there, and they read the passage, and sang a psalm, and prayed.

She asked whether she might go on to Ur of the Chaldees, where Abraham came from. And the bishop told her, very politely, that it was ten days further on, deep inside the empire of the Persians, and that the Romans could not go there. So that was that.

And then she turned for home.

* * *

She went back to Antioch, and rested there for a week, and got ready for the road. Then she went west, along the coast, and over the hills, and into the province of Cilicia, and came to the city of Tarsus.

Tarsus, as you may know if you have been listening to the episodes about Saint Paul, was where Paul was born. And it is at Tarsus that Egeria tells her sisters about her plan.

From Tarsus, she says, it is three staging posts to the shrine of Saint Thecla. Three days' travel. In the wrong direction, more or less, but only three days. And it would be a very pleasant thing, she says, to go there, especially since it is so close.

I would like you to notice the word close. Three days on foot or on a mule, through country famous for bandits, to a place that was not on her way, and Egeria calls it close. That is a pilgrim talking. After three years of crossing deserts and climbing mountains, three days is a stroll to the shops.

And so she set out.

The road went along the coast. Egeria names the places. First a city by the sea called Pompeiopolis, still in Cilicia. Then she crossed into the next province, which was called Isauria, and stayed the night at a town called Corycus. And on the third day she came to Seleucia.

Now, Isauria. I need to tell you about the Isaurians, because Egeria does, and because they explain the wall.

The Isaurians were the people of the mountains just inland from that coast. And they had a reputation. A Roman historian of that very time, a soldier called Ammianus Marcellinus, writes about them at some length, and the gist of it is that they came down out of the mountains whenever they felt like it, and robbed everybody, and went back up again, and that the Roman army found this extremely difficult to do anything about. Egeria herself puts it with admirable simplicity. The Isaurians, she says, are very bad, and they often rob.

So we can imagine that the three days from Tarsus were not entirely relaxing. Let us imagine Egeria on her mule, looking up at the mountains rather more than usual. Let us imagine the mule, which the old letter does not mention, but which I am sure existed, because somebody had to carry the notes, plodding along with the patience of all mules everywhere, quite unconcerned about the Isaurians, and mainly interested in the grass at the side of the road.

And let us imagine Egeria thinking, as she rode, about her friend.

She did not know whether Marthana would be there. She did not know whether Marthana was alive. It had been a long time. People travelled. People fell ill. People were sent to look after other communities in other places. Egeria had no way of knowing. All she knew was that her friend had once said, on a wall on the Mount of Olives, you should come. And here she was. Coming.

* * *

When Egeria arrived at Seleucia, the first thing she did was go and see the bishop.

This was always the first thing she did, wherever she went, and it was good manners. A pilgrim arriving in a city went to pay her respects to the bishop, who was the father of the church there. And the bishop of Seleucia, Egeria says, was a truly holy man, who had been a monk. That phrase comes up again and again in her letter. A truly holy man, who had been a monk. It seems to have been one of the things she noticed most about the bishops she liked, that they had first spent years praying in some cell or desert before anybody made them do paperwork.

Then she went out to the shrine.

It was outside the city, she says, about a mile and a half away, on a low hill. So let us walk there with her. Out of the city gate. Along a road through a green valley, with a river nearby. The sea somewhere off to the left, catching the light. The road rising gently. And then the hill, and on the hill, the wall.

It was a big wall. Egeria noticed it at once and explained it to her sisters straight away. It had been built, she said, to protect the church from the Isaurians, so that they would not try to break in and rob the monastery. Which tells us something rather sad and rather practical about the shrine of Saint Thecla. It was holy. It was beautiful. And it was rich enough, with all the gifts of the pilgrims, to be worth robbing. And so the people who lived there had built a wall, and got on with their prayers behind it.

Inside the wall was the church, which Egeria calls very beautiful. And all round the church, all over the hill, were cells. Little houses, little huts, little rooms, where men and women lived who had come to give their lives to God near the place where Saint Thecla had prayed. Countless, Egeria says. Countless cells, of men and of women.

And somewhere among them, Egeria writes, I found a very dear friend of mine.

That is her phrase. A very dear friend. Marthana.

And then she writes the sentence that I think is the heart of this whole story. She writes to her sisters, and I am giving it to you as plainly as I can,

"Could I ever describe to you how great was her joy, and mine, when she saw me?"

That is all. She does not describe it. She asks whether she could. And she leaves it to her sisters to imagine.

So let us imagine it, gently.

Let us imagine that someone ran ahead. One of the younger women, perhaps, who had seen a party of pilgrims coming up the path, and heard that among them was a lady from the far west, from the very end of the world, a Latin lady, who was asking for the deaconess Marthana by name. And let us imagine that this young woman ran to Marthana's little house and knocked, and Marthana came to the door with her sleeves rolled up and flour on her hands, because even the deaconess in charge of a great many respectable women sometimes had to help with the bread.

And let us imagine that the young woman said, "There's a lady from the west at the gate. She's asking for you. She says she knew you in Jerusalem."

And Marthana stood very still. And then she wiped her hands on her apron, not very well. And then she walked out of the door, and down between the cells, and then walked faster, and then, which was not at all what anybody on that hill expected from the deaconess Marthana, she ran.

And Egeria, at the gate, standing beside her mule, saw her coming. And put her hand over her mouth.

And then they were holding each other, there in the gateway of the shrine, two women, one with flour on her hands and one with three years of road on her cloak, laughing and crying at the same time, while the mule ate the grass by the wall and the young woman who had run ahead stood a little way off, not sure where to look, and smiling.

"You came," said Marthana.

"You told me to," said Egeria.

"I didn't think you'd listen."

"I always listen," said Egeria. "I'm rather curious."

* * *

Egeria tells us what they did next, and it is exactly what you would expect from her.

They went into the church. And they prayed at the martyrium, which is what she calls the shrine of the saint. And then, she says, the whole of the Acts of Saint Thecla was read. The whole thing. There, in the place itself.

You remember her habit. Prayer, reading, psalm, prayer. At the burning bush, the book of Exodus. At Harran, the book of Genesis. And here, at the shrine of Thecla, the story of Thecla.

Now. The Acts of Thecla is not the Bible. I want to be clear about that, as Egeria's listeners in the fourth century would have been. It is an old story, written in the second century, by a priest in Asia Minor who, as the North African writer Tertullian tells us, wrote it out of love for Saint Paul, and was removed from his office for doing it. If you listened to our episode about Thecla, you will remember all that.

But it was the story that was loved at that shrine. It was the story the pilgrims came to hear. And so, let us imagine, as the light began to slant through the windows of the church, someone stood up with a book and began to read it, in Greek, while Marthana stood beside Egeria and whispered it in Latin, the way the helpers had done for her in Jerusalem.

The story of a girl in the town of Iconium, who sat at a window for three days and nights, listening to Saint Paul preaching in the house next door. Who left her mother and the young man she was meant to marry, to follow Christ. Who was brought before the governor, and was not afraid. Who was sent to the fire, and a great storm came, and put the fire out. Who was sent to the wild animals in the arena at Antioch, and a lioness lay down at her feet and would not let the other animals near her. Who was saved again and again, by God and not by any power of her own. Who cut her hair short and put on a traveller's cloak and went to find Saint Paul. And who, at the end, the story says, came to Seleucia, and taught many people the word of God, and after a good life fell asleep, with a beautiful sleep.

And here they were. In Seleucia. On the very hill.

Let us imagine that, as the reader came to the end, Egeria looked at the place where the story said Thecla's long life had ended, and thought of all the women behind her in the church, in their plain veils, who had said goodbye to everything to be here. Women from Isauria and Cilicia and Cappadocia and Syria. Women who were each, in their own way, still sitting at that window, listening.

And she thought of her own sisters, at the far end of the world, and how she would tell them about this.

And then they sang a psalm. And then they prayed.

And then, Egeria tells us, they gave thanks to God. And then they went to supper.

She does not mention the supper. I am fairly sure there was one.

* * *

Egeria stayed two days on Thecla's hill.

Two days. That is all. After three days to get there, and three days to get back. If you ever think you have wasted a journey on a short stay, remember Egeria, who thought six days on the road for two days with a friend and a saint was obviously a bargain.

She spent them, she says, seeing the holy monks and the apotactites, the men and the women. Going from cell to cell. Meeting people. Hearing their stories. Being, I have no doubt, rather curious.

So let us imagine her, the next morning, being taken round by Marthana. Up and down the little paths between the cells. Stopping at doorways. Here an old man who had been a soldier, and had come to Thecla's hill to pray away the rest of his life. Here two sisters from a town in Cappadocia, who had come together and never gone back. Here a woman who copied books, and was halfway through a psalter, and let Egeria look at it. Here a man who kept bees on the slope above the wall, and gave Egeria a little honey in a fig leaf, and told her the Isaurians had never once tried to steal the bees, because the Isaurians were not stupid.

Let us imagine that at some point in the afternoon the two women found a place on the hillside, in the shade, where they could see down the valley to the sea, and sat down. And talked.

About the three years.

"Tell me everything," said Marthana. "Where have you been?"

And Egeria told her. Sinai. The long, hard climb, which was not up a slope, she said, but straight up, as if up a wall, and the little church on the top, and the monks who gave them fruit from their gardens. The burning bush, which was still growing, the monks said, behind the church at the bottom of the mountain. The land of Goshen, and the Roman soldiers who escorted them across the desert. Mount Nebo, where Moses saw the promised land. Edessa and its letters. Harran and its well.

"And you wrote it all down," said Marthana.

"Of course I wrote it all down."

"For your sisters."

"For my sisters," said Egeria. And then she was quiet for a moment. "I don't know if I'll ever get home to them, Marthana. It's such a long way. And I keep finding things on the way."

Marthana looked at her, and smiled. "That's not a bad thing to keep finding."

"No," said Egeria. "But it makes for a very long letter."

And then, let us imagine, Egeria asked the question she had been wanting to ask since Jerusalem. "What is it like," she said, "to live here? All the time? Right next to it?"

And Marthana thought about it for a long while, looking down at the sea.

"Most of the time," she said at last, "it's very ordinary. There's bread to make. There are quarrels to settle. Somebody's roof leaks. Somebody's sick. The pilgrims come, and they need somewhere to sleep, and somebody has to find them blankets. Thecla went to the fire and the lions. I mostly go to the storeroom."

Egeria laughed.

"But then," said Marthana, "every so often, somebody comes up that path who's walked for months to get here. And they go into the church. And they read the story. And I watch their faces. And I remember why I came."

They sat for a while without talking. Down in the valley, a bell rang for the evening prayer.

"Come on," said Marthana, getting up and brushing herself down. "You'll want to hear the psalms here. We sing them slower than in Jerusalem."

"Is that better or worse?" said Egeria.

"It's longer," said Marthana. "You can decide."

* * *

Egeria does not mention another visitor to that hill. But we know about him from his own writings, and I think she would have liked to know.

A few years before Egeria came, a great bishop and theologian called Gregory of Nazianzus, one of the most famous preachers of the whole Eastern Church, came to Seleucia worn out. He had lost his father and his mother and his sister and his brother. He was tired of quarrels. He wanted to be quiet. And so he came, as he tells us himself in a long poem about his own life, to the shrine of the virgin Thecla, and stayed there for some years, praying and resting, before he was called away to Constantinople to preach in the capital, which he did magnificently, and found very stressful.

So it is quite possible that some of the people Egeria met on that hillside had known Saint Gregory. Had seen him walking up and down the same paths. Had heard him sing the same psalms, perhaps, slower than in Jerusalem. Egeria does not say. But I like to think of it. A tired bishop and a curious pilgrim, a few years apart, on the same quiet hill, both of them finding there exactly what they had come for.

* * *

On the third morning, Egeria left.

There is no getting round goodbyes, in the end. Not in the fourth century, and not now.

Let us imagine that Marthana walked with her down to the gate in the big wall. That the mule was loaded, and grumbling about it. That the young woman who had run ahead on the first day came too, and brought a parcel of bread and figs for the road, and a little pot of the beekeeper's honey.

And let us imagine the two friends standing in the gateway, where they had met two days before.

"You'll write?" said Marthana.

"I'll try," said Egeria. "You know what letters are like."

"I know," said Marthana. And then she said, "Will you put me in your letter? The one to your sisters?"

"You're already in it," said Egeria. "You're the best part."

And then they embraced again, and Egeria got on her mule, and went down the hill, and along the green valley, and back towards the city. And at the bend in the road she turned round, and Marthana was still standing in the gateway. And she waved. And Marthana waved. And then the road turned, and the hill was behind her.

And Egeria tells us, quite simply, that she went back to Tarsus, and stayed there three days, and then set out again, on the road for home.

[pause]

What happened next, Egeria tells us in the last pages we have.

From Tarsus she went north, over the Taurus mountains, the great wall of mountains that runs along the south of Asia Minor. Through Cappadocia, and Galatia, and Bithynia, names that you may remember from the letters of Saint Peter and Saint Paul. Down to the town of Chalcedon, on the water opposite Constantinople, where she prayed at the shrine of the martyr Saint Euphemia, which she says was very famous, and which she had known about, she says, for a long time.

And then she crossed the water, and came to Constantinople, the great new capital of the empire. And she went round its churches, and its shrines of the apostles and the martyrs, and gave thanks to God, who had brought her safely so far.

And there, in Constantinople, she sat down and wrote to her sisters.

And the letter ends, the part that we have, with a plan. Of course it ends with a plan.

I want you to know, my ladies, my light, she writes, more or less, that I am planning to go, in the name of Christ our God, to Asia, to Ephesus, to pray at the shrine of the holy and blessed apostle John. And if after that I am still alive, and able to go and see other places, I will either tell you about them myself, face to face, if God allows it, or, if I decide to do anything else, I will write to you about it. And meanwhile, she says, please remember me.

Please remember me.

And that is where the letter stops.

We do not know if she went to Ephesus. We do not know if she ever got home, to the far shore of the western ocean, to the women she called her light. We do not know when she died, or where. All we have is the letter, and three hundred years later a monk in the mountains of Spain who had read it, a fuller copy than ours, and who loved her for it, and called her blessed. She has never been canonised as a saint, in East or West, and so we will not call her one. But she was remembered. Which is what she asked for.

And Marthana? We know nothing more about Marthana at all. Only what Egeria wrote. A deaconess, in charge of the women who had said goodbye to everything, on a hill near Seleucia. Praised by everyone in the East. A very dear friend. Whose joy, when she saw Egeria coming up the hill, was more than words could say.

That is all we have of her. But I think, if you had to be remembered for only one thing, being someone's dearest friend is not a bad thing to be remembered for.

And the shrine?

The shrine of Saint Thecla went on for a long time. About a hundred years after Egeria's visit, the emperor Zeno, who believed Saint Thecla had helped him win back his throne, built a great basilica there, one of the largest churches in that part of the world. A Church historian called Evagrius tells us about it. Pilgrims came for centuries more. Then, slowly, the world changed, and the pilgrims stopped coming, and the cells on the hillside fell down, and the grass came back.

But the place is still there. If you go to the town of Silifke, on the south coast of Turkey, which was once Seleucia, and walk out of town, you will find a low hill, with the ruins of Zeno's great church on it, a tall piece of the apse still standing against the sky. And under the hill, down some steps, there is a cave church, cool and quiet, where people say Thecla prayed. You can still go down into it.

And Saint Thecla is still honoured in both East and West. In the Orthodox Church her feast is on the twenty-fourth of September, and they call her Protomartyr among women and Equal to the Apostles. In the older Roman calendar she was remembered on the twenty-third. And many, many girls and women have carried her name.

And now, some notes for the curious.

Almost everything tonight about Egeria's journey comes from Egeria herself, from the letter that Gamurrini found in Arezzo in eighteen eighty-four. The visit to Thecla's shrine is in chapters twenty-two and twenty-three. Her habit of prayer and reading is in several places, the "I am rather curious" is in chapter sixteen, the three years in Jerusalem in chapter seventeen, Edessa and Harran in chapters seventeen to twenty-one, the guarded wood of the Cross in chapter thirty-seven, and the translators in the Jerusalem church in chapter forty-seven. The most useful English translation is John Wilkinson's, called Egeria's Travels, which also prints the letter of the monk Valerius.

What is history. The journey and the route. The bishop of Seleucia who had been a monk. The wall against the Isaurians. The countless cells of men and women. Marthana, the deaconess Egeria had known in Jerusalem, in charge of communities of consecrated women, and Egeria's question about how great their joy was. The prayers at the shrine and the reading of the whole Acts of Thecla. The two days there, and the road on to Constantinople and the plan for Ephesus. Gregory of Nazianzus's stay at the shrine, in his poem about his own life. Zeno's basilica, in Evagrius.

What is legend. Thecla's own story, from the second-century Acts of Paul and Thecla, which is not Scripture.

What we invented. Where and how the two women first met, and the Good Friday joke about the deacons, though the deacons and their guard are real. Every word the two women say to each other. The flour, the running, the mule, the honey, the beekeeper, the old soldier, the copyist, the slower psalms, and the wave at the bend in the road. Egeria tells us only that their joy was too great to describe. We have tried to describe it anyway, and we hope she would forgive us.

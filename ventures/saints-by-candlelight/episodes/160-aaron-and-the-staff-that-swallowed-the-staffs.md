* * *

Let me tell you about the room they are going back to.

A king's court in ancient Egypt was a crowded place. The king was at the centre of it, of course. But around him there were a great many people whose whole job was to make the king's business happen. Officials and governors and treasurers. Men who looked after the king's granaries, and his cattle, and his building works. Bodyguards. Fan bearers, who held up great fans on long poles, which sounds like a small job, and in that heat was probably a very popular one with the person being fanned.

And there were scribes. A great many scribes.

Egypt ran on writing. Every jar of grain that went into a royal granary was written down. Every brick, more or less, was written down. Every order from the king was written down, copied, sent off, filed and written down again. A scribe in Egypt sat cross-legged, with his kilt pulled tight across his knees to make a sort of desk, and a writing board or a roll of papyrus on top of that. He carried a palette, a long narrow piece of wood with a slot for his reed pens and two little hollows in it for his ink. One hollow had black ink, made from soot. The other had red ink, made from red ochre. Black was for the ordinary writing. Red was for headings, and dates, and the important bits.

There are old Egyptian school texts, written to persuade boys to become scribes, which more or less say, be a scribe, because every other job is terrible. The builder is always covered in mud, the farmer's harvest is eaten by worms and mice, the fisherman has to put up with crocodiles. But the scribe sits in the shade and tells other people what to do. I am summarising. But not by very much. Egyptian teachers were not above a little exaggeration, which I find quite comforting.

And there was one more group of people at the court that matters for tonight.

The book of Exodus calls them three things. The wise men. The sorcerers. And the magicians.

That last word, in Hebrew, is a word that is only used in the Bible for the magicians of Egypt, and in the Book of Daniel, for the magicians of Babylon. Many scholars think it comes from an old Egyptian title, something like chief lector priest. A lector priest, in Egypt, was the priest who read the sacred books aloud at the temple rites and at funerals. They were the keepers of the old writings. They knew the words for every ceremony. And the Egyptians told stories about them, the way we tell stories about wizards.

There is one of these stories on an old papyrus that is now in a museum in Berlin. It is about a lector priest called Webaoner, who made a little crocodile out of wax, and said words over it, and had it thrown into the water, and it became a real crocodile, a big one, which then did something rather unpleasant to a man who had annoyed Webaoner. It is a story, an Egyptian tale, told to entertain people at court. It is nothing to do with Moses, and I am not saying it happened. But it tells us something about the room. When Moses and Aaron walk in tonight, they are walking into a court where everybody has grown up on stories of clever priests who could make things come alive.

So they are not going to be impressed easily.

Last thing about the room. Almost everyone important in Egypt carried a staff. You can see them in the statues and the tomb paintings. A long straight stick, held upright, the sign that you were somebody. The king's officials had them. The priests had them. A staff, in that court, was not a walking aid. It was more like a badge.

And into that room, tonight, there are going to come two old men from the wilderness, with one shepherd's stick between them.

The book does not tell us the names of any of the scribes at Pharaoh's court. So I am going to invent one, and I want to be clear that he is invented. Let's call him Kha.

Kha is about twelve. He has been at the scribes' school for five years, copying out the same old sayings over and over again on flakes of limestone, and being told off for his spelling. And this year, for the first time, he has been allowed to come to the palace, to sit behind his master at the edge of the great hall, and take notes.

His master is invented too. He is an old scribe called Senu, who has been writing down the king's business for forty years. Senu has a bad knee, a very neat hand, and a firm opinion that nothing interesting has happened at court since he was a young man.

Kha was there the first time the two old Hebrews came. He wrote it down. He was very proud of it, actually. He wrote, in black, the names of the two men, as near as he could spell them. He wrote down what they asked for, which was three days in the wilderness to hold a festival for their God. And he wrote down, very carefully, what the king said. Who is this Lord? I do not know him.

And then, in red, because it was an order from the king, he wrote down the order about the straw.

He did not think very much about it after that. It was one order among a great many orders. Somewhere out in the land of Goshen, he supposed, some people would be cross about it. People were usually cross about orders.

And then, some days later, one morning, the doorkeeper came in, and bowed, and said something to the king's steward. And the steward said something to the king. And the king said, in a bored sort of voice, "Very well."

And old Senu, sitting in front of Kha, said, without turning round, "Get your pen ready. It's those two again."

* * *

But before they walk in, let's go back a little, and see how they got there. Because the book tells us something about the conversation the brothers had with the Lord before they came.

It begins, the book says, on the day the Lord spoke to Moses in the land of Egypt. And the Lord said, "I am the Lord. Tell Pharaoh, king of Egypt, everything that I say to you."

And Moses said, and you may remember this from last time, because he had said it before, "But look. I am a man of clumsy lips. How will Pharaoh listen to me?"

I find this very human, and very touching. It is the third or fourth time Moses has said something like this. At the burning bush, he said, I am slow of speech and slow of tongue. Last time, he said, the people of Israel would not listen to me, so how will Pharaoh? And now he says it again. My lips are clumsy. I am not the man for this.

Some of us, I think, have a sentence like that. A sentence we say to ourselves whenever we are asked to do something hard. I am not good with people. I am not clever enough. I always say the wrong thing. Moses had one too. And he said it to God, several times, quite honestly.

And the Lord did not tell him he was wrong about his lips. That is interesting, I think. The Lord did not say, nonsense, Moses, you're a wonderful speaker. He said something better.

He said, "See. I have made you like God to Pharaoh. And Aaron your brother shall be your prophet."

Now, that is a sentence that needs a moment, so let's give it one.

It does not mean that Moses was going to be divine. Of course not. Moses was a man, an old man with clumsy lips, and he knew it better than anyone. It means something about how the message would travel. Back at the burning bush, the Lord had already said that Aaron would be Moses' mouth, and that Moses would be like God to him. That is, Moses would hear the word from the Lord, and give it to Aaron. And Aaron would say it out loud.

And that is what a prophet is, in the old sense of the word. Not, first of all, someone who tells the future. A prophet is someone who speaks for another. Someone who is given words, and passes them on.

So the Lord is setting up a sort of chain. The Lord speaks to Moses. Moses speaks to Aaron. Aaron speaks to Pharaoh. And the words go all the way along the chain, from the Lord to the king of Egypt, without any of them being lost on the way.

Moses' clumsy lips do not break the chain. They just mean that there is one more link in it. And the extra link is his brother.

I like that very much. The Lord does not fix the weakness by taking it away. He fixes it by giving Moses somebody to lean on.

I like to imagine them on the walk to the palace that morning. This is my invention, and the book does not tell us what they said to each other. But I think there may have been a short conversation, the kind brothers have.

Aaron, perhaps, with the staff in his hand, turning it over. "This one?"

And Moses saying, "He said it would."

So let's go back into the hall, and sit with Kha.

The two old men came in. They looked, Kha thought, exactly the same as last time. Same dusty cloaks. Same white beards. The younger one, the taller one, who did not say much, walked a half step behind. The older one, the one who did the talking, carried a long plain wooden staff, a shepherd's staff, nothing carved on it, nothing painted. Some of the courtiers looked at it and smiled a little.

They stopped in front of the throne. And the older one, Aaron, began to speak.

Kha wrote it down. It was the same message as last time, more or less. The Lord, the God of the Hebrews, says, let my people go.

And here I have to be careful, because the book does not give us Pharaoh's words this time. It tells us what the Lord said Pharaoh would say. It says the Lord told them, when Pharaoh says to you, prove yourselves. And then it tells us what Moses and Aaron did. So we can guess that the king asked for some sign, some proof. But his exact words are not there, and I am not going to put any in his mouth.

Let us just say that the king looked at the two old men, and the old men understood that they were being asked, in the way kings ask things: well, then. Show me.

And Aaron took his staff in both hands. And he threw it down, on the painted floor, in front of the king, and in front of all the king's servants.

And it became a serpent.

Kha's pen stopped.

* * *

I should tell you about that word, serpent, because it is a little bit interesting.

When the Lord first showed Moses this sign, at the burning bush, and Moses threw down his staff and it became a snake, and Moses ran away from it, which I think was very sensible, the Hebrew uses the ordinary word for a snake. The word you would use for something you found under a rock in your garden.

But tonight, in Pharaoh's hall, the book uses a different word. It is a bigger word. It is the word used in the creation story in Genesis for the great creatures of the sea. Elsewhere in the Bible it can mean a sea monster, or a dragon, or something like a crocodile. Some translations say serpent here. Some say snake. A few say something closer to dragon.

And there is a place, much later, in the Book of the prophet Ezekiel, where the Lord speaks to a Pharaoh of Egypt, and calls him by that very word. The great dragon, lying in the middle of his rivers, who says, my Nile is my own, I made it for myself.

I am not going to make too much of that. The book of Exodus does not explain why it uses the bigger word, and readers have suggested different reasons. But I will say this. If you were a king of Egypt, and you liked to think of yourself as the great creature of the river, the one who owns the Nile, then seeing an old shepherd's stick turn into one, on your own floor, might have given you something to think about.

Anyway. Whatever it looked like, it was big enough that the people near it stepped back. And Kha, from the edge of the hall, could see it moving over the floor, and he found that he was holding his breath.

And old Senu, in front of him, did not turn round. But Kha saw that his master's neat pen had stopped too.

The book says, "Then Pharaoh summoned the wise men and the sorcerers. And they, the magicians of Egypt, also did the same, by their secret arts."

So in they came. Kha knew some of them by sight. Everybody at court did. The old priests who knew the sacred books, in their fine white linen, who walked as if the floor ought to be grateful. Each one of them had a staff. Each one of them stepped forward. And each one of them threw his staff down on the floor beside Aaron's.

And their staffs became serpents too.

So now there were a good many of them on the floor. How many, the book does not say. Two, ten, twenty. I have no idea, and nor has anyone else, so I am going to say a good many, and leave it there.

I should be honest with you about something here. People have wondered, for a very long time, how the magicians of Egypt did this. Some have said it was a trick, a sleight of hand. There have been snake charmers in Egypt for a very long time, and some people have suggested that the magicians had snakes about them that could be made to go stiff, like sticks, and then wake up when they were thrown down. Other people, including some of the old Christian writers, thought it was something darker than a trick. And some people have thought it was simply something the book does not want to explain.

The book only says, by their secret arts. And I think that is as far as we should go tonight. The book does not tell us how. So we do not know how. And I am quite happy for it to stay that way.

What the book does want us to notice is what happened next.

* * *

There they all were, on the floor. A good many serpents. All the courtiers stepping back. The king leaning forward, now, just a little. The magicians standing in a row, looking rather pleased with themselves, I imagine, the way a man does when he has just shown that he can do the same trick as the new person.

And Kha, at the edge of the hall, with his pen in the air, and the drop of black ink gathering on the end of it.

And then Aaron's staff, the first one, the one that had been a shepherd's stick that morning, the one Aaron had knocked thistles over with, opened its mouth.

And it swallowed them.

The book says it in five words, more or less. Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs.

Not all at once, I imagine. One. And then another. And then another. Until there was nothing on the painted floor but one great creature, lying quite still, in front of the throne.

And then Aaron bent down, and took hold of it. And it was a staff again. A plain wooden shepherd's staff, in the hand of an old man of eighty-three.

And the drop of ink on the end of Kha's pen finally fell off, and landed on his writing board, right in the middle, in a big round blot.

He did not even notice.

* * *

I want to point out something about the way the book tells this, because I think it is quite deliberate, and quite funny, in a quiet way.

Notice what it says. It does not say Aaron's serpent swallowed their serpents. It says Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs.

So the book is reminding us, even in the middle of all this, what these things really were. Sticks. Their badges. Their signs of rank, the long staffs that said, I am somebody at this court. And when the morning was over, the magicians of Egypt did not have them any more. They were gone. Inside Aaron's staff.

I have always rather wondered what it was like for them, walking home that afternoon. Every official in Egypt carried a staff. And there they were, the king's own magicians, the cleverest men at court, walking out of the palace with nothing in their hands. Every servant they passed must have looked at their empty hands. And I imagine there were a great many people at that court who knew exactly what had happened by dinner time.

The Book of Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians also read as Scripture, looks back on all this many centuries later. And it says, about the magicians of Egypt, that the delusions of their magic art lay humbled, and their boasted wisdom was put to shame.

Lay humbled. Swallowed, in fact.

But, and this is the important part, as far as the book tells us, nobody was hurt. No magician was bitten. No courtier was harmed. The king was not touched. It was a sign. It was the Lord saying, very clearly, to the whole court of Egypt, in a language that court understood: you have your arts, and your old wise men, and your staffs of office. And here is an old shepherd's stick. And it has swallowed them all.

It was, I think, an extremely gentle way to begin. A warning that was also a kindness. A chance to listen, before anything worse happened.

And the king did not listen.

The book says, "Still Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he would not listen to them, just as the Lord had said."

That's it. That's the end of the visit. No order this time. No more straw taken away. Just a king, sitting very still on his throne, looking at two old men and one plain stick, and deciding, no.

I imagine the hall was quite quiet, for a moment. And then the steward said something, the way stewards do, to fill up a silence, and the next piece of business was called. And the two old brothers turned round, slowly, the way old men do, and walked back out through the painted columns, with one staff between them, and the magicians standing there with nothing.

And Kha looked down at his writing board. And he saw the blot.

And he saw that he had written almost nothing. Just the names, at the top, and the first line of the message. Let my people go. And then the blot. And then nothing.

And old Senu, in front of him, without turning round, said, very quietly, "Write it down, boy. Write all of it down."

And Kha said, "What do I write?"

And Senu was quiet for a long time. And then he said, "Write what you saw."

So Kha did. He wrote it in black, because it was not an order from the king. But when he got to the part about the staff, he stopped, and thought about it, and then he dipped his pen in the red ink after all, and wrote that part in red.

He was told off for that later, for wasting the red. He did not mind.

[pause]

Now, I said I would come back to the hard question, and here it is, quietly, while the hall empties and the lamps are lit.

The Lord said, before they went, I will harden Pharaoh's heart. And afterwards the book says, Pharaoh's heart was hardened, just as the Lord had said.

People have puzzled over this for a very long time, and it is right to puzzle over it. Does it mean that the Lord made Pharaoh say no? That Pharaoh never had any choice?

I am not going to pretend I can settle this in a bedtime story. Wise people have written whole books about it. But I can tell you three things that the Church's teachers have noticed, and that I find a help.

The first is that the book says it in more than one way. Sometimes it says the Lord hardened Pharaoh's heart. Sometimes it says, as tonight, simply that Pharaoh's heart was hardened, or grew stubborn. And sometimes, a little later in the story, it says, plainly, that Pharaoh hardened his own heart. When the frogs were gone, and the king saw that he had some relief, he hardened his heart, and would not listen. So the book itself is telling us that Pharaoh was not a puppet. He chose. Again and again, he chose.

The second is an old picture, from a Christian teacher called Origen, who lived in Alexandria, in Egypt, as it happens, about eighteen hundred years ago. In his book On First Principles, he thought about Pharaoh's heart, and he used the picture of the sun. The same sun, he said, shining with the same warmth, melts wax, and hardens clay. It is not that the sun wants one to melt and the other to go hard. It is that they are different stuff, and they meet the same warmth in different ways. The Lord sent the same signs to Pharaoh that he sent to everyone else. To the people of Israel, in the end, those signs were deliverance. To Pharaoh, who kept on saying no, every new sign made the no a little harder.

And the third is very simple. The Catechism of the Catholic Church says plainly that God predestines no one to go to hell. Nobody is made to be lost. A heart can go hard. But a heart only stays hard by refusing, freely, again and again, to be softened.

And there is a psalm that the Church has prayed, every single morning, for a very long time. In the Liturgy of the Hours of the Roman Church, the first psalm of the day is very often Psalm ninety-five. And in the middle of it there are these words. O that today you would listen to his voice. Harden not your hearts.

Harden not your hearts. The Letter to the Hebrews quotes those words, too, and says them again and again, to Christians, as a warning, and as an invitation. Today. If you hear his voice. Today.

So the story of Pharaoh is not really a story about someone else. It is a story about a door that every heart has. And the Lord standing at it, knocking, with the same warmth, every morning.

Here is what happened next, very briefly, because it is for another night.

Next, the book says, the Lord told Moses to go to Pharaoh in the morning, and wait for him by the river, when the king went down to the water. And to take in his hand, the book says, the staff that had turned into a serpent. And there, at the edge of the Nile, the signs that we call the plagues began. They were much harder than a staff on a floor. But that is another story, and we will not tell it tonight.

And the staff itself has a long story of its own. Many years later, out in the wilderness, when some of the people grumbled that Aaron had no right to be the priest, the Lord told Moses to take a staff from the leader of each of the twelve tribes, and to write each man's name on his staff, and to write Aaron's name on the staff of the tribe of Levi. And Moses put them all in the tent of meeting overnight. And in the morning, Aaron's staff had sprouted. It had put out buds, and blossoms, and ripe almonds, all at once, on a dry stick.

And the Letter to the Hebrews says that it was kept, after that, inside the ark of the covenant, along with the golden jar of manna and the tablets of the covenant. A plain wooden shepherd's staff, that had once swallowed the staffs of Egypt, and once grown almonds in a night.

Aaron himself lived to a great age. He was a hundred and twenty-three years old when he died, the book says, on the top of Mount Hor, at the edge of the land of Edom, with Moses beside him. And the whole house of Israel mourned for him for thirty days.

So let's go back, one last time, to that evening in Egypt.

The great hall is empty now. The servants have swept the painted floor. There is nothing on it. No serpents. No staffs. Just the tiles, cool, in the last of the light.

In a small room at the back of the scribes' quarters, a boy of twelve is sitting by a lamp, with a writing board on his knees. There is a blot in the middle of it, a big round black one. And underneath the blot, there are a few lines of black writing. And then a few lines of red.

He is reading them over. And over.

He has written down a great many things this year. Orders and counts and lists. Jars of grain and bricks. But this is the first thing he has ever written that he does not understand. And he is not sure, yet, whether that frightens him, or makes him want to know more.

Outside, the city is going quiet. Somewhere a dog is barking, and then it stops.

And somewhere out to the east, in the land of Goshen, in a small house, two old brothers are lying down to sleep. One of them is eighty. One of them is eighty-three. Leaning against the wall, by the door, is a plain wooden staff.

It is just a staff. Nothing carved on it. Nothing painted. It has been knocking over thistles for years.

And soon, one morning, early, they are going down to the river.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Exodus, chapter six, verse twenty-eight, to chapter seven, verse thirteen, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. Moses' clumsy lips are chapter six, verses twenty-eight to thirty. "I have made you like God to Pharaoh," the warning about the hardened heart, and the brothers' ages are chapter seven, verses one to seven. The staff and the serpent, the magicians, and Aaron's staff swallowing their staffs are verses eight to thirteen. The first sign at the burning bush, and Aaron as Moses' mouth, are chapter four. The river the next morning is chapter seven, verse fifteen. Pharaoh hardening his own heart is in chapter eight, verses fifteen and thirty-two in most English Bibles.

The budding staff is Numbers, chapter seventeen, and Aaron's death on Mount Hor is Numbers, chapters twenty and thirty-three. The staff in the ark is from the Letter to the Hebrews, chapter nine. The great dragon in his rivers is Ezekiel, chapter twenty-nine. The magic art humbled is the Book of Wisdom, chapter seventeen. Harden not your hearts is Psalm ninety-five, and Hebrews, chapter three.

The magicians are not named in Exodus. The Second Letter to Timothy calls two men who opposed Moses Jannes and Jambres, names from Jewish tradition. The Egyptian tale of Webaoner and the wax crocodile is on the Westcar Papyrus; it is a story, not history. Origen's sun is in On First Principles, book three. The book does not name this Pharaoh, so we have not guessed.

Kha, his master Senu, the look of the hall, the blot, the red ink, and the brothers' talk on the road are our own invention.

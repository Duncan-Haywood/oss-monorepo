We have to begin, I'm afraid, with a sad family, because the house of King David, for all its glory, was a sad family by this point.

David had a great many sons, by several wives. That was how kings did things then, and the books of Samuel and Kings are very honest about how badly it tended to go. The eldest son was called Amnon. The third son was Absalom. And Absalom had a full sister, the same mother and the same father, called Tamar.

Amnon did a terrible wrong to Tamar. The book tells it plainly, and we will not, because this is a bedtime show. He was very angry, and he did nothing.

Absalom did nothing either, for two whole years. He did not speak to Amnon at all, the book says, neither good nor bad. He simply waited.

And then, at the time of sheep shearing, which was a great feast in those days, a bit like a harvest supper, Absalom invited all the king's sons out to his estate in the country. And at that feast, when Amnon's heart was merry with wine, Absalom's servants struck him down. And so Amnon died, and all the other princes leapt on their mules and fled home.

David has lost one son to death and another to exile, and the second loss is his own son's doing, and the first, if we are honest, is partly the king's own, because he was angry and did nothing. Now, there was a man in Jerusalem who watched all this, and he did not like it.

His name was Joab. He was the son of Zeruiah, who was David's sister, so he was the king's nephew. He was also the commander of the king's army, and he had been for a long time. Joab is not a gentle man. In the books of Samuel he is clever and loyal and brave, and also hard, the sort of man who gets things done and does not much mind how. If you met him, I think you would be glad he was on your side, and a little nervous about what he did when he wasn't.

But Joab had noticed something. The book says that Joab perceived that the king's heart was toward Absalom. And Joab, being Joab, decided to do something about it.

He did not go to the king himself. That was the clever part. A general who walks in and says, your majesty, bring your son home, is a general giving the king an order. And kings do not like orders, even good ones. Especially good ones, perhaps.

No. Joab sent to a town called Tekoa, and fetched a wise woman.

Tekoa was a small hill town, about ten miles south of Jerusalem, a few miles past Bethlehem, which you will remember was David's own home town. It stood high up, right on the edge of the wilderness of Judah, where the green hills give way to that great tumbled country of dry valleys running down to the Dead Sea. It was sheep country. Shepherd country. Much later, a herdsman of Tekoa called Amos would be called away from his flocks and his fig trees to be a prophet. One of David's own champions, a man called Ira son of Ikkesh, came from Tekoa too. And later kings built walls there and made it a fortress, because if you stood on the hill at Tekoa, you could see anyone coming up out of the desert a long way off.

So it was a town of shepherds and soldiers and, if the book is anything to go by, at least one very clever woman.

Now, what was a wise woman? That is a good question, and honest scholars do not entirely agree on the answer. The phrase in the book is simply a woman who was wise. Some think it was almost a title, that certain towns had a woman everyone went to when there was a quarrel to settle or a hard thing to say. There is another one, later in the same book, in a town in the far north, who talks a whole army out of a siege from the top of the city wall. Others think the book just means what it says, that she was a woman, and she was wise. We do not know. But we do know that Joab, the hardest man in David's army, did not send for a soldier or a priest or a prophet. He sent for her. And I think that tells us something about her reputation.

I like to picture the day the message came.

I imagine a small stone house, near the top of the town, with a courtyard and a fig tree, because every house in Tekoa should have a fig tree. I imagine it is early in the morning, and she is crouching by the hearth, the little clay fireplace in the corner, coaxing last night's embers back to life. Because that is what you did, every morning, in a house with no matches. You did not let the fire go out. You banked it at night, buried the coals in ash, and in the morning you uncovered them and blew on them gently until they glowed. And if they ever went out, you had to go to a neighbour's house and borrow a coal to carry home in a pot. Everybody did it. It was the oldest thing in the world.

Hold onto that, because it is going to matter.

In those days, a king of Israel sat to hear cases, a bit like a judge in a court. If you had a problem that your town elders could not settle, you could, in principle, bring it to the king himself. Do you see? The last coal.

Every woman in that hall, and I should think every man too, knew exactly what she meant. The last ember in the hearth. The one you bank in the ashes at night and blow on in the morning. If that goes out, the fire is gone, and the house is cold. Her husband was dead. One son was dead. And now the family wanted to take the only son she had left. And then there would be nobody. No fire in the house. No name for her husband. Nothing.

Now, you should know a little about the law, so you can see why she needed the king.

In those days there was no police force. If someone was killed, it was the dead man's nearest relative who was expected to see justice done. That man was called the avenger of blood. It sounds terribly fierce, but it was really the only way a village could keep from becoming lawless. And the law of Moses set limits on it. There were cities of refuge, six of them, where someone who had killed a person by accident, without meaning to, could run and be safe until there was a proper hearing.

But the widow's son had not killed by accident, not quite. He had killed his brother in a fight, in a field, with nobody to see. It was a hard case. Exactly the kind of case a village might decide the wrong way, especially if somebody in the family had their eye on the dead husband's land, which, if the last son were gone, would go to them. And so she had come to the one man who could override them all.

And the king listened, and he was moved, and he said, go home. I will give orders about you.

Which is a perfectly kind thing to say. But it is also, if you think about it, the sort of thing people say when they want to think about it later. Go home, I'll sort it out. I imagine she heard that, and knew she had not finished.

That is a very clever thing to say. She is saying, if there is any wrong in sparing my son, let it fall on me, not on you. She is making it easy for him.

So did the wise woman fail?

I do not think so. I think she did the thing she was asked to do, and she did it beautifully, and she told the truth when she was caught. What the king and his son did afterwards was up to them. A good word can open a door. It cannot make anyone walk through it with their whole heart. The king opened the door a crack, and then halfway, and then he kissed his son, but I am not sure either of them ever quite came all the way through. That was not her fault. That was the old sad business of a family that had never learned to say sorry, or to mean it.

We are like water spilt on the ground, which cannot be gathered up again. But God devises ways, so that the banished one is not cast out from him for ever.

It is very hard for a Christian to hear that line and not think of another story. A story Our Lord told, about a man with two sons, and a younger son who went away into a far country.

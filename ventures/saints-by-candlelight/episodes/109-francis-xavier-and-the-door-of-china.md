It is a cold night at the very end of November, on a small island, and the wind is coming in off the sea.

The island is mostly hills and scrub and sand. On the beach there are a few huts made of straw and branches, the kind of huts traders put up for a season and leave to fall down when they go. Most of the huts are empty now. The ships have gone. Only one ship is left, riding at anchor in the bay, with her lanterns out.

In one of the huts, a man of forty-six is lying on a mat, with a fever. Beside him, keeping watch, sits a young Chinese man called Antonio. Every so often the sick man opens his eyes and looks out through the doorway, across the dark water, towards the north. Over there, not very far away, a few hours' sail at most, is the coast of China.

He has come all the way round the world to get to that coast. And he is not going to get there.

If you were here for the first part of this story, you will remember Francis as a proud young nobleman from Navarre, in the north of Spain, who shared a room in Paris with a shepherd called Peter Faber and a limping older student called Ignatius of Loyola, and who ended up, rather to his own surprise, as one of the very first Jesuits. In fifteen forty-one he sailed from Lisbon to India. The voyage took thirteen months. He washed his own shirts on board, which shocked everybody.

If you were not here for the first part, that is all you need to know. That, and one more thing. By the time our story starts tonight, Francis has been in the East for more than five years, and he has hardly stood still for a week.

He has walked up and down the hot southern tip of India, on what the Portuguese called the Fishery Coast, ringing a little bell in the villages and teaching children their prayers in Tamil, a language he never really learned, from a translation he had made with help and learned by heart. He has gone on to Malacca, the great trading port on the narrow strait between Sumatra and the Malay peninsula. He has gone further still, to the Spice Islands, the Moluccas, where the cloves come from, where the volcanoes smoke and the sea is so deep and so blue that it looks like ink. There is an old story from those islands, about a crucifix that Francis dropped overboard in a storm, and a crab that came out of the sea the next day and carried it back to him on the beach. It is a legend, and I tell it to you as a legend. I like the crab very much.

And now, in the year fifteen forty-seven, he has come back to Malacca. He is forty-one years old. He is thin and brown and his cassock is patched. And he is about to meet a man who will change the direction of the rest of his life.

Malacca, in those days, was one of the busiest harbours in the world. Think of an airport, crossed with a market, crossed with a very hot and very noisy harbour. There were ships from India and Arabia and China and the islands, and merchants from everywhere, speaking a dozen languages at once, buying and selling pepper and silk and porcelain and sandalwood. The Portuguese had taken the city by force less than forty years earlier and had built a great stone fortress on the hill. Francis did not much care for some of the Portuguese out there, and he said so, often, in his letters. But the harbour was a crossroads. If you wanted to go anywhere in the East, sooner or later you went through Malacca.

And one day at the end of that year, a Portuguese captain he knew came to find him, bringing a stranger. The stranger was a Japanese man, in his thirties, called Anjirō.

* * *

Anjirō had a story, and he told it to Francis, and Francis wrote it down in a letter.

He came from a town called Kagoshima, at the southern end of Japan. And he had done something terrible. In a quarrel, he had killed a man. He had run away to escape the law, and hidden on a Portuguese ship that was in the harbour, and the ship had carried him away from Japan altogether. And all the time, he said, his conscience gave him no peace. Some of the Portuguese on board, seeing how troubled he was, had told him about a priest called Francis, who they said was a holy man and might be able to help him. So Anjirō had come looking. He had sailed all the way to Malacca to find him. The first time he came, Francis was away in the islands, and Anjirō, discouraged, had set off home again. A storm drove his ship back. And so here he was, in Malacca, being introduced.

I find this a very moving story. A man with a heavy weight on his heart, crossing half an ocean to find somebody who could tell him what to do with it.

Francis liked him at once. He wrote that Anjirō was very curious, that he asked a great many questions, and that he remembered everything he was told. And Francis asked him questions too. What was Japan like? Were the people there like Anjirō? If Francis went to Japan, would they become Christians?

Anjirō, by Francis's account, gave a careful answer. He said that his people would not become Christians all at once. First they would ask a great many questions. They would want to see whether what Francis said was reasonable, and whether he lived the way he talked. And if he did, after perhaps half a year, the lord of the place and the educated people would think it over, and then they might.

Francis took this as a challenge. Here was a people, he thought, who would be won not by force or by trade, but by reason, and by a good life. He sent Anjirō to the Jesuit college at Goa, in India, to learn the faith properly and to learn Portuguese. And at Pentecost the next year, fifteen forty-eight, Anjirō was baptised there, and took the name Paul of the Holy Faith. Two other Japanese men who had come with him were baptised too, and took the names John and Anthony.

And Francis began to make plans for Japan.

* * *

They sailed from Malacca on the twenty-fourth of June, fifteen forty-nine, the feast of Saint John the Baptist.

There were eight of them. There was Francis. There was Father Cosme de Torres, a Spanish priest who had joined the Jesuits out in the East, a steady, patient man, the kind you want with you on a long journey. There was Brother Juan Fernández, a young Spaniard from Córdoba who had been a silk merchant before he joined the Jesuits, and who turned out to have a remarkable gift for languages. There was Paul, who had been Anjirō, going home at last. There were John and Anthony. And there were two young servants, Manuel, who was Chinese, and Amador, from India.

No Portuguese ship was going to Japan that season, so they went on a Chinese junk, a big flat-bottomed sailing ship with sails like folded fans. The captain was a man the Portuguese in Malacca called, rather alarmingly, the Pirate. I do not know if that was a fair name or not. Francis does not seem to have been very worried by it. What did worry him was that the captain kept a little idol in the stern of the ship, and every time he had to make a decision, he lit candles and burned incense and cast lots before it, and did whatever the lots said.

Should we stop at this island? Ask the idol. Should we go to China first, or straight to Japan? Ask the idol. Should we wait for a better wind? Ask the idol.

Francis wrote, a little crossly, that they were at the mercy of the devil and his servants, and that the captain would not do anything without asking. And then, being Francis, he added that he and his companions had put their whole trust in God, and that this was a great comfort, and that they were not afraid. I think he was also trying very hard not to tell the captain how to run his ship.

On the voyage, the captain's daughter fell overboard and was drowned. Francis tells it in one sad sentence. The poor man was in great grief. There was nothing anyone could do.

And then, after seven weeks at sea, on the fifteenth of August, fifteen forty-nine, they came into the great bay of Kagoshima, with the volcano smoking on its island in the middle of the water, and Paul showed them his home.

It was the feast of the Assumption of Our Lady. Francis noticed that, as he noticed all such things. Fifteen years before, on that same feast day, he had climbed the hill of Montmartre with Ignatius and Peter and the others, and made his vows in a little chapel. Now here he was, at the far side of the world, on the same day, stepping off a junk onto the beach in Japan.

* * *

Japan surprised Francis. And Francis, I think, surprised Japan.

Let me set the scene. Japan in fifteen forty-nine was in the middle of a long, long time of civil war. There was an emperor, in the capital, which the Portuguese called Miyako and which we now call Kyoto. But the emperor had almost no power left. There was a great military commander, the shogun, who was supposed to rule in the emperor's name, but he had very little power either. The real power was in the hands of dozens of local lords, each with his own castle and his own warriors, and they were forever fighting one another. It was a bit like a country made entirely of quarrelsome neighbours.

But in the middle of all that fighting, there was a great deal of learning, and a great deal of courtesy. The people bowed. They were very clean, and they bathed often, which was not true of most Europeans at the time, and Francis noticed. They were proud, and they cared very much about honour. Many of them could read and write. There were Buddhist monasteries everywhere, some of them very large, full of monks who studied and debated.

Francis wrote a long letter about it all, that first autumn, to his brothers in Goa. It is one of the most famous letters he ever wrote. He said that the Japanese were the best people he had yet met, and that he did not think any better would be found among the peoples who did not yet know Christ. He said they were good-natured and not malicious, and that they cared about honour more than anything. He said that most of them were poor, and that among them poverty was not thought to be a disgrace. He said that the nobles and the ordinary people treated each other with great courtesy. And he said they loved to hear about God and about the things of the soul, and that they asked endless questions.

That last part was true. Anjirō had told him so.

The lord of that part of Japan, the province of Satsuma, was a man called Shimazu Takahisa. He received Francis kindly, at his castle, in the autumn. He looked with great interest at a painting of Our Lady and the Child that Francis had brought, and knelt before it, and had his mother shown it too. And he gave them permission to teach.

There was one small problem. Francis could not speak Japanese.

* * *

He tried. He really did. But Japanese is a very hard language for a grown man from Navarre to learn, and Francis was forty-three, and busy, and he had never been a great one for languages. He wrote, a little ruefully, that among the Japanese they were like statues. People spoke to them, and about them, and they just stood there.

So they did what Francis had done in India. With Paul's help, they translated a short account of the faith into Japanese: the creation of the world, the life of Christ, the Commandments, the Last Judgement. Then they wrote the Japanese out in the letters of our alphabet, so that Francis could read it aloud even though he did not really understand what he was saying. And every day he would go out, and stand on the steps of a building, or in the street, and read from the book, out loud, in Japanese, in the accent of Navarre.

I imagine that went down about as well as you would expect. People came to listen. Some of them laughed. Some of them were very interested. Some of them, I suspect, were mostly interested in watching a tall foreigner read something he did not understand. And some of them, slowly, began to ask questions, and stayed to hear the answers, and were baptised. Paul's own family were among the first. By the end of the first year there were about a hundred Christians in Kagoshima.

There was also a problem with the translation, and it took them a long time to find out about it. When Paul was looking for a Japanese word for God, he chose a word from his own Buddhist background, Dainichi, which is the name of a great Buddha, the Great Sun. Paul meant well. He was looking for the highest and holiest word he knew. But it meant that, for about two years, Francis was going around Japan preaching the Great Sun Buddha, and a good many Buddhist monks were delighted to hear it, and thought this was simply a new kind of Buddhism from India. When Francis finally understood what had happened, he was horrified. From then on he used the Latin word, Deus, and told people plainly that it was not the name of anything they already knew.

I think there is something rather wonderful about that mistake. Two men, a Basque priest and a Japanese convert, trying with all their hearts to say the name of God in a new language, and getting it wrong. And then noticing, and starting again. Most of us get God's name a little wrong, one way or another. The thing is to keep trying.

* * *

In Kagoshima, Francis made a friend.

There was a large Zen monastery in the town, and its abbot was an old man called Ninshitsu. He was about eighty years old. Francis wrote that his name meant something like heart of truth, and that he was greatly respected in the town, and that he and Francis talked together often and became good friends. The old abbot was courteous and kind and very wise, and he was not at all sure, Francis wrote, whether the soul lived on after death or not. Sometimes he said it did, and sometimes he said it did not.

We have none of their actual conversations. But I like to picture them, the old Japanese abbot and the thin Basque priest, sitting together in the monastery garden in the autumn, with the maples turning red, and Paul beside them translating.

"You are a long way from home," the old man might have said.

"I am," said Francis. "Further than I ever expected to be."

"And you came all this way to tell us about your God."

"I came because I think he would want me to. And because I think you would want to know."

The old abbot smiled. "We will see," he said. "Tell me about the soul. Your monks say it does not die?"

"It does not die," said Francis.

"I am an old man," said Ninshitsu. "I would like to believe that. I have not made up my mind."

"Then we will talk again tomorrow," said Francis.

That part is our invention. What is true is that they were friends, and that they argued, gently, about the deepest things, and that the old man never did become a Christian, as far as we know. And Francis never stopped speaking of him with respect.

* * *

After about a year, the lord of Satsuma changed his mind about the Christians, perhaps because the Buddhist monasteries complained, perhaps because the Portuguese ships he had hoped for had gone to another port instead. Francis was not allowed to make any more converts there. So he left Paul in Kagoshima to look after the little Christian community, and set off with Torres and Fernández and a new Japanese Christian called Bernardo, to go north.

They went first to the port of Hirado, where a Portuguese ship had come in, and where the lord received them kindly, and about a hundred more people were baptised. They left Torres there. Then Francis and Fernández and Bernardo went on, to the great city of Yamaguchi, the capital of one of the most powerful lords in the west of Japan, a man called Ōuchi Yoshitaka.

Yamaguchi did not go well, the first time. Francis and Fernández stood in the streets and read from their book, and people laughed at them, and children threw stones. The lord received them, listened for an hour, and dismissed them without a word. They were poor and dusty and odd-looking and could barely speak the language, and in Japan, Francis was beginning to understand, how you looked and what you wore mattered a very great deal.

But Francis had a bigger plan. He thought that if he could only get to the capital, to Miyako, and speak to the emperor, then the emperor might give him permission to preach all over Japan. So in the middle of winter, at the very end of fifteen fifty, he and Fernández and Bernardo set off for the capital.

* * *

It was a terrible journey.

They went partly by sea and partly on foot, and it was the coldest part of the year. There was snow on the roads. There were rivers to wade, so cold that their legs went numb. They had very little money and very little food, and they slept where they could. Francis's feet swelled and bled. At one point, to get through a dangerous stretch of country, Francis ran alongside the horse of a rich traveller, as a sort of servant, so as to travel in his company.

There is a story, told by a later Jesuit writer, that Francis was so happy on that road that he went along tossing an apple in the air and catching it, like a boy. I cannot vouch for the apple. But it is a nice picture, and it is the sort of thing he might have done.

Bernardo, who walked every step of that journey with him, told the Jesuits years later that Francis was cheerful the whole way. I am going to give you a little scene of the two of them on the road, and I will tell you plainly that we have made it up.

The snow was coming down thickly. Bernardo was cold and wet and very tired, and his straw sandals were soaked through.

"Father," he said. "May I ask you something?"

"Of course," said Francis.

"Why are you so happy? We are freezing. We are hungry. We do not know if the emperor will see us. We do not even know if there will be anywhere to sleep tonight."

Francis thought about it, with the snow on his eyebrows.

"Do you remember the night you were baptised, Bernardo?"

"I remember."

"Were you happy that night?"

"Very happy."

"Well," said Francis. "Everyone in Miyako could have a night like that. Every one of them. That is what we are carrying. It is not very heavy, and it keeps you warm."

Bernardo thought that sounded more like something a priest would say than something that was true about feet. But he kept walking.

* * *

They reached the capital in the middle of January, fifteen fifty-one. And it was a disappointment.

The great city had been burned and fought over again and again in the wars, and much of it was in ruins. The emperor lived in a palace whose walls were crumbling, with almost no money and no power at all. The shogun had fled the city. To be received at court, you needed gifts, expensive ones, and Francis had nothing. They stayed eleven days. Nobody would let them in. Nobody seemed to be in charge.

So they turned round and went back, all the way, through the cold.

I find this part of the story strangely comforting. Even saints make plans that do not work. Francis had walked hundreds of miles through snow to see an emperor, and there was, in a sense, no emperor to see. He did not sulk about it, as far as we know. He simply learned something. He learned that Japan was not going to be won from the top, through one ruler, because there was no one ruler. And he learned something else too, about clothes.

* * *

When he got back to Yamaguchi, in the spring, Francis did something rather surprising for a man who washed his own shirts. He dressed up.

He had left some things at Hirado: letters from the Portuguese Viceroy of India and from the Bishop of Goa, and a collection of presents that had been meant for the emperor. He fetched them. He put on good clothes. He went to the lord of Yamaguchi, Ōuchi Yoshitaka, not as a poor wandering preacher this time, but as an ambassador, with letters and gifts.

The gifts were wonderful things for Japan in those days, because they were things nobody there had ever seen. The lists that come down to us include a clock that struck the hours by itself, a musical instrument, a fine glass, a mirror, Portuguese cloth and wine, and books, among others. The lord was delighted. The clock, I imagine, was the great success. I picture the whole court sitting around it in silence, waiting for it to strike.

And this time the lord listened. He gave them permission to preach in his lands, and published it on notices in the streets, saying that anyone who wished might become a Christian. And he gave them an empty Buddhist monastery to live in.

Francis wrote afterwards that he had learned, in Japan, that it mattered how you came. A poor man in a patched cassock could not get a hearing. The same man, in good clothes, with letters, could. He did not much like it. But he was not too proud to do it, if it meant people would listen. I think that is a kind of humility too, the kind that will even wear a fine coat if the work needs it.

* * *

The months in Yamaguchi were the happiest of his time in Japan.

People came to the old monastery from morning till night. They came with questions, hundreds of questions, so many that Francis and Fernández could hardly answer them all. Learned monks came to argue. Warriors came. Ordinary townspeople came. And they did not only ask about God. Francis was startled to find that they wanted to know about everything. Why does it rain? What is lightning? Why does the moon change its shape? What makes a comet? Is the earth flat, or round? Where does the sun go at night?

Francis knew a little astronomy from his years at the university in Paris, and he answered as well as he could. And the people were delighted, and he noticed that once they saw that the foreign priests knew things about the sky and the weather, they were more ready to listen when he talked about God. He wrote to Rome, afterwards, asking that any Jesuits sent to Japan should be learned men, good at argument, and that they should know about the heavens, because the Japanese were very fond of knowing why things happened.

Brother Juan Fernández, meanwhile, had become the real voice of the mission. He had learned Japanese far better than Francis ever would, and it was Fernández who did most of the talking. One day, the story goes, he was preaching in the street, and a man came up and spat in his face. Fernández wiped his face, and carried on preaching, as if nothing had happened. And a young man in the crowd who saw it was so struck by this that he came to the priests that evening and asked to be baptised. That is told in the old Jesuit accounts, and I think it is very likely true. It is exactly the sort of thing that changes people's minds.

And in Yamaguchi, they met Lorenzo.

Lorenzo, as he became, was a wandering minstrel, one of those Japanese storytellers who went from town to town singing the old tales of war and heroes, and accompanying themselves on the biwa, which is a kind of lute. He was nearly blind. He was also, by all accounts, very funny, and very clever, and a wonderful speaker. He came to listen to the foreign priests, probably out of curiosity. He kept coming. And Francis baptised him.

Lorenzo went on, after Francis had gone, to become one of the most famous preachers in all of Japan. For thirty years and more he travelled the country, half blind, telling people about Christ the way he had once told them about warriors. I like to think that his biwa went with him.

Here is a little scene. It is our invention, but I think it is close to the truth of him.

"Father," said Lorenzo one evening, tuning his biwa. "In the old stories that I sing, the heroes are all great warriors. They win great battles. They die gloriously. And everyone sings about them for a thousand years."

"I know some stories like that," said Francis.

"But the hero of your story," said Lorenzo, "is a carpenter's son, who loses. He is taken prisoner, and killed, on a cross. And then he comes back. That is a very strange story for a minstrel."

"It is a very strange story for anyone," said Francis.

Lorenzo plucked a string, and listened to it fade.

"I think," he said, "it is the best one I have ever heard."

* * *

There is one thing from the Yamaguchi months that I want to tell you, because it shows what kind of man Francis had become.

Many of the new Christians had a terrible sorrow. Their parents and grandparents had died before Francis came to Japan. They had never heard of Christ. And the new Christians came to Francis and asked him, with tears, whether their ancestors were lost forever, and whether there was anything at all they could do for them.

Francis wrote about this in a letter. He said that the people wept for their dead, and that their sorrow was so great that he could hardly bear it, and that he wept too.

I want to be careful here, because this is a deep question, and the Church has thought about it a great deal since. The Church teaches that God desires all people to be saved, and that those who, through no fault of their own, have never known the Gospel, but who seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will as their conscience shows it, can be saved by his grace, in ways known to him. That is the teaching of the Church in our own time. Francis, in fifteen fifty-one, did not have all the words for it that we have now. But he had something that came first. He had tears. He sat with people who were grieving, and he did not pretend their grief was small, and he wept with them. And I think that is where every good answer to a question like that has to start.

* * *

In the autumn of fifteen fifty-one, word came that a Portuguese ship had arrived at Bungo, on the east coast of the island of Kyushu. Francis went to meet it. The young lord of Bungo, Ōtomo Yoshishige, who was only about twenty-one, received him with great honour. The Portuguese merchants, to impress the Japanese, made a great show of treating Francis with respect, walking bareheaded beside him, as if he were a prince. I suspect Francis found this tiresome. But he knew, by now, how much it helped.

The young lord did not become a Christian then. He became one many years later, long after Francis was dead, and took the baptismal name Francis, after him. I like that very much. It is as if a seed was planted on an autumn afternoon, and took twenty-seven years to come up.

And then, in November fifteen fifty-one, Francis left Japan. He had been there two years and three months. He left Torres and Fernández behind to carry on the work, and Lorenzo, and Paul, and perhaps a thousand Christians, in Kagoshima and Hirado and Yamaguchi and Bungo. He took Bernardo with him. Bernardo went on, later, all the way to Portugal and to Rome, the first Japanese person, as far as we know, to set foot in Europe. He died in Portugal, at Coimbra, a few years afterwards, among the Jesuits there.

Why did Francis leave, when things were going so well?

Because of China.

* * *

All through his time in Japan, Francis kept hearing about China. Whenever he argued with the learned men in Japan, they would say, sooner or later, something like this. If your religion is true, why have the Chinese never heard of it? The Chinese are the wisest people in the world. Our writing comes from China. Our books come from China. Our learning comes from China. If the Chinese had believed this, we would have heard of it.

And Francis began to think that if he could only bring the Gospel to China, then Japan would follow, and all of East Asia would listen.

There was one great problem. China was closed. The Ming emperors did not allow foreigners in. Foreign traders who came to the coast without permission could be arrested, and some had been, and some were still in Chinese prisons. The Portuguese were allowed to trade only on a few small islands off the coast, for a season at a time, and only unofficially.

But Francis had an idea. On his way back from Japan, he met an old friend, a merchant called Diogo Pereira, who had been very generous to him over the years. And together they worked out a plan. The Viceroy in India would send Diogo to China as an official ambassador, with letters and gifts for the emperor, and Francis would go with him. An ambassador could not be turned away. And once they were inside, they might ask that the Portuguese prisoners be set free, and that Francis be allowed to preach.

It was a good plan. Francis sailed back to India, and the Viceroy agreed to it, and Diogo spent a great deal of his own money on the ship and the presents. And in April fifteen fifty-two, Francis set off from Goa for the last time, on Diogo's ship, the Santa Cruz, heading for Malacca and then China.

And at Malacca, it all went wrong.

* * *

The captain of the fortress at Malacca was a man called Álvaro de Ataíde. He was a son of the great navigator Vasco da Gama. And he did not like Diogo Pereira. Perhaps he was jealous. Perhaps he wanted the profits of a voyage to China for himself. Whatever the reason, he simply refused to let the embassy go. He took the rudder off the Santa Cruz so it could not sail. He would not let Diogo leave.

Francis argued. He pleaded. He showed letters from the Viceroy. He showed a document from the Pope that gave him authority as the Pope's own representative in the East, a thing he had carried for eleven years and, the old accounts say, never once used. None of it made any difference. In the end, the captain agreed to let the ship go to the islands off China, to trade, and to let Francis go with it, but not as part of any embassy. Diogo had to stay behind.

So the great plan was finished. And Francis, who had used his Pope's document at last, and with a heavy heart, went on anyway, without an ambassador, without letters, without presents, as a poor priest, to see if he could find a way in by himself.

He took with him a young man called Antonio. Antonio was Chinese. He had studied at the Jesuit college in Goa, and he was going to be Francis's interpreter and companion. Remember Antonio. He is the reason we know how this story ends.

* * *

At the end of August, fifteen fifty-two, they came to Shangchuan.

Shangchuan is a small island, a few miles off the coast of southern China, south and west of where Hong Kong is now. It is hilly and green, with sandy bays, and in those days, during the trading season, the Portuguese ships would anchor there and the Chinese merchants would come out from the mainland in their boats, and the two sides would trade, quietly, with nobody asking too many questions. The traders put up huts of straw and branches along the beach. When the season ended, they sailed away, and left the huts to fall down.

Francis built himself a little chapel of branches, and said Mass there, and looked after the Portuguese sailors and merchants, heard their confessions, taught the children of the crews, visited the sick. And he looked for a way across.

He found a Chinese merchant who agreed, for a large sum, paid in pepper, to take him over to the mainland secretly by night, and leave him at the gates of the city of Canton, with his books. It was a very dangerous plan. If he was caught, he would probably be thrown in prison, or worse. And the merchant would be in trouble too, if anyone found out. The Portuguese on the island begged Francis not to do it. He said he was going anyway.

In October and November, he wrote his last letters. In one of them he wrote that the greatest danger of all would be to lose trust in God's mercy, for whose love and service he had come, and that he would rather face all the dangers of the mainland than that.

And then the merchant did not come.

The day was fixed. Francis waited on the beach with Antonio. The merchant did not come. They waited the next day. He did not come. One by one the Portuguese ships finished their trading and sailed away, until only the Santa Cruz was left. The weather turned cold. And still the merchant did not come. We will never know why. Perhaps he took fright. Perhaps he had only ever wanted the pepper.

[pause]

* * *

On the twenty-first of November, after saying Mass, Francis fell ill with a fever.

He went on board the Santa Cruz, where there were more comforts, but the rocking of the ship made him worse, and after a day or two he asked to be taken back to shore. They carried him to one of the straw huts on the beach. A Portuguese merchant there took him in. And Antonio stayed beside him.

Here we have Antonio's own account, which he gave some years later to the Jesuits who wanted to know how Francis died. It is simple, and it is very gentle, and I will tell it to you in my own words.

The fever grew worse. For some days Francis lay with his eyes on the heavens, Antonio said, and talked a great deal to God. Sometimes he talked in a language Antonio did not understand. Antonio thought it might have been his own mother tongue, the Basque language of his childhood, from the castle of Xavier in Navarre, which he had not spoken properly for thirty years.

I find that very moving. At the very end, after all those years of struggling with Tamil and Malay and Japanese, Francis went back to the words he had learned on his mother's knee.

Antonio remembered some of the things he said, in Latin and in Portuguese. Jesus, son of David, have mercy on me. And, O Virgin, Mother of God, remember me. Over and over, quietly.

Then he grew very still.

And on the night of the second of December, going into the third, a little before dawn, Antonio saw that he was dying. And he did what Christians had done for centuries at a deathbed. He lit a candle, and he put it into Francis's hand.

And so, by candlelight, in a straw hut on the beach of a small island, within sight of the coast he had wanted so badly to reach, with only one young Chinese man beside him, Francis Xavier said the name of Jesus, and went home to God.

He was forty-six years old.

[pause]

That is nearly the end of the story. But not quite.

They buried him on the island, the next day, in a wooden box, and packed the box with lime, so that his bones might one day be carried home. Only a handful of people came. Antonio was there. It was cold, and there was a wind.

Ten weeks later, when the Santa Cruz was ready to sail, they opened the grave to take the bones with them. And the old accounts say that they found his body as fresh as if he had been buried the day before. They took him to Malacca, in the spring, and then, the next year, to Goa, in India, where he had begun. He lies there still, in a silver casket, in the great church of the Good Jesus. Every ten years or so, the casket is brought out, and people come from all over the world, and from all the villages of the Fishery Coast, to see him. His right arm, the arm that baptised so many people, was sent to Rome, and it is in the Jesuit church there, the Gesù, not far from the tomb of his old roommate, Ignatius.

They were made saints on the same day, in sixteen twenty-two, Ignatius and Francis together, along with Teresa of Ávila and Philip Neri and Isidore the farmer. Rome had a very crowded day.

And the door of China?

Thirty years after Francis died on Shangchuan, another Jesuit, an Italian called Matteo Ricci, came to the coast. He did not try to rush in. He learned Chinese, really learned it, spoken and written, better than Francis ever learned any language. He dressed as a Chinese scholar. He made friends with Chinese scholars, and made maps for them, and talked with them about mathematics and the stars, just as Francis had said the missionaries should. In fifteen eighty-three, he was allowed to settle in China. In the end he reached the capital, Beijing, and he is buried there, the first foreigner given a burial place by the emperor.

Francis knocked on the door. Ricci was let in. I like to think that the knocking mattered.

And Japan? The little Church that Francis started there grew, for a while, very fast. By the end of the century there were hundreds of thousands of Christians in Japan. And then the persecutions came, terrible ones, and the missionaries were thrown out, and for more than two hundred years it was forbidden, on pain of death, to be a Christian in Japan.

And then, in eighteen sixty-five, when foreigners were allowed back, some French priests built a new church in Nagasaki. And one day in March, a small group of Japanese villagers came quietly into the church, and knelt beside the priest, and one of them whispered to him that their hearts were the same as his. And then they asked him, where is the statue of Holy Mary?

They were the hidden Christians. For seven generations, with no priests, no churches, no books, passing the prayers down in secret from parents to children, they had kept the faith that Francis and Fernández and Torres and Paul and Lorenzo had brought them. They had baptised their babies. They had kept the feasts. They had waited.

So that is the end of the story of Francis Xavier. It is, in a way, a story of plans that did not work. The emperor he walked through the snow to see had no power. The embassy to China was stopped at Malacca. The merchant never came. He died on the wrong side of a few miles of water.

And yet. A Japanese man who had run away found peace. An old abbot found a friend. A nearly blind minstrel found the best story he had ever heard. A young lord was baptised with Francis's name, twenty-seven years later. A door opened, thirty years later. And some villagers in Nagasaki, three hundred years later, still had their hearts in the same place.

God does not waste anything, you see. Not the snow on the road. Not the long wait on the beach. Not one candle in one straw hut.

And so, tonight, if there are plans of yours that did not work out, you can let them go. Put them down. They are not wasted. You can leave them on the beach. The tide will come in and the tide will go out, and the One who keeps the stars in their places will keep these too.

Let the wind blow softly in off the sea. Let the little waves come in, and go out, and come in. There is a light in the hut, and somebody is keeping watch. You can close your eyes now.

That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source tonight is again Francis Xavier's own letters, in Joseph Costelloe's complete English translation. The long letter from Kagoshima in November fifteen forty-nine, and the letter written after he left Japan, in January fifteen fifty-two, are the two to read. For the full story, the fourth volume of Georg Schurhammer's great biography covers Japan and China in astonishing detail, and gives the account of Antonio. For the wider history of the Church in Japan, Charles Boxer's The Christian Century in Japan is the classic. For the Jesuits in Japan and China together, Andrew Ross's A Vision Betrayed, and for the China mission after Ricci, Liam Brockey's Journey to the East.

What is history. The meeting with Anjirō at Malacca and his story, which Francis tells himself; his baptism at Goa as Paul; the voyage on the junk and the captain who cast lots; the landing at Kagoshima on the feast of the Assumption; the friendship with Ninshitsu; the book read aloud in Latin letters; the word Dainichi and the change to Deus; Hirado; the first visit to Yamaguchi; the winter journey to the capital and the eleven days there; the return to Yamaguchi with letters and gifts and the empty monastery; Lorenzo; Bungo; the plan with Diogo Pereira and its ruin at Malacca; the months on Shangchuan; his death there in early December fifteen fifty-two with Antonio beside him; the later history of Ricci and of the hidden Christians of Nagasaki.

What is the old accounts' story. The apple on the road to the capital, the spitting in Fernández's face, the candle in Francis's hand, his last words, and the language Antonio did not understand are told as the early Jesuit accounts and Antonio remembered them, and may have grown a little in the telling. The state of his body when the grave was opened is reported by the early accounts, and the Church has never made it an article of faith. The crab is a legend.

What we invented. Every word of the conversations with Ninshitsu, Bernardo, Lorenzo and the others, and most of the pictures of the roads, the snow and the island. We have told the story from where Francis stood. The Portuguese trade in the East, and the later history of Christianity in Japan, are more tangled than one bedtime story can hold, and the books above tell them honestly.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Francis Xavier for tonight. The Church keeps his feast on the third of December, the day he died on Shangchuan, and he is a patron of the missions. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Here are the words Antonio remembered him saying, again and again, at the end. Jesus, son of David, have mercy on me. O Virgin, Mother of God, remember me. Saint Francis Xavier, pray for us. Goodnight, and God bless.

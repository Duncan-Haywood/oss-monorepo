There are three men on a mountain.

It is early in the morning, and the sun has only just come over the ridge, and the rock is the colour of a ripe apricot. The three men are climbing slowly, one step and then another, along a goat path that winds up between the boulders.

One of them is younger. He has grey in his beard, but next to the other two he looks almost like a boy. He walks a little behind, and he keeps reaching out a hand, and then drawing it back, the way you do when you want to help someone who has not asked to be helped.

One of them is very old, with a staff, and a long white beard, and he does not look back at all.

And one of them is older still. Older than anyone you know. He wears a robe of deep blue, all one colour, and round the bottom of it there is a fringe of little pomegranates made of coloured yarn, and between every pomegranate there is a tiny golden bell.

And as he climbs, the bells ring.

Ting. Ting-ting. Ting.

Far below, at the foot of the mountain, a whole camp of people has stopped what they are doing, and they are standing in the doorways of their tents, shading their eyes, watching the three of them go up.

Nobody is saying very much.

So. Where are we?

After Kadesh, the people of Israel wanted to go north and east, round the bottom of the Dead Sea and up towards the river Jordan, because the land of promise lay on the other side of it. And the quickest way went straight through a country called Edom.

The people of Edom were cousins. Very distant cousins, but cousins. The book of Genesis says they came from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob, and the people of Israel came from Jacob. So Moses sent messengers to the king of Edom, and the messages were extremely polite. They said, more or less: your brother Israel says hello. You know what a hard time we have had. We have been slaves in Egypt and wandering in the desert for a very long time. Please may we walk through your country. We will stay on the king's road, the main road. We will not go into your fields or your vineyards. We will not even drink from your wells. And if our animals drink any of your water, we will pay for it.

Which, if you think about it, is the most courteous request ever made by a crowd of several hundred thousand people and their goats.

And the king of Edom said no.

They asked again, even more nicely. And he said no again, and came out to the border with a large army to make the point. So the people of Israel turned away. They did not fight their cousins. They went the long way round.

And the first place they came to, on the long way round, at the edge of the land of Edom, was a mountain.

The book calls it Hor. Which, as it happens, is not much of a name. In Hebrew the book calls it Hor ha-Har, Hor the mountain, and some people think Hor may itself be an old word for a mountain. As if you lived near a big hill and called it Big Hill. Where exactly it was, nobody is sure. We will come back to that at the end, because there is an old tradition about it, and a very beautiful place that people have climbed for a long time.

But for now, all we need is a mountain. Red rock, and boulders, and a goat path, and a top you can see from the camp.

* * *

Now I want to tell you about Aaron, because he is a hundred and twenty-three years old tonight, and that is a lot of years to sum up in a few minutes. I will do my best.

Aaron was the older brother of Moses, by three years. The book of Exodus tells us that when they first stood in front of Pharaoh, Moses was eighty and Aaron was eighty-three. And here we are, forty years later. You can do the sum.

When the Lord first called Moses, at the burning bush, Moses said something that I have always found very comforting. He said: I am not a good talker. I never have been. I am slow of speech and slow of tongue. Please send somebody else.

And the Lord said, a little sharply: is not Aaron your brother? I know he can speak well. He is coming out to meet you now, and when he sees you he will be glad in his heart. He will be your mouth.

So that is how Aaron came into the story. As a mouth. As the brother who was glad to see you, and who would say the hard things out loud when you could not get them out. He stood next to Moses in front of the king of Egypt. He threw down his staff and it became a snake. He was there for the frogs, and the gnats, and the long night when the people walked out.

Later, at the holy mountain of Sinai, the Lord chose Aaron and his sons to be priests. Aaron became the first high priest of Israel. He was the one who went into the tent of meeting, where the Lord met with his people, and offered the sacrifices, and burned the incense, and lifted his hands at the end and blessed everyone.

He was not perfect. I should be honest about that, because the Bible is. When Moses was up the mountain for forty days, and the people got bored and frightened, it was Aaron who collected all their gold earrings and made them a golden calf. And when Moses came down and asked him what on earth he thought he was doing, Aaron gave what is possibly the worst excuse in all of Scripture. He said: well, I threw the gold into the fire, and out came this calf.

As if it had just happened. As if the calf had hopped out on its own.

I mention it not to be unkind to him, but because I think it matters that he was forgiven, and that God went on using him. The Bible does not make its holy people out of marble. It makes them out of the same stuff as the rest of us, and then it shows you what God can do with that.

And Aaron had sorrows, too. His two eldest sons, Nadab and Abihu, died suddenly, on the very first days of the priesthood, because they offered fire before the Lord that he had not commanded. It is a hard story and we will not tell it tonight. But there is one line in it I want you to have. After it happened, and Moses spoke to him, the book says simply: and Aaron held his peace.

He did not shout. He did not run away. He stayed, and he was quiet, and he went on serving.

So that is our Aaron. The mouth of Moses. The priest. The brother who was glad. The man who made a terrible mistake and was forgiven, and the father who held his peace. And in the last few years, the man who ran into the middle of a plague with a censer of incense in his hands and stood between the dead and the living, and whose old dry staff budded overnight and grew almonds.

He has two sons left. Eleazar, and Ithamar. Eleazar is the elder of the two, and he has been working beside his father for nearly forty years. He is in charge of the oil for the lamps, and the sweet incense, and the anointing oil, and all the holy things of the tent. He is the one who will matter tonight.

And Aaron has his wife, Elisheba, whom we met a few episodes ago. The book does not tell us whether she is still alive at Mount Hor, so I will not tell you either.

Now. The robe.

I have to tell you about the robe, because it is going to be very important, and because it is one of the loveliest things in the whole of the Old Testament.

When Aaron became high priest, at Sinai, the Lord told Moses exactly what he should wear. It takes a whole chapter of the book of Exodus, and it is very detailed, the kind of detail that makes you think whoever wrote it down had actually held the cloth in their hands.

Next to his skin, there was a long coat of fine linen, woven in a pattern. Round his waist, a sash. On his head, a turban of linen, and tied to the front of the turban with a blue cord, a little plate of pure gold, and engraved on the plate, like the engraving on a seal, three words. Holy to the Lord.

Over the coat went the robe. And the robe was all of blue. Not blue with stripes, not blue with a pattern. All one deep blue, from the shoulders to the feet, with a hole for the head that was bound round like the neck of a coat of mail, so that it would not tear.

And round the hem, as I said, there were pomegranates. Little round pomegranates made of yarn, blue and purple and scarlet. And between them, all the way round, golden bells.

The book even says why the bells were there. So that the sound of them would be heard when Aaron went into the holy place before the Lord, and when he came out.

Think about that for a moment. The people outside could not see into the holy place. They could not go in. But they could hear. They could stand in the courtyard and listen to the little bells moving about inside, and know that the priest was there, praying for them, going to and fro. And when the bells came nearer and nearer the curtain, they knew he was coming out to bless them.

The bells were a way of saying: I am still here. I have not forgotten you.

Over the blue robe went the ephod, which was a kind of apron, or tabard, of gold and blue and purple and scarlet and fine linen, all woven together. On each shoulder it had an onyx stone, set in gold, and on the two stones were engraved the names of the twelve sons of Israel, six on each. So that Aaron carried the names of the people on his shoulders whenever he went in.

And on his chest, tied to the ephod with gold chains and blue cords, was the breastpiece. A square pocket of the same rich cloth, with twelve precious stones set in it, in four rows of three. Every stone had the name of one of the tribes engraved on it. So Aaron carried the names of the people over his heart, too.

On his shoulders and over his heart. I think that is a good picture of what a priest is for.

And here is the part I want you to remember. The book of Exodus says that these holy garments were not only for Aaron. They were to be passed on. The son who became priest after him would wear them, and be anointed in them, and be ordained in them. The robes were made to outlast the man.

Now let me introduce you to someone who is not in the Bible.

Her name is Meira, and she is our invention. She is about fifty-two years old, and she is a grandmother, and she lives in a tent on the edge of the camp with her son's family, and she has a grandson of about seven who follows her everywhere and asks a great many questions.

And when Meira was a girl of twelve, at Sinai, she helped to make the robe.

I don't mean she wove it. That was the work of the master craftsmen, Bezalel and Oholiab and their helpers, the people the Lord had filled with skill. But the book of Exodus says something lovely about how the materials were made. It says that all the women whose hearts stirred them up, and who had skill, spun with their hands, and brought what they had spun. Blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine linen. And some of them spun goats' hair.

So we have imagined that Meira was one of those girls. That she sat at the door of her mother's tent, at the foot of the mountain of God, with a lump of wool dyed the deep blue on her lap, and a spindle turning in her fingers, and her mother saying, thinner, thinner, keep it even, it's for the priest.

And that some of that blue thread, her thread, went into the robe.

She has never told anybody this, except her grandson. It is not the sort of thing you go around saying. Oh, by the way, that bit of the high priest is mine. But every time Aaron came out of the tent in his blue robe, for forty years, Meira looked at it, and she thought: there it is. Thinner, thinner, keep it even.

She is the one who is going to watch the mountain for us.

The people came to Mount Hor, and they set up camp at the bottom of it, the way they had done in so many places. The tents went up. The fires were lit. The tent of meeting was pitched in the middle, with the tribes all round it, and the cloud of the Lord above it.

And the Lord spoke to Moses and Aaron, there at Mount Hor, on the border of the land of Edom.

I am going to tell you what he said quite carefully, because it is Scripture, and I do not want to add anything to it.

The Lord said: Aaron shall be gathered to his people. He shall not enter the land which I have given to the people of Israel, because you rebelled against my command at the waters of Meribah. Take Aaron, and Eleazar his son, and bring them up Mount Hor. And strip Aaron of his garments, and put them on Eleazar his son. And Aaron shall be gathered to his people, and shall die there.

That's it. That is all he said.

There is a phrase in there that I love, even though it is a sad one. Gathered to his people. It is what the Old Testament says when somebody good dies. Abraham was gathered to his people. Isaac. Jacob. It is a phrase that sounds like a family reunion. Like coming in from the fields at the end of the day, and all the people you love are already sitting round the table, and somebody shifts up to make room.

It does not tell us everything about what happens after death. The Old Testament is quite quiet about that, and it was Our Lord, much later, who would open that door properly. But even here, at the very edge of the wilderness, the people of Israel did not talk about death as a wall. They talked about it as a gathering.

The book does not tell us what Moses and Aaron did that evening, between the word of the Lord and the morning. So we are going to imagine it. Remember, this part is ours.

Let's say it is after dark. The camp has gone quiet. The tent of meeting is glowing faintly in the middle of everything, the way it always does, and the cloud is over it.

And two very old men are sitting outside a tent, on two stones, with a little fire between them.

They do not talk for a long time. Brothers often don't. They have known each other for a hundred and twenty years, and they have been through Egypt and the sea and the mountain and the calf and the quails and the spies and the plague and the staffs and the rock together. There is not very much left that needs to be said out loud.

After a while, Aaron says, "Do you remember the bush?"

And Moses says, "I'm not likely to forget it."

"No. I mean, do you remember what you said? You said you couldn't talk."

"I still can't," says Moses.

"You've been talking for forty years," says Aaron. "You talk to the whole camp. You talk to kings. You talk to God, face to face, like a man talks to his friend. I've heard you."

Moses pokes the fire with the end of his staff and says nothing.

"I used to talk for you," says Aaron. "At the beginning. In front of Pharaoh. You'd whisper in my ear and I'd say it louder. I liked it. I always liked it. Being your mouth." He smiles in the firelight. "You don't need me to do that any more. You haven't for years."

"I still need you," says Moses.

"I know," says Aaron. "But you'll manage. And Eleazar will be there. He's a good boy."

"He's got grey in his beard."

"He's a good boy," says Aaron firmly, the way fathers do.

And then they are quiet again. And the fire burns down. And somewhere in the camp a baby cries, and somebody sings to it, and it stops.

And after a long time, Aaron gets up, slowly, the way you do at a hundred and twenty-three, with a hand on his knee. And he puts his other hand on his little brother's head, just for a moment, the way he used to when Moses was small. And then he goes inside to sleep.

That is all our invention. But I don't think it is very far from the truth.

In the morning, the book says, Moses did as the Lord commanded. And they went up Mount Hor in the sight of all the congregation.

In the sight of all the congregation. Everybody saw them go.

I don't know how the word got round the camp. Maybe Moses told the elders. Maybe Eleazar told his wife and she told her sister. Maybe nobody said anything at all, and people just looked out of their tents and saw the high priest in his full robes walking away from the tent of meeting, towards the mountain, with his brother and his son, and they understood.

However it happened, they knew.

And Meira knew. She was standing at the door of her tent with her grandson, and she had her hand on his shoulder, and she was watching the three small figures start up the path at the foot of the mountain.

"Where are they going?" said the boy.

"Up the mountain," said Meira.

"Why?"

"Because God told them to."

"Why is the priest wearing his best clothes?"

Meira did not answer that one for a moment.

"Because he is going to meet God," she said.

The boy thought about this. "He meets God every day," he said. "In the tent."

"Yes," said Meira. "He does."

And they watched. The path went up, and turned, and went up again. Every now and then, when the sun caught it, you could see the blue of the robe against the red rock. Just a speck. Then gone behind a boulder. Then there again, a little higher.

All across the camp, people were doing the same. Standing still. Holding their children's hands. Not cooking, not mending, not driving the goats out to whatever grass there was. Just watching the mountain.

It took a long time. Old men climb slowly. And the higher they went, the smaller they got, until even Meira, who had very good eyes, could hardly tell which speck was which.

And then they were at the top, and they went over some little rise or behind some rock, and she could not see them at all.

* * *

What happened at the top of the mountain, the book tells us in one sentence.

And Moses stripped Aaron of his garments, and put them upon Eleazar his son. And Aaron died there on the top of the mountain.

That's all. One sentence. I have read it many times, and I think it is one of the gentlest deaths in the Bible.

I am going to tell you how I picture it, but please remember that the order of things, and the way they did it, is my imagining, not the book's.

I picture the three of them at the top, out of breath, in the wind. The whole wide country spread out below them, the red hills going away into the haze, and the camp like a scattering of seeds at the bottom.

And I picture Moses, who had been given Aaron as a mouth all those years ago, now doing a very quiet thing with his hands.

He unties the gold plate from the front of the turban. Holy to the Lord. And takes off the turban.

He unfastens the gold chains and the blue cords of the breastpiece, with the twelve stones and the twelve names. The names Aaron has carried over his heart for forty years.

He lifts off the ephod, with the two onyx stones and the other names. The ones Aaron carried on his shoulders.

And last of all, I think, the blue robe. Over the old man's head. The bells ringing a little as it goes, ting, ting, and then still.

And each thing, as Moses takes it off his brother, he puts on Eleazar. The robe over Eleazar's head. The bells at Eleazar's feet. The ephod on Eleazar's shoulders, with the names. The breastpiece on Eleazar's heart. The turban on his head, and the gold plate on his forehead. Holy to the Lord.

So that the priesthood never stops. Not even for a minute. Not even on a mountaintop. One priest takes off the robe and the next one is already wearing it.

And then Aaron, in his plain linen, just an old man now, sitting down on a rock in the morning sun, or lying down, perhaps, with his son's hand and his brother's hand.

And he was gathered to his people.

The book does not give him any last words. I have thought about whether to give him some, and I have decided not to. I think the book is right. Sometimes there is nothing left that needs saying, and the best thing is to be quiet together.

And Aaron held his peace.

There is a lovely saying of the Jewish rabbis about this, which I want to give you, as long as you remember it is a saying of the rabbis, written down a long time later, and not part of the Book of Numbers.

In another chapter, the book says that Aaron went up Mount Hor, and died there, at the command of the Lord. In Hebrew, at the command of is literally at the mouth of. At the mouth of the Lord. And the rabbis of the Talmud noticed this. And they said that Moses, and Aaron, and Miriam, the three of them, did not die the way other people die. They said that the Lord took them, as it were, with a kiss.

I don't know how you would ever check a thing like that. But I like it. I like that the people who read this story most carefully, for the longest time, came away thinking that the death on Mount Hor was not a punishment so much as a goodnight.

At the bottom of the mountain, people were still watching.

The sun had climbed. It was hot now. This was the fifth month of the year, the book tells us, the first day of it, which in our calendars falls somewhere in high summer. The rocks would have been shimmering. Some people had gone back inside, or into the shade. Some had sat down where they were.

Meira had not moved. Her grandson had got bored, as small boys do, and wandered off, and come back with a lizard, and been told to put it down, and wandered off again, and come back without the lizard. But Meira stayed at the door of the tent, watching the top of the mountain.

And then she saw them.

Coming over the top. Coming down.

Two of them.

Only two.

She did not say anything. Neither did anybody else. All across the camp, the people who had been watching stood up, very slowly, and stopped talking, and the people inside came out to see why it had gone so quiet.

Two figures, coming down the goat path. One of them, the old one, with the staff. Moses.

And the other one was wearing blue.

That was how they knew. That was how Meira knew. The book says that when all the congregation saw that Aaron was dead, they wept. And I have always thought that the way they saw it was the robe. Because the blue robe was coming down the mountain, but it was on the wrong man.

It took a long time for the two of them to come down. Long enough for the whole camp to gather at the bottom of the path, in a great silent crowd. Long enough for the grandson to come and stand by Meira's leg and take hold of her skirt without being told.

And when they came at last to the foot of the mountain, onto the flat ground, and walked into the camp between the tents, Meira heard it.

The bells.

Ting. Ting-ting. Ting.

The same bells. The same sound she had heard every day for forty years, coming out of the tent of meeting. I am still here. I have not forgotten you. Only now it was Eleazar walking in them, a little stiffly, a little shy, the way you are in clothes that were made for someone else. * * *

The book says: all the house of Israel wept for Aaron thirty days.

All the house of Israel. Not just his family. Not just the priests and the Levites. Everybody.

The Jewish commentators who read this book very carefully over many centuries noticed that. Later, when Moses dies, the book says that the people of Israel wept for him thirty days. But for Aaron it says all the house of Israel. And the old teachers explained it like this. They said Aaron was the one who made peace. Between neighbours who had quarrelled. Between husbands and wives. Moses was the one who gave the law, and the law is sometimes hard. But Aaron was the one who came round afterwards and helped people make up. So everybody had a reason to miss him.

There is a saying in the Mishnah, a Jewish book from about the year two hundred, from the great teacher Hillel. He said: be one of the disciples of Aaron. Loving peace, and pursuing peace. Loving people, and bringing them near.

I think that is a good thing to be said about you, when you have gone up your mountain. That you loved peace, and went looking for it. That you loved people, and brought them near.

So for thirty days, the camp did not move on. They stayed at the foot of Mount Hor. People sat on the ground, the way mourners did. They told stories about him. The old people, the few who were left from Egypt, told the young ones about the day Aaron stood in front of Pharaoh, and the day he lifted up his hands at the first sacrifice at Sinai and blessed them all, and fire came out from before the Lord and the whole people shouted and fell on their faces.

And the young people told each other about the plague, and the censer, and the almonds. And somebody probably told the story of the golden calf, and somebody else probably said, not now, and somebody else said, no, it's all right, he'd have laughed. He did say it just came out like that.

After the thirty days, the people moved on from Mount Hor. They went the long way round the land of Edom, as the king had insisted, and it was a hard road, and they grumbled about it, and there were more stories still to come. But that is for another night.

Moses led them on. He was alone now, the last of the three. Miriam at Kadesh, and Aaron on Mount Hor, and Moses would go up one more mountain, Mount Nebo, and see the whole land spread out in front of him, and die there, towards the end of that same year. When the Lord spoke to him about it, he said: you shall die on the mountain which you go up, as Aaron your brother died on Mount Hor, and was gathered to his people. So even then, at the very end, Moses was thinking of his big brother. Going up a mountain the way Aaron had gone up one.

Eleazar served as high priest for many years. He stood beside Joshua when Joshua was given the leadership of the people. He and Joshua together divided the land of promise among the tribes, when at last they crossed the river. And when Eleazar died, very old himself, they buried him on a hill in the hill country of Ephraim, in the town of his own son, Phinehas. And Phinehas served the Lord after him.

And as for the mountain.

Nobody knows for certain where Mount Hor was. There is one very old tradition, as old as the first century at least, because the Jewish historian Josephus writes about it, that places it in the mountains near the city that the Greeks called Petra, the rose-red city carved into the rock, in what is now the country of Jordan. There is a tall mountain there that the people of the region have called, for a long time, Jabal Harun. The mountain of Aaron.

On the very top of it there is a small white shrine with a dome, a holy place for Muslims, who also honour Aaron, whom they call Harun, as a prophet and the brother of Moses. And on a high shoulder of the mountain, just below the top, there are the ruins of an old Christian monastery from the Byzantine centuries, dedicated to Saint Aaron, where monks lived and prayed and welcomed pilgrims who had climbed all the way up. Archaeologists from Finland have spent years digging it out of the rubble, very carefully, with little brushes.

Many scholars think that Jabal Harun is too far from Kadesh to be the Mount Hor of the book, and they look for it elsewhere, nearer the wilderness of Zin. And the book of Deuteronomy, in a short list of places, says that Aaron died and was buried at a place called Moserah, which may be a camp near the foot of the mountain, or may be another tradition altogether. We do not know. We have not settled it, and I don't think we need to.

But I like to think of those monks, high up on the mountain of Aaron, ringing their bell for prayers in the morning. Bells on a mountain again.

There is one more thing.

Christians have always read the Book of Numbers alongside the New Testament. And there is a letter in the New Testament, the Letter to the Hebrews, which spends a long time thinking about Aaron and the priesthood. It says that nobody takes the honour of being a priest for himself. He is called by God, just as Aaron was.

And then it says something that I think the people at the foot of Mount Hor would have understood very well. It says: the former priests were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing in office. But Christ holds his priesthood for ever, because he continues for ever. And so he is always able to save those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them.

Many priests, because each one had to take off the robe in the end, and pass it on, and go up the mountain. And one priest who never has to take it off.

The bells, you might say, never stop.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty, verses twenty-two to twenty-nine, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The journey from Kadesh, the Lord's words to Moses and Aaron at Mount Hor on the border of Edom, the climb in the sight of all the congregation, the garments taken from Aaron and put on Eleazar, Aaron's death on the top, the two coming down, and the thirty days of weeping are all the book's own, told plainly. Chapter thirty-three gives the date, the first day of the fifth month of the fortieth year, and Aaron's age, a hundred and twenty-three. The refusal of the king of Edom is in chapter twenty, verses fourteen to twenty-one.

The robes are described in Exodus, chapters twenty-eight and thirty-nine, and the women spinning blue yarn are in chapter thirty-five. That the garments pass to the son is Exodus, chapter twenty-nine. Moses's words about his brother's death are in Deuteronomy, chapter thirty-two, and Deuteronomy, chapter ten, names the place Moserah; we have not settled that. Eleazar's later life is in Numbers, chapter twenty-seven, and Joshua, chapters fourteen and twenty-four.

The saying about a kiss is from the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Bava Batra, and Hillel's words are in the Mishnah, tractate Avot. Both are later Jewish tradition, told as tradition. Josephus wrote of the place near Petra in his Jewish Antiquities. The words from Hebrews are chapters five and seven.

And the woman Meira, her blue thread, her grandson, the order in which the robes were taken off, and every word between Moses and Aaron on that last evening, are our own invention.

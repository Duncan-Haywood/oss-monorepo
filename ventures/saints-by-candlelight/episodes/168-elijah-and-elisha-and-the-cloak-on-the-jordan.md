Let me tell you where we are.

We are in the land of Israel, in the northern kingdom, about eight hundred and fifty years before the birth of Christ. It is a small country, and a hilly one. If you walked across it from the sea to the river Jordan, you could do it in a few days, and most of those days would be uphill or downhill, and very few would be flat.

In the middle of it runs a spine of hills, the hill country, with olive trees on the terraces and stony fields between them and little towns on top of every hill that could hold one. To the east, the hills drop away, suddenly and steeply, into a long, deep trench. At the bottom of the trench is the Jordan, a green ribbon of a river, winding south towards a sea so salty that nothing lives in it.

And the drop is real. From the hill town of Bethel, where part of tonight's story happens, down to the oasis of Jericho, near the river, you go down well over three thousand feet, in not much more than a day's walk. Jericho sits below the level of the sea. It is warm there in winter when the hills are cold. It is the place, even now, where people go to get out of the rain.

If you have been listening to this show for a while, you have met our two travellers before.

The older one is Elijah. Elijah the Tishbite, from Tishbe in Gilead, on the far side of the river. He is the one the ravens fed, bread and meat in the morning and bread and meat in the evening, which is better catering than most of us get. The Second Book of Kings, the first chapter, gives us a description of him. Somebody asks, what did this man look like? And the answer is: he was a hairy man, with a belt of leather about his waist. Some translations say he wore a garment of hair, which is perhaps more likely. Either way, you get the picture. He is not a tidy man. He is a man who lives outdoors, and who has not been near a barber since the reign of the last king but one.

It is also, by the time of tonight's story, a complicated one. "It's sour again," says Adah, sniffing the jar he has brought her. "You can smell it."

And Elisha says: Bring me a new bowl, and put salt in it.

A new bowl. Not an old one. One that has never been used for anything else. And salt. Plain salt. You might think that the very last thing a bad spring needs is salt. Salt is what you put on ground so that nothing will grow there. And that, I think, is rather the point. Nobody could look at a bowl of salt and think that the salt had done it.

And they brought it to him.

I like to imagine that Adah had a hand in that. That when word went round the lane that the new prophet wanted a new bowl, she sent Ezer running home for the one she had been keeping on the high shelf for a wedding. Not a grand bowl. Just a new one, of red clay, from the potter by the gate, never used. And a handful of salt from her own jar, because if it was going to be salt, it might as well be good salt.

That is my invention, and you may keep it or not.

And Elisha went out to the spring of the waters, the place where the water came up out of the ground at the foot of the old mound, and he threw the salt in there.

And he said: Thus says the Lord. I have healed these waters. From now on, there shall not be from there any more death, or unfruitfulness.

Listen to how he says it. Not, I have healed these waters. Thus says the Lord, I have healed them. The Lord is the one speaking. The Lord is the one healing. Elisha is the one who brought the bowl and said the words he was given. That is how it always is with the prophets, and with the saints. They pray, and they do the small, obedient thing, and God acts.

And the book says: So the waters were healed, to this day, according to the word that Elisha spoke.

There is a spring at Jericho to this day. It comes up at the foot of the old mound that archaeologists have been digging into for more than a century, and it still runs out into the gardens and the date palms, and the people there call it the Spring of the Sultan, and Christians and pilgrims have long called it Elisha's Spring. Nobody can prove that it is the very same spring as the one in the book. But it is the great spring of Jericho, the reason there was a Jericho in the first place, and it has been watering that oasis for longer than anyone has been writing things down. If you go there, they will show it to you. And the water is good.

As for Elisha, he went on. He went on for a long time. The Bible gives him chapter after chapter: the widow's jars of oil, that filled and filled until there were no more jars. The woman of Shunem, and her little room on the roof, and her son. The Syrian general Naaman, who dipped seven times in this same Jordan, very grumpily, and came up with skin like a little child's. The borrowed axe head that floated. The hills full of horses and chariots of fire, around the little town of Dothan, that only his servant could not see, until Elisha prayed, Lord, open his eyes. We have told some of those on other nights, and you can find them, if you like.

He lived to be an old man. And when he was lying ill at the end of his life, as I said, the king of Israel came and wept over him, and said: my father, my father, the chariots of Israel and its horsemen. The same words Elisha had cried by the river. The son had become the father in his turn.

And Elijah?

The last book of the Old Testament, the prophet Malachi, ends with a promise. Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the Lord comes. And he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children, and the hearts of children to their fathers. Fathers and children. You can see why, after tonight, that would be the thing Elijah would be sent to do.

Centuries later, an angel in the temple told an old priest called Zechariah that his son would go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children. And that son was John the Baptist. Who wore a garment of camel's hair, and a leather belt round his waist, and who baptised people in the Jordan, and an old tradition puts his baptising very near the place where tonight's story happened.

And on a high mountain, one day, three disciples saw the Lord Jesus shining like the sun, and with him, talking with him, two men. Moses, and Elijah. And Saint Luke tells us what they were talking about. They were speaking of his departure, which he was going to accomplish at Jerusalem.

Christians have always loved the story of Elijah going up, and have often seen in it a sign pointing ahead, to a greater going up. Elijah was taken. The Lord, forty days after Easter, ascended in his own power, with his disciples watching, as Elisha watched. And a cloud took him out of their sight. And angels said to the disciples, why do you stand looking up into heaven? And they went back to Jerusalem, with the Spirit promised to them. A double portion, you might say. That is a reading, an old and much loved one, and I give it to you as that.

And the Church honours them both. Elijah is one of the most loved saints of the East, with churches on hilltops all over Greece, because so many of his stories happen on mountains. And the Carmelites, in the West, look back to him on Mount Carmel as a father of their way of life, which is a tradition they have kept with great love for a very long time.

So tonight, as you lie in the dark, I would like you to think about the people who have walked in front of you. The ones who taught you, or carried you, or put a cloak round your shoulders when you did not know yet what it meant. Some of them are still on the road with you. Some of them have gone on ahead.

And if you are standing at the edge of some river tonight, with somebody's old cloak in your hands, wondering whether God went with them or stayed with you, I would like you to know what Elisha found out. Where is the Lord, the God of Elijah? He is right there. He did not go anywhere. The water parts just the same.

Somewhere in Jericho, an old woman is sitting by her door in the cool of the evening, with a jar of water beside her that smells of nothing at all. And her grandson, back from three days in the hills, with mud to his knees, is telling her all about it, and she is listening, for once, without any opinions.

And down by the spring, the water is running out into the channels, the way it always did, past the date palms, past the gardens, out into the dark. And somewhere a new bowl, of red clay, that was meant for a wedding, has been washed, and dried, and put back on the high shelf.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Kings, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The walk from Gilgal to Bethel to Jericho to the Jordan, the companies of the prophets, the cloak striking the water, the double portion, the chariots and horses of fire, the whirlwind, the torn clothes, the cloak taken up, the fifty men and their three days' search, and the salt in the new bowl at the spring of Jericho, are all in chapter two, verses one to twenty-two. The words of Elijah, of Elisha, of the companies of prophets and of the men of Jericho are kept as the book gives them, in a plain rendering.

The call of Elisha at the plough is in the First Book of Kings, chapter nineteen. Elijah's hairy garment is in the Second Book of Kings, chapter one, and the line about pouring water on his hands is in chapter three. The king weeping at Elisha's bedside is in chapter thirteen. The calf at Bethel is in the First Book of Kings, chapter twelve. The double portion of the firstborn is in Deuteronomy, chapter twenty-one. The promise of Elijah is at the end of Malachi, John the Baptist in the spirit of Elijah is in the first chapter of Luke, and the Transfiguration is in Luke, chapter nine. The Book of Sirach, chapter forty-eight, praises both prophets.

Which Gilgal the walk began from is a real open question. That the spring called Elisha's Spring today is the one in the book is an old identification, not a proved one. The verses that follow tonight's passage, at Bethel, are a hard passage that we have left for another time.

And Ezer and his grandmother Adah, her bowl, the conversations in Jericho, and what Elijah and Elisha said to each other on the road, are our own invention.

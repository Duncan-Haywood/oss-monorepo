A big clay pot is sitting on a fire in a courtyard, and it smells wonderful.

That is the first thing to say about it, because it matters later. It smells of onion, and of something green, and of the thin, sweet steam that comes off a pot when it has been bubbling for a good long while. Round the fire, sitting on the ground with their knees up, there are a great many thin young men, and every single one of them is watching that pot the way a dog watches a door.

Someone ladles it out. Bowls go round. The first spoonfuls go down.

And then one young man, about halfway round the circle, stops chewing. His face changes. He looks at the man next to him, and the man next to him has stopped chewing too.

And somebody, nobody can remember afterwards who, shouts across the courtyard at the old man sitting by the door.

Man of God! There is death in the pot!

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the Prophet Elisha and a pot of stew in a hungry year, and twenty small loaves of barley bread, from the Second Book of Kings, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a young man called Ira who gathers the wrong thing, and an older man called Hodiah who keeps the stores, and the small conversations around them, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let me tell you where we are.

We are in the land of Israel, in the northern kingdom, a few years after the last story we told about Elisha. If you heard that one, you will remember how it ended. The Prophet Elijah was taken up in a whirlwind beyond the river Jordan, and his cloak fell, and his disciple Elisha picked it up, and walked back to the water, and struck it, and said, Where is the Lord, the God of Elijah? And the water parted. And Elisha was on his own.

Well. Not quite on his own.

Because all through those stories there is a group of people the Bible calls the sons of the prophets. That does not mean their fathers were prophets, though some of them may have been. It is an old Hebrew way of saying the members of something, the way you might say a son of the village, or one of the lads from the regiment. A better English phrase might be the company of the prophets. They were men, and some of them had wives and children, as we know from another story, who gathered round a prophet to learn from him, and pray with him, and run his errands, and live, as far as we can tell, a fairly simple and rather poor life.

There were companies like this at Bethel, and at Jericho, and at a place called Gilgal. And Elisha, after Elijah was taken, seems to have gone round them, the way a bishop goes round his parishes, or a headmaster round the classrooms, except with more walking and fewer car parks.

Now, Gilgal.

There is a little difficulty with Gilgal, which is that there seems to have been more than one. The word means something like a circle, or a ring of stones, and it is the sort of name that a place gets when it has a ring of stones in it, and there were rings of stones in more places than one. The most famous Gilgal is down in the Jordan valley, near Jericho, where Joshua set up twelve stones from the river bed when the people of Israel first crossed into the land. It is the place where they kept their first Passover in the land, and the very next day, the book of Joshua says, the manna stopped. From then on they ate the produce of the land.

I mention that because it is a nice thing to have in your head tonight. Gilgal was the place where the bread from heaven stopped and the people had to start growing their own.

Whether tonight's Gilgal is that one, or another Gilgal up in the hills, nobody is quite sure, and I am not going to pretend I know. Either way, it was a place where a company of prophets lived, and Elisha came to visit.

And he came in a bad year.

The book says it very simply. There was a famine in the land.

I want to stop on that for a moment, because it is easy to hear the word famine and imagine a film. Something dramatic, sudden, people falling down in the road. Usually it was not like that. Usually it was slow. The rains came late, or came too little, or came at the wrong time, so the barley came up thin and the wheat came up thinner. The stores from last year ran down. The price of a measure of flour went up, and up, and kept going up. People ate less, and then they ate less again. They ate the things they usually gave to animals. They went out into the fields and the wild places and they gathered whatever was green and might, with a bit of luck and a long time in the pot, be eaten.

That last part is where our story begins. Hold on to it.

And one more thing before we start, about the company of the prophets. If you go a little further on in the Second Book of Kings, there is a story where they come to Elisha and say, very politely, Look, the place where we live under your charge is too small for us. So they go down to the Jordan to cut beams and build a bigger one, and someone drops a borrowed axe head in the river, and it is a lovely story which we told a long while ago.

So we know two things about this group. There were a lot of them, enough that their house kept getting too small. And they did not have much. Even the axe was borrowed.

Now put those two things together with a famine, and you have a great many young men, in a house that is too small, in a year when there is nothing to eat.

And into that walks Elisha.

* * *

Let me introduce you to Hodiah.

Hodiah is our own invention. The book does not give him to us, but I think every community that has ever existed has a Hodiah, so I am fairly confident there was one at Gilgal, whatever he was really called. Hodiah was the one who kept the stores.

He was not old, exactly, but he was older than most of the others, and he had a short grey beard and a habit of counting things under his breath. When the company of the prophets came back from a day out and someone said, I am starving, what is there, it was Hodiah who said, There is half a jar of oil, a bag of lentils that I am saving, and a quantity of dried figs which I am not going to tell you about.

In a good year, this made him slightly annoying. In a famine year, it made him the most important man in the house.

On the morning Elisha came, Hodiah was standing in the store room looking at the bottom of the flour jar.

There was some flour in it. Not a lot. If you had held the jar up and shaken it, you would have heard a soft sort of hiss rather than anything with weight to it. He had been keeping it back for a very bad day, and he was trying to decide whether today was a very bad day or only a fairly bad one.

And then someone ran in from the gate and said, He's here. The master's here. Elisha's coming up the road.

And Hodiah put the lid back on the flour jar, and thought, well, that settles it. Today is a day for guests.

* * *

Now let me introduce you to Ira.

Ira is also our own invention. Ira was young, not long in the company, and keen in the way that young people are keen when they have just joined something and very much want to be useful. He had grown up in a town, not on a farm, which is relevant. He was the sort of young man who, if you asked whether anyone could fetch water, would be halfway to the well before you had finished the sentence, and would then come back and ask which well.

He was also, that year, very hungry, which made him keener still. There is nothing like an empty stomach for making you want to go and find something to put in it.

So the house got ready.

Somebody swept the courtyard. Somebody went to find the one good mat. Several people tried to look as if they had been studying all morning. And when Elisha came in through the gate, with his servant behind him, carrying a staff and a bag, they all stood up, and then sat down again, on the ground, in front of him.

The book says it like that. The sons of the prophets were sitting before him. That is what disciples did. They sat at the feet of the teacher. Not on chairs, there were no chairs, but on the ground, in a half circle, with the teacher on the one stool, or on the step by the door, and they listened.

I do not know what Elisha taught them that day. The book does not say, and I am not going to make it up, because it would be putting words into the mouth of a prophet. But I like to think it was something about the Lord who feeds. It would have been on everybody's mind. A teacher does not usually choose the hungriest day of the year to talk about anything else.

And at some point, Elisha looked round at all those faces. All those thin young faces, looking at him very attentively and also, if he was honest, slightly past him, at the door to the store room.

And he turned to his servant, and he said, in the words the book gives him: Set on the great pot, and boil stew for the sons of the prophets.

* * *

I would like to say a word about the great pot, because I am fond of it.

Every house of that kind, every monastery and every school and every barracks, has one. It is the pot that comes out for feasts, and for funerals, and for the day the whole family comes. It lives on a shelf, or under a table, or in a corner where people trip over it. It is too heavy for one person to lift when it is full, and probably slightly cracked on one side, and someone's grandmother once said it must never be put on the fire empty, and nobody now remembers why but nobody dares to do it.

At Gilgal, the great pot came out. It went on the fire in the courtyard. Water went into it, from the jar by the gate. And Hodiah came out of the store room with whatever he had decided they could spare, which was not much. A handful of lentils, perhaps. A couple of onions. Salt.

And everybody looked at the pot, which was very large, and at the things going into it, which were very small, and nobody said anything, because you do not say things like that in front of the master.

That is when Ira had his idea.

He said to Hodiah, quietly, we need greens. There must be greens in the fields. Things grow wild out there whatever the weather. I'll go and find some.

And Hodiah, who was counting the onions, said, without really looking up, Yes, go on. Good lad.

The book says it like this. One of them went out into the field to gather herbs.

The word is a little uncertain in Hebrew. It might mean herbs, or it might mean mallows, the low green leafy plants that people in that part of the world have eaten in hard times for thousands of years, and still do. Either way, it is exactly the right thing to go and look for. In a famine, the wild greens in the field are the last thing standing between a family and real hunger. People who know the land know which ones you can eat.

The trouble is that Ira did not know the land.

* * *

So out he went, past the edge of the town, into the fields. It was a dry day. The ground was the colour of an old biscuit. The crops that should have been there were thin and short and yellow at the tips, and a few people were out among them, doing whatever could be done, which was not a great deal.

Ira walked along the field edges, and along the stony bank of a dry stream bed, and he looked for green.

And there, on the stones and the sandy ground at the edge of the field, where nothing else would grow, he found a vine.

Not a grape vine. A vine that crawled along the ground, with rough, deeply cut leaves, spreading out across the dry earth as if it had never heard of a famine. And all along it, sitting in the dust like a row of little green and yellow balls, about the size of an orange, there were gourds.

The book calls it a wild vine, with wild gourds on it. And I would like you to imagine, for a moment, what that looked like to a hungry young man from a town.

It looked like a miracle. It looked like a garden that someone had planted and forgotten. In a dry field, in a dry year, here was a plant that was doing beautifully, covered in fat round fruit, and nobody had picked it.

He did not stop to wonder why nobody had picked it.

That is the lesson, by the way, if you want one. When you find a great deal of free food that nobody else has taken, it is worth one moment's thought about why. But Ira was young, and hungry, and keen, and he had promised to bring back greens. So he took off his outer cloak, and he spread it out, and he filled it, and when it was full, he gathered up the corners and carried it in his lap, the way the book says, his lap full, all the way back to Gilgal, smiling.

* * *

Now the scholars, who are careful people, will tell you that we do not know for certain what plant this was. The Bible does not give it a name that we can match with a label in a botanical garden.

But most of them think it was very probably a plant now called the colocynth. It is sometimes called the bitter apple, or the desert gourd. It still grows in that part of the world, in dry, sandy, stony places. It creeps along the ground, it has deeply cut leaves, and its fruit is round, about the size of an orange, green with yellow stripes when it is young, and yellow when it is ripe.

And it is extraordinarily bitter. It is one of the bitterest things that grows. A small piece of it in a pot would ruin the whole pot. And a larger amount, eaten, is a very strong purge, which is a polite word, and if you eat enough of it, it can make you dangerously ill.

People who live in the country where it grows know all this, and leave it alone. That is why nobody had picked it.

Ira came back into the courtyard with his cloak full of colocynths, and he was very pleased with himself.

The pot was bubbling. Hodiah looked up from the fire, and saw the cloak, and saw the round things in it, and said, What are those?

And Ira said, I don't know. But there are loads of them.

And here is the part of the book I find most honest. It says: he came and cut them up into the pot of stew, for they did not know what they were.

They. Not he. They. The whole lot of them. A courtyard full of men, some of them older and wiser than Ira, and not one of them recognised the gourds. Perhaps they were all from towns. Perhaps they were all so hungry that nobody wanted to be the one who said, Hang on. Perhaps somebody did think, hmm, and then looked at the size of the pot and the size of what was in it and decided not to say anything.

I think we have all been in that courtyard, one way or another. A plan goes ahead, and everybody has a small doubt, and everybody assumes somebody else must know what they are doing.

So in they went. Chopped up, rind and seeds and all. And the pot bubbled away, and it smelled, as I told you at the beginning, wonderful.

* * *

They poured it out.

Bowls went round the circle. Elisha's servant carried them, and Hodiah, and anyone else who had two free hands. Everyone was sitting in that half circle on the ground, the young ones at the back, and each of them took a bowl, and held it, and gave thanks, because they were the company of the prophets after all, and then they began to eat.

And the book says: as they were eating, they cried out.

I think it was quick. I think it was the second or third spoonful. The first one would have been hot, and full of onion, and you would not taste much but heat. And then the bitterness would come up underneath it, like a cold tide coming in. A bitterness so strong it is almost a feeling rather than a taste. Your mouth goes dry. Your throat closes. Something very old and very sensible inside you says, Stop.

And all round the circle, spoons stopped.

And someone shouted, in the words the book gives us: O man of God, there is death in the pot!

And the book adds, very simply, And they could not eat it.

Now I want to be fair to them. That sounds like a terribly dramatic thing to shout about a bad stew. But it was not just a bad stew. Something in that pot tasted so wrong that every instinct in every body in the courtyard said poison. And they were right to stop. They were right to shout. If you take one thing from tonight, you might take that. When something tastes of death, you put the spoon down, and you call for help.

And they knew who to call. Not the cook. Not the man who kept the stores. They called out to the man of God.

Poor Ira, meanwhile, was sitting at the back with his bowl in his hands and his face going the colour of the gourds. Not because he had eaten much, though he had eaten some. Because he had just realised what his cloak was full of.

* * *

Elisha did not shout back.

That is one of the things I like about Elisha in these stories. He is not, on the whole, a man who makes speeches. Elijah, his master, made speeches. Elijah stood on mountains and called down fire. Elisha mostly says short, practical things, the sort of things you would say to someone in a kitchen.

He said: Then bring flour.

That is all. Then bring flour.

Hodiah heard that, and his heart, I imagine, sank and rose at the same time. Sank, because that was the very last of it, the soft hissing quarter of a jar that he had been saving for the worst day. Rose, because, well, if this was not the worst day, he did not want to see the worst day. He went into the store room and he brought out the jar, and gave it to the master.

And Elisha took a handful of flour, or perhaps the whole jar, the book does not say how much, and he threw it into the pot.

And he said: Pour it out for the people, that they may eat.

And they did. Somebody, and I am fairly sure it was not Ira, was the first to lift a spoon. And put it in his mouth. And chew. And swallow. And sit there, for a long moment, with everyone in the courtyard watching him, while he decided what he thought.

And the book says it like this, in six plain words: And there was no harm in the pot.

* * *

I want to be careful here, and honest.

Flour does not cure colocynth. If you had a pot of real poison in your kitchen tonight, a handful of flour would not make it safe, and please do not try it. Some old commentators have tried to explain the story by saying the flour thickened the stew and soaked up the bitterness, but it does not work like that, and the book does not say it did.

What the book tells us is something like what happened at Jericho, in the last story, with the bad spring. There, Elisha asked for a new bowl, and put salt in it, and threw the salt into the water, and said, Thus says the Lord, I have healed this water. Salt does not heal springs any more than flour heals stews. In both stories, the prophet takes some small, ordinary thing that was already there in the house, and does a small, ordinary action with it, and God does the rest.

That is how the Church has always read these stories. The prophet is not a magician, and flour is not a spell. Elisha did not mutter anything over the pot. He did not wave his staff. He asked for what was in the cupboard, and put it in, and told them to eat. The power was the Lord's. The flour was just the sign of it, the way water is the sign in baptism, and bread in the Eucharist. Ordinary things, in God's hands, doing what only God can do.

And so they ate. The whole courtyard. A great pot of stew, made from a few lentils and a couple of onions and a cloakful of the bitterest gourds in the land of Israel, and the last of Hodiah's flour.

I do not know what it tasted like afterwards. The book does not say it tasted nice. It only says there was no harm in it. I suspect it was the sort of meal you remember for the rest of your life, not for the flavour, but for the company, and for the moment the first man swallowed and nodded, and everybody laughed, the way people laugh when something terrible has just not happened.

And later that evening, I think Hodiah found Ira sitting on his own by the wall with his empty cloak folded on his knees.

And Ira said, I'm sorry. I nearly killed everybody.

And Hodiah, who had been counting under his breath all day and had now run out of things to count, sat down next to him, and said: You went out to find food for your brothers. In a famine. On your own. That is not nothing. Next time, though, bring me one first, and I will tell you what it is.

And then he said, There's no flour left, by the way. None. So I hope the master knows what he's doing.

* * *

The next part of the story happens soon after. The book puts it right after the pot, and does not say exactly how long, but I like to think it was the very next day.

A man came up the road. He was a farmer, from a place called Baal-shalishah. We do not know where that was exactly. There is a land of Shalishah mentioned in the First Book of Samuel, in the hill country of Ephraim, when a young man called Saul is out looking for his father's lost donkeys. It was probably somewhere in those hills, not too far away. But no one can point to it on a map tonight.

The book does not tell us his name. So I am not going to give him one. I would rather he stayed exactly as the book leaves him, a man from Baal-shalishah, walking up the road in a famine year with a sack over his shoulder.

And what was in the sack?

Bread. Twenty loaves of barley bread. And fresh ears of grain as well. The book calls them the bread of the firstfruits.

Let me tell you why that matters.

Barley was the first crop of the year to be harvested in that country. It ripens in the spring, a few weeks before the wheat, around the time of the Passover. In the law of Moses, the very first of every harvest belonged to the Lord. You did not eat your new barley until you had brought the first sheaf of it to God. And the firstfruits, the books of Numbers and Deuteronomy say, were given to the priests, the men who served at the altar and had no fields of their own.

So in a famine year, this farmer had grown some barley. Not a great deal, I should think, in a famine. But the first of it came up. And the first of it was the Lord's. And he baked it into loaves, twenty of them, and he put some fresh ears in his sack as well, and he walked to Gilgal, and he gave it to the man of God.

Many readers have noticed that he did not take it to the priests. This was the northern kingdom, and the official shrines there, at Bethel and at Dan, had golden calves in them, set up by a king who had wanted his own religion. A faithful farmer from the hills might well not have wanted to take his firstfruits to Bethel. So he brought them, instead, to the prophet of the Lord. That is a reading, and an old one, and I think a likely one, but the book does not explain why he came. It just says he did.

What I love is the timing.

Think about it. The day before, the very last flour in the house had gone into a pot of poisoned stew. Hodiah's jar was empty. A famine was on. A hundred hungry men were sitting in a courtyard with absolutely nothing in the store room.

And up the road comes a man with bread.

Not a great deal of bread, mind you. Twenty loaves of barley, in those days, would have been flat round loaves, not much bigger than a dinner plate and not much thicker than your thumb. The kind you tear with your hands. Barley bread was the bread of the poor, coarse and dark and filling. Twenty of them would feed a family for some days. They would not feed a company of the prophets.

* * *

And Elisha looked at the sack, and he said to his servant: Give it to the people, that they may eat.

And the servant, who I think was standing there doing the arithmetic, said the line which is in the book, and which I find completely delightful. He said:

What? Should I set this before a hundred men?

There it is. That is the number. A hundred men. We know at last how big the company of the prophets at Gilgal was. Large enough that their house was too small. Large enough that twenty loaves was an absurdity. Five men to a loaf. A piece of barley bread about the size of your palm each, if you were lucky and nobody dropped any.

I would like to defend the servant here. It is a very reasonable thing to say. Somebody in every household has to be the one who says, but there isn't enough. Hodiah, I am sure, was standing right behind him nodding.

But Elisha said it again. And this time he told them why.

Give it to the people, that they may eat. For thus says the Lord: They shall eat, and have some left.

Not, I think, or I hope, or let's see. Thus says the Lord.

So the servant did as he was told. He took the twenty loaves out of the sack. He tore them, and set them before the hundred men, who were sitting, I imagine, in the same half circle in the same courtyard where the pot had been. And the bread went round.

And the book finishes the story in one sentence, which is the last sentence of the chapter:

And they ate, and had some left, according to the word of the Lord.

That is all. It does not say how. It does not describe the bread multiplying, or the sack refilling, or anybody gasping. It just says they ate, and there was some left over. And that it happened the way the Lord had said.

And I think, somewhere in that courtyard, Hodiah was walking round afterwards with the empty sack, picking up what was left over, the way a man who keeps the stores always does. And counting it, under his breath. And getting a number he did not expect.

Now let me tell you why Christians have loved this little story so much.

About nine hundred years later, on a green hillside by the Sea of Galilee, a very large crowd had followed Jesus a long way, and evening was coming, and there was nothing to eat. And one of the disciples, Andrew, found a boy, and the boy had a small lunch with him. And Saint John, in his Gospel, tells us exactly what was in it. Five barley loaves, and two fish.

Barley loaves. John is the only one of the Gospel writers who mentions that they were barley. And you can see why he might. Anyone who knew the Scriptures well would have heard it and thought of Elisha.

And Philip, one of the disciples, said almost what Elisha's servant said. Two hundred days' wages would not buy enough bread for each of them to have a little. And Andrew said, There is a lad here with five barley loaves and two fish, but what are they among so many?

What are they among so many. What, should I set this before a hundred men.

And Jesus had the people sit down on the grass, and took the loaves, and gave thanks, and gave them out. And they all ate as much as they wanted. And then he told the disciples to gather up what was left over, so that nothing would be lost. And they filled twelve baskets.

Elisha fed a hundred men with twenty loaves, and there was some left. The Lord Jesus fed five thousand men with five loaves, and there were twelve baskets left. Christian readers noticed that very early, and have gone on noticing it ever since. They have read the story at Gilgal as a small sign pointing ahead to a much larger one, the way a candle in a window points ahead to the house. Elisha said, thus says the Lord. Jesus did not need to say that. That is a reading, and an old and lovely one, and I give it to you as that.

If you heard our episode about the boy with the five loaves, you will know I am fond of that boy. I like to think he had heard the story of Elisha at Gilgal, sitting on his own mother's knee, without the faintest idea he was about to walk into the sequel.

[pause]

So what happened to them all?

The book does not tell us about Ira, or Hodiah, because we made them up. But I like to think Ira learned his plants. I like to think that, years later, he was the one who stood at the edge of the field with the young ones and said, That is mallow, you can eat that. That is the bitter apple. Leave it alone. Do not even pick it to look at it. And I like to think Hodiah got his flour jar filled again, in the end, when the rains came back, and went on counting things under his breath for the rest of his life.

As for the famine, there is a famine of seven years mentioned a little later in the Second Book of Kings, in chapter eight, when Elisha tells a woman he had once helped to take her family away and live somewhere else until it was over. Whether that is the same famine as tonight's, or another one, the book does not say, and nobody can be sure.

And the company of the prophets went on. They outgrew their house again. They went down to the Jordan and cut beams for a bigger one. Someone lost an axe head. Elisha went on visiting them, and teaching them, and sitting with them in their courtyards, for many years, all through the reigns of several kings.

He lived to be an old man. And when he died, he was buried, and the Church remembers him, the holy Prophet Elisha, on the fourteenth of June, in both the East and the West.

So tonight, as you lie in the dark, I would like you to think about the cupboard.

Every house has one, one way or another. The place where we keep what we are saving for the bad day. The money. The patience. The strength we are holding back, in case. And most of us, like Hodiah, stand in front of it some mornings, looking at the bottom of the jar, wondering whether today is the day.

And I would like you to think about the gourds. All the things we gather, very keenly, with the best intentions, that turn out bitter in the pot. And how good it is to have somebody who will shout, when it tastes of death, and somebody to shout to.

And then I would like you to think about the man from Baal-shalishah, coming up the road with a sack on his shoulder, the morning after the last of the flour had gone. Not with a great deal. Just twenty loaves. Just the first of what he had. Given away, in a bad year, to the Lord.

They ate, and had some left.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Kings, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, chapter four, verses thirty-eight to forty-four. The famine, Elisha at Gilgal, the sons of the prophets sitting before him, the great pot, the one who went out for herbs and came back with wild gourds in his lap, the shout that there is death in the pot, the flour, the man from Baal-shalishah with twenty barley loaves of the firstfruits and fresh ears in his sack, the servant asking how he could set this before a hundred men, and Elisha's answer, they shall eat and have some left, are all there. The words of Elisha, of his servant and of the company of the prophets are kept as the book gives them, in a plain rendering.

The book does not name the servant in these verses, so we have not either. It does not name the man who gathered the gourds, or the man from Baal-shalishah. That the gourds were the colocynth is a likely identification, not a certain one. Which Gilgal this was, and where Baal-shalishah lay, are open questions.

The firstfruits belonging to the priests are in Numbers, chapter eighteen, and Deuteronomy, chapter eighteen, and the first sheaf in Leviticus, chapter twenty-three. Gilgal and the end of the manna are in Joshua, chapter five. The house that was too small is in Second Kings, chapter six, and the seven years of famine in chapter eight. The five barley loaves are in John, chapter six.

And Ira and Hodiah, the flour jar, the cracked pot, and everything said in the story that the book does not give, are our own invention.

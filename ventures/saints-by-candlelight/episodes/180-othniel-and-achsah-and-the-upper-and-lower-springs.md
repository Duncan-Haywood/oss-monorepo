There is a young woman riding a donkey up a hill road, in the heat of the afternoon, and she is in a hurry.

The donkey is not in a hurry. The donkey has never been in a hurry in its life, and does not intend to start now. It stops to look at a thistle. It stops to look at a different thistle. It stops, as far as anyone can tell, to look at nothing at all.

At the top of the road there is a house, and in the doorway of the house there is an old man, a very old man, with a face like a walnut and a back as straight as a spear. He shades his eyes with his hand, and he sees his daughter coming, and he sees the look on her face.

And before the donkey has quite come to a stop, the young woman slides down off its back, so quickly that the donkey looks round at her in astonishment, and she stands in the dust of the road in front of her father.

The old man folds his arms. He has known this daughter all her life.

"Well," he says. "What is it? What do you want?"

If you have been listening to this podcast for a while, you have met Caleb before.

He was one of the twelve men Moses sent up from the wilderness to look at the promised land. They went all the way up through the hills and came back after forty days with a bunch of grapes so big that it took two of them to carry it on a pole. Ten of the twelve said, the land is wonderful, but the people there are giants, and we looked like grasshoppers next to them, and we cannot possibly go in. Two of the twelve said, yes we can, because the Lord is with us. Those two were Joshua and Caleb.

The people believed the ten, and so they spent forty years in the wilderness instead. And at the end of the forty years, when the people had finally crossed the Jordan, and the land was being shared out, Caleb came to Joshua. He was eighty-five years old. And he said, do you remember what Moses promised me? I am as strong today as I was on the day Moses sent me. Give me that hill country, the one where the giants lived. And Joshua blessed him, and gave him Hebron.

That is where we left Caleb, a few weeks ago, sitting in the shade in his own hill country, with a quick mention at the very end of a daughter and some springs. Tonight we are going back to Hebron, to tell that part properly. Because it is one of my favourite small stories in the whole Bible. It is only five verses long. It is told twice, almost word for word, once in the Book of Joshua and once in the Book of Judges, which tells you that somebody, a long time ago, thought it was worth telling twice. And at the heart of it is a young woman who knew exactly what she needed, and was not too shy to ask for it.

But first, let me tell you a little about the country, because the whole story turns on one thing, and that thing is water.

Hebron is high up, in the hills of Judah, about three thousand feet above the sea. It is good country. It has the old vineyards, the ones the spies found, and olive trees, and fields of wheat and barley in the valleys between the hills. It gets real rain in the winter, sometimes even snow, which surprises people who think of that part of the world as all desert.

But go south from Hebron, a long day's walk, and the country changes. The hills get lower and rounder. The green gets thinner. The rain gets less every mile, until you come down into what the Bible calls the Negeb. The word means something like the south, or the dry country. It is not sand dunes, exactly. It is wide brown land, rolling and stony, where grass comes up green for a few weeks after the winter rain and then goes the colour of a lion. You can graze sheep and goats there, and in a good year you can grow a crop of barley. But in a bad year you can grow a great deal of nothing at all.

In country like that, everything depends on water. Not on how much land you have. On how much water you have. A family with a thousand acres and no spring is poorer than a family with ten acres and a good one. People dug cisterns, which are big chambers, shaped like jars, cut into the rock under the ground, to catch the winter rain and keep it for the summer. They dug wells. They argued about wells. If you remember the story of Isaac, who kept digging wells and having them taken away, you will remember that people in that part of the world could get very worked up about wells.

So when someone in this story says, you have given me land in the Negeb, everybody listening a long time ago would have nodded. Ah. Dry land. That is going to be a problem.

And one more thing, before we start. The people in tonight's story came from a family that was not quite like the families around them. The Bible calls Caleb a Kenizzite. That seems to mean his family came from a clan called Kenaz, who may have joined the tribe of Judah from outside, some time before. And Caleb's younger relative, the hero of tonight's story, is called Othniel, son of Kenaz. So this is the story of a family who had come in from the edge, and made their home, and then, as we shall see, ended up holding the whole country together. I like that very much. But we will come to it.

* * *

When Caleb was given Hebron, it was not a gift in the way that a house with the keys on the table is a gift. It was more like being given a field with a bull in it, and told, it is yours, if you can get in.

Hebron had an older name. It was called Kiriath-arba, which means the town of Arba, and the book says Arba was the greatest man among the people called the Anakim. The Anakim were the big people. The very big people. The ones who had made the ten spies feel like grasshoppers, forty-five years before. And in Hebron there were three of them in particular, three brothers, called Sheshai, and Ahiman, and Talmai.

The ten spies had come home and said, we cannot possibly beat them. And forty-five years later, the one spy who had said we can went up to Hebron, an old man of eighty-five, and the book says simply that Caleb drove out from there the three sons of Anak, Sheshai, and Ahiman, and Talmai.

That is all it says. It does not describe a battle, which suits us very well at bedtime. It does not say whether it took a day or a year. It just says he did it. As if, after all that, the giants had not been so very hard to move after all. Which, I think, is part of the point. The things that frightened the ten spies for forty years turned out to be smaller than they looked, once someone went up to them with faith.

So Caleb lived in Hebron. And I like to imagine his household there. The Bible tells us, in the First Book of Chronicles, the names of three of his sons, who were called Iru, and Elah, and Naam. And it tells us about one daughter. Her name was Achsah.

Achsah is a lovely name. It seems to come from the Hebrew word for an anklet, the little bangle that women wore around their ankles that jingled when they walked. The prophet Isaiah mentions them, in a long list of the finery of the fashionable ladies of Jerusalem. So Caleb named his daughter, more or less, Anklet. Or Jingle. Which tells you, I think, that the fearless old spy who drove out giants was also the kind of father who had once sat beside a cradle and thought, what is the prettiest thing I can think of?

Now. Caleb had Hebron. But a little way to the south and west of Hebron there was another town, which was still held against him. It was called Debir. And it, too, had an older name. It used to be called Kiriath-sepher. And Kiriath-sepher means, as far as anyone can tell, the town of the book. Or the town of the scroll. Or the town of the writing.

Nobody knows quite why it was called that. Perhaps there was a famous scribe there. Perhaps there was a place where records were kept, a sort of ancient office of documents, which is not the most romantic thing to name a town after, but it is the kind of thing that does happen. Elsewhere in the Book of Joshua it is given yet another name, Kiriath-sannah, and nobody is certain what that one means either. Ancient towns collected names the way a cupboard collects odd socks.

In any case, Debir stood on its hill, and it was strong, and it was not yet in the hands of the people of Israel.

And so, the book says, Caleb made an announcement.

* * *

I am going to tell you what Caleb said, exactly as the book tells it, and then I am going to say something about it, because to our ears it sounds strange.

Caleb said, whoever strikes Kiriath-sepher, and takes it, I will give him Achsah my daughter for a wife.

That is the kind of thing that happens in old stories, all over the world. The king says, whoever kills the dragon may marry the princess. And it can sound, to us, as if the princess is being handed over like a prize at a fair, without anybody asking her opinion.

The Bible does not stop to explain it, so I will be careful not to put words in its mouth. But it is worth remembering that in that world, almost all marriages were arranged by fathers, for everybody, sons as well as daughters. A father's great job was to find his daughter a good husband, a man who was brave, and faithful, and would protect her and her children in a dangerous country. And a promise like Caleb's was, in its way, a way of finding out who that man was. Not the richest man. Not the man with the most sheep. The man who would go up the hill when it was hard.

And as we shall see in a minute, this particular daughter was not a prize who just sat on a shelf. She had a mind of her own, and a voice of her own, and she was going to use both of them before the week was out.

I imagine the household in Hebron that evening. I imagine Achsah hearing about her father's announcement from one of the women of the house. Let us say from a maid called Tova, who had looked after her since she was small. Tova is our own invention, and so is everything she says. But somebody must have told Achsah, and somebody must have had an opinion about it.

"Well," said Tova, coming in with a basket of washing, and setting it down very hard. "He has done it now."

"Done what?" said Achsah.

"Your father. Standing up in front of the whole family and all the men. Whoever takes the town of the book can have Achsah. As if you were a goat."

Achsah thought about it.

"He would not have said it," she said slowly, "unless he knew who would do it."

Tova stopped folding.

"You think he knows?"

"My father," said Achsah, "has never in his whole life gone up a hill without knowing who was going up behind him."

And Tova looked at her for a long moment, and then went back to folding the washing, and said nothing. But she was smiling.

* * *

The man who went up the hill was called Othniel.

The book calls him Othniel, son of Kenaz, the brother of Caleb. And then, in the Book of Judges, it adds that he was Caleb's younger brother. Now, the old Hebrew sentence can be read in two ways. It might mean that Othniel himself was Caleb's younger brother. Or it might mean that Othniel's father, Kenaz, was Caleb's younger brother, which would make Othniel Caleb's nephew, and Achsah's cousin. Readers have argued about this for a very long time. Marriages between cousins were quite usual in that world, and so were families where an older brother was old enough to be a younger brother's father. So either way works. Tonight I am going to think of him as a much younger relative, a young man of the same family, who had grown up listening to the old spy's stories about the giants and the grapes.

And Othniel went up against Kiriath-sepher, and took it.

Again, the book does not tell us how. It does not describe the fighting, and neither shall we. It just says that he took it. There is a small puzzle here, which I will mention because some of you will notice it. Earlier in the Book of Joshua, in the account of Joshua's campaign in the south, it says that Joshua's army took Debir. And here, in chapter fifteen, Othniel takes it. People who study these books have suggested several answers. Perhaps the town was taken once and then lost again, which happened to towns all the time. Perhaps the first passage is a quick summary of the whole campaign, and this one is the closer view of how one part of it went. The book does not explain, and I will not pretend to know.

There is a lovely old tradition about Othniel and the town of the book, which I want to tell you, as long as you understand that it is a tradition and not part of the story. In the Talmud, the great collection of Jewish teaching written down many centuries later, there is a passage that says that when Moses died, and all Israel mourned for him, many of the fine points of the law were forgotten in the grief. And it says that it was Othniel son of Kenaz who won them back, by careful reasoning, thinking through what must follow from what. So the old rabbis remembered Othniel not only as a brave man with a sword, but as a man who could think. And it is hard not to enjoy the fact that the town he took was called the town of the book. Whether the rabbis had that in mind, I do not know. But I like to think so.

In any case, Othniel took the town. And Caleb kept his word.

* * *

I think there was a wedding.

The book does not describe it. But there would have been one. Weddings in that country were week-long affairs, with feasting and music and a great deal of standing around in the sun telling the same stories. I imagine Caleb telling, for the hundredth time, about the bunch of grapes on the pole, and the young men who had heard it ninety-nine times groaning, and the old men saying, tell the bit about the grasshoppers again.

And after the wedding, it was time for Achsah to go to her new home, with her new husband. And her father had given her land, as fathers sometimes did, as a wedding gift to go with her. A dowry, we would call it. Land of her own.

The land was in the Negeb. The dry country. The south.

And here is where the story gets interesting.

The book says, in the Hebrew, that when she came to Othniel, she urged him to ask her father for a field. She nudged him. She said, go on. Go and ask him.

Now, I have to tell you that the old translations do not quite agree about who nudged whom. In Saint Jerome's Latin Bible, and in the old Douay English translation made from it, it is the other way round: she was persuaded by her husband to ask a field of her father. And the old Greek translation has its own way of putting it as well. So depending on which Bible your great-grandmother read, either Achsah talked Othniel into it, or Othniel talked Achsah into it.

I rather like that the translations cannot agree. Because it means that what we know for certain is this: they talked about it together. Husband and wife, on the first days of their marriage, sat down and looked at what they had been given, and talked about what they needed, and agreed that somebody ought to go and ask. Which is not a bad way to begin a marriage.

So let me imagine the conversation. It is our own invention, of course.

They went down to look at the land first. That seems sensible. You would want to see it. I imagine them riding south out of the hills on a bright morning, Achsah on the donkey and Othniel walking beside it, and Tova coming along behind with the bread, because Tova was not going to let Achsah go off to the south without her. And I imagine the country getting browner, and the hills getting lower, and the sky getting bigger, until they came over a rise, and there it was.

It was good land, in its way. Wide. Open. You could see a long way. There were a few low thorny bushes, and a dry streambed winding across it, white with stones, where water had run in the winter and run away again. There was a hawk, turning round and round very high up, looking down at them as if it wondered what they were doing there.

Achsah got down and walked out into it. She bent down and picked up a handful of earth, and let it run through her fingers. It ran through her fingers very easily. It was very dry.

"Well," said Othniel, behind her. "It is big."

"It is big," said Achsah.

"There is a lot of it," said Othniel.

"There is a lot of it," said Achsah.

They stood there for a while, in the wind.

"Where," said Tova, who had sat down on a rock with the bread, and who had never in her life been able to stay quiet for long, "does anybody get a drink around here?"

And nobody answered her. Because that was exactly the question.

* * *

Achsah walked a long way that day. Up the dry streambed and down again. Along the low ridges. Round the edges of the land her father had given her. Othniel walked with her some of the way, and some of the way he let her go on alone, because he was the kind of man who knew when to do that.

And at the end of the day, she came back and sat down by the fire, and she said, "There is no water."

"There will be rain," said Othniel. "In the winter."

"And it will run straight down the streambed and away," said Achsah. "I have been looking at it. There is nowhere for it to stop. We could dig cisterns. We will dig cisterns. But it will take years, and in the meantime, the flocks will need to drink, and so will the people, and so will we."

Othniel was quiet. He had taken a town with a sword. But you cannot take water with a sword. Or rather, you can, and people often did, but neither of them wanted to begin their life together by starting a fight over a well.

"There are springs," said Achsah, "up in the hills. Between here and my father's house. I know there are. I have seen them, when we came down. Two of them, one above the other, in the valley, where the water comes out of the rock."

"Those are your father's," said Othniel.

"Yes," said Achsah. "They are."

And she looked at him across the fire. And I imagine that he looked back at her for a long time, and then laughed.

"You want me to go and ask your father," he said, "the man who drove out the sons of Anak, the man who is eighty-five years old and still stronger than I am, to give us his springs."

"I want somebody to ask him," said Achsah.

And so, the next day, they set off back up into the hills, to Caleb's house.

* * *

Now here is where the story does something I love.

All the talk on the road, all the planning, all the nudging, was about Othniel asking. Go on. You ask him. And then they got to the house. And the book does not say that Othniel asked anybody anything.

The book says that Achsah got down from her donkey.

There is a word in the Hebrew there that is quite rare, and nobody is completely sure what it means. Most translations say she alighted, or got down, or dismounted. Some of the old translators thought it meant something more. One old guess is that she cried out, or sighed, or clapped her hands, from the back of the donkey. Whatever it was, it was something that made her father notice. You do not get the impression of a girl climbing off a donkey politely and adjusting her veil. You get the impression of someone arriving.

I said, in the cold open, that she slid down so quickly the donkey looked round. That is our own invention. But I think it fits.

And Caleb looked at his daughter standing in the road. And he said to her, what do you want?

In the Hebrew it is just two words. Mah lakh. What to you? What is it with you? What do you want? It is the kind of thing a father says when a daughter comes in the door with a look on her face. Not unkindly. Rather the reverse. It is the kind of thing a father says when he already half knows, and is ready to say yes, and just wants to hear her say it.

And Achsah said:

Give me a blessing.

That is the word she used. A blessing. In Hebrew, a berakah. It can mean a gift, a present, something good given from one person to another. But it is the same word as a blessing from God. She did not say, give me some property. She did not say, I think you will find that according to the custom of the tribe. She said, give me a blessing.

Since you have set me in the land of the south, she said, give me also springs of water.

And the word she used for springs is a funny one too. It is not the usual word for a spring. It is the word gullot, and it seems to mean bowls, or basins. The same word is used, in other books of the Bible, for the golden bowl at the top of a lamp, and for a bowl at the top of a lampstand in the vision of the prophet Zechariah. So what she asked for, word for word, was bowls of water. Basins of water. Places in the rock where the water collects and stays, round and full, like water held in two cupped hands.

Give me also bowls of water.

And Caleb gave her the upper springs, and the lower springs.

That is the whole thing. That is the end of the story, in the book. He did not argue. He did not say, I will think about it. He did not say, but those are the best springs I have, or, have you any idea how much those are worth, or, what has your husband got to say about this? He did not even, as far as we know, say anything at all. He just gave her both of them. The upper springs, and the lower springs. Not one. Both.

* * *

I want to stay with that for a moment, because I think it is why somebody thought this little story was worth telling twice.

There is something very old and very simple here. A child comes to a father and asks for what she needs. Not for something silly. Not for a golden anklet to go with her name. For water. For the thing that her household, and her flocks, and her children not yet born, would need to live. And the father, who loves her, does not give her a little bit, carefully measured. He gives her more than she asked for.

Many hundreds of years later, Our Lord said something to his disciples that has always made me think of Achsah on the road outside Hebron. He said, ask, and it will be given you. Seek, and you will find. Knock, and it will be opened to you. And he said, which of you fathers, if your son asks for a fish, will give him a snake instead? Or if he asks for an egg, will give him a scorpion? If you then, who are evil, as he put it plainly, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him.

Caleb was a good father, and a faithful man. But he was a man. And if a man like Caleb, asked for springs, gave both the upper and the lower ones, how much more.

I do not think it is a stretch to say that Achsah is in the Bible partly to teach us how to ask. She asked plainly. She asked for what she really needed. She did not pretend she did not need it. She did not wait to be noticed. She got down off the donkey and stood in front of her father and said, give me a blessing. And she was not at all surprised when he did.

And now I imagine the three of them, Achsah, and Othniel, and Tova, walking back down into the valley where the springs were, that evening. The upper spring, where the water came out of the rock high up on the hillside, cold and clear, into a round basin of stone that generations of hands had worn smooth. And the lower spring, further down, where the water from the hill came out again, and gathered in a pool among the reeds, with frogs in it.

And Achsah knelt down at the upper spring, and put her two hands in the water, and drank.

"Well," said Tova, sitting down heavily on a stone with the bread. "That answers my question."

* * *

Now the book of Joshua stops there. But Othniel's story does not.

The Book of Judges, which comes next in the Bible, begins by telling the story of Achsah and the springs all over again, almost word for word, as I said. And then it goes on. And it tells something sad, which I will tell gently.

Joshua died. And all the people of his generation died, the people who had seen the Jordan open, and the walls come down, and the manna in the wilderness. And the book says that there arose another generation after them, who did not know the Lord, or the work which he had done for Israel.

That is one of the saddest sentences in the Bible, and one of the most ordinary. It is the thing every parent worries about. That you will have seen great things, and your children will not have seen them, and will not believe you, or will not care. That the stories will become just stories.

And the people forgot. They began to worship the gods of the people around them, the Baals and the Asherahs, the gods of rain and harvest, which must have seemed very sensible gods to worship in a dry country where everything depended on the rain. And the book says that because of this, the Lord gave them into the hand of a king from far away, from the north and the east, from the land between the two great rivers. And the king's name was Cushan-rishathaim.

I should tell you about that name, because it is a bit of a joke, though not a very happy one. The second half, rishathaim, seems to mean double wickedness. So the king's name, as the book gives it, is something like Cushan of the Double Wickedness. It is very unlikely that his mother called him that. Much more likely, the people who had to live under him called him that, the way people give a nickname to someone they do not like, and the nickname stuck so well that it went into the history book.

For eight years, the people served Cushan of the Double Wickedness. And then, the book says, the people of Israel cried out to the Lord.

And the Lord raised up a deliverer for the people of Israel, who saved them. Othniel, son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother.

* * *

This is the start of the age of the judges. And I should explain what a judge was, because it was not a man in a wig.

In the Bible, the judges were people whom God raised up, one at a time, in the long years before there were any kings in Israel, to rescue the people when they were in trouble, and to keep them on the right road afterwards. Some of them did go to battle. Some of them sat under a palm tree and settled people's arguments, like Deborah. Some of them were very good, and some of them were a great deal more complicated than that, and we have met a few of the complicated ones on this podcast before. Gideon with his jars. Samson, whom we have not yet got to, and when we do I shall need a very long run-up.

But Othniel was the first. And the Book of Judges tells his story very short, as if it were a pattern for all the rest. And there is one line in it that it has never said about anybody before.

The book says, the Spirit of the Lord came upon him.

That is the first time those words appear in the Book of Judges. The Spirit of the Lord came upon Othniel. It was not his own strength, or his own cleverness, though he seems to have had plenty of both. It was the Lord's Spirit. And he judged Israel. And he went out to war. And the Lord gave Cushan of the Double Wickedness, king of the land between the rivers, into his hand. And his hand prevailed.

And the land had rest for forty years.

And Othniel, son of Kenaz, died.

That is the whole of it. Five verses. There are no fox tails on fire, no riddles, no jawbones, no fleeces left out in the dew. There is no foolish vow, and no sad ending. There is not a word against Othniel anywhere. His story is told so short and so clean that it reads like the book saying, this is what a judge is supposed to look like. Remember him, when you read about the others.

And I find it very moving that the first judge, the pattern for all of them, the one on whom the Spirit of the Lord came first, was a young man from the family that had come in from the edge. The Kenizzite clan. Caleb's people. The family of the old spy who had been one of only two, out of twelve, who believed that the giants could be beaten. Who had married Caleb's daughter, the girl named for an anklet. Who had walked with her round the dry land in the south, and gone back with her up the road to Hebron, and stood beside her while she asked for water.

So that is what happened to them.

The land had rest for forty years. That is a whole lifetime of rest, in those days. Children were born, and grew up, and had children of their own, and the flocks went down to drink at the upper and the lower springs, morning and evening, and nobody came over the hills to take them away.

The Bible does not tell us any more about Achsah. It does not tell us how long she lived, or how many children she had. The First Book of Chronicles names a son of Othniel called Hathath, and perhaps another called Meonothai, and I like to think they were hers, though it does not say. It does not tell us whether she ever went back to Hebron and sat with her old father in the shade. But I think she must have done. I think he would have wanted to hear how the springs were doing.

And Caleb, the old spy, died too, at some point, full of years, in his own hill country. He is one of the very few men of his whole generation whom the Bible praises without any but. Caleb, who followed the Lord wholly.

What happened to Debir, the town of the book? Later on, in the Book of Joshua, it is listed as one of the towns given to the priests and the Levites to live in, which is a quiet and rather lovely ending for a town that had been taken with a sword. A town of the book became a town of the people who kept the book.

Where it was, exactly, nobody now knows for certain. Archaeologists have argued about it for a hundred years. For a long time, many thought it was a mound called Tell Beit Mirsim, in the low hills to the southwest. Since then, many have come to think it was a place called Khirbet Rabud, a little nearer to Hebron. There is no sign on either of them that says Debir. You dig, and you find the walls, and the broken pots, and the grain pits, and you argue. And somewhere in those hills, if they have not dried up in all the centuries since, there may still be water coming out of the rock, into an upper basin and a lower one. I hope there is.

In the Orthodox Church, Caleb and Othniel and Achsah are not given days of their own, as far as I know. But they are remembered, along with everybody else in the Old Testament who was faithful to God, on a Sunday just before Christmas called the Sunday of the Holy Forefathers. It is the second Sunday before Christmas, and on that day the Church remembers all the faithful men and women who waited for the Lord before he came, and kept the faith, and passed it on. Abraham and Sarah. Moses and Miriam. Caleb, who was not afraid of the giants. And in among them, the first judge of Israel, and the young woman who got down from her donkey and asked for springs.

And it is a good thing to think of, I think, just before you go to sleep. That somewhere in the dry country there are two bowls of water in the rock, one above the other. That a father gave them both, without being asked twice. And that the Father of all of us is more generous than Caleb.

So if there is something you need tonight, something real, something you have been carrying around in the dry country of your own heart, it is all right to ask. Get down off the donkey. Say it plainly. Give me a blessing.

And then rest. The springs are running. The land has rest.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Joshua, chapter fifteen, verses thirteen to nineteen, and the Book of Judges, chapter one, verses eleven to fifteen, which tell the story of Achsah and the springs almost word for word, and from Judges, chapter three, verses seven to eleven, which tell the story of Othniel the judge. They are told straight, in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. Caleb's asking for Hebron is in Joshua, chapter fourteen; Hebron's three sons of Anak are first named in Numbers, chapter thirteen; and Caleb is called a Kenizzite in Numbers, chapter thirty-two. The other name for Debir, Kiriath-sannah, is in Joshua, chapter fifteen, verse forty-nine. Joshua's taking of Debir is in Joshua, chapter ten, and Debir given to the Levites in chapter twenty-one. The generation that did not know the Lord is in Judges, chapter two. Caleb's sons and Othniel's sons are named in First Chronicles, chapter four. The anklets are in Isaiah, chapter three, and the golden bowl in Ecclesiastes, chapter twelve. Saint Jerome's Latin, and the Douay English made from it, have the husband persuade the wife. The meanings of the names Achsah and Rishathaim are how most scholars read them, and are not certain. The tradition about Othniel and the forgotten laws is in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Temurah, and is a tradition, not Scripture. Ask, and it will be given you, is from Luke, chapter eleven, and Matthew, chapter seven. Where Debir stood is not settled.

Tova the maid is our own invention. So is the donkey's temper, the talk in Caleb's house, the walk round the dry land, the conversations by the fire and on the road, and what the springs looked like. The book does not tell us what Achsah and Othniel said to each other, only that one urged the other to ask. Nothing has been added to what Caleb, or Achsah, or Othniel, or the Lord say or do in the books.

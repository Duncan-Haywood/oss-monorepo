It is night, in Jerusalem, at the gate of a Roman fortress.

It is about nine o'clock. The city has gone quiet. The lamps in the houses are going out one by one. And in the courtyard of the fortress, by the light of torches, there are a great many soldiers trying to be quiet, which, if you have ever tried to make four hundred and seventy soldiers be quiet, you will know is not easy.

There are men in helmets with shields on their backs. There are horsemen, holding their horses' heads, and the horses are stamping and blowing because they would rather be asleep. There are men with spears. And there are two centurions going up and down the lines, in low voices, counting.

And in the middle of it all, a groom is leading up a horse, and holding it still, while a short man with a tired face and a bruise on his cheek is helped up into the saddle.

He settles himself. He takes the reins. He looks round at the torches, and the soldiers, and the great dark gate, and he says, to nobody in particular, I think this may be the most people who have ever walked anywhere on my account.

And everybody had stepped back from him rather quickly. And that is where we left him. Safe for the night, in the fortress, and nobody quite sure what to do with him.

And tonight is about what they did with him.

I should tell you at the start what kind of a story this is. It is a story with a meeting in it that goes wrong in about the first ten seconds. It has a man who is slapped in the face, and says something very rude, and then apologises, which is more than most of us manage. It has an argument about angels. It has a plot, with more than forty men in it, and a promise they could not possibly keep. It has a young man whose name nobody remembers, who did the bravest thing in the whole chapter. And it has a long ride in the dark, down out of the hills, towards the sea.

And in the middle of it, in the night, in a barracks, there is one short sentence from the Lord. And they are, I think, some of the kindest words in the whole book.

* * *

Let me set the scene.

It is the year fifty-seven or so, or near enough. Jerusalem is a city of perhaps tens of thousands of people, and at festival times a great many more. In the middle of it, on a huge stone platform that King Herod built, stands the temple, white and gold, with smoke going up from the altar. And at the north-west corner of the platform, glowering over it, is the fortress Antonia, where the Romans keep a garrison of soldiers, just in case.

The Romans are in charge, but they like to leave the day-to-day business to local people. So the city has its own council, which in Greek is called the Sanhedrin. It has about seventy members, and the high priest sits at the head of it. You can think of it a little like a town council, a supreme court and a synod of bishops all rolled into one, which, if you have ever sat through a meeting of even one of those, you will agree is a great deal of meeting.

And the council is divided, more or less, into two parties.

On one side are the Sadducees. They are mostly the priestly families, the old money, the people who run the temple. They are serious, careful people. They read the five books of Moses, and they are not very interested in anything else. And they do not believe in the resurrection of the dead. They think that when you die, that is that. And according to Luke, they do not believe in angels, or in spirits, either. They are, if you like, the sensible party. The no-nonsense party.

On the other side are the Pharisees. They are mostly not priests. They are scholars and teachers and craftsmen, people who have spent their lives studying the law and trying to keep every bit of it in daily life, in the kitchen and the market and the workshop. And the Pharisees believe in the resurrection. They believe that at the end, God will raise the dead. And they believe in angels too.

And these two parties have been arguing about this, on and off, for about a hundred and fifty years. It is the sort of argument where everybody knows exactly what everybody else is going to say, and says it anyway, at length.

Now. You will remember that Saint Paul, before he met the Lord, was a Pharisee. He had studied in Jerusalem under the great teacher Gamaliel. In one of his letters, he says of himself, as to the law, a Pharisee. And he never stopped thinking of himself as one of God's people, the children of Abraham. He had simply found, on the road to Damascus, that the resurrection the Pharisees had always hoped for had already begun. In one man. Raised from the dead, three days after he was crucified, just outside this very city.

Keep that in mind. It is going to matter in about ten minutes.

And there is one more person you need to meet before we begin, and that is the high priest. His name is Ananias, son of Nedebaeus. And I am sorry to say, he is not a nice man.

We know a fair bit about him, because the Jewish historian Josephus, who grew up in Jerusalem at about this time, wrote about him. And what Josephus says is that Ananias was very rich, and very fond of being rich, and that his servants used to go round the threshing floors at harvest and take the tithes, the share of the grain meant for the ordinary priests, and that some of the poorer priests went hungry because of it. He was the sort of man who is very generous to people who can be useful to him, and not to anyone else.

So that is the room. A council of seventy, split down the middle about the resurrection. A greedy high priest at the top. And a Roman commander, standing at the back, who does not understand any of it, and who would very much like somebody to explain to him what on earth is going on.

The next morning, the commander made up his mind.

His name, as we will find out later in the chapter, was Claudius Lysias. And he had a problem. He had a prisoner who was a Roman citizen, which meant he had to be careful with him. And he had a whole city that wanted that prisoner dead, and he had no idea why. The crowd on the steps had shouted a great deal, but none of it in a way that made sense to a Roman officer.

So, Luke says, wanting to know for certain what the man was being accused of, the commander unchained him, and ordered the chief priests and the whole council to meet. And he brought Paul down and stood him in front of them.

I do want to stop for a moment and admire the commander here. He has been a soldier for a long time, I should think, and he has learnt that the quickest way to find out what is going on is to put everybody in one room and let them talk. It is a good idea. It is the sort of idea that works nearly every time.

This was not one of those times.

Paul stood in front of the council. He knew a lot of these faces. Some of them, perhaps, he had sat with, thirty years before, when he was a young scholar. Some of them had given him letters, once, to go to Damascus and arrest the followers of Jesus. And now here he was, on the other side of the room.

He looked at them, Luke says, steadily. Straight at them. And he said, Brothers, I have lived my life before God with a perfectly clear conscience, right up to this day.

That was all he had said. One sentence.

And the high priest Ananias turned to the men standing next to Paul, and ordered them to hit him on the mouth.

And they did.

Now, I want you to imagine being slapped across the face in front of seventy people for saying one sentence. And I want you to imagine what you would say next. Because I know what I would say next, and it would not be very holy.

Paul was a saint. But he was not, at this point in the story, a patient saint. He put his hand to his face, and he said to the high priest, God is going to strike you, you whitewashed wall! You sit there to judge me by the law, and then you break the law by ordering me to be struck?

A whitewashed wall, by the way, is an old prophet's insult. The prophet Ezekiel talks about a cracked, crumbling wall that people have daubed over with whitewash so that it looks fine, until the rain comes and it falls down. It means, you look very respectable, and you are rotten inside. And the Lord himself used something very like it, about people who were whitewashed tombs, clean on the outside, and full of dead men's bones within. So it was a very good insult. It was the best kind of insult. It was a biblical insult.

And the people standing near Paul gasped, and said, are you insulting God's high priest?

And here is the bit I love.

Paul stopped. And he said, I did not know, brothers, that he was the high priest. Because it is written, you shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people.

That is from the Book of Exodus. And what Paul is saying is, I was wrong to say that. The office deserves respect, even when the man does not. And I take it back.

Now, people have argued for a very long time about why Paul did not know who the high priest was. He had been away from Jerusalem for years, and high priests in those days came and went quite quickly, so perhaps he had never seen this one. Perhaps the meeting had been called in a hurry, by a Roman, and nobody was sitting in their proper places or wearing their proper robes. Some people think Paul's eyesight was poor. He says, in one of his letters, that his friends in Galatia would have torn out their own eyes and given them to him if they could, and in another, look at what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand. And some people think he was being a little bit sharp, and meant, I did not think a real high priest would behave like that.

We do not know. Luke does not tell us. But I like the version where he simply did not know, and when he found out, he said sorry, straight away, in front of everybody, and quoted Scripture against himself. Because that is a hard thing to do. Especially with your face still stinging.

And I think, too, that Paul might have remembered, a moment later, another trial, in another room, perhaps not very far from this one. When the Lord Jesus stood before a high priest, and one of the officers struck him across the face. And the Lord said only, if I have said something wrong, tell me what was wrong. But if I spoke rightly, why do you strike me?

The Lord did not call anybody a whitewashed wall. And I think, perhaps, Paul knew that, and minded.

So the meeting was not going well. It had been going for about a minute and a half, and the defendant had been slapped, and had insulted the chairman, and had apologised.

And Paul, standing there, looked round the room again. And he saw, Luke says, that some of them were Sadducees and some of them were Pharisees.

And he had an idea.

He raised his voice, and he called out across the whole council, Brothers, I am a Pharisee, the son of Pharisees! And I am on trial here because of the hope of the resurrection of the dead!

And the room exploded.

I should say that this was not a trick. Not really. It was true. Paul was a Pharisee, born and brought up. And the whole reason he was standing there, the whole reason for everything he had done for twenty years, was that he believed a man had been raised from the dead. That was the heart of it. Everything else was detail. In his first letter to the Corinthians, he says, if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain, and your faith is in vain. So when he said, I am on trial because of the resurrection, he was telling the plain truth.

He was simply telling it in a room where half the people believed in the resurrection, and the other half thought it was nonsense, and both halves had been waiting a hundred and fifty years for an excuse to say so.

The Pharisees and the Sadducees began to argue. And then they began to shout. Luke says the whole assembly was split in two, and there was a great uproar. And then some of the scribes from the Pharisees' side stood up, and argued fiercely, and said, we find nothing wrong with this man! What if a spirit or an angel has spoken to him?

Which is a wonderful thing to hear from people who, the day before, had been quite happy for him to be torn apart by a crowd. But it is the kind of thing that happens in arguments. You find yourself defending someone you did not much like, simply because the other side is attacking him.

And at the back of the room, the commander, Claudius Lysias, was watching all this with his mouth slightly open.

He had brought Paul here to find out what he was accused of. And now, as far as he could tell, the council was having a fight about whether angels exist. And it was getting worse. People were on their feet. People were grabbing at Paul, from both sides, the ones who wanted to defend him pulling one way and the ones who wanted to get at him pulling the other.

And Luke says the commander was afraid that Paul would be torn to pieces between them.

So he ordered the soldiers to go down, and take Paul out of the middle of them by force, and bring him back to the barracks.

And they did. And that was the end of the meeting.

I have been to some bad meetings in my time. But I do not think I have ever been to one where the person being discussed had to be rescued from it by soldiers.

That night, Paul was back in the barracks.

I imagine it was not a comfortable place. Stone walls. A bench, perhaps, or straw on the floor. A soldier at the door. The noises of a fortress settling down for the night, boots on stairs, somebody laughing in another room, a horse in the stables below. And somewhere beyond the walls, a city that, mostly, wanted him dead.

And Paul, I think, was very tired.

Think about the last two days. He had been dragged out of the temple. He had been beaten by a crowd. He had been chained and carried up the steps. He had given a speech, and been shouted down. He had very nearly been whipped. He had been slapped in the face in front of the council. He had lost his temper, which he did not like doing. And he had been pulled about like a rope in a tug of war, and rescued by Romans.

And for years he had wanted to go to Rome. He had written to the church there, a long letter, the one we call the Letter to the Romans, and he had told them, I am coming to see you, I have longed for many years to come to you. And now here he was, locked up in a fortress in Jerusalem, with half the city against him, and no way out that he could see.

I wonder if he thought, that night, that he had got it all wrong. That he should have listened to Agabus, and to Philip's daughters, and to all the friends who had begged him not to come. I think anybody would have thought that.

And that night, Luke says, the night after the council, the Lord stood by him.

That is all Luke says about how. The Lord stood by him. Not in a dream, Luke does not say a dream. Not an angel. The Lord. Standing next to him, in the barracks, in the dark.

And the Lord said, Take courage. As you have borne witness about me in Jerusalem, so you must bear witness in Rome too.

That's all. One sentence. Take courage. You have done this here. You will do it there too.

I want to tell you why I think those are kind words.

The Lord does not say, everything will be easy now. It will not be. He does not say, you will be out of here tomorrow. He will not. He does not say, you were quite right to call the high priest a whitewashed wall, either, I notice.

What he says is, take courage. And then he says something that only someone who loves you would think to say. He says, what you did here counted. You thought it was a disaster. The riot, the speech that got shouted down, the meeting that ended in a brawl. But you bore witness. It counted. And it is not over. You are going to Rome.

Which was the one thing Paul wanted most.

I think that is what the Lord does, quite often. Not take the hard thing away. But come and stand next to you in it. And tell you that it is not wasted. And tell you that there is more.

And I like to think that Paul, after that, lay down on his straw, in his stone room, with the soldier at the door, and slept like a baby.

But not everybody in Jerusalem was sleeping.

When it was day, Luke says, some of the men of Jerusalem got together and made a plot. And they bound themselves with an oath. They called down a curse on themselves, and they swore that they would neither eat nor drink anything at all until they had killed Paul.

There were more than forty of them.

And they went to the chief priests and the elders, and said, we have sworn a great oath not to taste anything until we have killed Paul. So now, you and the council, send a message to the commander. Ask him to bring Paul down to you tomorrow, as if you wanted to look into his case more carefully. And we will be ready to kill him before he ever gets near you.

Now. I want to say two things about this plot.

The first is that it is a very serious and frightening thing. Forty men, in a city full of narrow streets, waiting in doorways for a prisoner being walked down from the fortress with a few guards. It would have worked. It would very probably have worked.

And the second thing is that, as plots go, it had one rather large weakness, which is that the men in it had promised not to eat or drink anything until it was done.

I do not know if you have ever gone a whole day without anything to drink, in Jerusalem, in the early summer. But I can tell you that by the second afternoon, an oath like that is going to start to feel like a very long oath indeed. These men had made a promise to themselves that put a clock on the whole thing. They could not wait. They could not try again next month. It had to be tomorrow.

And a plot that has to happen tomorrow, and that has forty people in it, and that has to be explained to the chief priests and the elders, and to whoever carries the message, is a plot that a great many people know about.

And one of the people who heard about it was a young man whose name we do not know.

Luke calls him the son of Paul's sister.

And that is very nearly all we know about him. It is the only time, in the whole of the Acts of the Apostles, or in any of Paul's letters, that anyone in Paul's own family is mentioned at all. We know Paul was born in Tarsus, a long way to the north. We know he came to Jerusalem as a young man, to study. And now we find out, almost by accident, that his sister must have come to Jerusalem too, or married there, and had a son. And that the son was living in the city. And that, one day in the early summer, he heard something he was not supposed to hear.

We do not know how he heard it. Luke does not say.

So here is what we have imagined, and I will tell you clearly that it is our own invention.

I think he was young. Luke calls him a young man, and later the commander takes him by the hand, which you might do with a boy, or a nervous young man, more than with a grown man of thirty. So let us say he is about sixteen or seventeen. Old enough to run errands. Young enough that grown-ups talk in front of him as if he were part of the furniture.

And I think that morning he was sent out to fetch something. Oil, perhaps. Or bread. And he was standing in a doorway, waiting his turn, and behind him two men were talking in low voices. And he heard one of them say the word tomorrow. And the other say, more than forty of us. And the first say, not a crumb, not a drop, until it is done. And then he heard the name.

Paulos. The one from Tarsus. The one in the fortress.

And I think he stood very still in that doorway, with his heart going like a drum, and his face as blank as he could make it, until the men had gone. And then he forgot all about the oil.

Now. Whatever happened, however he heard it, here is what Acts tells us he did. He went to the fortress. And he went in. And he told Paul.

I want you to think about that. He was a young man, one of the people of Jerusalem, walking up to the gate of the Roman fortress, the one that glowers over the temple, full of soldiers, the ones everybody in the city resented. And walking in. And asking to see a prisoner, the most hated prisoner in the city. While forty men, somewhere in the streets behind him, were waiting for exactly this kind of thing to go wrong.

That is brave. I do not know if he thought it was brave. I expect he thought, that is my uncle. And then he walked.

And he got in. Prisoners who were Roman citizens and not yet charged with anything were often allowed visitors. And so the young man was brought to his uncle.

Here is our invention again. I imagine Paul was sitting on his bench, and he looked up, and saw his sister's boy in the doorway, out of breath, and his face lit up. And then he saw the young man's expression. And he said, what is it?

And the young man told him. Everything. The doorway, the voices, the forty, the oath, tomorrow.

And Paul listened. And I do not think he was frightened. Because, you remember, the Lord had stood by him the night before and said, you are going to Rome. And I think Paul looked at his nephew and thought, ah. So this is how.

And Acts tells us what he did next. He called one of the centurions over, and he said, take this young man to the commander. He has something to tell him.

He did not say, please. He did not explain. He simply said it, the way a man says something when he is quite sure it is going to happen. And the centurion, who perhaps had been the very centurion who had nearly whipped Paul two days before, and had not forgotten it, did exactly as he was asked.

* * *

The centurion took the young man to the commander.

And he said, the prisoner Paul called me over and asked me to bring this young man to you. He has something to tell you.

And here is the detail I love more than any other in the whole chapter. Luke says that the commander took the young man by the hand, and drew him aside, where nobody else could hear, and asked him, what is it you have to tell me?

Took him by the hand.

This was a Roman officer, in command of a thousand men, in a city that hated him, who had had about the worst two days of his career. And a nervous young man was brought in, from the wrong side of every line there was. And the commander took his hand, and led him into a corner, and said, quietly, tell me.

I think that is a lovely thing. It is a small thing. But Luke noticed it, and wrote it down, and I think he wrote it down because it was kind.

And the young man told him. He said, the leaders have agreed to ask you to bring Paul down to the council tomorrow, as if they wanted to look into his case more carefully. But do not let them talk you into it. Because more than forty of their men are lying in wait for him. And they have sworn an oath not to eat or drink until they have killed him. And they are ready now. They are only waiting for you to say yes.

And the commander listened. And then he let the young man go. And he said to him, do not tell anybody that you have told me this.

And the young man went out of the fortress, and back down into the city, and home. And that is the last we ever hear of him.

I have always wanted to know what happened to him. Whether he told his mother. Whether he ever saw his uncle again. Whether, years later, he sat with his own children and said, I once walked into the Antonia fortress and told the Roman commander a secret, and they would all say, you did not, and he would say, I did, it is in the book. Luke never tells us. But I hope he was all right. I hope the forty never found out who it was.

He is not counted among the saints. We do not even know his name. But he did what a saint would do. He heard that someone he loved was in danger, and he went straight to him, through the front door, and told the truth. And because of that, as you will hear, Saint Paul lived to go to Rome.

* * *

Now the commander had to decide what to do.

And I think, by this point, Claudius Lysias was heartily sick of Paul.

He had had a riot. He had had a speech in a language he did not understand. He had very nearly whipped a Roman citizen, which would have been the end of his career. He had held a meeting that turned into a fight about angels. And now there was a plot, with more than forty men in it, and they were going to ask him to walk his prisoner into an ambush, and if anything happened to that prisoner, it would be the commander who would have to explain it.

So he did what a sensible officer does with a problem that is too big for him.

He sent it to his boss.

He called two of his centurions, and said, get ready two hundred soldiers to march to Caesarea, and seventy horsemen, and two hundred spearmen. You leave at the third hour of the night. And provide mounts for Paul to ride, and get him safely to Felix the governor.

The third hour of the night is about nine o'clock in the evening. So, not tomorrow. Tonight. Before the forty men had even finished making their arrangements.

And the escort. Two hundred soldiers. Seventy horsemen. Two hundred spearmen. That is four hundred and seventy armed men, to take one short tentmaker more than sixty miles down the road. It is, I think, the most dramatic overreaction in the New Testament. Although, to be fair to the commander, he had seen the crowd on the steps.

I should say that nobody is quite sure what the spearmen were. The Greek word Luke uses is a very rare one. It means something like right-hand-takers, and nobody has found it written down anywhere older than this. It might mean spearmen. It might mean slingers, or light troops, or men who led spare horses by the right hand. Scholars have argued about it for a very long time and have not agreed. So if you ever want to start an argument at a party full of New Testament scholars, which is a very particular kind of party, you could do worse than to ask them about the spearmen.

And Luke notices, too, that the commander said to provide mounts, in the plural. More than one horse for Paul. Perhaps so he could change horses on the way. Perhaps for his things. Perhaps, though Luke does not say so, for a companion. Either way, the commander was not going to have Paul walking.

* * *

Then the commander sat down and wrote a letter.

Luke gives us the whole letter. And I want to read it to you, in our own plain words, because it is a wonderful letter, and because it is the first time we hear the commander's name.

It says this.

Claudius Lysias, to His Excellency the governor Felix, greetings.

This man was seized by the Jews, and was about to be killed by them, when I came on the scene with my troops and rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman citizen.

And because I wanted to find out what they were accusing him of, I brought him down to their council. And I found that he was accused about questions of their own law, but there was nothing against him that deserved death or chains.

And when I was told that there would be a plot against the man, I sent him to you at once. And I have told his accusers to state their case against him before you.

That is the letter.

Now, did you notice anything?

Having learned that he was a Roman citizen, I rescued him.

That is not quite what happened, is it. What happened, if you remember, is that the commander rescued him first, and wondered if he was an Egyptian troublemaker. And then chained him. And then ordered him to be whipped. And only found out he was a Roman citizen when Paul mentioned it, rather pointedly, while they were tying him up.

But that is not how it reads in the letter. In the letter, the commander is a dashing officer who heard that a Roman citizen was in trouble and rode to the rescue. And the bit about the whip has somehow been left out.

I find this very comforting. Nineteen hundred and fifty years ago, there was a man writing a report to his boss, and tidying up the story just a little bit so that he came out of it looking good. People have not changed very much. And Luke, who must have got hold of a copy of the letter somehow, or heard it read out at Caesarea, put it into his book without a word of comment. He just let it sit there, so that we could notice it ourselves. Which I think is very funny, and very gentle.

And the important part of the letter is true. Nothing against him that deserved death or chains. A Roman officer, who had no reason to like Paul at all, wrote that down and sent it to the governor. And Paul would need that letter, later on.

* * *

[pause]

So now we come back to where we started. The courtyard of the fortress, at nine o'clock at night. The torches. The horses blowing. Four hundred and seventy men trying to be quiet.

And Paul being helped up into the saddle.

And the gate opened, and they went out.

I do not know which way they went. The road from Jerusalem down to the coast goes west and north, out of the hills, and one old way runs down past a place called Beth-horon, down a long steep ridge, where, a very long time before, Joshua had chased the kings of the Amorites, on the day the sun stood still over Gibeon. It is a likely road. But Luke does not name it, so I am only guessing.

Let us imagine it, though. Our own invention, all of it.

Let us imagine that the moon was up. Not full, but bright enough. The road pale in front of them, going down and down between the terraces, the olive trees silver on either side. The sound of four hundred and seventy pairs of feet, or rather four hundred feet and seventy horses, which is a different sort of sound. The clink of buckles. A horse blowing. A centurion's voice, quiet, at the front.

And Paul, on his horse, in the middle of it all, with a soldier on either side.

He had been bound in Jerusalem, just as Agabus had said. And now he was going back down to Caesarea, back to the city by the sea, where, not two weeks before, his friends had wept and begged him not to go up. Bound, and with a guard of honour that a king might envy.

I wonder what he thought about, on that ride. I wonder if he thought about the road to Damascus, the other time a lot of soldiers had travelled a long road with him at the centre of it, and he had been the one with the letters, and the power to arrest. And now here he was, the prisoner, and the soldiers were there to keep him alive.

I wonder if he thought about his sister's boy, and prayed for him.

And I think, most of all, he thought about those words in the barracks. Take courage. You must bear witness in Rome too.

And I think, somewhere on that long slope down out of the hills, with the stars going round overhead and the horse rocking under him, he very probably fell asleep in the saddle. Which is a thing that tired travellers have always done, and is not recommended, but there were four hundred and seventy people making sure he did not fall off.

* * *

They went on all night. And Luke says they brought him by night as far as Antipatris.

Antipatris was a town at the edge of the hills, where the high ground runs down into the flat coastal plain. It was built at a place with springs, where a river rises. It was a very old place, much older than its name. But King Herod the Great had rebuilt it, and named it after his father, whose name was Antipater. So it was, if you like, Antipater-town. Herod liked naming things after his family. He named a tower in Jerusalem after his brother, and another after his wife, and the Antonia fortress itself, where Paul had just come from, after his friend Mark Antony. If he had had a dog, I expect he would have named a city after the dog.

Now, Antipatris is a long way from Jerusalem. Something like forty miles. And people who know about marching have wondered ever since how the foot soldiers managed to walk forty miles in a single night, some of it downhill on a bad road, with their armour on. It would be a very hard march. Perhaps they went faster than we think. Perhaps they went only part of the way that night, and Luke has simply run the journey together. Perhaps the soldiers on foot went with them only for the dangerous first stretch, out of the hills, and the rest happened in the morning. We do not know. Luke does not say. All he tells us is where they got to.

And at Antipatris, the next day, the soldiers on foot turned round and went back to the fortress in Jerusalem. Because once you are out of the hills, and onto the flat open plain, there is nowhere for forty men to hide. You can see anyone coming for miles. So the commander's orders, I imagine, were to take the foot soldiers as far as the edge of the hills, and then let the horsemen go on alone.

So the seventy horsemen went on with Paul. Across the plain. Another twenty-five miles or so, north and west, towards the sea.

And I like to think that the morning was bright, and that there was a wind off the sea, and that Paul smelled salt before he saw the water. That is our invention. But it is the sort of thing that happens on the road to Caesarea.

* * *

And so they came to Caesarea.

Caesarea was Herod's other great city. He had built it on the coast, on a stretch of open shore with no proper harbour, and then, because there was no harbour, he had built one, out into the open sea, with great stone breakwaters, which was one of the marvels of the age. There was a theatre, and a racecourse, and a temple to the emperor, and aqueducts bringing water down from the hills. And it was where the Roman governor lived, because the Romans, very sensibly, preferred the sea breeze to the politics of Jerusalem.

The horsemen rode in through the gates, and delivered the letter to the governor, and handed Paul over to him.

The governor's name was Antonius Felix. And I am afraid I have to tell you that he was not a nice man either. He had been born a slave, and freed, and he had a brother called Pallas who was one of the most powerful men at the emperor's court in Rome, which, people said, is how Felix had got the job. The Roman historian Tacitus, who did not like him at all, said that Felix used the power of a king with the mind of a slave. Which is a very Roman sort of insult, and not a very kind one, but I am afraid it tells you how people felt about him.

Felix read the letter. And he asked Paul one question. He asked him what province he came from.

That is the sort of question an official asks while he is working out whether something is his problem. If Paul had come from a province that belonged to some other governor, Felix might have been able to send him on somewhere else, and let somebody else deal with all this.

And Paul said, Cilicia.

And Felix, I imagine, sighed. Because Cilicia was close enough, and Roman enough, that there was no way of passing it on.

So he said, I will hear your case when your accusers have arrived too.

And he gave orders for Paul to be kept under guard in Herod's palace.

And that is where the chapter ends.

[pause]

The palace, most archaeologists think, was on a little rocky point that sticks out into the sea, at the edge of the city. Herod had built it there so that the waves would come right up to the walls. It had a pool in the middle of it, of fresh water, with the salt sea all round, and, from what the diggers have found, painted walls and patterned floors, and a great hall where the governor gave judgments.

And it is there, in that palace by the sea, that Paul would stay. Not for a few days. For two whole years.

The high priest Ananias came down to Caesarea five days later, with a lawyer, to make the case against him. That is in the next chapter, and we will save it for another night. Felix listened, and put off deciding, and listened again, and put off deciding again. He brought his wife Drusilla to hear Paul talk about faith in Christ, and about justice, and self-control, and the judgment to come. And Luke says that Felix became frightened, and said, that is enough for now, I will send for you again when I have a moment. Which I think may be the most honest thing anyone in this story ever says.

And after two years Felix was called back to Rome, and another governor came. And Paul, at last, said the words that would take him where the Lord had promised. He said, I appeal to Caesar. And he went to Rome. By ship, of course. With a shipwreck, of course. That was always going to happen.

And the forty men, who had sworn not to eat or drink until Paul was dead?

Acts does not say. Luke never mentions them again.

But I am fairly confident that none of them starved. A later book of Jewish law, the Mishnah, written down a century and a half afterwards, has a little list of the kinds of vows that the teachers said could be let go. And one of them is a vow that circumstances made it impossible to keep. I suspect that, about two days after that night, a great many very hungry men in Jerusalem discovered that their vow came under that heading. And I hope they had a very good dinner. And I hope, somewhere in the middle of it, one or two of them stopped, with the bread in their hands, and wondered whether God had been trying to tell them something.

As for Ananias, he did not stay high priest very long. Some years later, when the great war against Rome broke out, he came to a sad end in the troubles in the city, as many people did. And a few years after that, the temple and the fortress were both gone.

But Caesarea is still there. Not the city, exactly, but its stones. The harbour is mostly under the sea now, and divers swim over the old breakwaters. And the rocky point where Herod's palace seems to have stood is still there, with the outline of the pool, and the sea still coming up round it, on a windy day, just as it did when Paul looked out of his window.

And in the year nineteen sixty-one, archaeologists digging in the old theatre at Caesarea turned over a block of stone that had been reused in some later building, and found letters cut into it. And when they read the letters, they found the name of a Roman governor, from about thirty years before Paul arrived. The governor's name was Pontius Pilate. It is the only stone inscription with his name on it that anyone has ever found. It is in a museum in Jerusalem now, and you can go and look at it.

So the governors came and went, in the palace by the sea. Pilate, and Felix, and the ones after. And their names are mostly on stones, or in the pages of historians who did not like them very much.

And the man they kept in that palace for two years, under guard, the short man with the bruise on his cheek, who rode down out of the hills one night in the middle of four hundred and seventy soldiers. His letters are read out loud, every Sunday, in churches all over the world.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twenty-two, verse thirty, to chapter twenty-three, verse thirty-five. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept. The words of Paul, the high priest, the bystanders, the Pharisee scribes, the Lord, the forty men, the nephew, the centurion and the commander, and the commander's letter, are in our own plain rendering, adding nothing to what Luke reports. Some old copies of Acts add, at the end of what the Pharisee scribes say, let us not fight against God; we have kept the shorter text.

Why Paul did not know the high priest is not told, and the reasons we gave are readings, not settled. So is how far the foot soldiers marched that night, and so is the road they took. The exact meaning of the word we called spearmen is genuinely unknown.

The high priest Ananias, his servants taking the tithes, and his end are from Josephus. So are the Pharisees and Sadducees, Felix's brother Pallas, and Herod naming Antipatris after his father. The line about the mind of a slave is from the Roman historian Tacitus. The list of vows that could be released is in the Mishnah, tractate Nedarim, written down long after; whether the forty men used it, we do not know. The Pilate stone is real.

How the nephew heard, his age, his walk to the fortress, what he and Paul said, the horse, the moon and the sea wind are our own invention.

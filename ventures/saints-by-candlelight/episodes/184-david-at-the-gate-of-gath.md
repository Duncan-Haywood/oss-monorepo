There is an old man standing at the gate of a city, and he is very cross about his doors.

They are good doors. Two great leaves of wood, taller than three men, studded with bronze, and every morning for thirty years he has swung them open at sunrise and swung them shut at dusk, and in between he has sat on a stone bench in their shadow and kept an eye on who comes in. He knows every farmer and every potter and every donkey in the district by sight.

And this morning there is a stranger at his doors. A young man, tall, with a fine sword at his side and no luggage at all. And the young man is scratching at the wood. Making marks on it with his fingernails, up and down, round and round, like a child with a stick in the sand. And he is muttering. And there is spit running down into his beard, and he is not wiping it away.

The old gatekeeper stands up. He opens his mouth to say, now look here, young man.

And behind him, a voice from the guardroom says, quietly, that's him. That's David. That's the one who killed Goliath.

Let me tell you what David had with him, as he walked out of that field.

Nothing.

No food. No water bottle that we know of. No spare cloak. No sword, and no spear, which for a soldier is a little like going out without your shoes. He had left the king's table in a hurry two days before, and he had been hiding in the fields since, and now he was a wanted man in his own country, with the king's whole army between him and anywhere he might have called home.

We are about a thousand years before Christ, give or take, and historians argue about the dates, as they always do, so let us just say: long ago, in the middle of the reign of King Saul, the first king of Israel.

Now, David is a young man here, and already famous. Only a few years before, he had been a shepherd boy from Bethlehem who walked out into the valley of Elah with a sling and five smooth stones and brought down the Philistine champion, Goliath of Gath. Since then he had become Saul's best captain, and married the king's daughter, and won battle after battle. And the women of Israel had made up a song about him, and sung it in the streets with tambourines when the army came home. Saul has killed his thousands, they sang, and David his tens of thousands.

That song, I am sorry to say, is where all the trouble started. Saul heard it. And Saul did not like it one bit. Thousands for me and tens of thousands for him, he thought. What more can he have but the kingdom? And from that day, the book says, Saul eyed David.

Keep that song in your mind, because it is going to come back tonight, in a place where David very much did not want to hear it.

So. A young man with nothing, walking south from Gibeah in the early morning. And just a few miles down the road, on a hill looking across at the city of Jerusalem, there was a little town called Nob.

Jerusalem, at this point, did not belong to Israel at all. It was a fortress of another people, the Jebusites, up on its ridge, and it would be years before David took it and made it his city. But near it, in sight of its walls, was Nob, and Nob was a town of priests.

The priests at Nob were the family of Eli. You may remember Eli, the old priest at Shiloh, who taught the boy Samuel to say, speak, Lord, for your servant is listening. Shiloh was long gone by now. But Eli's family had carried on, and the chief priest at Nob was a man called Ahimelech, the son of Ahitub, the son of Phinehas, the son of Eli. Eli's great-grandson. They looked after the sanctuary there, the house of God, and they kept the old customs.

And one of those customs, which we need to know about tonight, was the bread.

In the house of God there was a table, overlaid with gold. And on that table, every Sabbath, the priests set out twelve loaves of bread, in two rows of six, one loaf for each of the twelve tribes of Israel, with a little pure frankincense laid beside each row. It was called the bread of the Presence, because it was set out before the face of the Lord. It stayed there all week, and the next Sabbath fresh hot loaves were baked and set out, and the old ones were taken away, and the priests ate them, in a holy place. Only the priests. That was the rule in the Book of Leviticus.

They were big loaves, by the way. Each one was made with about two tenths of an ephah of fine flour, which is a measure we no longer use, and which works out to several pounds of flour a loaf. So if you were a hungry young man who had not eaten properly for two days, the bread of the Presence was, quite apart from being holy, a great deal of bread.

Ahimelech the priest came out to meet David trembling.

The book says that. Trembling. I think he could tell something was wrong. Here was the king's own son-in-law, the most famous captain in the land, who normally rode about with a troop of men at his back, coming up the path to the sanctuary alone, on foot, dusty, with no weapon and no servant.

Why are you alone, said Ahimelech, and no one with you?

And David told him a story.

The king has sent me on a matter, he said, and he told me, let no one know anything about the business I am sending you on. So I have arranged to meet my young men at such and such a place.

Now, I am going to be honest with you, because the book is honest with us. That was not true. The king had not sent David anywhere. The king wanted him dead. David, frightened and hungry and alone, told a priest a lie in front of the house of God, and the book reports it plainly and does not praise it. And much later, David himself would look back on that morning at Nob with great sorrow, for reasons that belong to a sadder story on another night.

So we will not pretend David was perfect. The Bible never does. That is one of the things I love about it. Its heroes are real people, and real people, when they are very frightened, sometimes do the wrong thing.

Then David said, now then. What have you got to hand? Give me five loaves of bread. Or whatever there is.

And Ahimelech looked a little helpless, and said, I have no ordinary bread. There's only the holy bread.

You can imagine the moment. It is not a bakery. It is a sanctuary. There are priests, and there is incense, and there is the golden table, and on it, perhaps just taken down that very morning to make way for the fresh hot loaves, there are the twelve old loaves of the Presence.

Ahimelech thought about it. And he asked one question, which was the question a careful priest would ask: have the young men kept themselves ritually clean? Because if they have, he meant, then perhaps, just this once.

And David said, yes, of course. Whenever I go out on campaign the young men keep themselves holy, and how much more today.

So the priest gave him the holy bread. The bread of the Presence, which had been taken away from before the Lord to be replaced by hot bread on the day it was taken away.

I want you to remember that moment. A priest, faced with a hungry man and a rule, chose to feed the hungry man. A thousand years later, somebody else would remember it too, and we will come to him at the end of the story.

One of Saul's servants was there, detained before the Lord, which probably means he was staying at the sanctuary for some vow or some purification and could not leave yet. His name was Doeg. He was an Edomite, a foreigner, and he was the chief of Saul's herdsmen.

And he saw everything.

Remember Doeg. That is all I am going to say about him tonight. He matters in a later chapter, and it is not a bedtime chapter.

You haven't got a spear or a sword here, have you? he said. I didn't bring my sword or my weapons with me, because the king's business was urgent.

That was the same story again, of course, the secret errand for the king. But his trouble was real. He needed a sword.

And Ahimelech said, there's one.

The sword of Goliath, the Philistine, whom you killed in the valley of Elah. Look, there it is, wrapped in a cloth, behind the ephod. If you want to take it, take it. There's no other here.

Isn't that wonderful? After the great fight, all those years ago, someone had brought the giant's sword to the sanctuary and laid it up there, as a thank-offering to God for the victory. And there it had stayed, wrapped in a cloth, behind the priest's ephod, the special garment the priest wore. And now the young man who had won it was standing in the doorway with nothing in his hands but bread.

And David said, there's none like that. Give it to me.

I have always liked that line. There's none like that. He knew that sword. He had last held it on the day of the greatest victory of his life, when he was a boy with a sling and nobody had ever heard of him. And now here it was again, on the worst day of his life, waiting for him.

It was, I should mention, an enormous sword. Goliath was a very large man. I like to imagine David hitching it onto his belt and finding that the end of it nearly touched the ground.

So he went down the hill from Nob with the bread of the Presence and the sword of Goliath. And the book says, David rose and fled that day from Saul.

And he went to Gath.

Now, I have to stop here, because when I first read this, I said out loud, he went where?

Gath was one of the five great cities of the Philistines, out to the west, where the hills of Judah slope down towards the coastal plain and the sea. The Philistines were Israel's enemies in those days. And Gath, in particular, was the city of Goliath. Goliath of Gath. That is what everybody called him.

So David, on the run, carrying Goliath's own sword, walked to Goliath's own home town.

The book does not tell us what he was thinking. So let me guess, and you must remember that this part is only my guess.

I think he was thinking, Saul will look for me everywhere in Israel. Every village, every cave, every shepherd's hut. But Saul will never look for me in Gath. Saul's soldiers cannot go into Gath. And the Philistines do not know my face. They know my name, but who in Gath has ever seen me up close, except Goliath, and Goliath is not going to say anything.

It is the logic of a desperate man. It is also, if you think about it, the logic of a man who has not fully considered how famous he is.

It is a long day's walk, perhaps two, from Nob to Gath. Down out of the hills, through the low rolling country where the olive trees and the barley fields are, and then out onto the wide flat plain. Let us imagine him walking it alone. Eating the holy bread as he goes, a little at a time. Sleeping under a terebinth tree with the giant's sword beside him.

And the next morning, early, he came up to the gate of Gath.

Now, our old gatekeeper. He is our own invention, and I want you to know that, but I think there must have been somebody like him, because there always is.

Let us say he is a Philistine, born and raised in Gath, sixty years old or thereabouts, with a bad knee and very strong opinions about the right way to oil a hinge. His job is the gate. The city gate in those days was not just a door in a wall. It was a whole building, a passage through the wall with rooms on each side, and benches, where the elders sat and heard cases and the merchants gossiped and the guards sat in the shade. Everything that mattered in a town happened at the gate.

And the gatekeeper's pride is his doors.

So when this tall young stranger comes in at sunrise, the old man notices him at once. He notices the sword, which is far too big for him. He notices that he has no luggage. He notices, because he is a gatekeeper and noticing is his whole life, that the young man looks like a soldier and walks like an Israelite.

And he is not the only one who notices.

Because the servants of Achish, the king of Gath, were there that morning too. And they looked at the stranger. And they looked again. And then they went to the king, and they said this. I am giving it to you exactly as the book has it, only in plain words.

Isn't this David, the king of the land? Isn't this the one they sang about to each other in the dances? Saul has killed his thousands, and David his tens of thousands?

There it is. The song.

The song that had made Saul so jealous had travelled. Of course it had. It was a catchy song. It had crossed the border and gone down onto the plain and into the Philistine cities, and the Philistines knew it too, and they knew exactly whose tens of thousands it meant. It meant theirs.

I find it rather funny, in a dreadful sort of way, that the Philistines called David the king of the land. He was not the king. He was a hunted man with a borrowed sword. But that is how it looked from the outside. The servants of Achish had heard the song, and they did their own sums.

And the book says, David took these words to heart, and he was very much afraid of Achish the king of Gath.

What would you do?

You are in the city of your enemies. You are carrying the sword of their dead champion. They have just recognised you. There are guards at the gate behind you, and the king's servants in front of you, and the king himself is about to send for you, and the last time the people of Gath saw you, you were cutting off the head of their favourite son.

David did something very strange, and very clever.

He changed his behaviour, the book says, in front of them, and he pretended to be mad in their hands. He made marks on the doors of the gate. And he let his spittle run down onto his beard.

Our old gatekeeper's doors.

I like to imagine the scene. The proud old man and his beautiful bronze-studded doors, and this young man suddenly scrabbling at the wood with his fingernails, scribbling, muttering, drawing who knows what, a sort of mad graffiti. The old Greek translation of the book says something slightly different: that he drummed on the doors of the city, beating on them with his hands. Either way, it was the behaviour of a man who had lost his wits. And the spittle on the beard was the final touch, because in that world, as in ours, a grown man who lets his spit run into his beard and does not wipe it is a man who is not himself.

It was, if you think about it, a terrible risk. And it was also, I am afraid, another deception. David was very good at those, in his young days. But I want to be fair to him. He was not tricking a kind priest out of his bread this time. He was a captured man in an enemy city, trying to stay alive without hurting anybody. If we are honest, most of us would have been quite pleased to think of it.

And it worked. Because there was a feeling, in many ancient places, that madness was a sort of touch from the gods, and that you did not lay hands on a madman. And also, more simply, because nobody wants to deal with a madman before breakfast.

So they brought him to the king. Or perhaps the king came down to the gate to see, because a commotion at the gate is always worth seeing. And Achish the king of Gath looked at this dribbling, scratching, muttering young man, and he turned to his servants, and he said the funniest line in the whole of the First Book of Samuel.

Look, you can see the man is mad. Why have you brought him to me?

And then, with feeling: Am I short of madmen, that you have brought this fellow to rave in front of me? Is this one going to come into my house?

Am I short of madmen.

I love Achish. I think he is one of the great tired men of the Bible. You can hear in that line a king who has a palace full of difficult people already, and courtiers who bring him every problem, and a city full of characters, and he looks at his servants and he says, honestly, do you think I need another one?

And so they let David go.

Not into the king's house. Not into prison. Just out. Out of the gate, past the old gatekeeper, who was, I imagine, already on his knees with a bucket and a cloth, grumbling about the state of his doors. And away across the plain.

I wonder whether the gatekeeper ever found out. Whether some years later, when David was king of all Israel, the old man sat on his bench and said to the young guards, did you know he scratched up my doors once? Right there. You can still see the marks if you look. We can never know. But I hope so.

David went back east, into the low hills between Philistine country and Bethlehem. And there, in a hillside full of caves, near a town called Adullam, he hid.

We are in the next chapter now, chapter twenty-two, and the book says something quite lovely.

When his brothers and all his father's house heard it, they went down there to him.

His family. His older brothers, who had once scolded him for coming to watch the battle in the valley of Elah. His old father Jesse. They heard where he was, and they came. Partly, I suppose, because it was no longer safe for them at home either, being the family of the man Saul wanted. But they came.

And then other people started coming too. The book gives the list, and it is not a list of heroes. Everyone who was in distress, it says, and everyone who was in debt, and everyone who was bitter in soul, gathered to him. And he became their captain. And there were with him about four hundred men.

I think that is one of my favourite verses in the whole of the Old Testament. Not the bravest or the strongest or the richest. The people in trouble. The people who owed money they could not pay. The people who were angry and sad and could not see a way forward. That was David's first army. That is who gathered round the future king in a cave. And I find it very hard to read that verse without thinking of another son of David, a thousand years later, who would gather round him exactly the same sort of people.

Let us imagine the cave, then, on the first night they were all there. A fire in the mouth of it. Four hundred unlikely men and their families, sitting about in the dark, sharing out whatever food they had. Somebody's grandmother complaining about the damp. Somebody's child asleep on a sack of barley. And the young captain sitting a little apart, by the fire, with Goliath's sword across his knees, and his own thoughts.

He did one more kind thing, in those days. His father and mother were old, and a cave in the hills is no place for the old. So David took them away east of the Dead Sea to the land of Moab, to a place called Mizpeh, and he asked the king of Moab, please, let my father and my mother come and stay with you, until I know what God will do for me.

Why Moab, of all places? The book does not say. But if you were with us for the story of Ruth and the barley harvest, you will remember that Ruth was a Moabite. And Ruth was the mother of Obed, and Obed was the father of Jesse. David's own great-grandmother came from Moab. So perhaps there were still cousins there, a long way back, who would take in an old man and an old woman for the sake of family. I think that is rather beautiful. The kindness Ruth was shown in Bethlehem coming back, three generations later, as kindness to her great-grandson's parents.

And they stayed there, the book says, all the time that David was in the stronghold.

Then a prophet came to the cave, a man called Gad, whose name you will meet again in David's story. And he said, do not stay in the stronghold. Go into the land of Judah. And David went, to a place called the forest of Hereth. And there, for tonight, we will leave him.

But before we go, there is one more thing to tell you, and it is the reason I wanted to tell this story at all.

In the Book of Psalms, many of the psalms have a little title at the top, a line or two saying whose song it is, or what tune to sing it to, or when it was made. These titles are very old, older than the Gospels. And two of them point straight at tonight's story.

Psalm fifty-six, which is fifty-five in the Greek and Orthodox numbering, has a title that says: of David, when the Philistines seized him in Gath.

And Psalm thirty-four, which is thirty-three in the Greek, has a title that says: of David, when he changed his behaviour before Abimelech, who drove him out, and he went away.

Now, you will have noticed that the king of Gath in our story was called Achish, not Abimelech. That is a real puzzle, and I will not pretend to settle it. Some think Abimelech was a title for Philistine kings, rather as every king of Egypt was called Pharaoh; there was a king of the Philistines called Abimelech back in the days of Abraham and Isaac. Others have other explanations. Nobody knows for sure. What the title is plainly pointing at is the day at the gate.

So let me tell you what David sang, if the old title is right. I am going to give you a few lines of each, in plain words.

From Psalm fifty-six, the Gath psalm, which sounds like a man who is still frightened:

When I am afraid, I put my trust in you. In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust; I will not be afraid. You have kept count of my tossings and turnings. Put my tears in your bottle. Are they not written in your book?

I find that so tender. A man who cannot sleep, in a strange city, tossing and turning, and the thought that God has counted every turn, and kept every tear, like something precious, in a bottle.

And then Psalm thirty-four. And this one does not sound frightened at all. This one sounds like a man who has got out, and is so relieved and so grateful that he cannot stop singing. In Hebrew it is an alphabet poem; each line begins with the next letter of the alphabet, as if he were going through every letter he had to thank God with.

I will bless the Lord at all times. His praise shall always be in my mouth. I sought the Lord, and he answered me, and delivered me from all my fears. This poor man cried, and the Lord heard him, and saved him out of all his troubles. The angel of the Lord camps round those who fear him, and rescues them. O taste and see that the Lord is good. Happy is the man who takes refuge in him.

The tradition does not tell us where David was when he made it. So here, and only here, I am going to let myself imagine. I like to think of him in the mouth of the cave at Adullam, a few nights after Gath, with the fire going and the four hundred asleep, and the words coming to him in the dark. This poor man cried, and the Lord heard him. That poor man was him. Not the king of the land. Not the giant-killer. A poor man, who had scratched on a door and dribbled into his beard and walked away alive.

And there is a line a little further on that I cannot help noticing, in a psalm about this particular week. Come, children, listen to me, it says. I will teach you the fear of the Lord. Keep your tongue from evil, and your lips from speaking deceit.

I don't know what David was thinking when he sang that. But I know what he had done at Nob. And I think perhaps a man who has told a frightened priest a lie, and got away with it, and then lain awake about it, is exactly the man to write that line.

Here is how it all came out, in the years after, told more quietly.

David did not stay a fugitive for ever. Saul hunted him through the wilderness for a long time, and twice David could have killed him and would not. Later on, David went to Gath again, and this time Achish took him in and gave him a town of his own, which is a whole other story we told on another night. And in the end, after Saul and Jonathan died on Mount Gilboa, David became king, first in Hebron and then in Jerusalem, the very city whose walls the priests of Nob used to look across at.

Years later, when the book lists David's mighty men, the captains who were the backbone of his kingdom, it remembers some of them as men who came down to him at the cave of Adullam. The people in debt and in distress and bitter of soul had become the king's own.

And the bread.

A thousand years later, on a Sabbath day, a young teacher from Nazareth was walking with his friends through the grainfields of Galilee, and his friends were hungry, and as they walked they picked some of the heads of grain and rubbed them in their hands and ate them. And some of the Pharisees said, look, why are they doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?

And Jesus said to them, have you never read what David did, when he was in need and was hungry, he and those who were with him? How he went into the house of God and ate the bread of the Presence, which it is not lawful for any but the priests to eat, and gave it also to those who were with him?

And he said, the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. And in Saint Matthew's telling he adds the words of the prophet Hosea: I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.

So the hungry young man at Nob, with an armful of holy bread, ends up in the Gospels, held up by the Son of David himself as an example of what the law is for.

I should mention one small puzzle there too, since we are being honest tonight. In Saint Mark's Gospel, Jesus says this happened in the time of Abiathar the high priest. In the First Book of Samuel the priest who gave David the bread is Ahimelech, and Abiathar is his son, who escaped from Nob and later became David's priest. Saint Matthew and Saint Luke do not name the priest at all. Scholars and the Fathers have explained it in several ways, and people still discuss it. It does not change the story in the least.

And Psalm thirty-four went on, too, and it went a very long way.

Saint Peter quotes it in his first letter. Like newborn babies, he says, long for the pure spiritual milk, if you have tasted that the Lord is good. And a little later he quotes the line about keeping your tongue from evil.

And then, in the early Church, the psalm found its way to the altar. Saint Cyril of Jerusalem, teaching the newly baptised in Jerusalem in the three hundreds, tells them that when they come up to receive Communion they will hear the singer inviting them, with a holy melody, to the holy mysteries, singing, O taste and see that the Lord is good.

O taste and see. The words of a hungry young man who had eaten the holy bread and escaped from the gate of Gath, sung as Christians came forward to receive the true bread from heaven. In the Orthodox Church those words are still the Communion hymn at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, on the weekdays of Lent. And in Catholic churches the psalm is sung often at Mass, and the line O taste and see has long been sung at Communion.

There is one last thing, and it is the strangest and the loveliest. Saint Augustine, the great bishop of Hippo, preached on this psalm in North Africa, more than fifteen hundred years ago. And the old Latin text he was reading said something odd about David at the gate of Gath. It said that David was carried in his own hands.

And Augustine stopped, the way a good preacher does, and asked his people: how can a man be carried in his own hands? Nobody can do that. A man can be carried in someone else's hands. But in his own?

And then he answered himself. It could not be true of David, he said, not in the plain sense. But it was true of Christ. Because at the Last Supper, when the Lord took the bread into his hands and said, this is my body, he was carrying himself in his own hands.

I think that is a beautiful thing for a preacher to find in a story about a frightened young man scratching on a door. It is the kind of thing the Fathers loved to do: to look at the Old Testament the way you look at a stained glass window, and see the light of Christ coming through it.

As for Gath itself, the city's ruins are still there, on a hill above the plain, at a place called Tell es-Safi. Archaeologists have been digging there for many years, finding walls and houses and pottery from the time of the Philistines. They have not, as far as I know, found any old doors with scratch marks on them. But then wood does not last three thousand years. Even very well oiled wood.

And Nob, the town of priests, is gone so completely that nobody is quite sure where on the hills above Jerusalem it stood.

But the bread is still given. And the psalm is still sung.

A few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the First Book of Samuel, chapter twenty-one, and chapter twenty-two, verses one to five, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. The goodbye in the field is the end of chapter twenty. The rules for the bread of the Presence are in the Book of Leviticus, chapter twenty-four, and the golden table in the Book of Exodus, chapter twenty-five. Ahimelech's family line goes back to Eli through Ahitub and Phinehas, which the book gives in chapter fourteen. The women's song is in chapter eighteen. Nob's view of Jerusalem comes from the Book of Isaiah, chapter ten, and Ruth's family line from the last chapter of the Book of Ruth.

The titles of Psalms thirty-four and fifty-six are quoted as the psalter has them. That David made Psalm thirty-four in the cave of Adullam is our own imagining; the title only ties it to the time he acted mad. The drumming on the doors is in the old Greek translation called the Septuagint. Augustine's sermon is the first of his expositions on what his Bible numbered Psalm thirty-three, and Cyril's words are from his fifth Mystagogical Catechesis. Our Lord's words about David and the bread are in Saint Mark, chapter two, Saint Matthew, chapter twelve, and Saint Luke, chapter six.

The old gatekeeper, his doors, his bench and his bucket are our own invention, and so are the road, the weather, the terebinth tree and the evening at the cave. Where Nob stood is not known for certain. Gath is usually identified with Tell es-Safi, and Adullam with a site in the low hills south-west of Bethlehem.

Picture a man on a flat roof, at noon, in the sun, and he is very hungry.

Below him a woman is working in a kitchen. He can smell it. Something with onions, something with bread. Not for him, not yet, and it is not quite ready. So the man does what a sensible person does when he is waiting for dinner and has nothing to do. He prays. And somewhere between the smell of the onions and the words of the prayer, the sky over the sea opens, and something starts to come down out of it.

It looks like a sheet. A very large sheet. It is tied at four corners, and it is being lowered slowly, the way you would lower a basket of washing from an upstairs window. And it is full.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter ten, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the road, the weather, the supper, and the talk of people waiting, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

We begin, as Luke does, not with the fisherman, but with the soldier.

His name is Cornelius. Luke gives it to us in the very first verse, and he gives us a good deal more, so let us take it in order.

He lives in a city called Caesarea. Now this is not the Caesarea you may have heard of in the north, the one with the springs, where Peter once said what he thought of Jesus. This is another one, on the coast, on the Mediterranean, about sixty miles from Jerusalem, and it is a very new city. It was built, not very long before this story, by King Herod the Great, who liked building things more than almost anything else. He built a harbour there out of huge blocks of stone, sunk into the sea. He built a palace, and a theatre, and a temple. And he named it Caesarea, after Caesar, the emperor, who was the most important man in the world and, as Herod rather hoped, would be pleased.

By the time of our story, Caesarea is where the Roman governor of the province has his headquarters. So it is a city full of officials, and clerks, and ships coming and going, and, of course, soldiers.

And Cornelius is one of the soldiers. Luke says he is a centurion, of what is called the Italian Cohort. The word centurion has the word for a hundred in it, because it began as an officer who commanded about a hundred men. The real number varied, and we need not count. What matters is that he is a man used to giving orders, and used to being obeyed, and, I should think, used to being tired.

Now here is the thing about Cornelius. Luke says that he is a devout man. That he fears God, together with all his household. That he gives alms generously to the people, and prays to God always.

Let us stop on that for a moment, because it is easy to hurry past.

A Roman officer. A man who is not a Jew. A man who, as far as anybody can tell, was not born into the faith of Israel, and has not been formally joined to it, as some others in those days were. And yet he has found the God of Israel, somehow, in the middle of a Roman army in a Roman city, and he has taken that God seriously. He prays. He gives money to people who have none. His whole household, wife and children and servants and all, fear God as well. And nobody has told him to.

The old writers have a name for people like him. They call them God-fearers. People who stand at the edge of the synagogue, so to speak, who love what they hear inside, and who have not yet been invited in. They are not unusual in those days. But they are, I think, a little bit lonely.

I should like you to keep that in mind. Because the evening we are about to tell is, in a way, the story of how somebody finally opened a door for a God-fearer. And it took a great deal of work. It took two visions, a long walk, a good deal of hunger, and a very surprised fisherman.

[pause]

Let us go to the afternoon.

It is about three o'clock. Luke says the ninth hour, which in those days meant the ninth hour counted from sunrise, so about the middle of the afternoon. It is a regular hour of prayer for the Jews, and Cornelius, though he is not a Jew, keeps it. So he is at prayer in his house, and he sees, Luke says, quite clearly, an angel of God coming in to him and saying, Cornelius.

And what does the soldier do?

He looks at the angel. He is afraid. Luke says that plainly. Fixing his eyes on him, and being terrified. And I think that is the most reasonable thing a man can do. He has faced an enemy across a field, perhaps. He has stood in front of a governor. But he has never, until this moment, had an angel say his name.

But he is a soldier, and a soldier knows what to say when he does not know what to say. He says, What is it, Lord?

And the angel tells him.

Your prayers and your alms, the angel says, have ascended as a memorial before God.

Listen to that, for it is a lovely thing to be told. Everything you have prayed, everything you have given, God has been keeping, as you might keep a letter you were fond of. Nothing was lost. None of it went unnoticed. All those quiet afternoons at the ninth hour, in a city where no one was asking him to, and every one of them was remembered.

And then comes the instruction. It is very exact. The angel says, send men to Joppa, and bring a man named Simon, who is called Peter. He is lodging with another Simon, a tanner, whose house is by the sea.

Notice how much detail there is. A name, and another name, and a trade, and a house by the sea. The angel is not vague. It is almost as though he were giving directions to a delivery boy. And I find that rather comforting. When God sends somebody to find somebody, he does not leave it to guesswork.

Now, a small word about the tanner. A tanner is a man who turns animal hides into leather. It is, to be honest, a smelly trade, and in those days tanners often worked at the edge of a town, and by the water, because of all the washing. So a house by the sea is just where you might expect to find one. Luke does not tell us anything more about why, and we should not make up more than a sensible guess.

Then, Luke says, when the angel who spoke to him had departed, Cornelius called two of his servants, and a devout soldier from among those who waited on him. He told them everything. And he sent them to Joppa.

Think of that, for a moment. Everything. He does not keep it to himself. A Roman officer sits down with two house servants and a soldier and says, an angel stood in this room, and this is what he said. And they believe him. Or at the least, they go.

That says a good deal about the sort of household it is.

* * *

Now we leave Caesarea, and we follow the three men down the coast.

Joppa is the town we know today as Jaffa, and it is south of Caesarea. Luke does not give the distance, but the old sources reckon it at something like thirty miles, which is about fifty kilometres. On foot, that is a long day and a bit. On the road with the sea on your right hand, and little villages every so often, and dust.

So they set out, that evening, as I think they must have done, and they walk. And I should like to give them something to talk about, because three men on a long road will always talk about something. I think the soldier, the devout one, who is used to marching, walks a little ahead. And I think the two servants, who are not used to marching at all, walk behind him, and complain, gently, about their feet.

And I think one of them says, quietly, what do you suppose he is like, this Peter?

And the other says, a fisherman, they say.

A fisherman, says the first. And he thinks about that. He is a house servant, from a good Roman house, and he has seen plenty of men who give orders and a good many who take them. He has never, as far as he knows, been sent to fetch a fisherman.

That is our own invention, and a small one. Luke tells us only that they went. But I like to think it was something like that.

[pause]

And now, while they walk, we go ahead of them, to Joppa, and we find Peter.

Peter is not in the best spot. I mean this kindly. He has been at Lydda, and at Joppa, and Luke has just finished telling us about the lovely thing that happened to the woman Tabitha. So Peter is, in a sense, a celebrity in the town. And he has been staying on, Luke says, for many days, in the house of Simon, the tanner. By the sea.

So we have, in the same story, two men called Simon. One is a fisherman, and the other is a tanner. The fisherman is a guest, and the tanner is the host. And I think, if you wanted to say something about how the church began, you could do worse than to say this: it began with a fisherman living in a tanner's house, by the sea, and nobody in town thinking it was at all unusual.

Luke does not say how Peter feels about the smell.

Now it is the next day. About noon, Luke says. The sixth hour. And the three men from Caesarea are, by this time, drawing near the city.

And Peter goes up on the housetop to pray.

Houses in those lands often had flat roofs, with a stair on the outside, and a low wall around, because the roof was a place where people sat in the cool of the evening, or hung washing, or slept in summer, or prayed in peace. And the roof of a house by the sea, in the middle of the day, would have had a very fine view. Blue water, white sails, gulls.

And Peter, Luke says, became very hungry, and wanted something to eat.

I like Luke for putting that in. He could have said, and Peter was transported. He could have said, and Peter was in rapt contemplation. Instead he says, he was hungry. And while they were getting the meal ready, Luke says, he fell into a trance.

We do not know quite what the word means, and we need not try to say. The word is the Greek ekstasis, which gives us our English word ecstasy, but it simply means being taken out of oneself. Something happened to Peter that was not ordinary seeing. That is all we are told, and all we should say.

And what he saw was this. Heaven opened. And something like a great sheet, Luke says, descending, being let down by four corners to the earth. And in it were all kinds of four-footed animals and reptiles of the earth and birds of the air.

Now I need to stop here, and tell you something, because it is the heart of the story, and it will not make sense without it.

Peter is a Jew. He has been brought up on the law of Moses, from his earliest days, as the law was given on Mount Sinai. And that law tells his people what they may eat, and what they may not. It is written down in the book of Leviticus, in chapter eleven, and again in the book of Deuteronomy, in chapter fourteen. Some animals are clean, which means they may be eaten. Some are unclean, which means they may not. Pigs are unclean. Hares are unclean. Shellfish. Many kinds of birds. Camels. And the list goes on, quite exactly.

And Peter has kept every word of it. Every day of his life. He has never in his life, he will tell us in a moment, eaten anything unclean. His mother did not cook it. His wife, if you remember the Gospels, did not cook it. His whole people did not cook it. For many hundreds of years, it was one of the things that marked them out as God's own. In a world where everybody else ate anything.

So now you see the sheet. And you see what is in it. Everything. Clean animals and unclean. All together, in a heap, in a sheet, coming down out of heaven, in front of a hungry man.

And a voice says: Rise, Peter. Kill and eat.

[pause]

Now, I should like to tell you what Peter does, because it is exactly what you would expect Peter to do. He argues.

By no means, Lord, he says.

Think of that. The Lord, or what he takes to be the Lord, has just said, kill and eat. And Peter says, by no means. Which is a remarkable thing to say to somebody you call Lord. It is rather like saying, certainly not, sir, to the person who is your master. And yet it is just what he says, because, as he goes on to explain, I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean.

He is not being rude. He is being faithful. The word he uses, common, means something that is ordinary, profane, not set apart. And the word unclean means what it says. And Peter, who has kept the rules so carefully, simply cannot bring himself to break one, even when asked, even in a vision. And I find that, in a way, to be one of the most honest things in the whole book.

The voice says it again. And this is what the voice says: What God has cleansed, you must not call common.

That is all. Just that. No explaining. No saying, you see, the law has changed. Just, what God has cleansed, you must not call common.

And Luke says that this happened three times. Three times the sheet came down. Three times Peter said no. Three times the voice said what it said. And then the sheet was taken up again to heaven.

And Peter is left on the roof. In the sun. Still hungry, as far as we know. And still, as Luke says in a lovely understated way, inwardly perplexed about what the vision might mean.

I find that comforting too. Peter does not understand it. He does not leap up and say, I see, it is about the Gentiles! He sits there on his rooftop, in the noon heat, and does not know what it means. And I think that is how most of us receive most of the things that matter. We are shown something, and we do not understand it, and we sit down with it for a while.

* * *

[pause]

But he does not have to sit very long. Because just at that moment, Luke says, the men sent by Cornelius, having asked their way to Simon's house, were standing at the gate.

Look at the timing. The vision ends. And the knock comes. And Luke puts the two things in one sentence, as though to say, now put them together.

They call out, and ask whether Simon, who is called Peter, is lodging there.

And Peter, up on the roof, is still thinking about the vision. Then, Luke says, the Spirit said to him: Behold, three men are looking for you. Rise, go down, and go with them, without hesitating, for I have sent them.

Without hesitating. Remember those two words. They are the whole point of the day. Because for a man who has just refused to eat an unclean animal three times, and who has never in his life been inside the house of a Gentile, it is a very large thing to say. Go with them. Without hesitating. For I have sent them.

And Peter goes down.

Now, I should like you to picture the scene at the gate. Peter comes down the outside stair, and there are three men standing there. A soldier, in his Roman dress, or something like it. And two servants, in the dust of the road. They are tired and dirty, and, I should think, a little nervous. And Peter says, in a simple and honest voice, I am the one you are looking for. Why have you come?

And they tell him. Cornelius, a centurion, an upright and God-fearing man, well spoken of by the whole nation of the Jews, was directed by a holy angel to send for you, to come to his house, and to hear what you have to say.

Do hear what they say there. They do not say, he wants to convert you. They do not say, he wants to arrest you. They say, he wants to hear what you have to say. It is a small and humble errand, and the three men have walked thirty miles for it.

And here is what Peter does. Luke says, so he invited them in to be his guests.

Think about what that means. A Jew, who keeps the law, is inviting three Gentiles to spend the night. And not just in the yard. In the house. At supper. It was not forbidden outright, perhaps, in every case, but it would have raised eyebrows, and it was the sort of thing that a strict Jew did not do lightly. And Peter does it, without a word. Because the vision has been working on him. I think he is beginning to see.

And here, where Luke says nothing, we may be permitted to imagine, gently, the supper. It is evening, and the lamp is lit, and the tanner's wife has done what she can with a larger table than she was expecting. And there is a soldier and two servants and a fisherman and a tanner, eating together, in a house by the sea. And I think there is some bread. And I think it is a rather quiet meal, at first, as meals are when strangers do not yet know one another. And then somebody says something about the road. And somebody laughs.

That is our own invention, and a small one. Luke says only that they were his guests. But a thing like that does not happen without a supper.

[pause]

The next day, Luke says, Peter rose and went off with them. And some of the brethren from Joppa went along, six of them, as Peter will say later, when he tells the story in Jerusalem. And that is a clever detail, I think. Because Peter, who is a careful man, takes witnesses. He does not go alone. Six believers from Joppa, who will be able to say, afterwards, we saw it too.

And the next day, they enter Caesarea.

That means two days of walking, or something like it. It is a little longer than the servants took, because there are more of them, and I should think they stopped on the way. And then, at the end of it, there is the house of Cornelius.

And here Luke gives us one of the most touching details in the whole story.

Cornelius, he says, was expecting them. And he had called together his relatives and close friends.

Think about that. The soldier has had two days, perhaps three, to wait. He sent his men on the road, and he has heard nothing. He does not know whether they have found the man. He does not know whether the man will come. He has had a vision, but a vision is a very strange thing to hold on to while the days go by. And what does he do? He does not sit and worry. He invites everybody. His relatives. His close friends. He fills the house.

I think, in his place, I would have waited quietly by myself, in case nobody came. But Cornelius does the braver thing. He prepares the house for a guest he has no proof is coming, and he gathers the people he loves, because he has been told something wonderful, and he wants them to hear it.

And then, a voice at the door. Or footsteps in the courtyard. And the soldier, who has faced all kinds of things, comes out to meet the man he has sent for.

And this is what he does. When Peter entered, Cornelius met him, and fell at his feet, and worshipped him.

Now, we should be careful here. The word Luke uses means to bow down, to give reverence. Whatever exactly Cornelius meant by it, Peter understands it as too much. And he does not accept it. He lifts the man up, at once, and says: Stand up. I too am a man.

I love that. I am a man. It is such a plain and simple thing to say, and it says so much. Peter has just been through three days that would turn most heads. He has seen heaven open. He has been called, by name, by the Spirit. A Roman officer has walked thirty miles, and is on his knees. And Peter says, stand up, I am a man like you.

And then, we read, as he talked with him, he went in. And he found many persons gathered.

[pause]

And now Peter says something that I think is the bravest sentence in the chapter.

He looks round at the room. All the Gentile faces. The relatives, the friends, the servants, the soldiers. And he says: You yourselves know how unlawful it is for a Jew to associate with or to visit anyone of another nation. But God has shown me that I should not call any person common or unclean.

Do you hear it? The sheet has come down, and it has been put to its right use. The vision was never about the animals. Or not only about the animals. It was about the people. What God has cleansed, you must not call common. And Peter, who sat on the rooftop not understanding, now stands in a Roman house and understands.

Luke does not say that Peter worked it out on the road. He may have. A man has a good deal of time to think on thirty miles. But he says it plainly, and he says it here, standing in the room, in front of everyone, and it costs him something.

Notice, too, that Peter does not say, I have a new rule. He says, God has shown me. It is not his idea. It is not a clever argument. It is something that was shown to him, in a vision, by somebody who knew better.

And then he asks the question that is, really, the hinge on which the whole story turns. He says: So I ask, why did you send for me?

And Cornelius tells him.

He tells the story from the beginning. Four days ago, he says, about this very hour, I was keeping the ninth hour of prayer in my house. And behold, a man stood before me in bright clothing. And he said, Cornelius, your prayer has been heard, and your alms have been remembered before God. Send therefore to Joppa and ask for Simon, who is called Peter. He is lodging in the house of Simon, a tanner, by the sea. So I sent for you at once. And you have been kind enough to come.

And then, and I think this is the finest line in the chapter, he says: Now therefore we are all here in the presence of God, to hear all that you have been commanded by the Lord.

All that you have been commanded by the Lord. Everything. Not just the pleasant parts. Not just what we would like to hear. We are all here, he says. In the presence of God. We are ready. Say it.

I can think of few better welcomes to give to a preacher. A room full of people, ready to listen, without being forced. A whole household sitting in a row, asking only to be told whatever there is to tell.

* * *

[pause]

And now Peter speaks.

And the first thing he says is a sentence that I think is worth saying slowly. He opens his mouth, Luke says, and he says: Truly I perceive that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him.

God shows no partiality. Let us leave that in the air for a moment.

Notice that he says, I perceive. He has not always known. He has just learned it, today, on a rooftop, in front of a sheet. A man who was raised to know that God had chosen his people from among the nations is saying, with great care, that this choosing does not mean what he thought it meant. That in every nation there are people who fear God and do what is right, and that God is pleased with them. Not because they are Jews, and not because they are not. But because they fear him, and do what is right.

I want to say that this is not a new teaching, in the sense of being a different one. The prophets said it. Peter says himself, a little later, in this very speech, that all the prophets bear witness to Jesus. But it is new for Peter, in the sense of being a thing he had not fully taken in until this week. And Luke tells it exactly so. He does not make Peter wiser than he was. He lets him say, I perceive.

And then Peter tells them the Gospel. I should like to give it to you very briefly, because it is the same story we have told on many other nights, and Luke gives it in a short space.

He tells them about the word God sent to Israel, preaching the good news of peace through Jesus Christ, who is Lord of all. He tells them what happened throughout all Judea, beginning from Galilee, after the baptism that John preached. How God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power. How he went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him. He says: and we are witnesses of all that he did, both in the country of the Jews and in Jerusalem.

We are witnesses. Peter was there. He can say it with his own mouth. He saw the healings, and he saw the cross, and, as he goes on, he saw the risen Lord. They put him to death, Peter says, by hanging him on a tree. But God raised him on the third day, and made him manifest, not to all the people but to us, who had been chosen by God as witnesses, who ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead.

Who ate and drank with him. I hope you will notice that. Peter is not an expert in the abstract. He is a man who sat at table with the risen Lord, and he tells it to a room of people at table with him.

And then he speaks of the command. That Jesus ordered them to preach to the people, and to testify that he is the one ordained by God to be judge of the living and the dead. And that all the prophets bear witness, that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name.

That is the last thing Peter gets to say. Because he does not finish.

[pause]

While Peter was still saying these things, Luke tells us, the Holy Spirit fell on all who heard the word.

I would like you to notice that sentence, because I think it is the most surprising in the chapter. Peter has not asked for the Spirit to come. He has not laid on hands. He has not given any kind of signal. He has not even reached the end of his sermon. He is in the middle of a sentence, I think, and the Spirit simply comes. As if to say, there is no more need to wait.

And what happened, Luke says, is that the believers from among the circumcised, who had come with Peter, were amazed. That means the Jewish Christians. The six from Joppa, among others. They were astonished that the gift of the Holy Spirit had been poured out even on the Gentiles. For they heard them speaking in tongues, and extolling God.

Now we should say what we can say and no more. Luke does not describe in detail what this was. He says that they spoke in tongues, and that they were praising God. It was the same sign, in short, that the disciples had at the first Pentecost, in Jerusalem. And that is exactly what Peter will say, a little later, when he is telling the story: the Holy Spirit fell on them just as on us at the beginning.

Just as on us at the beginning. Remember those words. That is the whole argument. It is not that the Gentiles were allowed to share. It is that the very same thing happened to them, in the very same way, without anyone's permission. A room of Romans, in a house in Caesarea, receiving exactly what a room of Galileans had received in Jerusalem.

And now Peter has to decide what to do.

And what he does, I think, is the finest thing about him. He does not say, well, this is awkward. He does not say, I had better consult the others first. He turns to the six, and he says, in front of everyone, Can anyone withhold water for baptizing these people, who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?

It is a question, but it is not really a question. It has only one answer. And no one gives it, because no one has anything to say. The six are silent, and they know what the answer is.

So Peter commands them to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ. And they are. A Roman household, and probably a bucket or a basin or whatever water they could find, and a fisherman standing by, and the sound of a great many people being very happy.

And at the end of it, Luke says something wonderfully ordinary. Then they asked him to remain for some days.

Of course they did. Whoever has received a great gift, and has just met the man who brought it, wants him to stay a while. And I think Peter said yes. I think he sat down, for a few days, in a Roman house, and ate what was set before him. All the things that, a few days before, he would have turned down. Whatever the household was serving, he ate it. And Luke does not tell us a thing about the menu, but I should like to think there was bread, and perhaps fish, and Peter was not hungry any more.

[pause]

Now, if we stopped there, it would be a lovely story. But Luke goes on. Because, as you might expect, news travels.

Back in Jerusalem, Luke says, the apostles and the brethren who were in Judea heard that even the Gentiles had received the word of God. And when Peter went up to Jerusalem, the circumcision party, which means the believers who were strict about the law, criticised him. They said: you went to uncircumcised men and ate with them.

You went in, and you ate. That is what they are upset about. Not the baptism. Not the Spirit. The eating. Because to a strict Jewish believer, sitting down at table with Gentiles was the real crossing of the line.

And what does Peter do? I like this part. He does not get angry. He does not say, I am an apostle, and I know better. He does not quote a rule. He simply tells them the story, from the beginning, in order. And that is what Luke gives us in chapter eleven. The roof. The sheet. The voice. The three men at the gate. The six brethren who came along. The angel in the house of Cornelius. The Spirit falling.

And he finishes with a very honest line. If then God gave the same gift to them as he gave to us when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, who was I, that I could withstand God?

Who was I, that I could withstand God? He does not say, I made a decision. He says, I did not stand in the way. He sees himself as someone who, almost by accident, got out of the way of something God was already doing.

And now, here is the last line of the story, and I think it is the loveliest.

When they heard this, Luke says, they were silenced. And they glorified God, saying: Then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance unto life.

They were silenced. And then they praised. It is, I think, a good example of how an argument ought to end. A group of people who came with objections sat and listened to a plain story, and they let it change them. And that was the first of many such arguments in the early church. We shall hear about a larger one, another night, when the whole church gathers in Jerusalem to talk about it. And even then, Peter stands up, and says what he saw in Caesarea. But that is another night.

Now let us slow down, because the night is getting on.

What became of the people in our story? Luke tells us very little. Peter, of course, we know a good deal about, and we have told some of it on other nights. Cornelius, after this chapter, is not named again in the New Testament. We do not hear what happened to him. Luke tells us a household was baptized, and then moves on.

What we have after that is tradition, and we should say plainly that it is tradition. Some of the later writers of the church say that Cornelius became a bishop, in Caesarea, and that he served there for many years. Others have other stories. We cannot check them, and we shall leave them where we found them. What we can say is that both our traditions honour him as a saint. And the Catholic and Orthodox churches each have a day for him, and calendars differ, so do look at your own.

And Peter has his feast, too. The Catholic Church and the Orthodox Church both keep the feast of Saints Peter and Paul at the end of June. The Orthodox, in many places, follow the older calendar for the date, so it falls a couple of weeks later. But either way, he is remembered together with his friend Paul, who, you will recall, went on to carry this very news to a great many Gentiles indeed.

And there is a small thing I should like to say before we go.

If you ever feel that you are standing on the edge of something, that you are the sort of person who prays in a house where nobody else prays, who gives where nobody is looking, who loves a God whom nobody has quite introduced you to, then I should like you to think of Cornelius. His prayers were not wasted. The angel says so, in so many words. They went up, and they were kept, and a long way off, on a roof by the sea, somebody was being prepared to bring him the rest.

And if you are the other sort, the sort who knows the rules, and is rather careful about them, and who finds, to your surprise, that God has just come down the sky and asked you to do something you have never done, then I should like you to think of Peter. He argued. He was perplexed. He did not understand until he was standing in the room. And God was patient with him all the same.

I think most of us are a bit of both.

The sun has gone down on the coast. In Caesarea, the lamps are being lit in the big house of the centurion, and people are sitting on the floor, and somebody is passing the bread. There is a stranger at the table, in a plain cloak, and nobody is looking at him as a stranger any more. Out in the harbour, the great stones that Herod laid are lapped by a quiet sea. And far down the coast, in a tanner's house, a woman is putting out the last of the lamps, and wondering whether her guest will be back for supper.

Nobody is hungry now.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is told from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter ten, with Peter's report in chapter eleven, verses one to eighteen, and a short look back in chapter fifteen. It is Scripture for both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches, and we told it straight.

What is Scripture: Cornelius, the centurion of the Italian Cohort at Caesarea, devout and God-fearing, giving alms and praying; the angel at the ninth hour, and the instruction to send to Joppa for Simon called Peter, lodging with Simon the tanner by the sea; the two servants and the devout soldier; Peter on the housetop at noon, hungry; the trance, the sheet let down by four corners, the animals, the voice, Peter's refusal, the words what God has cleansed you must not call common, three times; the Spirit's word about the three men; the invitation to lodge; the journey with brethren from Joppa; Cornelius waiting with his relatives and friends; Peter raising him with the words I too am a man; Peter's words about what is lawful and what God has shown him; Cornelius's account; Peter's sermon; the Spirit falling before he had finished; the tongues; the question about water and the baptism; the request to stay; and, in chapter eleven, the criticism in Jerusalem, Peter's telling in order, and the words of praise at the end.

What we flagged as not in the text: Luke does not give the distance from Joppa to Caesarea. The old sources reckon something like thirty miles, and we gave it only as that. He does not say why the tanner's house was by the sea, and our remark about the trade was a guess. He does not say what the sheet's contents were beyond the kinds, and we did not add to them. He does not describe the tongues beyond saying that people spoke in them and praised God, and we did not say more.

What we invented: the talk of the three men on the road, the supper in the tanner's house, the thoughts of Cornelius as he waited, and the small things said around the table in Caesarea. None of it changes what happens or what it means.

What is tradition: that Cornelius later became a bishop at Caesarea is a later report, and we left it out of the story.

For further reading, the Acts of the Apostles itself is the best place, and the book of Leviticus, chapter eleven, shows the food laws which Peter had kept all his life.

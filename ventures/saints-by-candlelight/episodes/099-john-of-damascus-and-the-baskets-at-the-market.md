Imagine a market, a long time ago, in a city in Syria.

It is a hot afternoon. The street is narrow and noisy and full of people. There are men selling figs, and men selling cloth, and a man selling copper pots who bangs one with a spoon every few minutes, in case anybody had forgotten he was there.

And at the edge of the street, on the ground, sits a monk.

He is not a young monk. He has grey in his beard. His robe is old and patched and very dusty, as if he has walked a long way in it. In front of him on the ground is a pile of baskets. Plain baskets, woven out of palm leaves, the sort you might keep onions in.

A woman stops and picks one up. "How much?" she says.

The monk tells her.

She puts the basket down again, rather quickly. "For that," she says, "I could buy a donkey to carry the onions."

A little crowd gathers, the way crowds do when there is something to laugh at. And then a man pushes to the front, a well-dressed man, and he looks at the monk. And he looks again. And his face goes quite still.

"Master Mansur?" he says. "Is that you?"

Last time, we met Saint Andrew of Crete, the archbishop who wrote the Great Canon, the longest hymn the Church still sings. And you may remember that Andrew was born in Damascus, in Syria, in about the year six hundred and sixty. And I said that if you had walked through Damascus a few years later, you might have passed another little boy who would also grow up to write hymns. Well. Tonight is that little boy's turn.

His name, as a saint, is John. Saint John of Damascus. Some books call him John Damascene, which means the same thing, only in a slightly grander voice. But at home, when he was a boy, he was very probably called by his family's name, which was Mansur. It is an Arabic name. It means something like "the one who is helped to win", which is a good name for a family, and I will come back to it, because a lot of people later on were very rude about it.

Let me tell you about the city first.

Damascus is one of the oldest cities in the world that people have never stopped living in. It sits on a green plain at the edge of the desert, and the reason it is green is a river that comes down from the mountains and spreads out into a great many channels and gardens and orchards. The old Greeks had a lovely name for that river. They called it the Chrysorrhoas, which means "the golden stream". Hold on to that. It comes back at the end.

When John was born, in about the year six hundred and seventy-five, though nobody is quite sure, Damascus was the capital of the whole Arab caliphate. That was an enormous empire. It stretched from the edge of India to North Africa, and very soon it would reach into Spain. And its rulers, the caliphs, lived in Damascus.

The caliphs were Muslims. But most of the people of Syria, at that time, were still Christians. They had their churches and their bishops and their monasteries, and they went on with their lives. And when the new rulers arrived, they found that running an empire involves an enormous amount of paperwork. Taxes have to be counted. Accounts have to be kept. Somebody has to know how much grain came in from which village and where it went. And the people who already knew how to do all that, who had done it for the old Roman government, were mostly Christians who wrote in Greek.

So for a good many years, the caliphs kept them on. It is a bit like a new owner buying a very large shop and very sensibly keeping the people who know where everything is.

John's family was one of those. His grandfather, called Mansur, had been an important official in the city's finances. An old chronicle says it was he who arranged the terms when Damascus opened its gates to the Arab armies, which, if it is true, means he helped the city come through a change of rulers without being burned down, and that is not nothing. John's father, called Sergius in Greek and Sarjun in Arabic, served the caliphs too. We know about him from Arabic historians as well as Christian ones. He seems to have been something like a very senior treasury official. Which means that when John was small, his father's job, more or less, was to know where the caliph's money was.

I imagine there were not many arguments in that house about who had taken the last coin from the jar.

Now, what about John's schooling? Here we come to the old Life for the first time, so let me say a word about it.

Most of the colourful stories about John come from a Life of him written in Greek, and there is an Arabic version too, two or three hundred years after he died. A Life, in this sense, means a biography of a saint written to be read aloud to monks and the faithful. These Lives are precious, and they often keep real memories. But they also gather legends the way a coat gathers burrs on a long walk. So when I tell you something from the Life, I will say so, and you can hold it in that light.

The Life says that one day John's father was walking through the slave market in Damascus. In those days captives from wars were sold there, which was a cruel and ordinary thing in the world at the time. And among the captives was a monk from Italy, from across the sea, called Cosmas. He was sitting there weeping. And when someone asked him why, he said, more or less, "I am not crying because I am a captive. I am crying because I have spent my whole life learning things, and I have never had the chance to teach any of it to anyone, and now I shall die and it will all be wasted."

Which is, I think, the most scholarly reason for crying I have ever heard.

Sergius, the Life says, bought his freedom on the spot, took him home, and gave him two pupils. One was his own son, John. The other was a boy Sergius had adopted, an orphan, also called Cosmas, which must have made mealtimes confusing. Did you mean Cosmas the teacher, or Cosmas the boy? Somebody probably had to point.

And the old monk taught them everything. Grammar and poetry and music. Mathematics, and astronomy, which in those days meant knowing the stars by name. Logic, which is the art of arguing properly, and which was going to be very useful to John later on. And, above all, the Scriptures and the teaching of the Church.

We cannot check this story. But we can say this much. When John grew up, he wrote Greek like someone who had been very, very well taught. And the boy Cosmas grew up to be a hymn-writer too, and a bishop, and a saint, honoured in the Orthodox Church as Saint Cosmas the hymn-writer. So whoever their teacher was, he did not waste his learning after all.

* * *

When John grew up, he did what you might expect a boy from that family to do. He went into the government. The Life says he took over his father's position. Historians are not sure exactly what job he held, but it seems clear he was an official of some importance, at the caliph's court, in the richest city in that part of the world.

He would have had a good house. Servants. Fine clothes. People who stood up when he came into a room. People who wanted favours. I expect he was very good at it. He had been taught logic, after all, and if there is one thing a treasury needs, it is somebody who can say, very calmly and reasonably, "I'm afraid the numbers do not add up."

But two things were changing in the world around him.

The first was close to home. Around the year seven hundred, the caliphs decided that the government's accounts should be kept in Arabic, not Greek. Which was a reasonable thing for an Arab empire to want. But it meant that, little by little, the old Christian families who had run the paperwork found there was less room for them. And at about the same time, in the middle of Damascus, the great old church of Saint John the Baptist was taken down, and on its site the caliph built a magnificent mosque, which still stands today. And here is something rather beautiful. Inside that mosque, even now, there is a shrine which has been honoured for many centuries as the resting place of the head of John the Baptist. Christians and Muslims both come to pray there. So the Baptist did not leave the middle of Damascus. He just found himself with different neighbours.

Some historians think that these changes are part of why John, at some point, left his career behind. We do not know for certain. The Life tells a different story about why he left, a much more dramatic one, and I will tell it to you shortly.

The second change was far away. It was in Constantinople, the great Christian capital, in the empire the caliphs were often at war with.

In about the year seven hundred and twenty-six, the emperor there, a hard and able soldier called Leo the Third, began to turn against holy images. Icons, we call them. The painted pictures of Christ, and of his Mother, and of the saints, which Christians had kept in their churches and their homes for a very long time. And Leo, and those who agreed with him, came to think that these pictures were idols. That honouring a painting of Christ was like the old pagans bowing down to statues. That it was against the commandment, "You shall not make for yourself a graven image."

The old chronicles say that one of the first things that happened was that soldiers were sent to take down a great image of Christ that hung over one of the palace gates in Constantinople, and that some of the people who saw it happen were so upset they tried to stop them. And then, over the next years, it grew from there. Icons were taken down from churches. In seven hundred and thirty, the patriarch of Constantinople, Saint Germanos, an old man who would not agree to it, had to give up his throne. Some of you may remember Germanos from last time, from Andrew of Crete's story.

People had a long Greek word for those who broke the images. They called them iconoclasts, which means exactly that, image-breakers. We still use the word today for somebody who knocks down old ideas. But at the time it was not a figure of speech. They meant it.

Now, if you were a bishop inside the empire, and you disagreed with the emperor, you could lose your church, or your freedom, or worse. But John was not inside the empire. He was in Damascus, under the caliph, and the emperor in Constantinople had no power over him at all. Which is a strange gift of history. The one man in a position to say exactly what he thought about the emperor's policy, without the emperor being able to touch him, was a Christian living under a Muslim ruler.

And John said it. He wrote three treatises, which is a long word for long essays, in defence of the holy images. And they were read, and copied, and carried across the borders, and they became the great defence of icons that the whole Church would come back to.

I will tell you what he said in them a little later, when we have him somewhere quiet to say it. But first, the Life's story.

So. Here is how the old Life tells it.

The Life says that the emperor Leo heard about these writings, and was very angry, and could not reach John to punish him. So he did something sly. He found some letters John had written, and had a clever scribe study the handwriting until he could copy it perfectly. And then the scribe wrote a forged letter, as if from John to the emperor, offering to betray Damascus. Saying, more or less, "The city is poorly guarded. Send an army, and I will help you take it." And Leo sent this forged letter to the caliph, with a polite note of his own, saying, "I thought you would want to see what your trusted official is writing to me."

You can imagine how that went down.

The caliph sent for John and showed him the letter. And John said, "That is my handwriting. Or something very like it. But I never wrote that letter." And the caliph did not believe him.

Now, here is the part I will tell quickly, because it is bedtime. The Life says the caliph ordered that John's right hand, his writing hand, be cut off as a punishment, and hung up in the market for people to see. That is a hard sentence even to say. So I will not say any more about it than that.

But the Life says that in the evening, John asked for his hand to be given back to him. And he took it home. And he had in his house an icon of the Mother of God, Mary holding her Son. And he knelt in front of it, and he put the hand against his wrist, and he prayed. He prayed something like this. "Lady, you know why this happened. It happened because I wrote for the holy images. Ask your Son to give me back my hand, and I will use it to write for him for the rest of my life."

And he fell asleep there, praying, in front of the icon.

And the Life says that while he slept, he saw the Mother of God, looking at him kindly out of the picture. And she said to him, "Your hand is whole. Now keep your promise." And when he woke up, his hand was joined again. There was only a thin red line around the wrist, like a thread.

The Life goes on. It says the caliph heard about it, came to see, understood that John had been innocent all along, and begged his pardon. And he offered John his old job back, with more honour than ever. And John, very politely, said no thank you. Because he had made a promise, and he meant to keep it. And soon after that, he gave away his wealth to the poor and left Damascus for the monastery.

And there is one more part to this story. The Life and later tradition say that John, in thanks, made a little hand out of silver and fixed it to the bottom of the icon. And so that icon came to be known as the Mother of God of the Three Hands. Her two hands, as she holds her Son, and the silver one. There is an icon by that name today in the monastery of Hilandar on Mount Athos in Greece, and the tradition of that monastery is that it is the very one, and that it came there by way of the great Serbian saint, Sava, who was given it on a visit to John's monastery hundreds of years later.

Now, what are we to make of all this? Historians, including people who love John very much, mostly think the story of the hand is a legend that grew up long after he died. For one thing, many think he had already left Damascus and become a monk before he wrote about icons at all. So we cannot tell it to you as history. We tell it to you as what the old Life says. But I will say this. The Church has kept that story, and painted that icon, and prayed in front of it, for a very long time, because of what it says about John. It says that everyone who knew him thought of his writing hand as something given to God. And that, at least, is true.

* * *

However and whenever John left Damascus, we know where he went.

About ten miles from Jerusalem, out to the east, the land drops away into a dry, brown, rocky country that runs down to the Dead Sea. And through it runs a deep gorge, the valley of the Kidron. And there, on the side of the cliff, about two hundred years before John was born, a monk called Saint Sabbas had founded a monastery. It was built partly into the rock itself, with little cells clinging to the cliff face like swallows' nests, and towers, and a church, and a well. It is called the Great Lavra of Saint Sabbas, or in Arabic, Mar Saba. And here is a thing I find marvellous. It is still there. Monks still live in it today. It is one of the oldest monasteries in the world that has never stopped being a monastery.

It is not a comfortable place. In summer it is very hot and in winter the wind comes up the gorge like a cold dog sniffing round the door. There is not much water. Monks there wove baskets and mats out of palm leaves to earn their bread, and prayed, and sang the psalms, and kept quiet a great deal.

So imagine it. A man who has been one of the great officials of a great city, who has had servants and a fine house and people standing up when he came in, walks up to the gate of this place in the dust, and asks to be let in.

And now I want to give him some company. Because the old Life does not tell us about the young monks John lived among, but there must have been some. So let us imagine one of them. Let us call him Elias. He is about fifteen. He comes from a village near Bethlehem, he has two older brothers and no money, and his job is to carry water up from the well, which he does all day, and which has given him very strong arms and a strong opinion about the well.

Elias is the one who opens the gate. And he looks at the man standing there, and at his clothes, which are still rather fine, and his hands, which have clearly never woven a basket in their life.

"Are you lost?" Elias says.

"I hope not," says John. "I hope I've arrived."

* * *

The Life says that the abbot did not quite know what to do with John. Everybody had heard of him. He was famous, and clever, and used to giving orders. And none of the older monks wanted to be in charge of him, because he was so learned that they were afraid he would argue with them and win.

At last one old monk agreed to take him. We will call him the elder, which is what the monks called a spiritual father. And the elder gave John some rules.

Do nothing by your own will. Do what you are told, and do it gladly.

Do not write letters to anyone.

Do not talk about the things of the world, or about how important you used to be.

And do not write anything at all. No books. No poems. No hymns. Nothing.

You can imagine Elias's face when he heard about that last one.

"No writing?" he says to John, that evening, as they sit on the wall in the last of the light. "But writing's the one thing you're good at. I mean, the one thing you're good at that's any use. You're terrible at baskets."

"I am terrible at baskets," John agrees. He holds up the one he has been working on all afternoon. It is lopsided, and there is a gap in the side through which you could easily lose an onion.

"So why did he say no writing?"

John thinks about it for a while. The gorge goes blue below them. Somewhere a bird is making one small sound over and over again, as if it is trying to remember a tune.

"I think," he says at last, "he wants to find out whether I came here to be a monk, or to be a famous writer who happens to live in a monastery. And to be honest, Elias, I'm not entirely sure myself yet. So it seems a very good question."

Elias considers this. "I'd still have said yes to the writing," he says.

"That," says John, "is why nobody has made you an elder."

* * *

Here is my favourite of all the old Life's stories, and I think the elder must have smiled a little when he thought of it.

One day the elder gave John a pile of baskets. Not his own lopsided ones, I hope. And he said, "Take these to Damascus and sell them." And then he named a price. And the price was enormous. Ridiculous. Something like ten times what any basket was worth, perhaps more.

Damascus. Of all the places in the world. The city where everyone knew him. Where he had been one of the great men. The elder was sending him back there, in a patched robe, to sit in the market and make a fool of himself.

So John went. The Life does not tell us about the road, so let us give him Elias for it, to carry half the baskets and complain about the hills. It was a long walk, more than a week each way, up through the hills of Galilee and over the Golan and on across the plain, sleeping in villages and at other monasteries along the way. And Elias, who had never been further than Jerusalem, saw more of the world in that walk than in his whole life before. He saw snow on top of Mount Hermon, which he had not known was possible. He saw the green of the Damascus gardens coming up out of the brown, like someone had spilled paint. And he saw John go quieter and quieter as they came nearer.

"Are you all right?" Elias asks, on the last morning, as they come in under the city gate.

"Ask me tonight," says John.

And so they sat down in the market, and you know how the rest of it goes, because I told it to you at the beginning. People asked the price. People laughed. Some of them were not very kind about it. A few of them recognised him, which was worse. "Isn't that Mansur? The one from the treasury? Look at him now." And John said nothing back, but only sat there with his baskets, being laughed at, in the city where he had once been important.

And then, the Life says, a man came along who had once worked in John's own household. A servant, or a steward. And he recognised his old master. And he did not laugh. He was very moved. And without a word about the price, he paid it, the whole ridiculous sum, and took the baskets. And the Life does not say what he did with them. I hope he put onions in them.

Elias, who had watched all of this with his mouth slightly open, walks with John out of the gate that afternoon with the money in a little bag.

"Well," says Elias. "That was horrible."

"Yes," says John.

"But we sold them."

"We did."

"So," says Elias, "are you all right now? You said to ask you tonight."

John walks for a while before he answers. And when he does, he sounds surprised.

"Do you know," he says, "I think I am. I think I minded much more about them laughing on the way here than I did when it actually happened. I spent the whole road dreading it. And then it came, and it was only people laughing, and then it was over. And the man was so kind."

He looks back at the city once, golden in the evening.

"I think," he says, "I've been carrying Mansur around for a very long time. The important man. I may have left him in the market."

"Good," says Elias. "He sounded heavy."

* * *

The next story from the Life is the one that let John write again.

Some time later, one of the monks at the monastery heard that his brother had died. His brother after the flesh, as they used to say, meaning his real brother, back home. And the monk was heartbroken. He could not stop crying. And he came to John and said, "Please. You know how to write. Write me something. A song. A hymn I can sing for my brother. Something that says how I feel. I can't find the words and you can."

And John said, "I'm not allowed. My elder has forbidden it."

And the monk begged. And begged. And at last John gave in. Not because he wanted to be a writer again, but because the man in front of him was so unhappy. And he wrote a hymn for the dead. The tradition links it to the hymns that are still sung at funerals in the Orthodox Church today, which are very beautiful. They say, more or less, that there is no sweetness in this life that does not have some grief mixed into it. That all the glory of the world is like a shadow and a dream. That we look at the graves and see the beauty that God made in us lying there without form or glory. And then they turn, and ask Christ to give rest to his servant, in the land of the living, where there is no pain and no sighing.

And John sang it for the monk, in his cell, quietly.

But the elder heard him.

The Life says the elder was furious. "Is this how you obey?" he said. "I told you no writing. And here you are, singing your own songs, like a poet at a banquet." And he told John he could not stay with him any more. He was to leave.

And John was very sad, because he had come there to learn obedience, and now he had failed at it. And the other monks went to the elder and pleaded for him. "Give him a penance," they said. "Any penance. Just don't send him away."

So the elder thought, and then he named one. And it was the worst job in the monastery. It was to go round all the cells and clean out the places where, well, how shall I put this. In a monastery without any plumbing at all, in the desert, there are certain places that need cleaning, and nobody ever volunteers. And John was to clean them. With his own hands. The hands that had kept the caliph's accounts. The hand, if you believe the earlier story, that the Mother of God had given back to him.

And John, the Life says, went and got a bucket, and did it. Without a word.

Elias, I expect, found him at it, and stood in the doorway holding his nose.

"You could have said no," Elias says.

"I could," says John, scrubbing.

"You used to be the most important man in Damascus."

"Not the most important," says John. "There was the caliph. And several of his cousins."

"You know what I mean. Why are you doing this?"

John sits back on his heels for a moment.

"Because I did the thing he told me not to do," he says. "And he was right to be angry. I didn't do it for a bad reason. But I did it, and I'd promised not to. And Elias, I'm not going to learn to obey anyone by only doing it on the days when it's easy." He looks at the bucket. "Also, someone has to do this, and it may as well be the person with the least excuse."

Elias goes away and thinks about that for quite a long time. Then he comes back with a second bucket.

And here is the end of the Life's story. That night, it says, the elder had a dream. And in the dream, the Mother of God came to him and said, more or less, "Why have you blocked up the spring? Let the water flow. Let him write. His songs will be sung all over the world." And the elder woke, and went straight to John, and said he was sorry, and told him to write.

"Write," he said. "Write as much as God gives you."

And John did.

* * *

Now I can tell you what he wrote, because he finally has somewhere quiet to write it. Whatever the truth of the old stories, this part is history. In his cell at Mar Saba, John wrote and wrote and wrote.

Let us start with the icons, since that is what he is most famous for.

Elias, let us say, is the one who brings it up. He has been helping John by sharpening pens, which he is now very good at, and he has seen the word icon on the pages over and over.

"I don't understand what the fuss is about," he says. "The emperor's people say a picture of Christ is an idol. Are they wrong?"

"Yes," says John. "But let's do it properly. What's the commandment they keep quoting?"

"You shall not make for yourself a graven image."

"Good. And why did God tell Moses that? Why, back then, were the people not to make a picture of God?"

Elias thinks. "Because God hasn't got a face?"

"Exactly. In the time of Moses, nobody had ever seen God. He has no body. He is not a shape. You cannot paint what has never been seen, and if you tried, you would be making it up. You'd be making a god out of your own head. That is an idol." John taps the page. "But then what happened?"

Elias knows this one. Every child at Mar Saba knows this one. "God became man."

"God became man. The Son of God was born of Mary, in Bethlehem, a few miles from your own village. He had a face. He had hands. People saw him. They saw him eat and sleep and walk about on the roads. John the Apostle says it right at the start of his letter. What we have seen with our eyes, what we have looked at and touched with our hands. And Elias, what people have seen, you can paint. When I paint Christ, I'm not making up a picture of the invisible God. I am painting the one who chose to be seen."

Elias turns this over. "So the picture is allowed because of Christmas."

John laughs out loud. "That is a better summary than I've managed in three whole treatises. Yes. Because of Christmas."

"But what about bowing to it? Kissing it? Isn't that worshipping a piece of wood?"

"Ah," says John. "Now that is the question. Tell me. When you go home, and your mother hugs you, are you hugging cloth?"

"What?"

"Her dress. It's cloth. When you hug her, your arms are round the cloth."

"I'm hugging my mother," says Elias, slightly offended.

"Of course you are. The cloth is just how you reach her. And when someone kisses an icon, they are not kissing paint. They are greeting the one in the picture. Saint Basil the Great said it hundreds of years ago, and I've quoted him in here because he put it better than me. The honour you give to the image goes over to the one it shows."

And then, the story goes, John says something that is in his treatises, and I will give it to you in my own plain words, not the exact old ones. He says, I do not worship matter. I worship the one who made matter. The one who became matter for my sake, and chose to live in matter, and saved me through matter. And I will not stop honouring the matter through which my salvation came.

Which is a very good thing to remember the next time you hold a cross, or light a candle, or dip your finger in holy water. God made the stuff of the world, and called it good, and then came into it himself. And so the world's stuff can be holy.

And John was careful about one thing, and it matters. He drew a clear line between two words. One word means worship, the kind that belongs to God alone. Nobody gives that to a picture, ever, and nobody should. The other word means honour, or veneration, the sort of respect you show to something precious or someone holy. That is what you give to an icon of a saint, or to the saint. Keeping those two apart was exactly what the image-breakers said the icon-lovers had failed to do. And John showed that the Church had kept them apart all along.

* * *

But icons were not the only thing. Over the years in his cell, John wrote a great book that he called the Fount of Knowledge. The last part of it is called the Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith. And it does exactly what it says. It sets out, clearly, in order, the whole teaching of the Church as the Fathers before him had taught it. God, the Trinity, creation, angels, the world, man, the Incarnation, the sacraments, the resurrection of the dead. Not his own new ideas. He was very firm about that. He said, more or less, I am going to say nothing of my own. I am going to gather up what the great teachers said and put it where you can find it.

It was so useful that people went on reading it for a thousand years. It was translated into Slavonic and into Arabic. About four hundred years after John died, it was put into Latin in Italy, and in the West, great teachers like Saint Thomas Aquinas read it and quoted it. And so John, who had lived his whole life in Syria and Palestine, under the caliphs, became a teacher of the whole Church, East and West.

And then there are the hymns. Remember the funeral hymn? That was only the beginning. John wrote canons, the long hymns in nine odes that we learned about with Andrew of Crete last time. And old tradition says he did a great deal to arrange the Church's music into its eight tones, which are something like eight families of melody, one for each week, going round and round through the year. Historians think the eight tones are older than John. But he certainly wrote for them, and wrote a very great deal, and his songs are sung in them still.

And the most famous of all his hymns is the one for Easter.

* * *

Let me give you an Easter night at Mar Saba. This is our own picture, though it is the sort of night John would have known well.

It is very late. Past midnight. The whole monastery has been in the dark church for hours, in the quiet of Holy Saturday, which is the quietest day of the year. Elias is standing near the back. He is older now. He is not fifteen any more. He has a beard of his own, a rather thin one, which he is proud of.

And then, at midnight, a light is brought out from the altar. One candle. And the light is passed from candle to candle, from monk to monk, until the whole church is full of small flames, and everyone's face is lit up gold from underneath. And the doors are opened, and the brothers go out into the cold night air, out on to the cliff above the gorge, with the stars over them, and the candles shaking a little in the wind.

And the old man with the grey beard, John, who once sold baskets for a ridiculous price, lifts up his head and begins to sing the hymn he wrote.

I will give it to you in my own plain words. It goes something like this.

It is the day of Resurrection. Let us be radiant, O people. This is the Passover of the Lord, the crossing over. From death to life, from earth to heaven, Christ our God has brought us over, and we sing the song of victory.

And then all the brothers sing it back, and the sound goes off down the gorge in the dark, and comes back to them off the far cliff, so it sounds as if the rocks are singing too.

And here is the wonderful thing. That hymn is still sung, every Easter, at midnight, in Orthodox churches, and in the Eastern Catholic churches, all over the world. In Greek, and Arabic, and Slavonic, and Romanian, and Georgian, and English, and many other languages. In the eighteen hundreds an English priest called John Mason Neale turned it into an English hymn that begins, The day of resurrection, earth, tell it out abroad. So on Easter night every year, millions of people sing John's words, very likely without knowing whose words they are.

Elias, standing on the cliff with his candle, wipes his eyes on his sleeve and hopes nobody noticed.

Afterwards, when the singing has died down, and the brothers are breaking their long fast with eggs and bread and, if it has been a good year, a little cheese, he finds John sitting on the wall, where they sat all those years ago.

"You know," Elias says, "I'm glad your elder had that dream."

John smiles. "So am I," he says. "But I'm also glad of the baskets. And the bucket. I don't think I could have written that song when I was Mansur. Mansur would have written a song about how clever he was. I needed to get small enough first, so there was room in the song for the Resurrection."

[pause]

Saint John lived a long time at Mar Saba. The old Life says the patriarch of Jerusalem made him a priest, and that he went on writing and praying and singing until he was very old. He died there, at the monastery in the cliffs, some time around the middle of the seven hundreds. Nobody is quite sure of the year. A tomb is still shown there as his.

And now I have to tell you about his name, because I promised.

A few years after John died, in the year seven hundred and fifty-four, the emperor in Constantinople, Leo's son, called a great council of bishops at a palace called Hieria, to declare that icons were forbidden. And at that council, they read out the names of those they condemned. And when they came to John, they did not call him John. They called him Mansur, his family's Arabic name, and they said it with a sneer, as if to say, what can you expect from a man who works for the caliph?

But thirty-three years after that, in the year seven hundred and eighty-seven, there was another council. This one was in Nicaea, in the same city as the very first great council of the Church. It is called the Seventh Ecumenical Council, and both the Catholic and the Orthodox Church honour it. And that council said that holy icons should be honoured in the churches, and on the walls, and in homes, and along the roads. And in the meeting, the bishops called out praise for those who had defended them. And among those names was John. They wished him memory eternal.

And a chronicler called Theophanes, writing a little later, gave John a nickname. He called him Chrysorrhoas. Do you remember that word? The golden stream. The old name of the river of Damascus. People said the name suited him, because of the gold of grace that flowed in his words and in his life. So the boy from beside the golden river became the golden river himself. I like that very much.

Many centuries later, in the year eighteen hundred and ninety, the Catholic Church gave him another title. It named him a Doctor of the Church, which is a title given only to a few saints whose teaching has served the whole Church in a special way.

His feast is on the fourth of December, in both the Orthodox and the Catholic Church.

And the monastery at Mar Saba is still there. Still on its cliff. Still with monks in it, praying, and singing his hymns, and very probably still making baskets.

[pause]

And I think there is something you can take from him to bed tonight.

John had a great gift, and he was told to put it down for a while. To be small. To be laughed at in the market. To do the job nobody wanted. And it was not wasted time. It was the time that made the rest of it good.

So if today you were not as important as you would have liked. If somebody laughed. If you got the bucket instead of the pen. You can let that go now. It might be doing more for you than you know.

And when you see an icon, or a cross, or a candle, or anything holy that is made of ordinary stuff, you can remember why it is allowed. Because God made the stuff of the world. And because, one night in Bethlehem, he came into it himself, and had a face.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

What we know about John comes from a few places. His own works, above all: the three treatises on the divine images, which you can read in English in Andrew Louth's translation, and the Fount of Knowledge with its Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith. The chronicle of Theophanes, written in the early eight hundreds. The acts of the Seventh Council at Nicaea. Arabic historians who mention his father. And a Life of him, in Greek and in Arabic, written two or three hundred years after he died. Andrew Louth's book on him is the best modern guide.

What is history. That he was born in Damascus, late in the six hundreds, into a Christian family called Mansur that served the caliphs' finances. That he was an official himself, then a monk and priest at Mar Saba. That he wrote the treatises on the images against the emperor Leo's iconoclasm, and the Fount of Knowledge, and many hymns, and that the Easter canon is held to be his. That the council of seven hundred and fifty-four condemned him by his family name, and the council of seven hundred and eighty-seven honoured him. That he died around the middle of the seven hundreds.

What is the Life's report. The captive teacher from Italy. The forged letter, the hand and the icon. The baskets. The funeral hymn, the penance and the elder's dream. We have told these as the Life tells them, and we cannot check them. That the icon on Athos is John's is that monastery's tradition.

What we do not know. Exactly when he left Damascus, and why. Exactly when he wrote each work.

What we invented. Elias, the walk to Damascus, the Easter night, and almost all the words anybody says. John's own words are given in our own plain paraphrase.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint John of Damascus for tonight. His feast is on the fourth of December, in the Orthodox Church and in the Catholic Church, and his Easter hymn is sung every year at midnight. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. It is the day of Resurrection; let us be radiant, O people. Holy John of Damascus, pray to God for us. Goodnight, and God bless.

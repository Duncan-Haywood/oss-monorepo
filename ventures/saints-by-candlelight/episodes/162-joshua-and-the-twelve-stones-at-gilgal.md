It is very early in the morning, and a man is standing in the middle of a river.

Or rather, he is standing where the middle of a river ought to be. Under his sandals there are stones, round grey river stones, wet and cold and slippery with weed, and between the stones there are little pools left behind, with tiny fish in them looking extremely surprised. To his left, upstream, there is no water. To his right, downstream, there is no water either, only a long muddy bed winding away towards the south, steaming faintly in the first sun.

He bends down. He chooses a stone. Not the biggest one. A good one. About the size of a loaf of bread, smooth, with a white line running round it like a belt. He lifts it onto his shoulder. It is heavier than it looks. Stones always are.

Behind him, a few paces off, a group of priests are standing perfectly still, with a golden chest on poles resting on their shoulders. They have been standing there a long time. One of them catches his eye and gives him a very small nod, the kind of nod that means, please hurry up, this is heavy.

Some time ago on this show, we climbed a mountain with Moses. Mount Nebo, on the east side of the river Jordan, high above the plains of Moab. And from the top, the Lord showed him the whole land. Gilead in the north. The hills of the west, as far as the sea. The south country. And right below, across the river, Jericho, the city of palm trees.

And the Lord said to him, this is the land. I have let you see it with your eyes. But you shall not go over there.

And Moses died there, on the mountain, and the Lord buried him, and nobody knows to this day where his grave is.

He was a hundred and twenty years old, and the book says his eye was not dim and his strength had not gone. And the people wept for him on the plains of Moab for thirty days.

And then, as always happens after a funeral, there was the question of what came next.

What came next was Joshua.

Let me tell you a little about Joshua, because he has been in the background of this show for a long time and tonight he is in the front.

His name, to begin with, was not Joshua. It was Hoshea, which means something like "salvation" or "he saves". Moses changed it, early on, to Yehoshua, which means "the Lord saves". It is the same name, in Hebrew, as Jesus. When the old Greek translators came to write it down, they wrote it Iesous, exactly the way they would later write the name of Our Lord. And early Christians noticed that, as you can imagine, and we will come back to it at the end.

Joshua had been with Moses since he was young. When Moses went up the mountain of God, Joshua went partway up with him and waited. When Moses went into the tent of meeting to speak with the Lord face to face, the way a man speaks with his friend, Joshua stayed in the tent, the book says, and did not leave it. He led the fighting at Rephidim, the day Aaron and Hur held up Moses' hands. He was one of the twelve spies who went into the land and came back with a bunch of grapes so big it took two men on a pole to carry it, and he and Caleb were the only two who said, we can do this, the Lord is with us.

So he was not new. He was not young any more either. By now he was an old soldier with grey in his beard, who had spent forty years in the wilderness watching another man lead.

And then, one day, the other man was gone, and it was his turn.

I think that is one of the hardest things there is. Not starting something. Carrying on something that someone greater than you started.

The Lord seems to have known that. Because the first thing the Book of Joshua tells us is what the Lord said to him, and it is said over and over again, like a parent saying it to a child on the first day of school. Be strong. Be of good courage. Do not be frightened. Do not be dismayed. As I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not fail you or forsake you. Be strong and of good courage, for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go.

When someone says "be strong and of good courage" three times in one speech, you can be fairly sure they have noticed you are nervous.

* * *

Now, the place.

The river Jordan runs from north to south, from the snows of Mount Hermon down through the Sea of Galilee and on, winding back and forth like a ribbon dropped on the floor, all the way down to the Dead Sea, which the book calls the Salt Sea, the lowest place on the face of the earth. Near the bottom, before it reaches the Salt Sea, it runs through a wide flat valley, very hot, with thickets of tamarisk and willow along the banks.

On the east side of the river, the people of Israel were camped at a place called Shittim, which means the acacia trees. On the west side, a few hours' walk away across the valley floor, was Jericho, with its walls and its palm trees and its spring of fresh water.

Most of the year, the Jordan down there is not a very impressive river. People who come to see it today are often a little disappointed. You could throw a stone across it in a lot of places. In summer, there are spots where you could almost wade.

But this was not summer.

The book tells us, quite casually, as if it hardly matters, that the Jordan overflows all its banks throughout the time of harvest. The harvest here is the barley harvest, in the spring, which is also the time when the snow on the mountains far to the north is melting. So all that melted snow was coming down the river at once. The Jordan was brown and fast and full, over the banks and into the thickets, pushing branches and mud along with it.

And on the other side of it was the land the Lord had promised.

You can imagine standing on the bank and looking at that.

The spies had already been across. That's another story, and we told it on this show, the story of Rahab and the scarlet cord. They had swum across, presumably, or found a ford, two young men travelling light. But two young men travelling light is one thing. A whole nation is another. Grandmothers. Babies. Sheep. The tent of meeting, in pieces, on carts. All the pots and pans. Nobody was going to swim that.

So Joshua did what good leaders do when they do not know how something is going to work. He got up early.

* * *

The book says Joshua rose early in the morning and set out from Shittim with all the people, and they came to the Jordan, and they camped there, on the bank, before they crossed over. And they stayed there three days.

Here is where our friend from the cold open comes in.

He was a man of the tribe of Asher. The book does not give his name, so I will not make one up. He was not a priest or a captain or anybody important. He was the kind of man who is good with his hands and does not say much, who had been born in the wilderness and had never in his life slept in a house. He had lived in a tent since the day he was born. He had eaten manna every morning since the day he was weaned. He had never seen a field of barley, except from a distance, across a river.

And on the first evening by the Jordan, he went down to the bank with some of the others, and they looked at the water.

"Well," said one of them. "There it is."

"There it is," said our man from Asher.

They watched a whole tree go past. Not a branch. A tree. Roots and all, turning slowly in the current.

"I suppose," said somebody, "there'll be a bridge."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to build a bridge out of, and nobody among them who had ever built one, and the river would have taken it away in an afternoon anyway.

"Boats, then."

"For all of us? And the flocks?"

They went back up to the camp and had their supper, which was manna, as always, and went to bed. The river made a noise all night like a crowd of people talking in the next room.

On the third day, the officers went through the camp. You can picture them, going from tent to tent, from family to family, saying the same thing over and over until their voices went hoarse.

This is what they said, more or less in the book's own words.

When you see the ark of the covenant of the Lord your God, being carried by the priests, then set out from your place and follow it. But keep a space between you and it, about two thousand cubits. Do not come near it. So that you may know the way you shall go. For you have not passed this way before.

Two thousand cubits is a bit more than half a mile. Nine hundred metres, roughly. A long gap. Long enough that a whole crowd of people, standing at the back, could all see the ark out in front of them, going on ahead, the one bright thing in the valley.

And I love that last line. For you have not passed this way before.

It is a very honest thing to say to people. It doesn't pretend. It doesn't say, this will be easy, or, you know what you're doing. It says, nobody here has done this. So watch the ark. Go where it goes.

Then Joshua said to the whole people, Sanctify yourselves, for tomorrow the Lord will do wonders among you.

Sanctify yourselves means, more or less, get ready the way you get ready for something holy. Wash. Put on clean clothes if you have them. Settle your quarrels. Say your prayers. The kind of getting ready you would do the night before a great feast, not the night before a battle.

Our man from Asher washed his face in a bucket of river water, which was so cold it made his teeth ache, and lay awake a long time listening to the river.

* * *

Somewhere else in the camp, that same night, I imagine, Joshua was not asleep either.

The book does not tell us this. I am guessing. But I think it is a fair guess. Think about it. Tomorrow morning, he was going to tell a group of priests to pick up the holiest thing in the whole camp and walk into a river in flood. And he was going to tell all the people to follow them. And the only reason he had for thinking this would work was that the Lord had told him so.

That is actually a very good reason. It is the best reason there is. But it is still the kind of reason you lie awake thinking about.

The Lord had said to him, Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel, so that they may know that as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. And command the priests who carry the ark, when you come to the edge of the water of the Jordan, stand still in the Jordan.

Stand still in the Jordan.

Not, wait on the bank until the water goes away. Not, put one toe in and see. Walk in. And stand.

I imagine Joshua remembering. He had been there, forty years before, a young man, at the edge of the other water, the sea, with Pharaoh's chariots coming up behind and the whole people screaming. He had seen Moses stretch out his hand. He had seen the wind blow all night, and the water stand up like a wall on the right hand and on the left, and the people go through on dry ground.

He had been young then, and Moses had been the one stretching out his hand.

Now it was his turn, and there was not even a wind.

* * *

In the morning, the camp packed up. Everything. Tents down, ropes coiled, the pots tied on the donkeys, the children counted, and counted again. The flocks bleating. The whole long noisy business of a people on the move.

And then everybody stood still and looked towards the river.

The priests came out with the ark.

If you have never heard of the ark of the covenant before, except perhaps in a film, here is what the book of Exodus says about it. It was a chest made of acacia wood, about the size of a large blanket box, covered all over with gold. It had gold rings at its four feet, and long poles of acacia wood, covered with gold, slid through the rings, so that it could be carried on the shoulders of the priests without anybody touching the ark itself. On top was a lid of solid gold called the mercy seat, with two golden cherubim on it, one at each end, facing each other, with their wings spread out over it. Inside it were the two stone tablets of the commandments.

It was not a magic box. That is important. The ark had no power of its own. It was the sign that the Lord was with his people, and the place where he had promised to meet with them. The book calls it the ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth. Which is a very big name for a box, and that is the point. The box was small. The one it pointed to was not.

So the priests went first, carrying it. Barefoot, probably, or in sandals; the book doesn't say. Down the slope, through the thickets, to the edge of the brown water.

And the people waited, half a mile back, and watched.

I like to think of the priests at that moment. The front two especially. The ones whose feet would get wet first.

They came to the edge. The water was rushing past, right there, full of mud and sticks. You could hear it. You could feel the cold coming off it.

And they stepped in.

And the book says this, and I will tell it to you as plainly as it tells it.

As soon as the feet of the priests who bore the ark were dipped in the brink of the water, the waters coming down from above stood still, and rose up in a heap, far off, at a city called Adam, which is beside a place called Zarethan. And the waters flowing down towards the Salt Sea were completely cut off. And the people passed over opposite Jericho.

[pause]

Far off. That's the bit that always catches me. The water did not stop right next to the priests, like a glass wall you could have reached out and touched. It stopped a long way upstream, at a town called Adam, out of sight round the bends of the river. So what the people actually saw, standing on the bank, was the river going down.

The water in front of them simply ran away. Off downstream, towards the Salt Sea, the last of it going round the bend and gone. And no more came behind it.

Little by little, the riverbed appeared. Mud. Stones. Weed lying flat. Puddles. A heron, perhaps, standing in one of them, looking around with great interest at what was suddenly on offer.

And the priests walked on, into the middle of it, and stopped there, and stood. On dry ground, the book says, firm ground, in the middle of the Jordan.

* * *

Then the people went over.

Not all at once. You cannot move a whole nation all at once. Family by family, tribe by tribe, keeping their distance from the ark, which stood there in the middle of the riverbed with the priests holding it, like a lamp left on in a dark house.

Down the bank. Across the stones. Past the ark, at a respectful distance, all eyes on it. Up the other bank, through the thickets on the far side. And out onto the plain of Jericho.

The book mentions that the men of the tribes of Reuben and Gad and half of the tribe of Manasseh went across first, armed and ready, about forty thousand of them. These were the tribes who had already been given their land on the east side of the river, back where the people had been camped. They could have stayed at home. They had promised Moses that they wouldn't; that they would cross over with their brothers and help them until everyone had a home, and only then go back. And they kept their promise. Which I think deserves a mention. Being the first to step down onto a riverbed when you do not even live on the other side is a good thing to do.

The book also says, a little further on, that the people hurried over. Which I completely understand. If I were crossing a riverbed where a flood had been an hour ago, and could be again at any moment, I would not stop to look at the fish.

And the priests stood. All that time. All day, maybe; the book doesn't say how long. Holding up the ark on their shoulders in the middle of the river, until every last person was across. The last grandmother. The last stubborn goat. The last little boy who dropped something in a puddle and had to go back for it.

That is a long time to hold something heavy. If you were listening to the last episode, you might remember three old men on a hill doing something rather similar.

* * *

Now, before all this started, the Lord had told Joshua to choose twelve men. One from each of the twelve tribes. And our man from Asher was one of them.

I don't know how he was chosen. Perhaps the elders of Asher picked him because he was strong. Perhaps because he was reliable. Perhaps because he happened to be standing nearest when somebody said, we need a man. That is how most of us get chosen for most things.

And when all the people had finished crossing over, Joshua called the twelve men together and said this.

Go over, back into the middle of the Jordan, in front of the ark of the Lord your God. And each of you take up a stone on his shoulder, one for each of the tribes of Israel. So that this may be a sign among you.

And then he said the thing that I think is the whole heart of tonight's story.

When your children ask in time to come, What do these stones mean to you? then you shall tell them that the waters of the Jordan were cut off in front of the ark of the covenant of the Lord. When it passed over the Jordan, the waters were cut off. So these stones shall be to the people of Israel a memorial for ever.

So that is what our man from Asher was doing in the cold open. Walking back out into the riverbed, to where the priests' feet had stood. Looking for a stone.

I imagine the twelve of them standing there, looking down, a little embarrassed, because there were thousands of stones, and how do you choose one stone to stand for a whole tribe for ever?

"Any stone?" somebody said.

"A good one," said our man.

"What makes a stone a good one?"

He thought about that. "One the children will want to touch," he said.

So he picked the one with the white line round it, like a belt. And he put it on his shoulder, and nodded to the priests, who very much wanted him to hurry up, and he carried it across, and up the far bank, and on, a good long way across the plain, to the place where the people were going to camp that night.

And the book says one more thing about the stones, and it's easy to miss. Joshua also set up twelve stones in the middle of the Jordan, in the very place where the feet of the priests who carried the ark had stood. So there were two piles. One on dry land, where everybody could see it and touch it. And one in the riverbed, where, once the water came back, nobody could see it at all.

And they are there to this day, the book says.

I find that second pile very moving. A memorial nobody could see. Only God and the river knew where it was.

* * *

When the twelve men had brought their stones out, and every single person was across, Joshua gave the last command of the day.

He said to the priests, Come up out of the Jordan.

And the priests came up. Up out of the riverbed, up the bank, with the ark on their shoulders. And the book says that as soon as the soles of the priests' feet were lifted up onto dry ground, the waters of the Jordan came back into their place, and overflowed all its banks, as they had done before.

Just like that. Brown and fast and full of branches. As if nothing had happened.

And on the far bank, the bank they had left that morning, there was nothing at all. No people. No tents. No smoke from cooking fires. Just the acacia trees, and the empty plains of Moab, and somewhere up behind them, out of sight, the mountain where Moses was buried.

The book gives us the date. The tenth day of the first month. That's worth remembering, because of what comes next. And the people camped at a place called Gilgal, on the eastern edge of the land around Jericho.

And Joshua set up the twelve stones there.

I imagine our man from Asher set his stone down very carefully, with the white belt facing outwards, where people would see it.

The book says that on that day the Lord exalted Joshua in the sight of all Israel, and they stood in awe of him, as they had stood in awe of Moses, all the days of his life.

Notice who did the exalting. Not Joshua. He did not stand on a rock and make a speech about himself. He had walked behind a box all day, and told a few priests where to stand, and waited. The Lord did the rest.

* * *

Gilgal was where they stayed for a while. And several quiet but very important things happened there, which the book tells us one after another, and I want to tell you about them, because they are the kind of thing that does not get into the films.

The first thing was news.

The book says that when all the kings of the Amorites on the west side of the Jordan, and all the kings of the Canaanites by the sea, heard that the Lord had dried up the waters of the Jordan for the people of Israel until they had crossed over, their hearts melted, and there was no spirit in them any longer.

News travels fast in a valley. Somebody fishing downstream, I suppose, wondering where all his river had gone.

The second thing was a renewal of the covenant. The book tells us that a sign of the covenant which God had given to Abraham long ago, which every boy of Israel was meant to receive, had not been given to the children born on the long journey through the wilderness. So at Gilgal, it was given to all of them, the whole generation of our man from Asher, and the camp rested there until they were well. And the Lord said to Joshua, Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you. And the place has been called Gilgal ever since.

Gilgal sounds a little like the Hebrew word for rolling. Rolled away. A stone rolled away, if you like. Which is a phrase Christians may find themselves remembering at another, later tomb, in another spring.

The third thing was the Passover.

Do you remember the date? They crossed on the tenth of the first month. And on the fourteenth day of that month, in the evening, on the plains of Jericho, they kept the Passover. The same feast, on the same night of the year, that their parents had kept in Egypt, with their sandals on and their staffs in their hands, the night before they left.

Forty years before, almost to the night, the people had eaten the Passover in a hurry, packed and ready to run.

Now they ate it sitting down. On the other side of the river. In the land itself.

I imagine our man from Asher looking around him that night, at the fires and the families and the dark shape of the twelve stones against the stars, and thinking, this is the first Passover I have ever kept somewhere I am meant to stay.

* * *

And then the fourth thing, which is the quietest of all, and my favourite.

The next morning, the morning after the Passover, the people ate food from the land. Unleavened cakes, the book says, and parched grain. Parched grain is ears of barley roasted over a fire until they crack, which you rub between your hands and eat. It's a harvest-time food. The barley harvest, remember, was going on all around them. The fields on the plain of Jericho were full of it.

And the morning after that, the manna stopped.

The book says it very simply. The manna ceased on the next day, when they ate of the produce of the land. And there was no more manna for the people of Israel. They ate of the fruit of the land of Canaan that year.

[pause]

Think about that. For forty years, every single morning, there had been manna on the ground. Bread from heaven. Our man from Asher had never in his life known a morning without it. His whole idea of breakfast was a fine white flaky stuff like frost that you gathered before the sun got hot.

And one morning he went out of his tent with his basket, the way he always did. And the ground was just ground.

I imagine him standing there for a long time.

Not frightened, exactly. There was barley in the fields. They would not go hungry. But it was the end of something. The Lord had fed them by hand, the way you feed a very small child, for as long as they needed it. And now they were in the land, and there was barley, and the Lord was saying, gently, you can feed yourselves now. I will still be here. But you are grown up.

It is not a sad ending. It is the end of the wilderness. But I think it would have been a strange, quiet morning all the same.

And somewhere, I like to think, a very old woman who had been a girl in Egypt sat down on a stone in front of her tent and cried a little, and nobody could quite tell whether it was for joy.

* * *

And then the last thing. The strangest thing. Joshua's thing.

One day, while the camp was still at Gilgal, Joshua was out near Jericho. On his own, it seems. Perhaps looking at the walls. Perhaps thinking, the way generals do, about how in the world anybody was supposed to get into a city like that.

And he lifted up his eyes and looked, and there in front of him was a man standing, with a drawn sword in his hand.

Now, Joshua was an old soldier. He did what old soldiers do. He did not run, and he did not draw his own sword. He walked straight up to the man and asked him the obvious question.

Are you for us, or for our enemies?

And the man said, No.

[pause]

I have always loved that answer. It is such a strange answer. Joshua has asked an either-or question, us or them, and the answer is no. Neither. That is the wrong question.

The man went on. But I have come now, as commander of the army of the Lord.

And Joshua fell on his face to the earth, and worshipped, and said to him, What does my lord say to his servant?

And the commander of the Lord's army said to Joshua, Take off your sandals from your feet, for the place where you are standing is holy.

And Joshua did so.

[pause]

That is where the chapter ends. Just like that. And Joshua did so.

If those words sound familiar, they should. Forty years before, on the far side of the wilderness, at a bush that was burning and not burning up, the Lord had said exactly the same thing to Moses. Take off your sandals, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground.

Joshua had been told it was his turn. Be strong and of good courage. As I was with Moses, so I will be with you. He had crossed the river. He had seen the water stand up. And still, I suspect, some part of him was waiting to be sure. And here was his burning bush. His holy ground. His bare feet in the dust outside Jericho.

Who was the commander? The book does not say. It never tells us his name.

People have wondered about him for a very long time. Saint Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, thought this was the Son of God himself, appearing before he was born of Mary, the same one who would one day carry Joshua's name. Many others, in both the Eastern and Western churches, have seen in him the Archangel Michael, who in the Book of Daniel is called the great prince who stands up for God's people. The book leaves the question open, and so shall we. Either way, it was someone who could tell Joshua to take his sandals off and be right.

And I notice what he did not do. He did not give Joshua a battle plan. He did not tell him the enemy's weaknesses. He told him to take off his shoes.

Before you fight, before you plan, before you even think about the walls, notice where you are standing. You are standing in a holy place. Somebody else is in command here.

The walls of Jericho are a story for another night. Tonight we will leave Joshua there, barefoot, outside the city, with the commander of the Lord's army in front of him, and the whole camp behind him at Gilgal, eating barley from the fields.

[pause]

The wind-down is the slow part. If you are already asleep, well done; you have not missed anything important.

Joshua lived a long time after that. The book says he led the people for many years, and divided the land between the tribes, and at the very end of his life he called everyone together at Shechem, under a great oak tree, and set up one more stone, and said to them, Choose this day whom you will serve. As for me and my house, we will serve the Lord. And then he died, at a hundred and ten years old, and they buried him in his own piece of land in the hill country of Ephraim, at a place called Timnath-serah. He is remembered as a righteous man and a prophet. In the Orthodox Church his feast is on the first of September.

Gilgal did not stay quiet. It became one of the great meeting places of Israel. Long after, the prophet Samuel went round it every year on his circuit as a judge, and it was at Gilgal that Saul was made king. Nobody today is quite sure where it was. Somewhere on the plain between Jericho and the river. The stones, if they are still there, are lying in a field somewhere, under the dust, with the sun going across them.

And the river kept on doing what rivers do. Flooding at harvest time. Shrinking in summer. Running down into the Salt Sea.

But it had a way of being crossed by holy people.

Centuries later, the prophet Elijah came down to that same stretch of the Jordan, near Jericho, with his disciple Elisha. And Elijah took his cloak, and rolled it up, and struck the water with it, and the water parted, this way and that, and the two of them went over on dry ground. And on the far side, Elijah was taken up into heaven in a whirlwind. And Elisha picked up the cloak that had fallen, and walked back to the river alone, and struck the water, and said, Where is the Lord, the God of Elijah? And the water parted again, and he went over.

And centuries after that, at the Jordan again, a man in a camel-hair coat was baptizing people in the river. And one day a carpenter came down from Nazareth and stepped into the water. And as he came up out of it, the heavens were torn open, and the Spirit came down on him like a dove, and a voice said, You are my beloved Son.

His name, in the language he spoke, was Yeshua. The same name as Joshua.

The early Christians noticed all of that. The Letter to the Hebrews plays with the two names. Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria, preaching on the Book of Joshua, told his listeners that they too had crossed a Jordan, in the water of baptism, and come into a new country, and that they too had a leader called Jesus going in front of them. And the psalm that sings about all this, Psalm one hundred and fourteen, has the river turning round to run away, like a startled animal. The sea looked and fled. The Jordan turned back. What ails you, O sea, that you flee? O Jordan, that you turn back?

The psalm's answer is simple. At the presence of the Lord. At the presence of the God of Jacob.

And in my imagination, many years later, there is an old man from the tribe of Asher, very old now, with a white beard, sitting on the ground at Gilgal with his back against a pile of twelve stones. It is a warm evening. A little boy, one of his grandsons, or great-grandsons, has been told to fetch him for supper, and has not fetched him, because he has found the stone with the white line round it, like a belt, and is running his finger round and round the line.

And the boy asks, What do these stones mean?

And the old man tells him.

He tells him about the brown water and the tree going past roots and all. About the priests standing in the middle of the river, holding up the ark, and the long, long day. About the heron, maybe. About the cold of the stones in the riverbed, and how heavy the stone was on his shoulder, and how the water came back the moment the last priest stepped out, as if it had been waiting politely.

About the morning there was no manna.

About the river that turned back, at the presence of the Lord.

And the boy listens, with his finger on the white line. And the sun goes down behind the hills to the west. And somebody calls them both for supper, crossly, for the second time. And the old man gets up, slowly, one hand on the stones, and the two of them go home.

That is what the stones were for. Not for the people who crossed. They knew. For the ones who would ask.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Joshua, chapters three, four and five, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, with a little from chapter one, where the Lord tells Joshua to be strong and of good courage. The order of events, the two thousand cubits, the water standing up at Adam beside Zarethan, the twelve stones in two places, the tenth day of the month, the Passover, the parched grain, the end of the manna and the commander with the drawn sword are all the book's own, told plainly.

The man from the tribe of Asher is our invention. There really were twelve stone-carriers, one from each tribe, but the book gives none of their names, and nothing they said or thought. His stone with the white line, his talk on the riverbank, his grandson, and Joshua lying awake on the eve of the crossing are invented, told in the spirit of the original.

The book does not say how big the crowd was, how long the crossing took, or exactly where Gilgal and Adam were, and so we have not guessed. Places have been suggested for both, but we won't vouch for them.

The readings of the crossing as a picture of baptism are how early Christian writers read the story, among them Origen in his homilies on Joshua. Saint Justin Martyr, in his Dialogue with Trypho, saw the Son of God in the commander of the Lord's army, and others have seen the Archangel Michael; the book does not name him, and we have not chosen.

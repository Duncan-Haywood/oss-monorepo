It is very early in the morning, and an old man is pulling back a curtain.

It is a heavy curtain, woven of blue and purple and scarlet, and behind it it is dark and still and smells of incense from the evening before. He goes in slowly. That is what a long grumble can do to your memory. You end up homesick for the brick kilns.

Korah and his company came with their censers to the door of the tent of meeting. And the glory of the Lord appeared. And Moses and Aaron fell on their faces again, and prayed for the people, that the Lord would not be angry with all of them for the sin of a few.

That is all I will say about it. The Scripture does not dwell on it, and neither will we. It was a very solemn thing, and the people who saw it ran away from it in fear.

But there is a small sentence, much later in the Book of Numbers, which I want you to keep in your pocket, because it is a kind one. It says, the sons of Korah did not die.

Now, you would think that would settle the question. You would think everyone would say, well, clearly Aaron is the one.

But the Lord knew his people. He knew that after a fright, and a sickness, and a great deal of shouting, people need something they can see. Something quiet. Something they can look at, and touch, and remember.

There is a lovely thing hidden here which you can only see in the Hebrew. The word for a staff, a walking stick, is matteh. And the word for a tribe is also matteh. The same word. Because a tribe was a family that walked behind its leader's staff.

So when each leader brought his staff, he was not just bringing a stick. In a way he was bringing his whole tribe. Laying it down, in the dark, before the Lord, with his name on it.

You might ask, as people have been asking for a very long time, does that make twelve staffs or thirteen? Among the leaders of the twelve tribes there was, of course, a leader of the tribe of Zebulun. He was an old man, older than he liked to admit, and he walked with a staff because he needed one, not because he was important. And he had a granddaughter, who was about eight, and who thought he was the most important person in the whole wilderness.

When the word came round the camp, the granddaughter was the first to hear it, because she was always the first to hear everything.

"They want your stick," she said.

"My what?"

"Your stick. Moses wants everybody's stick. All the leaders. And they have to write their names on them. And put them in the big tent overnight. And in the morning one of them is going to grow."

Her grandfather looked at his staff. It was a plain staff, of some hard pale wood from a tree he had never seen growing, because he had been born in Egypt and had lived since in the desert. He had cut it from a bundle a trader had brought, years ago, and smoothed it, and it was worn dark at the top where his hand had held it, every day, for as long as she could remember.

"Grow," he said.

"Grow," she said. "Like a tree."

"It's a stick," said her grandfather. "It's been a stick for twenty years. It is not going to start growing now."

"One of them is," she said. "Moses said."

Her grandfather sat down on the ground at the door of their tent, and held his staff across his knees, and was quiet for a while.

Then he said, "Well. I don't think it's going to be mine."

"Why not?"

"Because it's not meant to be mine. It's meant to be Aaron's." He was quiet again. "And I think, after yesterday, I am glad it is not meant to be mine."

The granddaughter thought about that. She had seen Aaron run into the crowd. She had seen the smoke.

"I wouldn't want to do that," she said.

"No," said her grandfather. "Neither would I. That's how you know it's a real job."

Still, the name had to be written on.

Her grandfather's eyes were not good any more, and his hands shook a little. So, in my imagination, the granddaughter did it. He gave her his little knife, and showed her how to hold the staff steady across her knees, and she carved his name into the wood, a little below the dark worn place where his hand went, one letter at a time, with her tongue sticking out of the side of her mouth.

It took her the whole afternoon. The letters were not very straight. One of them was much bigger than the others.

"It's crooked," she said.

"It's perfect," said her grandfather. "Anyone will know it's mine."

All over the camp, I imagine, the same thing was happening. Eleven old men, and the people who loved them, sitting at the doors of their tents and cutting names into sticks. Some of them, I suspect, were secretly hoping. Some of them were secretly dreading. Most of them, I would guess, were simply tired, and wanted the grumbling to stop, and hoped this would do it.

And somewhere in the middle of the camp, near the great tent, Aaron's staff had his name on it too. On the staff of Levi.

I wonder who carved that one. The book doesn't say. Perhaps Aaron did it himself. Perhaps one of his sons, Eleazar or Ithamar. Perhaps Moses. I like to think it was Moses, his little brother, who had once been so frightened of speaking to Pharaoh that the Lord had sent Aaron to speak for him. I like to think Moses sat and cut the name of his brother into the wood, carefully, the way you would want to do it for somebody you love.

The granddaughter went with her grandfather as far as she was allowed, which was not very far. The camp was very quiet that night. After all the shouting, all the fear, all the running, it was just quiet. The cloud over the tent had turned, as it did every evening, into a soft glow of fire. The fires outside the tents burned down. The donkeys stopped complaining. The babies went to sleep.

I find it a very comforting picture. All twelve of them laid down, in the same place, with the same darkness over them, in front of the same Lord. None of them could make itself grow. None of them could try harder than the others. They just lay there, the way you lie in bed at night, and whatever happened was going to be the Lord's doing, not theirs.

The granddaughter could not sleep, of course. She lay in the tent listening to her grandfather snoring and thinking about his stick, lying in the dark in the big tent with his name on it, all by itself.

"Grandfather," she whispered.

The snoring stopped. "Mm."

"Do you think it's lonely?"

"Do I think what is lonely?"

"Your stick."

There was a long pause. She heard him turn over.

"No," he said. "I don't think it's lonely. I think it's in the best place in the whole camp. I think it's nearer to God tonight than I have ever been."

"You could have just told them," Aaron said. "That it was the Lord's choice. Not mine. Not yours."

"I did tell them," said Moses. "I've been telling them for years."

"I know."

"I never asked for it," said Aaron. "Any of it. I was a slave in Egypt making bricks. And then one day my little brother came back from the desert and said, the Lord wants you to speak to Pharaoh. And now I have bells on my hem."

"I don't think I could, now," said Aaron. "I think they're sewn on."

[pause]

That, I think, is the true thing hidden inside our invention. Much later, the Letter to the Hebrews says something very simple about Aaron. It says, no one takes this honour on himself. He is called by God, just as Aaron was.

He did not take it. He was given it. That is a very different thing to carry.

* * *

And in the morning, Moses got up and went into the tent of the testimony.

And this is what he saw. And this time it is not our invention. This is what the book says, word for word, as near as I can give it to you in plain English.

And behold, the staff of Aaron for the house of Levi had sprouted. It had put out buds. It had produced blossoms. And it bore ripe almonds.

[pause]

Not just one of those things. All of them. All at once.

If you have ever watched an almond tree, or any fruit tree, through a year, you will know that this is not how it goes. First, in late winter, the buds. Then, a little later, the blossom. Then the petals fall, and slowly, over the spring and the summer, the little green fruit swells, until it is ripe at last at the end of the summer. Months and months.

And here was all of it, on one stick, in one night. Buds and flowers and fruit, side by side. The whole year at once. As if the Lord was saying, I am not hurried by your seasons. I can do in a night what takes a tree the whole summer. And I can do it with something that was dead.

That is the thing, really. Not that a stick grew. That a dead stick came to life. Dry wood, cut off from any root, years before. And now it was alive.

The almond, by the way, is the first tree to blossom in that part of the world. While everything else is still bare and grey at the end of winter, the almond trees suddenly go white and pink all over, like a hillside covered in snow that smells wonderful. It is the tree that wakes up first.

The prophet Jeremiah, many centuries later, has a lovely little vision about it. The Lord says to him, Jeremiah, what do you see? And Jeremiah says, I see a branch of almond. And the Lord says, you have seen well, for I am watching over my word to perform it.

That only really works in Hebrew, because the word for almond, shaqed, sounds almost exactly like the word for watching, or keeping awake, shoqed. The almond is the wakeful tree. The one that is awake before all the others.

So on the morning of tonight's story, while the whole camp slept, the wakeful tree was waking up in the dark, on a dead stick, in front of the Lord who keeps watch over his word.

I think that is lovely.

Moses brought all the staffs out, all twelve, from before the Lord, and carried them out to the people.

The book says that the people looked. And that each man took his own staff.

[pause]

I imagine the crowd gathered outside the great tent in the early morning, very quiet now. No more shouting. The leaders standing at the front. And Moses coming out, with an armful of sticks.

And there, in the middle of all those sticks, in plain sight, was one that was covered in blossom.

You couldn't miss it. You couldn't argue with it. You couldn't say, well, it depends how you look at it. It was white and pink and green in the sunrise, and you could smell it from where you stood.

Nobody said anything, I think. Not for a long time.

Then Moses began handing the staffs back. One by one. Calling out the names.

And our old man from Zebulun went forward when his name was called, and Moses put his staff back into his hand.

It was exactly as it had been the day before. Plain, pale, worn dark at the top. With his name cut into it in crooked letters, one of them much bigger than the others.

He turned it over in his hands. Then he turned, and he looked at the staff of Aaron, glowing in Moses' other hand, covered in flowers.

And then, the way I like to imagine it, he did a very simple thing. He leaned on his own plain staff, the way he always had. And he bowed his head, a little, towards the one in blossom.

And he walked back through the crowd to where his granddaughter was waiting, and put his free hand on her shoulder.

"Well?" she said.

"Well," he said. "It was not mine."

"Are you sad?"

He thought about it, honestly, the way she liked him to. "No," he said. "I'm glad. I've got my stick back. And now we know."

And she reached up, and ran her finger along the crooked letters of his name, the way children do.

"I'm glad it's back," she said.

"So am I," said her grandfather. "I was lost without it."

Then the Lord told Moses one more thing.

Put the staff of Aaron back, in front of the testimony, to be kept as a sign. So that the grumbling against me may come to an end, and they will not die.

And Moses did so. As the Lord had commanded him, so he did.

So the eleven plain staffs went home with their owners, and were leaned against tent poles and taken on walks and used to poke the fire and point at the sheep. Ordinary sticks for ordinary days.

And the one in blossom went back into the dark of the holy place, in front of the golden chest, and stayed there.

Not as a magic stick. I want to be clear about that. It did not do anything after that. It was not a wand. Aaron did not go around waving it at people. It was simply kept, as a sign. A reminder, for whenever anyone forgot, that the choosing was the Lord's.

You would think that would be the end. But the book adds one more scene, and I think it's the most human scene in the whole story.

The people looked at all this. The ground opening. The fire. The plague. And now a stick blossoming in the holy place. And they suddenly understood just how close they were living to something very great and very holy. And they were frightened.

And they said to Moses, and you can hear the panic in it, look, we are dying. We are lost. We are all lost. Anyone who comes anywhere near the tent of the Lord will die. Are we all going to die?

[pause]

And here, I think, is why the story is in the Bible at all.

Because the Lord did not answer that with more fire. He answered it with a job.

He spoke, and the book says he spoke this time to Aaron himself, which does not happen very often, and he said, you and your sons, and your father's house with you, will carry the weight of the holy place. You will keep watch over the tent and the altar, so that there will be no more wrath on the people of Israel.

In other words: this is what the priesthood is for. Not so that Aaron can be grand. So that the people need not be afraid. So that someone stands in between. Someone keeps watch, so that everyone else can sleep.

And the Lord said one more thing to Aaron, and I want to give it to you exactly, because it is so beautiful.

You shall have no inheritance in their land, and you shall have no portion among them. I am your portion and your inheritance.

All the other tribes, when they came at last into the land, would get fields and vineyards and towns. Aaron and his sons would get none. No land of their own at all.

What they would have, instead, was the Lord.

I think if you asked Aaron that night, sitting outside his tent with his bells on his hem, whether that was a fair trade, he would have said it was the best trade anyone ever made.

[pause]

The wind-down now. The slow part. If you are already asleep, well done; you have not missed anything important.

Aaron did not live to see the promised land. Neither did Moses. Many years after tonight's story, near the end of the forty years, the people came to a mountain called Mount Hor, on the edge of the land of Edom. And the Lord told Moses that Aaron's time had come.

So Moses and Aaron, and Aaron's son Eleazar, climbed the mountain together, in the sight of all the people. Three of them, going up slowly. On the top, Moses took off Aaron's priestly robes, the linen and the blue and the bells, and put them on Eleazar. And Aaron died there, on the top of the mountain. And Moses and Eleazar came down. Two of them. And when the people saw, they wept for Aaron for thirty days, the whole house of Israel.

Moses and Aaron. The two old brothers. One to speak and one to stand in between. I like to think that Aaron, at the end, was not afraid of climbing a mountain to meet the Lord. He had been walking into the holy place every morning for forty years.

And the staff?

Well. That's an interesting question, and I'm going to be honest with you about it.

The Book of Numbers says it was put back in front of the testimony, which means in front of the golden chest. The Letter to the Hebrews, in the New Testament, says that the golden chest, the ark, held three things. The golden jar of manna, the staff of Aaron that budded, and the tablets of the covenant. But the First Book of Kings says that by the time of King Solomon, when the ark was brought into the temple in Jerusalem, there was nothing in it except the two stone tablets.

So where did the staff go? The Scripture doesn't say. Perhaps it was lost in some battle. Perhaps it simply crumbled away at last, as wood does. Nobody knows, and we shan't guess. And whether it was the very same staff that swallowed the serpents in Pharaoh's hall, many people have thought so, and it would be lovely, but the book doesn't tell us that either.

What we do know is that the almond blossom went on.

When the Lord told Moses how to make the great golden lampstand for the holy place, the one with seven lamps, he said its cups were to be shaped like almond blossoms. Each branch with its cups like almonds, each with a bud and a flower. So every night, in the holy place, there was light shining out of golden almond flowers. Buds and blossoms, the same as on Aaron's staff, keeping watch through the dark.

And Christians, when they read this story, kept noticing things.

They noticed the dry wood, cut off from any root, that brought forth life without being planted. And many Christian writers and hymn-writers, in the East and in the West, came to see in it a picture of the Virgin Mary, who brought forth a son without a human father. In the hymns of the Eastern Church especially, she is sometimes greeted as Aaron's staff that blossomed. And in the Middle Ages, writers in Latin enjoyed the fact that their word for a rod, virga, sounds almost exactly like their word for a virgin, virgo, and they put the two together with Isaiah's prophecy that there shall come forth a rod out of the root of Jesse, and a flower shall rise up out of his root.

They noticed, too, that dead wood came to life overnight in a dark place before the Lord. And they thought of another morning, and another dark place, and another stone rolled away.

All of that is how Christians have read the story, not something the Book of Numbers says itself. But I think it is a very natural way to read it.

And the sons of Korah went on singing.

And in my imagination, somewhere in the wilderness, years later, an old man from Zebulun is sitting at the door of his tent in the evening with his plain staff across his knees. His granddaughter is not eight any more. She is grown, with children of her own, and one of them, a small boy, is sitting next to his great-grandfather, running his finger along some crooked letters in the wood.

"Who did this?" the boy asks. "It's all wobbly."

"Your grandmother did it," says the old man. "When she was a little girl."

"Why?"

And the old man tells him.

He tells him about the twelve staffs. About the long night when nobody slept. About walking home without his stick, and having to lean on a little girl instead. About Moses coming out of the tent in the morning with an armful of wood, and the one in the middle that was all flowers.

"Was yours the one?" asks the boy.

"No," says the old man. "Mine was one of the other eleven."

"Oh," says the boy, a little disappointed.

"It's all right," says the old man. "Most of us are one of the other eleven. That's a good thing to be. Somebody has to poke the fire."

And he gives the boy the staff to hold, and the boy holds it very seriously, and the sun goes down behind the dunes, and in the middle of the camp the fire comes on over the great tent, as it does every night. And somewhere inside, in the dark, in front of the golden chest, there is a stick that is covered in flowers, keeping watch.

And everyone else can sleep.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapters sixteen, seventeen and eighteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. In some Bibles, including the Hebrew text, the verses about the staffs are numbered a little differently, near the end of chapter seventeen. The quarrel of Korah, Dathan and Abiram, the censers, Aaron running into the crowd and standing between the dead and the living, the twelve staffs with their names, the night in the tent, the buds, blossoms and ripe almonds, the staff kept before the testimony, the people's fear, and the Lord's words to Aaron about being his portion are all the book's own, told plainly. The sons of Korah who did not die are in chapter twenty-six, and Aaron's death on Mount Hor is in chapter twenty.

The leader of the tribe of Zebulun and his granddaughter are our invention. There really was a staff for every tribe, but the book gives none of the leaders' names in this chapter, and nothing they said or felt. The crooked letters, the night in the tent, and the conversation between Moses and Aaron are ours, told in the spirit of the original.

The book does not say where in the wilderness this happened, or what wood the staffs were made of, and it does not say whether this was the same staff that Aaron threw down before Pharaoh, so we have not settled any of that. Hebrews says the staff was kept in the ark and First Kings says that by Solomon's day only the tablets were inside it; we have told both and not tried to reconcile them. The reading of the staff as a picture of the Virgin Mary is how Christian writers and hymn-writers have read it, and the play on virga and virgo is a medieval Latin one.

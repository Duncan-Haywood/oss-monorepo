A hundred and fifty. From one lame little boy, carried out of a house in a hurry by his nurse, who fell, and was picked up again.

His grandfather Jonathan had been an archer too. It was with a bow and a handful of arrows that Jonathan once saved David's life, out in the field by the stone. And here, ten generations and more later, were Jonathan's descendants, archers, a whole crowd of them. The family that might have been ended on the day the news came from Jezreel was not ended. Because a nurse ran. Because a man in Nowhere opened his door. Because a king remembered a promise.

* * *

Christians have always loved this story, and you can probably guess why.

A thousand years after David, another son of David, born in Bethlehem, told a story about a man who gave a great dinner. And when the guests who had been invited all made their excuses, the man said to his servant: go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the town, and bring in the poor and the crippled and the blind and the lame. And when the servant had done that, and there was still room, the man said: go out to the roads and the hedges, and fill my house.

And in the same chapter, Jesus says to the man who had invited him to supper: when you give a feast, do not just invite your friends and your rich neighbours, who can invite you back. Invite the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind, and you will be blessed, because they cannot repay you.

Mephibosheth could not repay David. He knew it. That was exactly what he meant when he said, what am I, that you should look upon a dead dog such as I? And David did not want repaying. He wanted to keep a promise. He wanted to show the kindness of God.

Which is why, I think, so many people over so many centuries have heard this story and seen themselves in it. All of us, in one way or another, come to the King's table limping. None of us can pay for our place. And the King says to every one of us the same thing he said to Mephibosheth, first, before anything else. Do not be afraid.

And then: you shall eat bread at my table, always.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Samuel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The fall when he was five is in chapter four, verse four. The great scene at the king's table is the whole of chapter nine. Ziba on the Mount of Olives is in chapter sixteen, and Mephibosheth at the Jordan, unwashed and unshaven, is in chapter nineteen. The sparing of Mephibosheth because of the oath is in chapter twenty-one, verse seven. Jonathan's friendship and his covenant with David are in the First Book of Samuel, chapters eighteen, twenty and twenty-three, and Machir's beans and lentils and cheese are in Second Samuel seventeen. The family tree, with Merib-baal, Mica and the hundred and fifty archers, is in the First Book of Chronicles, chapters eight and nine. The parable of the great dinner is in the Gospel of Luke, chapter fourteen.

Everything David, Ziba, Mephibosheth and Machir do and say in the story is in the book, told in a plain rendering. What I invented is everything around it: the nurse's thoughts, the farmhouse at Lo-debar and the games there, the king's men on the road, the long ride to Jerusalem, the dinners, the olives, and Mica in the courtyards. The book does not say whether Mica came to Jerusalem, or who Mephibosheth's wife was. The meaning of Lo-debar and the reason for Mephibosheth's two names are what many scholars think, not certain. And where the book leaves open whether Ziba or Mephibosheth told the truth, I have told you which I believe and why, but the book leaves it open, and you are free to weigh it yourself.

Mephibosheth is not counted among the saints with a feast day of his own, in either tradition, and neither is Jonathan. King David is.

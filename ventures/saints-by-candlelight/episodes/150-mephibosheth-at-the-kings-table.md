The floor of the king's hall is cool, and very clean, and it is the only thing the young man can see.

He is lying on it. Face down. His hands are flat on the stone, either side of his head, and his forehead is touching the floor, and he is trying very hard to keep his breathing quiet, because the hall is so quiet that he can hear it.

He did not walk in here. He cannot walk. Two men carried him in between them, and set him down, and stepped back, and now he has done the only thing he knows how to do in front of a king, which is to fall on his face and wait.

Somewhere above him, there are footsteps. They stop.

And a voice says his name. Not his title. Not "the son of Jonathan", or "the grandson of Saul". Just his name, the way you say the name of someone you have been looking for.

"Mephibosheth."

And from the floor, very small, he says, "Here is your servant."

He is quite sure that he is about to die.

He is wrong. He is about to be given a seat at supper. For the rest of his life.

First, the name. Because I know you are worried about it.

Mephibosheth. Me-fib-o-sheth. Four syllables, and every one of them a little bit slippery, like stepping stones in a stream. If you are lying in bed tonight trying to say it, you are in good company. Generations of children in Sunday school have tried to say it, and generations of readers at the lectern have looked down the page, seen it coming two verses ahead, and quietly started to sweat.

The Bible itself seems to have had two goes at it. In the First Book of Chronicles, the same young man is called Merib-baal. Scholars think the scribes who copied the Books of Samuel changed the end of the name, for reasons we do not need to go into at bedtime. So he has two names, and both of them are long. I am going to call him Mephibosheth, because that is what tonight's chapter calls him, and because I have been practising.

Now. Where are we, and when?

We are in the land of Israel, about three thousand years ago. There are no great cities as you would picture them. Jerusalem, the city of David, is a small walled town on a ridge, with a spring at the bottom of the hill and houses climbing up the slope, and a new palace that the king is still rather proud of. If you could visit, you would find it dusty and steep and full of goats, and you would hear about six different languages in the market, and you would get lost, because nobody has invented street names.

And there is a king. His name is David, and he has been king for some years now. You may know some of his story. He was the youngest son of a farmer in Bethlehem. He looked after sheep. He played the harp. He fought a giant with a sling, which is the kind of thing that gets you noticed. And then for a long, hard time he was on the run, hiding in caves and deserts, because the king before him wanted him dead.

That king was Saul.

Saul was the first king of Israel, and his story is a sad one, and we are not going to tell all of it tonight. What matters for us is this. Saul had a son. And Saul's son was the best friend David ever had.

His name was Jonathan.

The First Book of Samuel says that when Jonathan first met David, the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. And Jonathan took off his own robe, and gave it to David, and his armour, and his sword, and his bow, and his belt. Which, if you think about it, is quite something. Jonathan was the king's son. He was supposed to be the next king. And here he is, the first day, handing his royal cloak to a shepherd boy, as if he already knew.

Later, when Saul turned against David, Jonathan stood between them as long as he could. He warned David. He hid him. He shot arrows into a field as a secret signal, which is one of the best scenes in the whole Old Testament, and I recommend it to you on another night. And on the last day they were together, before David had to run for his life, Jonathan asked him for one thing.

He said, more or less: one day the Lord will make you safe from all your enemies. When that day comes, do not cut off your kindness from my house. Not ever.

The word he used, in Hebrew, is hesed. It is one of those words that does not fit neatly into English. It means kindness. But it also means loyalty, and faithfulness, and the kind of love that keeps its promises when nobody is making it. It is the word the Bible uses for the way God loves his people. Faithful love. Kindness that stays.

And David promised. The two of them swore it before the Lord. The Lord shall be between me and you, Jonathan said, and between my children and your children, for ever.

Then they parted, and they never saw each other again.

Hold on to that promise. It is the hinge of tonight's story. It is going to sit very quietly in the background for a long, long time, like a seed in the ground in winter. And then it is going to come up.

* * *

Let's start with a small boy.

He is five years old, and he lives in his grandfather's town, which is called Gibeah, a few miles north of Jerusalem. At least, that is very probably where he lives. The book does not actually say. But Gibeah was Saul's home, and Saul's family lived there, and so I think we can picture him there, on a warm day, playing in a courtyard with whatever five-year-old princes played with three thousand years ago. Sticks, I expect. Small boys and sticks are, as far as I can tell, one of the constants of human history.

His father, Jonathan, is away. His grandfather, the king, is away. They are with the army, up north, at a mountain called Gilboa, fighting the Philistines.

And on this day, a messenger comes running.

The Bible does not linger here, and neither will we. It says only that the news came from Jezreel, the valley below Gilboa, that Saul and Jonathan were dead. The king and the king's son, both in one day. The army was scattered. And in a royal household, in those days, news like that meant one thing. Run. Because when a kingdom falls, the enemies of the old king do not always stop to ask which members of his family are five years old.

So the boy's nurse picks him up.

We are not told her name. The Bible calls her simply his nurse, the woman who looked after him, the one who washed his face and told him off and probably sang to him. In this episode we are going to call her Adah, which is an old Hebrew name, and which is entirely our own invention. She deserves a name. She is about to do the bravest thing in her life.

She picks him up, and she runs.

And here the Bible says one more thing, very simply, the way you would say something that still hurts. As she hurried to flee, he fell. And he became lame.

That is all. We are not told how. Perhaps she stumbled on the steps, with the boy in her arms. Perhaps he slipped from her shoulder in the crowd at the gate. Whatever it was, both his feet were hurt, and they never healed. The book says it twice, at the beginning and at the end of his story. He was lame in both his feet.

I want to be gentle with Adah here, because I suspect she was not very gentle with herself. She did what she could. She saved his life. The fall was an accident, in the middle of the worst day anyone in that house had ever had. And she kept running, with a hurt and crying child, and she got him away.

* * *

Where did they go?

The Bible does not tell us the road. But it does tell us where the boy ended up. Years later, when somebody asks, the answer is: he is in the house of Machir, the son of Ammiel, at Lo-debar.

Lo-debar is across the river Jordan, on the eastern side, in the hill country called Gilead. That is a long way from Gibeah, especially for a woman carrying a child who cannot walk. It is also where what was left of Saul's family went, for a while, after Gilboa. Saul's general set up one of Saul's other sons as king over there, in a town called Mahanaim, a little kingdom across the river, like a candle burning down. It did not last. But it may be why a frightened nurse would head east.

Nobody is quite sure where Lo-debar was. Archaeologists have suggestions. None of them are certain.

And the name. I love the name. Lo-debar, in Hebrew, sounds very like "no pasture", or even, if you squint at it, "no thing". "Nothing." The prophet Amos, a few hundred years later, makes a joke about it. He says to some boastful people, you who are so pleased about Lo-debar, which in his mouth means, you who are so pleased about nothing at all.

So that is where the grandson of the first king of Israel grew up. In the house of a man called Machir. In a place called Nothing.

* * *

Now we are going to spend a little while in Lo-debar, and you should know that this part is our own invention. The Bible tells us only that he lived there, in Machir's house. It does not tell us what it was like. But we can imagine it, in the spirit of the book, and I think we can imagine it kindly, because of something Machir does at the very end of the story, which we will come to.

So let us picture a farmhouse in the hills of Gilead. Thick mud-brick walls. A flat roof where you could sleep on hot nights, and dry figs in the autumn. A courtyard with an oven in the corner, and chickens, who were always in the wrong place, and a fig tree that had been there longer than anyone could remember.

Machir is a man of some standing. He has fields, and flocks, and servants, and a long table. He took in a hurt boy from a fallen royal house, at some risk to himself, and he never made a fuss about it. I like to think of him as a big, quiet man who said very little and noticed everything.

And Adah stayed. In our telling, she stayed in Machir's house for the rest of her life, and she looked after the boy as he grew.

He grew up clever, because there was not much else he could do. He could not run with the other boys in the fields. He could not go out with the shepherds. When the household went up to the threshing floor at harvest, someone had to carry him, and after a certain age he refused to be carried in front of people, so he stayed behind. He learned to get about the house on his hands and his knees, and later on a pair of sticks, and on a donkey for anything further. He learned to read, which was rare. He learned to listen. He sat in the courtyard by the oven and heard everything that went on, and Machir's servants found that if you wanted to know what anyone in the village was thinking, you asked the young man by the oven.

And he learned not to say his name to strangers.

Because the world outside Lo-debar was changing. News came across the river now and then, the way news does, in pieces, from traders and travellers. The little kingdom at Mahanaim fell. His uncle, the last of Saul's sons who had been king, was killed. And David, the shepherd boy, the giant-killer, the outlaw, was king of all Israel now, in a new city called Jerusalem.

In the house of Machir, nobody talked about that very much.

You see, everybody knew how these things went. In a great many kingdoms of the ancient world, when a new king took the throne from an old royal family, he made sure there was nobody left of that family who might one day claim it back. It was a grim custom, and a common one. And Mephibosheth was not just anybody. He was the son of Saul's eldest son. If there was anyone left in the world with a claim to Saul's throne, it was the young man sitting by the oven in Lo-debar.

So he kept his head down. He was not a threat to anybody. He could not even walk. But he knew that, to a king, a name can be more dangerous than a pair of legs.

He grew up. He married, though the book does not tell us anything about his wife, not even her name. And he had a little son of his own, whom he called Mica.

And on quiet evenings, in our telling, Adah would sit beside him in the courtyard while the bread baked, and sometimes he would ask her about his father.

"Tell me about him again," he would say.

And she would tell him. How tall Jonathan was. How he laughed. How he was the best archer in the army, and could put an arrow wherever he liked. How he was brave, braver than his own father, and how he once climbed up a cliff with only his armour-bearer and took on a whole Philistine garrison, which is true, it is in First Samuel, chapter fourteen.

"And David?" he would ask, once, when he was older. "Did my father really love David?"

And Adah would be quiet for a while, and then she would say, "Yes. Everybody knew it. Your father loved him like his own soul."

"Then why," Mephibosheth said, "am I hiding from him?"

And Adah did not have an answer to that.

* * *

Now let us cross the river, and go up the hill to Jerusalem, to the new palace, on a day some years into David's reign.

The wars are mostly over. The Lord has given David rest from his enemies round about, which is exactly the day Jonathan talked about, all those years ago. The king has a house of cedar. He has a court, and officials, and a recorder, and a scribe. He has, for perhaps the first time in his life, a little time to think.

And one day, the Bible says, David asks a question.

"Is there anyone still left of the house of Saul," he says, "so that I may show him kindness, for Jonathan's sake?"

Kindness. That word again. Hesed.

I want you to notice how strange a question this is, for a king in those days to ask. Every adviser David has would expect the question to go the other way. Is there anyone still left of the house of Saul? Yes, my lord, and here is where he lives, and shall I send the soldiers now? But that is not the question. David is not looking for a rival. He is looking for someone to be kind to.

Nobody at court seems to know the answer. But somebody remembers that there is an old servant of Saul's house still about, a man called Ziba. And so Ziba is sent for.

Ziba is an interesting man, and we will meet him again before the night is out. He had been a steward in Saul's household, the man who managed the land and the servants and the stores. When Saul's house fell, Ziba, it seems, did rather well for himself. He has fifteen sons, the book tells us, and twenty servants of his own, which for a former steward of a fallen king is not bad going at all. I picture him as a neat, careful, well-dressed man, with an excellent memory and a talent for landing on his feet.

Ziba comes in and bows.

"Are you Ziba?" the king asks.

"Your servant," says Ziba.

And the king asks him again, in slightly different words. "Is there not still someone of the house of Saul, that I may show him the kindness of God?"

The kindness of God. Not just David's kindness now. The kindness of God. The same faithful, promise-keeping love that God shows to his people, David wants to show to Saul's family. It is a remarkable thing to say.

And Ziba says, "There is still a son of Jonathan. He is lame in his feet."

I have always wondered about the way Ziba says that. As if he were saying, there is one, my lord, but you needn't worry about him. Or perhaps, there is one, my lord, but he is not much use. Or perhaps it is just the first thing anyone ever says about Mephibosheth, the thing that comes before his name.

"Where is he?" asks the king.

And Ziba says, "He is in the house of Machir, the son of Ammiel, at Lo-debar."

And King David sends, and fetches him.

The next part, again, is our own invention, because the Bible covers it in four words. David sent and brought him. But behind those four words there is a long journey, and a lot of fear, and I think we should go along.

So imagine the messengers coming up the track into Lo-debar. Three of them, say, on good mules, in the king's livery, with the dust of the Jordan valley on their cloaks. In a village like Lo-debar, three men from the king's court are a very big event. The children run ahead to announce them. The dogs bark. The chickens, who are always in the wrong place, are in the wrong place.

And the messengers stop at the gate of Machir's house, and the eldest of them says, "We are looking for Mephibosheth, the son of Jonathan."

In the courtyard, by the oven, a young man goes very still.

He has been dreading this day, quietly, for as long as he can remember. And now it is here, and it has come in the most polite possible way, with good mules and clean livery and a request to come to Jerusalem at the king's pleasure. He does not believe the politeness for a moment.

Machir comes out to meet them. He looks at the messengers for a long time. Then he looks at the young man by the oven. Then he says, in his quiet way, "He will come. And I will send men with him."

That night, in our telling, nobody in Machir's house sleeps very much.

Mephibosheth sits up late with Adah. She is very old now. Her hands shake a little. And she says what she has been wanting to say for twenty years.

"It was my fault," she says. "That day. I dropped you."

And Mephibosheth says, "You carried me."

"I dropped you."

"You carried me," he says again. "You carried me all the way across the Jordan. If you had not, I would not be here to be dropped. You carried me, Adah. That's the part I remember."

And they sit together by the last of the fire, and neither of them says anything for a long time.

In the morning, he says goodbye to his wife, and to little Mica, who is cross that he is not allowed to come and see the king. And they lift him onto a donkey, and Machir's men walk beside him, and they go down out of the hills, down into the deep, hot valley of the Jordan, and across the river at the ford, and up the long, steep road on the other side, up and up into the hill country, past Jericho, towards Jerusalem.

It is a long way. It takes days. And the whole time, Mephibosheth is thinking about what he will say. He has a speech ready. He has several speeches. He has the speech in which he explains that he has no interest in the throne at all. He has the speech in which he begs for his son's life, if not his own. He has a very short speech that is mostly just "please".

He is going to need none of them.

* * *

So now we are back where we started. The king's hall in Jerusalem. The cool stone floor.

The men who carried him in set him down, and step back. And Mephibosheth does what you do before a king. He falls on his face and does homage. Forehead to the floor.

And David says, "Mephibosheth."

And he says, "Here is your servant."

And then the king says the first thing anyone in the Bible ever says to this young man. He does not say, why have you been hiding. He does not say, are you plotting against me. He says,

"Do not be afraid."

Which, if you have been reading your Bible for a while, you will recognise. It is what angels say. It is what God says, over and over, to Abraham, and to Hagar, and to the prophets, and much later to the shepherds on the hills above Bethlehem, which is David's own town. Do not be afraid. It is the first thing heaven usually says when it comes close to somebody frightened. And here it is in the mouth of a king.

"Do not be afraid," says David, "for I will surely show you kindness, for the sake of your father Jonathan. I will give you back all the land of your grandfather Saul. And you shall eat at my table, always."

I like to imagine the silence after that. The young man still on the floor, trying to make the words mean what they seem to mean. The courtiers looking at each other. The king, perhaps, crouching down a little, so as to be nearer.

And Mephibosheth bows again, and he says this. "What is your servant, that you should look upon a dead dog such as I?"

A dead dog. It is the lowest thing a person could call himself, in those days. Dogs were not pets, mostly. They were scavengers in the streets. And a dead one is even less than that. It is how he sees himself. A lame man, from a fallen house, from a village called Nothing. Not worth a king's notice. Not worth a king's look.

David does not argue with him. He just goes on being kind, which is often the best way to argue with someone who thinks he is worth nothing.

He calls for Ziba.

"All that belonged to Saul and to all his house," David tells him, "I have given to your master's grandson. You and your sons and your servants will work the land for him, and bring in the harvest, so that your master's grandson will have food to eat. But Mephibosheth himself will always eat at my table."

And Ziba, with fifteen sons and twenty servants, bows very low, and says, "Your servant will do everything my lord the king commands."

I would give a great deal to know what Ziba's face looked like at that moment. The book does not say. It is very discreet about Ziba. But it is a very sudden change of fortune, for a man who has been doing rather well out of Saul's land for some years, to find that it all belongs to someone else after all, and that he and his fifteen sons are to be the farmhands.

And the book sums it up, very simply. So Mephibosheth ate at David's table, like one of the king's sons.

* * *

That evening, there is supper.

Again, I am going to imagine it, because the Bible gives us the fact and not the menu. But I think you should be there.

So picture a long room in the palace, with lamps along the walls, and a long low table, and cushions, and the smell of bread, and roast lamb, and lentils with garlic, and cheese, and figs, and honey, and a great deal of wine in clay jars. The king's sons are there, the princes, loud and young and very sure of themselves. The king's officials are there. The king's musicians are tuning something in the corner.

And they carry in Mephibosheth, and they set him down, not at the far end, by the door, but near the king. Where a son would sit.

He does not know what to do with his hands. He does not know which bread to take. He has eaten at Machir's table all his life, and Machir's table is a good table, but it is not this. One of the princes looks at his feet, and then quickly looks away, and Mephibosheth sees him do it, because he always sees people do it.

And then the king leans across and puts a piece of bread in his hand, the way you might for a guest you particularly want to make welcome. And the king says, quietly, under the noise of the room, "You have your father's eyes. Did you know that?"

And Mephibosheth says, "Nobody's ever told me that."

"Well," says David, "you have. I would know them anywhere." And then the king laughs, a little, and wipes his face, and says, "He would have been very pleased to see you here. He made me promise, you know."

"I know," says Mephibosheth. "She told me."

"Who told you?"

"The woman who carried me."

And David is quiet for a moment. And then he says, "Then I owe her something too."

That conversation is entirely ours. The Bible gives us none of it. But I do not think it says anything the Bible would not want said.

He ate at the king's table from then on. Always, the book says. Every day. Not once, as a nice gesture, and then back across the river. Every day. When the princes came and went, when the officials changed, when the musicians played the new songs, there he was, at the table, like one of the king's sons. Lame in both his feet. And it did not matter.

The book even tells us that his little son, Mica, came to Jerusalem too, and that the whole household of Ziba became Mephibosheth's servants. And so, in our telling, a small boy who had been very cross to be left behind in Lo-debar got to see the king after all, and grew up running in and out of the palace, and was told off by the guards for sliding down the steps.

* * *

That would be a lovely place to stop. But the Bible does not stop there, and it would not be quite honest to leave out the next part. It is a little more complicated. Real life usually is.

Some years later, there is great trouble in David's house. His own son, Absalom, turns against him, and raises an army, and David has to leave Jerusalem in a hurry, on foot, weeping, with his household and his loyal men around him. It is one of the saddest days of his life, and it is a long story for another night.

And as David goes over the top of the Mount of Olives, fleeing the city, who should come up to meet him on the road but Ziba. Neat, careful Ziba. With a pair of donkeys, saddled, and loaded with two hundred loaves of bread, and a hundred bunches of raisins, and a hundred summer fruits, and a skin of wine. For the king's household, he says. For the journey.

And David, who is grateful and exhausted and heartbroken, says, "Where is your master's grandson?"

And Ziba says, "He's staying in Jerusalem. He said, today the house of Israel will give me back my grandfather's kingdom."

Which is to say: Mephibosheth has betrayed you. He thinks his chance has come.

And David, on that terrible day, believes him. "Then everything that belonged to Mephibosheth," he says, "is now yours."

And Ziba bows very low, and says, "May I always find favour in your sight, my lord the king."

Hm.

Hold on to that "hm". We will come back to it.

* * *

Now, while David is on the run, he crosses the Jordan, and goes east, to Mahanaim, the same town where Saul's last son once had his little kingdom. His men are tired and hungry and thirsty in the wilderness.

And three men come out to meet them, bringing help. And the Bible lists, with what I can only describe as great affection, everything they brought. Beds. Basins. Clay pots. Wheat, and barley, and flour, and roasted grain. Beans, and lentils. Honey, and curds, and sheep, and cheese from the herd. For David, and for the people with him, to eat. For they said, the people are hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness.

One of those three men is a very old and very rich man of Gilead called Barzillai, of whom there is a lovely story another night.

And one of them, the Bible says, is Machir, the son of Ammiel, from Lo-debar.

Do you see? The quiet man who took in Jonathan's son, when Jonathan's son had nothing, now comes out from the place called Nothing, with beds and lentils and cheese, to take care of David, when David has nothing. The kindness goes round. It went from Jonathan to David, and from Machir to Mephibosheth, and from David to Mephibosheth, and now it comes back across the river to David, in the shape of a lot of cheese.

The Bible does not draw a line between these things. It just mentions Machir's name, and lets you notice. I think we are meant to notice.

* * *

The rebellion ends. It ends in sorrow, with Absalom dead, and David weeping for him, and we will not tell that part at bedtime. But the king comes home.

And on the day the king comes home, Mephibosheth comes to meet him.

And here the book tells us something very particular about how he looks. He had not cared for his feet, it says, nor trimmed his beard, nor washed his clothes, from the day the king left until the day he came back in peace.

In those days, that was what you did when you were in mourning. You let yourself go. You did not wash, or trim your beard, or put on clean clothes, because something terrible had happened and it would not be right to look as if it had not. Mephibosheth had been in mourning for the king. For all those weeks. Not in hiding. Not plotting. Mourning.

And David looks at him and says, rather sharply, I think, "Why did you not go with me, Mephibosheth?"

And Mephibosheth says, "My lord the king, my servant deceived me. I said to him, saddle me a donkey, so that I can ride on it and go with the king, because your servant is lame. And he slandered your servant to my lord the king."

In other words: I told Ziba to saddle a donkey for me. He saddled two. And he took them both, and went to meet you, and left me behind. A man who cannot walk cannot follow a king out of a city on foot.

And then Mephibosheth says something that I think is one of the most beautiful things anyone says in the Books of Samuel.

"But my lord the king is like an angel of God. So do whatever seems good to you. All my grandfather's house were nothing but dead men before my lord the king. And yet you set your servant among those who eat at your table. What right have I, then, to cry out to the king any more?"

He does not argue. He does not ask for his land back. He has had the table. He has had the kindness. That, to him, is the thing that matters.

And David, who has had a very long and very terrible few weeks, and who has two stories in front of him and no way of knowing for certain which one is true, says, "Why say any more about it? I have decided. You and Ziba shall divide the land."

And Mephibosheth says, "Oh, let him take it all. Since my lord the king has come home safely."

Let him take it all.

Now. Here is the honest bit. The Bible does not tell us, for certain, who was telling the truth, Ziba or Mephibosheth. It gives us both stories and lets us decide. It was a long time ago, and there was nobody taking notes on the road.

But it does give us the feet, and the beard, and the unwashed clothes. And it gives us those last words. Let him take it all. That is not the voice of a man who wanted a kingdom. That is the voice of a man who only ever wanted to be at the table.

I know what I think. And I suspect you do, too.

[pause]

So let us start to wind down now, and let the lamps burn a little lower.

There is one more thing the Bible tells us about Mephibosheth, and it is said in one line. Years later, there came a hard time, when the house of Saul was in danger once more, and others of Saul's family did not come through it. But the king spared Mephibosheth, the son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, because of the oath of the Lord that was between them, between David and Jonathan.

The oath. The promise, on that last day in the field, before David ran. Do not cut off your kindness from my house. Not ever.

David kept it. It held. Not once, but again, and again, for as long as both of them lived.

And then the Bible lets him go, quietly, the way it lets most people go. We are not told when Mephibosheth died, or where. I like to think it was in Jerusalem, near the table, an old man by then, with his sticks beside him and his son's children running about the palace, sliding down the steps.

Because his family did go on. In the First Book of Chronicles, there is a long list of names, the kind of list people tend to skip when they are reading the Bible straight through. But if you look at it closely, you will find Merib-baal, the son of Jonathan, and his son Mica, and Mica's sons, and their sons after them, generation after generation, a whole family tree growing out of one small lame boy who should not, by all the rules of kings, have lived to grow up.

And near the end of that list, many generations on, it says that some of them were mighty men, archers, with many sons and grandsons.

Archers. Like Jonathan. Who could put an arrow wherever he liked.

When David heard that Jonathan had died, he wrote a song, and in it he said that the bow of Jonathan never turned back. I like to think that, in a way, it never did.

[pause]

There is one more table I want to mention before we go.

Many centuries later, a teacher was at a dinner party, on a sabbath, in the house of an important man, and he noticed how the guests were all trying to get the best seats. And he told them a story about a man who gave a great banquet, and when the important guests made their excuses, the man said to his servant, go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the town, and bring in the poor, and the crippled, and the blind, and the lame.

That teacher was a son of David, a long way down that family tree. And his table, too, is for the people who think of themselves as dead dogs, and who come from places called Nothing, and who have to be carried in.

Christians have always heard something of that in tonight's story. The king who goes looking for the one who is hiding. Who says, do not be afraid. Who does not wait for him to earn it, or to walk in on his own two feet. Who just says his name, and sets a place for him, and keeps it set.

Always, the book says. He ate at the king's table, always.

So let us leave him there. Late in the evening, in the long room in Jerusalem. The lamps are low. The musicians are playing something slow. The princes have gone off to bed, and the bread has been cleared away, and the last of the wine is in the jars.

And at the table, near the king, there is a man with a pair of sticks propped against the wall beside him, and a cup in his hand, and a small boy asleep with his head on his knee.

He is not afraid any more.

It is morning in Jerusalem, and the room has gone so quiet that you can hear a baby breathing.

There are two women standing in front of the king. They are not rich. Their feet are dusty and their eyes are red, because neither of them has slept. One of them is holding a small bundle wrapped in cloth, and the bundle is alive. It makes the small snuffling sound that very new babies make, like a puppy dreaming.

The two women have been shouting at each other for some time. Now they have stopped, because the king has said something.

He has said, quite calmly, to the man standing by the door, "Bring me a sword."

And at the back of the room, a boy who is supposed to be writing everything down has stopped writing. His reed pen is in the air. He is looking at the king. And he is thinking, he cannot mean it. Surely he cannot mean it.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is King Solomon's dream at Gibeon and his judgment between the two mothers, from the First Book of Kings, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a young scribe and his grandfather, and everything they say, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. Last time on Saints by Candlelight, we watched King Solomon dedicate the Temple in Jerusalem. The cedar from Lebanon, the cloud that filled the house, the young king kneeling in front of everyone and saying, heaven cannot contain you, how much less this house that I have built.

And right at the beginning of that story, I mentioned, in passing, a dream. And a famous judgment about two mothers and one baby. And I said it was a story for another night.

Well. This is the other night.

So we are going back about ten years from where we were last time, to the very start of Solomon's reign. About nine hundred and seventy years before Christ, by the usual reckoning of historians, though the Bible itself, of course, does not count in our years. It counts in kings.

Let me tell you about the city first.

Jerusalem, in those days, was small. If you have been to the modern city, with its great golden walls and its crowds and its buses, put all that out of your mind. The Jerusalem of Solomon's first years was a narrow ridge of rock running south from the place where the Temple would one day stand, with steep valleys on either side. The old part, the part King David had taken, was called the City of David. You could walk from one end of it to the other in a few minutes, if nobody stopped you to talk, which in a small city somebody always does.

The houses were built of stone and mud brick, close together, with flat roofs. People used the roofs the way we use a back garden. They dried figs on them, and flax, and laundry. They slept on them in summer when it was too hot indoors. They talked to the neighbours across the gap. If you wanted to know what was happening in Jerusalem, you did not need a newspaper. You just needed a roof, and a neighbour with good hearing.

At the bottom of the ridge, on the east side, was a spring called Gihon, and every morning the women and girls went down to it with jars, and came back up with them full, and that is where a great deal of the news of the city was exchanged, and improved, and occasionally invented. It was roughly the ancient equivalent of a group chat, except that it was wetter, and you had to carry it home on your head.

At night, the houses were dark. A small clay lamp with olive oil in it gave about as much light as a candle, and oil was not free, so people went to bed when it got dark and got up when it got light. A family slept on mats on the floor, close together, for warmth. And a mother with a new baby slept with the baby right beside her, so that she could feed it in the night without getting up. Mothers have done this for as long as there have been mothers. It matters for our story, so I mention it now, gently, and we will come back to it.

* * *

Now, a word about judges.

In ancient Israel, if you had a quarrel with your neighbour, about a field, or a goat, or a debt, or a wall that was leaning on your side, you did not go to a courthouse. There were no courthouses. You went to the gate.

Every town had a gate, and the gate was not just a hole in the wall. It was a sort of covered passage, with stone benches along the sides, and in the shade of it the elders of the town sat. Old men, mostly, who had lived long enough to have seen most kinds of trouble before. And when you had a quarrel, you went and stood in front of them, and said your piece, and your neighbour said his, and they listened, and asked questions, and decided.

The Law of Moses had a great deal to say about how they should do this. In the Book of Deuteronomy, Moses tells the judges, hear the small and the great alike. Do not be afraid of anyone's face. Because the judgment belongs to God. And he says, do not take a bribe, because a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise. And, which is very important for tonight, he says that a matter must be established on the word of two witnesses, or three. Not one. You cannot condemn someone just because one person says so.

And if a case was too hard for the elders at the gate, it went up. In the wilderness, it had gone to Moses himself. His father-in-law Jethro had watched him one day, sitting from morning till evening with a queue of people in front of him, and had said, more or less, you will wear yourself out. Appoint judges for the small cases, and let them bring the hard ones to you. Which is the oldest piece of management advice in the Bible, and still one of the best.

By the time of the kings, the hard cases went to the king. That was part of what being a king meant. Not just wearing a crown and leading the army. Judging. Being the last place a poor person could go, when nobody else could help.

And a king who did this badly was in trouble. Solomon's own half-brother, Absalom, years before, had stood at the gate of Jerusalem in the early mornings, and whenever anyone came with a case for the king, he would put his arm round them and say, "Your case is good and right. But there is nobody here from the king to hear you. Oh, if only I were judge in the land. I would give everyone justice." And the Bible says that in this way Absalom stole the hearts of the people of Israel. It ended very badly, as I expect you know. But the lesson was not lost. People wanted a king who would listen.

* * *

And so, to Solomon.

He had become king while his father David was still alive, in a hurry, with a good deal of drama, which is a story of its own and not one for bedtime. And then David died, an old man, full of days, and was buried in the City of David. And Solomon was king alone.

He was young. The Bible does not tell us exactly how young. But in a moment, he will call himself a little child, which tells us at least how young he felt.

Now, the Bible says something very honest about Solomon at this point. It says that Solomon loved the Lord, and walked in the ways of his father David. Only, it adds, he offered sacrifices at the high places. Because the Temple had not yet been built, and people still worshipped on hilltops here and there across the land. And the greatest of these high places was at a town called Gibeon.

The Second Book of Chronicles explains why Gibeon was special. It says that the old tent of meeting was there, the tent that Moses had made in the wilderness. And the bronze altar that a craftsman called Bezalel had made, in the days of Moses, was there too, in front of the tent. The ark of the covenant itself was in Jerusalem, in its own tent that David had pitched for it. But the old tent and the old altar were at Gibeon.

So Solomon decided to go to Gibeon, and to begin his reign with worship. And not quietly. The Bible says he took the whole assembly with him, the commanders of thousands and of hundreds, the judges, the leaders, the heads of the families. And on the bronze altar at Gibeon, he offered a thousand burnt offerings.

* * *

Let me introduce you to two people who are not in the Bible.

They are our invention. But I think there must have been people very like them.

The first is an old man called Hanan. He is one of the elders who sit in the gate of Jerusalem. He has a white beard and a bad knee, and he has been listening to other people's quarrels for thirty years. He is not famous, and he is not important. But he is fair. If you asked the women at the Gihon spring which of the elders you would want for your case, most of them would say, Hanan. He is slow. But he is fair.

The second is Hanan's grandson, Gaddi. Gaddi is about twelve. He is learning to be a scribe. This means that he is learning to write, which in those days very few people could do, and it means that he spends a great deal of time sitting very still with a piece of broken pottery on his knee, scratching letters onto it with a sharp point, and being told he has done it wrong.

Broken pottery, by the way, was the scrap paper of the ancient world. Every household broke a pot now and then, and the pieces were free, and you could write a list, or a receipt, or a letter, on a curved bit of jar. Archaeologists find them by the thousand. Some of them are school exercises, children copying the alphabet, with mistakes. I find that very comforting. Three thousand years ago, someone was getting their letters the wrong way round, and someone else was sighing at them.

Now, when the king went up to Gibeon with the whole assembly, Hanan went too, because he was a judge, and judges were invited. His knee did not like the walk. Gibeon is only a few hours north-west of Jerusalem, but it is a few hours of up and down, and Hanan took it slowly, with Gaddi beside him carrying the water skin and a bag of bread and dried figs.

"Why are we going?" said Gaddi, somewhere around the second hill.

"Because the king has asked us to," said Hanan.

"Yes, but why has he asked us to?"

Hanan thought about it, the way he thought about everything, slowly. "Because he is new," he said at last. "And a new king has to decide what kind of king he is going to be. Some of them decide by fighting someone. Some of them decide by building something. This one has decided to begin by praying. I think that is a good sign. But it is only a sign. We will see."

* * *

I will not describe a thousand burnt offerings to you in detail. It is bedtime. Let us just say that it took a very long time, and there was a very great deal of smoke, and a great many priests, and that by the evening everyone was tired, and the hillside at Gibeon was covered with people settling down for the night in tents and under cloaks, and the smell of woodsmoke hung over everything.

Gaddi and his grandfather found a place by a wall, out of the wind, and ate their bread and figs, and lay down.

And somewhere on that hill, in a tent much grander than theirs, the young king lay down too.

And that night, the Bible says, the Lord appeared to Solomon in a dream.

And God said, "Ask. What shall I give you?"

[pause]

I want you to stop for a moment and think about that. Not about Solomon. About you.

If God said that to you tonight, in a dream. Ask. What shall I give you? What would you say?

I will be honest. I suspect most of us would need a minute. And I suspect that the first few things that came into our heads would not be the best ones.

Solomon did not need a minute. Or if he did, the Bible does not mention it. Here is what he said, in a plain rendering.

He said, "You showed great and faithful love to my father David, because he walked before you in truth, and in righteousness, and with an upright heart. And you have kept this great love for him, and given him a son to sit on his throne, as it is today.

"And now, Lord my God, you have made your servant king in place of my father David. But I am only a little child. I do not know how to go out or how to come in. And your servant is in the middle of your people, the people you have chosen, a great people, too many to number or count.

"So give your servant a listening heart, to judge your people, and to tell the difference between good and evil. Because who is able to judge this great people of yours?"

* * *

I said a listening heart. Most English Bibles say an understanding heart, and that is a perfectly good translation. But the Hebrew words are lev shomea, and shomea is the ordinary word for hearing, for listening. A heart that hears.

I love that. He did not ask for a clever head. He asked for a heart that listens.

And there is something else in that prayer that I love, and it is the bit about going out and coming in. "I do not know how to go out or how to come in." It is a Hebrew way of saying, I do not know how to do anything. I do not know how to get through an ordinary day. It is the kind of thing you might say in your first week in a new job, when you do not know where the kettle is, or who to ask, and everyone else seems to know exactly what they are doing.

That is what the new king of Israel said to God. I do not know where the kettle is. Please help.

And the Bible says that the Lord was pleased that Solomon had asked this.

And God said to him, "Because you have asked for this, and have not asked for a long life for yourself, or for riches, or for the lives of your enemies, but have asked for understanding, to hear what is just, then look. I am doing what you asked. I am giving you a wise and understanding heart, so that there has been no one like you before you, and no one like you will arise after you.

"And I am also giving you what you did not ask for, both riches and honour, so that no king will be your equal all your days. And if you walk in my ways, and keep my laws and my commandments, as your father David walked, then I will lengthen your days."

And Solomon woke up. And it was a dream.

* * *

The Bible tells us what Solomon did next, and it tells us very simply. He went back to Jerusalem. He stood before the ark of the covenant of the Lord. He offered burnt offerings and peace offerings. And he made a feast for all his servants.

On the road home the next day, Gaddi said, "Did anything happen?"

"What do you mean?" said his grandfather.

"Last night. At Gibeon. People are saying something happened to the king."

"People are always saying things," said Hanan. "I heard the king went back before the ark when he got home. That is the kind of thing a man does when something has happened to him." He walked on a little. "But a dream is between a man and God. What matters to us is whether he is a good judge. And we will not know that from a dream. We will know it from the gate."

"How will we know it from the gate?"

Hanan stopped, and leaned on his stick, and looked at his grandson. "Gaddi," he said. "When two people come to me with a quarrel, what is the first thing I do?"

Gaddi thought. "You listen."

"To whom?"

"To the first one."

"And then?"

"To the second one."

"And then?"

Gaddi knew this one, because he had heard it a hundred times. "And then you say back to each of them what they said. So that they know you heard."

"And why?"

"Because," said Gaddi, in the voice of a boy who has been taught something many times, "a man who has been heard can bear to lose. A man who has not been heard cannot bear to win."

Hanan smiled. "Good. You have it on your tongue. One day perhaps you will have it in your heart. Now. If two people come with a quarrel, and there is no witness, and each one says the other is lying, and there is no way at all to tell. What do you do then?"

Gaddi thought for a long time. They walked down one hill and up the next.

"I don't know," he said.

"No," said Hanan. "Neither do I. That is what the king is for."

* * *

Weeks went by. Summer came on. The figs ripened.

And in those weeks Gaddi sat at the gate beside his grandfather with his piece of pottery, and practised his letters, and listened.

There was a man whose donkey had eaten his neighbour's barley. The neighbour wanted the donkey. The man offered the barley back, which, the elders pointed out, was no longer available. There was a widow whose son-in-law had moved a boundary stone, just a little, just the width of a hand, every year, for six years, until her vegetable patch had become noticeably smaller and his had become noticeably larger. The Law of Moses says, cursed is the one who moves his neighbour's boundary stone, and the elders read it out to him, slowly, and the son-in-law went red, and moved it back, all six hands' widths of it, while half the street watched from the roofs.

None of those cases needed a king. They needed old men who had seen donkeys and boundary stones before. And Gaddi wrote them all down, badly, and his grandfather corrected his spelling.

And then, one morning, a messenger came to the gate and said that the elders were wanted. There was a case going before the king. A hard one. The king had asked for the elders to come and hear it.

Hanan got up, slowly, with his bad knee. "Bring your pottery," he said to Gaddi. "And a spare piece. And keep your mouth shut, whatever happens."

* * *

The Bible does not tell us where the king sat to judge, in those first years. Later, he built a great hall for it, the Hall of the Throne, panelled in cedar from floor to ceiling. But that was years away. In our story, let us just say it was a room in the king's house in the City of David, with a raised seat at one end, and the morning light coming in through the high windows, and a few of the king's officials standing around, and the elders along one wall, and a twelve-year-old boy at the back, trying to be invisible.

And here is how the Bible begins this part of the story. It says: then two women came to the king, and stood before him.

And the Bible tells us one more thing about these two women. It says that they were harlots. That is the old word. It means they were women who had lived hard lives, outside the protection of husbands and families, the kind of women respectable people crossed the street to avoid.

I think that detail matters, and I think it is there on purpose. These were not the kind of people anyone would expect to get the king's time. There was no rich father to speak for them, no husband, no brother, no family name. And yet they came to the king, and the king heard them. Hear the small and the great alike, said the Law of Moses. Here was the small. And here was the king, listening.

The first woman spoke.

* * *

I am going to tell you what she said almost exactly as the Bible gives it, because it is one of the most vivid speeches in Scripture, and you can hear in it a woman who has not slept and is trying very hard not to cry.

She said, "Oh, my lord. This woman and I live in the same house. And I gave birth to a baby while she was in the house. And on the third day after I gave birth, this woman gave birth too. We were together. There was no stranger with us in the house. Only the two of us, in the house.

"And this woman's son died in the night, because she lay on him.

"And she got up in the middle of the night, and took my son from beside me, while your servant was asleep, and laid him in her arms. And she laid her dead son in my arms.

"And when I got up in the morning to feed my son, there he was, dead. But when I looked at him closely in the morning light, I saw. It was not my son. It was not the son I had borne."

[pause]

That is as sad as tonight's story gets, and I will not dwell on it. A little baby died in the night, the way little babies sometimes did, and sometimes still do. The Bible says it in one line, and so shall we. We will leave that little one with God, who made him, and who loves him, and who has not forgotten him for one moment in three thousand years.

And then the second woman spoke. And what she said was short.

"No. The living one is my son. The dead one is your son."

And the first woman said, "No. The dead one is your son. The living one is mine."

And the Bible says, simply, that they spoke this way before the king. Which I take to mean that they went on saying it. Backwards and forwards. Mine. No, mine. Yours is the dead one. No, yours is. Until they were both hoarse.

* * *

Now. Put yourself in the king's place. Or in Hanan's place, along the wall. Or Gaddi's, at the back.

What do you actually know?

You know there were two women in one house. You know there were two babies, born three days apart. You know that one of the babies is dead and one is alive. And that is all you know.

There is no witness. The first woman said so herself, and it is the most important thing she said. "There was no stranger with us in the house." The Law of Moses says a matter is established on the word of two or three witnesses. Here there are none. Only two voices, each saying the other is lying.

You cannot look at the baby and tell. Babies of a few days old look very much like one another, I am told, to everyone except their mothers, and sometimes, in the first few days, even to them. There was no test you could do. Three thousand years later, we have ways of telling from the blood whose child is whose. They had nothing like that. They had two women and one baby.

You cannot ask the neighbours. They were asleep. And besides, the neighbours, if they knew these women at all, probably did not think much of either of them.

Gaddi, at the back, had his pottery on his knee and his reed pen in his hand, and he had written, the first woman says, and then, the second woman says. And then he stopped, because there was nothing else to write. He looked at his grandfather. His grandfather's face was very still. Gaddi knew that face. It was the face his grandfather made when he did not know.

If two people come with a quarrel, and there is no witness, and each one says the other is lying, and there is no way at all to tell. What do you do then?

That is what the king is for.

* * *

And then the king spoke.

And the first thing he did, which I think everyone forgets, is exactly what Hanan had taught Gaddi on the road from Gibeon. He said it back to them.

The Bible gives his words. The king said, "This one says, this is my son, the living one, and your son is the dead one. And that one says, no, your son is the dead one, and my son is the living one."

Gaddi, at the back, heard it, and something in him went very quiet. Because the king had said it exactly. Not taking a side. Not getting it wrong. Not making one of them sound better than the other. Just saying it back, so that both of them knew they had been heard.

A heart that listens.

And then the king said, "Bring me a sword."

And they brought a sword before the king.

[pause]

I need to tell you now, before we go any further, because it is bedtime and I do not want anybody lying awake: the baby is going to be all right. Nobody is going to hurt him. Not tonight, not in this story. Hold on to that.

Because the next thing the king said was this.

"Divide the living child in two. And give half to the one, and half to the other."

* * *

What was in Solomon's mind when he said that? The Bible does not tell us. It tells us what he said, and it tells us what happened next, and it leaves the rest to us. I am not going to pretend to know more than it says.

But I can tell you what happened in the room. Because the Bible tells us that.

The woman whose son was the living child spoke to the king. And the Bible says why she spoke. It says it was because her heart was moved for her son. The Hebrew is stronger than that. It says, something like, everything inside her yearned for him, the deep place where a mother feels for her child, all of it cried out at once.

And she said, "Oh, my lord. Give her the living child. Please. Do not kill him. Do not kill him."

And the other woman said, "He shall be neither mine nor yours. Divide him."

[pause]

And in the room, everyone heard both of those voices. And there was no longer any need for a witness. Because the two women had just been their own witnesses.

One of them, faced with the choice between losing the child and seeing him harmed, had chosen to lose him. She would rather walk out of that room with nothing, and see him grow up in another woman's house, and never be called his mother, than see him hurt. That is what love does. It gives up its own claim for the good of the one it loves.

And the other had chosen, if she could not have the child, that nobody should. Which is not love. It is something else. I will not call it by a harsh name, because that woman had also lost a baby in the night, and grief does strange and terrible things to people. But it was not love.

And the king answered, and said, "Give her the living child. Do not kill him. She is his mother."

He used her own words. Do not kill him. He gave them back to her. She had said, give her the living child. And he said, no. Give her the living child. Meaning the one who had said it. The one who had been willing to give him away. She is his mother.

And the sword went back where it came from, and was never used. And a woman who had not slept for a night and a day walked out of the king's house in the morning sun with her son in her arms.

* * *

The Bible ends this story with one sentence, and it is this. All Israel heard of the judgment which the king had judged. And they stood in awe of the king. Because they saw that the wisdom of God was in him, to do justice.

You can imagine how fast that story got around. It would have been at the Gihon spring before noon. On every roof in the City of David by the afternoon. In every town gate in the land within the week. Have you heard what the new king did? Have you heard about the two women and the sword?

That evening, Gaddi and his grandfather sat at the gate, in the last of the light, after everyone had gone home. Gaddi had his piece of pottery on his knee. On it, in his best letters, he had written, the first woman says. The second woman says. The king says. And then he had stopped, because he did not know how to write down what had happened next.

"Grandfather," he said. "Was it a trick?"

Hanan was quiet for a long while.

"No," he said. "I do not think it was a trick. A trick is when you catch someone out. He did not catch anyone out. He gave them each a chance to show what was in their heart. And they showed him." He shook his head slowly. "Thirty years I have sat in this gate. I have listened to the words people say. That young man listened to something underneath the words. I do not know how he knew it would be there. But he knew."

"You said a dream was between a man and God," said Gaddi. "And that we would know from the gate."

"I did," said Hanan.

"So do we know?"

His grandfather looked out at the darkening street, and the roofs, and the first lamps being lit.

"We know," he said. "Write that down."

* * *

And so, slowly now, let us come to the end.

The Bible says that God gave Solomon wisdom and understanding beyond measure, and a breadth of heart like the sand on the seashore. It says that he spoke three thousand proverbs, and his songs were a thousand and five. It says he spoke about trees, from the great cedar in Lebanon to the little hyssop plant that grows out of a wall. And about animals, and birds, and creeping things, and fish. And people came from all the nations, and from all the kings of the earth, to hear his wisdom.

One of them was a queen, who came from far away in the south with camels and spices and gold, to test him with hard questions. But that, I promise you, is a story for another night.

[pause]

And I have to tell you, honestly, because this show tries very hard to be honest, that Solomon's story does not end as brightly as it begins. The same Book of Kings that tells us about the dream at Gibeon tells us that when Solomon was old, his heart was turned away from the Lord, after other gods, and that the Lord was angry with him. The listening heart he had asked for as a young man, he did not keep listening with all his life. It is one of the saddest things in the Bible. And it is in the Bible, I think, partly so that none of us imagines that a gift from God, once given, looks after itself. A listening heart has to keep listening. Every day. Even when you are old and rich and everybody already agrees with you. Especially then.

But that is not where we leave him tonight. Tonight we leave him young. On a hillside at Gibeon, in a dream, saying, I do not know how to go out or come in. Please give me a heart that listens.

And there is a book in the Bible, the Book of Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, which speaks in Solomon's own voice and remembers that night. And it says, I prayed, and understanding was given me. I called on God, and the spirit of wisdom came to me. I preferred her to sceptres and thrones, and I counted riches as nothing beside her.

About a thousand years later, a teacher from Galilee stood in front of a crowd, and reminded them of the queen who came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon. And then he said, quietly, and something greater than Solomon is here.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the First Book of Kings, chapter three, which tells the dream at Gibeon and the judgment between the two women, with the Second Book of Chronicles, chapter one, which adds the tent of meeting and the bronze altar at Gibeon. We have told them straight, in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. The rest of Solomon's wisdom is from the fourth chapter of First Kings, and his turning away in old age from the eleventh.

The elders in the gate, the two or three witnesses, hearing the small and the great alike, and the boundary stone are from the Law of Moses in Deuteronomy. Jethro's advice is from Exodus, chapter eighteen. Absalom at the gate is from the Second Book of Samuel, chapter fifteen.

That lev shomea means a hearing or listening heart is a plain reading of the Hebrew. The Book of Wisdom speaks in Solomon's voice, and the lines we quoted are from its seventh chapter; the closing prayer is from its ninth. The words about something greater than Solomon are from Saint Matthew's Gospel, and the promise of wisdom to those who ask is from the Letter of James.

The Bible does not say what Solomon intended with the sword, or where he sat to judge, and we have not guessed. Scripture's date is reckoned in kings; about nine hundred and seventy years before Christ is the historians' estimate.

Hanan, Gaddi, the walk to Gibeon, the donkey, the widow's vegetable patch, and every word they say, are our own invention.

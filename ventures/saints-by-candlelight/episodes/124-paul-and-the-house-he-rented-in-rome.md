It is evening, in a small upstairs room in the biggest city in the world.

Outside the window the street is still noisy. Somebody is selling hot chickpeas. Somebody else is arguing about the price of them. Downstairs, in the wine shop, a man is singing, not very well.

Inside the room there is a lamp, and a table, and a short, bald man with a tired, kind face, talking quietly to a younger man who is writing very fast. And sitting on a stool beside the short man, with a light iron chain running from his own wrist to the short man's wrist, is a soldier. He is nineteen years old. He has been on duty for six hours. He is supposed to be bored.

And he is not bored. He is listening.

Because the short man has just said, write this down, and then, very slowly, so the young man can keep up: I have learned, in whatever state I am in, to be content.

And the soldier looks down at the chain. And then up at the short man. And thinks, he means it. He actually means it.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

If you have been with us over the last few nights, you have followed Saint Paul a long way. You have seen him arrested in the temple at Jerusalem, and taken down to the coast at night with four hundred and seventy soldiers round him. You have seen him kept for two years by a governor who would not make up his mind, and then heard him say, to the next governor, I appeal to Caesar. You have heard him tell a king about a light on the road to Damascus. And a while ago, on another night, you heard about the voyage that followed: fourteen nights in a storm, a ship broken up on a sandbank, and every single person on board carried safe to the shore of Malta.

And after Malta, in the spring, another ship. And then the long road north, up the coast of Italy, on foot, along the great stone highway the Romans called the Appian Way.

And here is a thing I love. Luke, who was walking with him, tells us that when the Christians in Rome heard Paul was coming, some of them walked out to meet him. Not to the edge of the city. Much further. Some came as far as a market town called the Forum of Appius, which is more than forty miles out. And some came to a place called the Three Taverns, which is about thirty. They walked a day or two down the road, just to walk the last part of it with him.

And Luke says that when Paul saw them, he thanked God, and took courage.

I find that a very comforting line. Saint Paul. Who had been shipwrecked, and beaten, and stoned, and had stood up in front of kings. He needed courage too. And the thing that gave it to him was not an angel, this time, or a vision. It was a group of people he had never met, standing at the side of a road, who had come a long way to say hello.

And then they all walked into Rome together.

Now, let me tell you about Rome. Because it is worth stopping at the gate for a moment and looking.

In the year sixty, give or take a year, Rome was the biggest city anybody had ever seen. Nobody knows exactly how many people lived there. The usual guess is somewhere near a million, which is about the size of a large modern city, packed into a space you could walk across in an afternoon. There were marble temples and enormous public baths and great sweeping squares. And between them, and round them, and piled on top of each other, there were people. Hundreds of thousands of ordinary people, living in tall, crowded apartment blocks that the Romans called islands.

An island, in Rome, was a block of flats. Four or five storeys, sometimes more. Shops on the ground floor, opening on to the street: a baker, a barber, a wine shop, a man who mended shoes. A rich family might have the nice rooms on the first floor. And the higher you went, the cheaper and smaller and draughtier the rooms became, until at the very top, under the roof tiles, there were people living in what we would call a cupboard.

A Roman poet called Juvenal, writing a generation or two after Paul, has a famous long grumble about the city. The rents, he says, are outrageous. You pay for one dark attic in Rome what would buy you a whole house with a garden in a country town. The buildings are held up by props and good luck, and the landlord, when a crack appears, simply paints over it and tells you to sleep well. Fires break out. Things fall out of upper windows on to your head. And you cannot sleep at night anyway, because carts were not allowed in the streets in the daytime, so all the deliveries happened after dark, and the wagons rumbled and the drivers shouted under your window till morning.

So if you have ever lived in a city flat with thin walls and a noisy street, you have, in a small way, lived like an ancient Roman. Congratulations. I am sorry about the neighbours.

And this was the city Paul had wanted to see for years. Years before, while he was still a free man, he had written a long letter to the Christians here. We still have it. It is the Letter to the Romans. And at the beginning of it he says, I long to see you. I am eager to preach the gospel to you who are in Rome. And at the end of it he says, I have wanted for many years to come to you, and I hope to see you on my way to Spain.

He had imagined arriving, I think, as a traveller. On his way somewhere else. With a bag over his shoulder.

He arrived with a chain on his wrist.

And yet he arrived. That is the thing. Back in Jerusalem, in the barracks, the night after he had stood before the council, the Lord had stood beside him and said, take courage, for as you have testified about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome. And here he was. It had taken more than two years, two governors, a king, a storm, a snake on Malta, and a very long walk. But here he was.

* * *

Here is what Luke tells us next. When we came into Rome, Paul was allowed to stay by himself, with the soldier who guarded him.

That is one sentence. But a whole way of life is folded up inside it.

You see, Paul was not put in a prison. Not a dungeon, not a cell. The Romans had several ways of holding someone who was waiting for trial, and the gentlest of them, for a prisoner who was not thought to be dangerous, was to let him live in a house, under guard. The guard was a soldier. And the soldier was fastened to him. A light chain, from the prisoner's wrist to the soldier's. Day and night. The soldiers took it in turns.

The philosopher Seneca, who was living in Rome at exactly this time, and was one of the most powerful men in the city, mentions this in one of his letters. He is talking about hope and fear, and how they always go together, and he says, it is just like the chain that ties the prisoner to the soldier who guards him. Where one goes, the other has to go too.

Seneca, by the way, had a brother called Gallio. And years before, when Gallio was governor in Corinth, some people had dragged Paul in front of him, and Gallio had thrown the whole case out of court before Paul had even opened his mouth, because he could not see what it had to do with him. So Paul, in a manner of speaking, had already met the family.

And so, in a rented room somewhere in this enormous city, Paul set up home. With a soldier on the end of his chain.

We do not know where the room was. Luke does not say. There are two old churches in Rome, today, which each have their own tradition that Paul lived on that spot, and I would not dream of choosing between them. All we can say is that it was somewhere in the city, and that it was his. He paid for it. The Greek words Luke uses at the end mean something like, at his own expense, or in his own rented place.

So let us imagine it. A couple of rooms, up a flight of stone stairs, in one of those islands. Not the top floor. A wine shop at street level. A window on to the street. A table, some stools, a bed, a lamp, and very soon, I imagine, a great many scrolls and writing tablets.

And a soldier.

Now, I want to introduce you to one of those soldiers, and I have to be honest with you from the start. Luke does not tell us any of their names. This one is our invention. We are going to call him Sextus. Sextus is a Latin name that means sixth, and Roman families sometimes named their sons by numbers when they ran out of ideas, so you can guess where he came in the family.

Sextus was nineteen, from a small town up in the hills of Italy, and he had joined the guard because it was well paid and the uniform was very smart. He had imagined, I think, that guarding the emperor's prisoners would be exciting. Standing at grand doorways. Escorting important people in chains through cheering crowds.

His first shift was a short bald Jew in a rented room above a wine shop, who was mostly writing letters.

Sextus sat down on the stool. A man of the guard clicked the chain shut, first on Sextus's wrist and then on the prisoner's. And the prisoner looked at him, and smiled, and said, good morning. I am Paul. I am afraid you are going to have a very dull day. Have you eaten? There is bread.

And Sextus, who had been told very firmly not to talk to the prisoners, said, I have eaten, sir. Thank you. And then wondered why on earth he had called a prisoner sir.

* * *

Luke tells us that three days after he arrived, Paul called together the leaders of the Jews in Rome.

I want to say a word about them, because it matters. There had been Jewish people in Rome for a long time, more than a hundred years. Many of the first had come as captives, after the Roman general Pompey took Jerusalem, and had been sold, and had later been set free. The philosopher Philo, who was Jewish himself, and visited Rome as an old man, tells us that they lived mostly across the river, on the far bank of the Tiber, and that they had their own houses of prayer, and kept the Sabbath, and sent offerings every year to the temple in Jerusalem. By Paul's time there were a great many of them, and several synagogues, each with its own leaders. Inscriptions from their burial places, found much later, still have the names of some of those congregations.

And they had had a difficult few years. About ten years earlier, the emperor Claudius had thrown the Jews out of Rome. The Roman historian Suetonius says, in one short, puzzling sentence, that it was because they kept quarrelling at the instigation of somebody called Chrestus. A great many people think that is a garbled Roman way of saying Christus, Christ, and that what had really happened was that the news about Jesus had arrived in the synagogues of Rome, and caused arguments, and the emperor had not bothered to find out who was arguing with whom, and had simply thrown everybody out. Luke mentions this too. It is how Paul first met his friends Aquila and Priscilla, in Corinth. They had just arrived from Italy, he says, because Claudius had ordered all the Jews to leave Rome.

When Claudius died, people drifted back. But you can imagine that the leaders of the Jewish community were a little wary of anything that might start that sort of trouble again.

And now here was a letter, or rather a message, from a man they had heard about, but never met. Saying, would you come and see me? I cannot come to you. I have a soldier attached to me.

And they came.

I like that very much about them. They did not have to. Paul was a prisoner, and a controversial one. But they climbed the stairs, a group of grave, bearded men in good cloaks, and they squeezed into the little room, and they sat down. And Sextus, on his stool, tried very hard to look like a piece of furniture.

And Paul said to them, and I give it in our own plain words, the way Luke reports it:

Brothers. I have done nothing against our people, or against the customs of our fathers. And yet I was handed over as a prisoner, from Jerusalem, into the hands of the Romans. And when the Romans had examined me, they wanted to let me go, because there was nothing in my case that deserved death. But the leaders in Jerusalem objected, and so I was forced to appeal to Caesar. Not because I have any charge to bring against my own nation. That is why I asked to see you and speak with you. Because it is for the sake of the hope of Israel that I am wearing this chain.

And he lifted his wrist a little. And the chain clinked. And Sextus's wrist went up with it, slightly, which spoiled the effect a bit, but nobody laughed.

Notice what Paul calls them. Brothers. And notice what he calls his faith. Not something new and foreign. The hope of Israel. The thing their fathers had prayed for. The Messiah. The resurrection of the dead. He was a Jew talking to Jews about the God of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, and he wanted them to know, before anything else, that he had not come to Rome to make accusations against his own people.

And the leaders answered him. And I think their answer is one of the most quietly fair things in the whole book. They said:

We have had no letters from Judea about you. And none of the brothers who have come here has reported anything bad about you, or said anything against you. But we would like to hear from you what you think. Because as for this sect, we know that everywhere, people speak against it.

Which is to say: we have no quarrel with you. We have heard nothing. We will not judge you on gossip. But we have heard plenty about this movement you belong to, and none of it good, and we would like you to explain it to us properly.

That is really all anyone can ask, I think. To be heard properly, by people who have not made up their minds already.

So they set a day. And they went home.

And when they had gone, Sextus, who had been forbidden to talk to the prisoner, found that he could not help it. He said, sir. What is a sect?

And Paul laughed, and said, it means a school. A party. A group of people who follow a particular teacher. They mean the people who follow Jesus of Nazareth.

And Sextus said, is he the one you are in trouble about?

And Paul said, yes. Although I would not quite put it like that. Sit down. Well, you are sitting down. You cannot really do anything else. Would you like me to tell you about him?

And Sextus thought about his orders. And about the six hours left on his shift. And said, all right.

* * *

On the day they had agreed, the Jewish leaders came back. And this time, Luke says, they came in greater numbers. Word had gone round. The little room must have been crammed. People on the stools, people on the bed, people on the floor, people standing in the doorway and craning in from the top of the stairs. The wine shop downstairs, I imagine, did good business.

And Paul talked to them, Luke says, from morning till evening.

From morning till evening. I would like you to think about that for a moment. Not a sermon. Not a speech. A whole day. Luke says he explained to them, and bore witness to the kingdom of God, and tried to convince them about Jesus, from the Law of Moses and from the Prophets.

So I think we have to picture it as a day of scrolls. Somebody unrolling the books of Moses. Somebody else finding the place in Isaiah, or the Psalms. Paul saying, look at this, and look at this, and see how it fits with this. And the others saying, but what about this? And how can you say that, when it says this? A day of questions, and answers, and more questions. A day of people leaning over a table in the lamplight, arguing about the Scriptures because they mattered more than anything else in the world.

If you have ever been part of a really good conversation, the kind that starts after breakfast and is somehow still going when somebody notices it is dark, you will know something of what that day was like.

And at the end of it, Luke says, very simply, some were convinced by what he said, and some would not believe.

That is all. Some were convinced. Some were not. Which, if you think about it, is how it has gone every single time anyone has told anyone about Jesus, from that day to this. Nobody is forced. Some say yes. Some say no. Some say, not yet. And some go home and think about it for years.

And they began to argue among themselves. And as they were getting up to go, Paul said one last thing. He quoted the prophet Isaiah. It is from a famous passage, the one where Isaiah sees the Lord in the temple, high and lifted up, with the seraphim singing holy, holy, holy. And the Lord tells Isaiah to go and speak to the people, and warns him that many will not listen. Paul said, again in our plain words:

The Holy Spirit was right, when he spoke to your fathers through the prophet Isaiah, and said: Go to this people and say, you will listen and listen, and never understand. You will look and look, and never see. For this people's heart has grown dull, and their ears are hard of hearing, and they have shut their eyes. So that they may not see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and turn, and I would heal them.

And then he said, so let it be known to you, that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles. They will listen.

Now I want to slow down here, because these are hard words, and they are easily misread.

The first thing to say is that these are not Paul's own words of anger. They are Isaiah's, and through Isaiah, God's, and they had been spoken to Israel by one of Israel's own prophets, seven hundred years before. The Lord Jesus himself quoted the very same passage, in the Gospels, about the crowds who heard his parables. It is the sorrowful cry of a prophet who sees that people can hear the truth and not take it in. And that is true of every people, Jews and Gentiles alike. It is true of me. I suspect, if you are honest, it is sometimes true of you.

And notice the last line. And turn, and I would heal them. Even in the warning, the thing God wants is to heal.

The second thing to say is that we know, from Paul's own letters, how he felt about his own people. Because a few years before this, he had written about it, in that same Letter to the Romans, to the very same city. He said, I have great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart. I could almost wish that I myself were cut off from Christ, for the sake of my brothers, my own flesh and blood. And then, a little later: Has God rejected his people? By no means! I am an Israelite myself. And: the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.

The Catholic and Orthodox Churches have always read these verses together. Paul's turning to the Gentiles in Rome does not mean that God had turned away from Israel. It never meant that. Paul himself would have been horrified by anyone who used his words to treat Jewish people badly, and to our great shame, over the centuries, people did. That is not what this scene is about. It is about a man who loved his people so much it hurt, at the end of a long day, watching some of them say yes and some of them say no, and sorrowing over it, and carrying on.

There is a verse that some old Bibles have here, verse twenty-nine, which says that the leaders went away arguing hard among themselves. It is missing from the oldest manuscripts, which is why many modern Bibles leave it out or put it in a footnote. But it is true either way, I think. They went away arguing. Down the stairs, past the wine shop, out into the dark street, still talking about the Law and the Prophets and a man from Nazareth. Which is not the worst way to go home.

And Sextus, who had been on the stool all day, because somebody had swapped his shift, sat in the quiet room after they had gone. And he rubbed his wrist. And he said, sir. Which ones were right?

And Paul, who was very tired, looked at him for a long moment. And said, that is a very good question. And it is not mine to answer for you. Come back tomorrow, and I will tell you why I think what I think. And then you must decide for yourself.

And Sextus said, I am not on tomorrow. I am on the day after.

And Paul said, then come back the day after.

* * *

And then Luke does something surprising. He has spent more than half of his whole book on Paul's journeys. He has given us storms and riots and speeches and shipwrecks and snakes, day by day, sometimes hour by hour. And now, in one sentence, he gives us two years.

He says: Paul stayed there two whole years, in his own rented house, and welcomed all who came to him.

Two whole years. Welcoming everyone.

Luke does not tell us who came. But Paul does. Because during his time as a prisoner, he wrote letters, and some of them are in the Bible, and they are full of names. Now, I have to be careful here. Paul was a prisoner more than once in his life, in more than one city. Which prison each of these letters was written from is something scholars still argue about. Some think one or two of them were written earlier, in a prison in Ephesus, or in Caesarea. But the old and traditional view, the one most Christians through the centuries have held, is that they were written here, in Rome, in these two years. And that is the view we will follow tonight, as long as you remember it is a view, and not something Luke tells us.

So. Who came up the stairs?

Luke, first of all. He had come all the way with Paul, through the storm. The beloved physician, Paul calls him. I like to think Luke was the one who saw to it that Paul ate properly and did not sit up writing too late, and was ignored, as doctors generally are.

Aristarchus, from Thessalonica, who had also been on the ship. Paul calls him my fellow prisoner, so perhaps, at some point, he shared the chain, or the house, or chose to. Some friends are like that.

Mark. And this is one of the loveliest things in the whole New Testament. Years and years before, on Paul's first journey, a young man called John Mark had set out with him and Barnabas, and then had gone home in the middle of it. And Paul had been so cross about it that when Barnabas wanted to take Mark again on the next journey, Paul refused, and he and Barnabas had such a sharp disagreement that they parted company. And now, here is Mark. In Rome. With Paul. And Paul writing to a church, if Mark comes to you, welcome him. Whatever had gone wrong between them had been mended. People can change. Friendships can be mended. Even with Saint Paul. Perhaps especially with Saint Paul.

Epaphras, from a little town called Colossae, far away in what is now Turkey, who had come with news of the church there, and who, Paul says, was always wrestling in his prayers for them.

Tychicus, who carried the letters. Demas. Others whose names we only know because Paul sent their greetings.

And Epaphroditus.

Epaphroditus came from Philippi, in northern Greece. The church at Philippi was the first church Paul had ever founded in Europe, years before, and they loved him. And when they heard he was a prisoner in Rome, they did what people who love you do. They took up a collection. And they gave it to Epaphroditus, and sent him all the way to Rome with it, overland and over the sea, a journey of weeks.

And Epaphroditus arrived, and gave Paul the gift, and stayed to help him. And then he fell ill. Very ill. So ill, Paul says, that he nearly died. And God had mercy on him, Paul says, and not only on him but on me as well, so that I should not have sorrow upon sorrow.

And when Epaphroditus was better, Paul sent him home, carrying a letter. And because Epaphroditus was worried, Paul tells us, that the people at home had heard he was ill and would be upset, which I think tells you exactly what sort of man Epaphroditus was. He nearly died, and his main worry was that his friends would be anxious.

That letter is the Letter to the Philippians. And if you only ever read one of Paul's letters, I think this is the one I would choose. Because it is the happiest thing he ever wrote. It was written by a man with a chain on his wrist, who did not know whether he would be set free or put to death, and it is full of joy. Rejoice in the Lord always, he says. Again I will say, rejoice.

And it is in that letter that he says the line our soldier heard in the cold open. I have learned, in whatever state I am in, to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to have plenty. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret, of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need. I can do all things in him who strengthens me.

* * *

Let me go back to Sextus for a moment. Because Paul says something else in that letter that I find very touching, and very funny.

He says: I want you to know, brothers, that what has happened to me has really helped to spread the gospel. Because it has become known throughout the whole imperial guard, and to everyone else, that I am in chains for Christ.

The whole imperial guard. Think about how that happened. Every few hours, a new soldier. Chained to Paul. Sitting in his room. Listening, whether they wanted to or not, to whatever he was dictating, or praying, or saying to whoever had come up the stairs. And then going back to the barracks, and the other soldiers saying, well? What is he like, the Jew with the letters? And the soldier saying, you would not believe it.

Paul could not go out to preach. So the Roman army, very kindly, sent him a fresh congregation every shift.

And at the very end of that same letter, Paul writes: All the saints greet you, especially those of Caesar's household.

Those of Caesar's household. That does not necessarily mean the emperor's family. It means the enormous staff of the palace. Slaves and freedmen, clerks and cooks and accountants and messengers. Hundreds and hundreds of them. And some of them, it seems, had become Christians. Right in the middle of the emperor's own house, the emperor being, at that time, a young man called Nero, who was not, to put it mildly, a promising candidate.

So let us imagine, and remember that this part is ours, that Sextus kept coming back. Every few days, his turn came round. And every time he sat down on the stool, Paul said good morning, and offered him bread. And over the weeks Sextus heard, in bits and pieces, between the letters and the visitors and the arguments, the whole story. A carpenter's son from Galilee. The sick healed. A cross. An empty tomb. A light on a road.

And one day he said, sir. You keep saying he is alive.

And Paul said, he is.

And Sextus said, then why does he not get you out of here?

And Paul looked down at the chain between them for a while. And then he said, I do not know. Perhaps he will. Perhaps he will not. For me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain, so either way I am not worried about it. But I will tell you something. If I were not chained to you, I would never have met you. So I cannot be entirely sorry about it.

And Sextus did not know what to say to that. So he said nothing. But he thought about it, on the long walk back to the barracks that night, under the noisy carts and the lit windows. And he did not stop thinking about it for a very long time.

* * *

There is one more visitor I want to tell you about. Perhaps the most surprising one of all.

His name was Onesimus. And he was a slave.

He belonged to a man called Philemon, a Christian, who lived in Colossae, the same far-off town that Epaphras came from. A church met in Philemon's house. And at some point Onesimus had left that house. The traditional reading of the letter is that he had run away, and perhaps taken something with him when he went. Paul does not quite say, and people have suggested other possibilities. But one way or another, Onesimus had ended up in trouble, and far from home, and somehow, in the enormous city, he had found his way to Paul. And Paul had told him about Jesus. And Onesimus had been baptised.

My son, Paul calls him. Whom I became the father of in my chains.

And now there was a problem. Because Onesimus belonged, by the law of the time, to Philemon. And Paul could not just keep him. So he sent him back. With a letter. A short letter, which is in the Bible, and which you can read in about three minutes. And it is one of the kindest, cleverest, most gently persuasive letters anybody has ever written.

He begins by telling Philemon how much he thanks God for him. Then he says, I could order you to do what is right. But I would rather ask you, as a favour, from an old man who is now a prisoner for Christ Jesus. And then he makes a pun. Because the name Onesimus, in Greek, means useful. And Paul says, he was once useless to you, but now he is useful, to you and to me.

He says, I am sending him back to you, and that is like sending my own heart. I would have liked to keep him here, to help me while I am in chains. But I did not want to do anything without your consent. Perhaps this is why he was parted from you for a while: so that you could have him back for ever, no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother.

And then Paul does a thing that always makes me smile. He takes the pen himself. The rest of the letter, like most of his letters, had been written by somebody else while he dictated. But here, he says, I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand. If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, put it on my account. I will pay it back.

You can almost see it. The short bald man, with the chain on his wrist, reaching across the table, taking the pen from the young secretary, and writing slowly, in his own hand. I will pay it back. Like signing for somebody else's bill.

And then a last line, which is pure Paul. And one more thing. Get a guest room ready for me. Because I hope, through your prayers, to be coming to see you.

Even in chains, he was planning his next visit.

We do not know for certain what Philemon did. But we can guess. The letter was kept, and copied, and treasured by the church, and read aloud in churches all over the world ever since. I do not think it would have been kept if Philemon had said no.

And there is an old tradition, which I will label as tradition, that Onesimus went on to serve the church, and some even think he became a bishop. The Orthodox Church honours him among the apostles of the seventy, and the Catholic Church honours him too. The runaway who was useless. Useful in the end, after all.

* * *

And all the while, Luke says, for two whole years, Paul did one thing.

He proclaimed the kingdom of God, and taught about the Lord Jesus Christ, with all boldness, and without hindrance.

That is the last sentence of the Acts of the Apostles. That is how the whole book ends. And the very last word, in the Greek, is a word that means unhindered. Without anything to stop him.

I want you to notice how strange and lovely that is. Here is a man in chains. Waiting for trial before Nero. Unable to go out of his rented room. Fastened, every hour of the day and night, to a Roman soldier. And Luke's final word about him is: unhindered.

Because the chain was on Paul's wrist. It was not on the word of God. Somewhere in one of his letters, the one to the Ephesians, Paul calls himself an ambassador in chains. An ambassador in chains is a contradiction, of course. Ambassadors are supposed to be the ones nobody can lay a hand on. But that is exactly what he was. Nobody had stopped the message. They had just given it a smaller room.

So let us go back, for the last time, to Sextus.

Let us say that it is the end of his very last shift with Paul. He has been posted somewhere else. Perhaps to the frontier, up on the cold river in Germany. Perhaps just to a different corner of the city. Soldiers did not get to choose.

And at the end of the shift the other soldier came up the stairs, the one who was taking over. And the chain was unlocked from Sextus's wrist and locked on to the new man's. And Sextus stood up. And for a moment he did not quite know how to say goodbye to a prisoner.

So Paul said it for him. He said, the Lord be with you, Sextus.

And Sextus said, and with you, sir. And then he blushed, because he had heard the visitors say it, and had not realised he had learned it.

And he went down the stairs, past the wine shop, out into the street. And the carts were rumbling, and somebody was selling hot chickpeas, and somebody was arguing about the price. And far away, up above the noise, he could see the lamp in the little upstairs window. And somebody was still talking, up there. And somebody else was still listening.

[pause]

It is time to slow down now. The lamps are low.

Luke does not tell us what happened at the end of the two years. He simply stops. People have wondered about that for a very long time. Some think he wrote the book at exactly that moment, and there was nothing more to tell yet. Some think he meant to write a third volume and never did. Some think he stopped there on purpose, because the story he was telling was not really the story of Paul. It was the story of the word of God, travelling from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth, and here it was, in the capital of the world, unhindered. That was the ending he wanted.

What the Church remembers, in its oldest traditions, is this.

A bishop of Rome called Clement, writing to the Christians of Corinth about thirty years later, says that Paul taught righteousness to the whole world, and reached the limits of the west, and bore witness before rulers, and so left the world and went to the holy place. Eusebius, the church historian, writing a long time afterwards, records the tradition that Paul was released after these two years, and travelled again, and was later arrested a second time, and that it was then, under Nero, that he gave his life. Some think the limits of the west means that he did get to Spain after all, as he had hoped. Nobody can say for certain. It is an old tradition, and a lovely one, but it is a tradition.

And there is a letter, the Second Letter to Timothy, which tradition places at the very end, written from a harder prison than this one. In it Paul says, I have fought the good fight. I have finished the race. I have kept the faith.

And then, the Church remembers, outside the city, not far from the road that runs down to the sea at Ostia, he went home to God.

A Roman Christian called Gaius, writing about the year two hundred, told someone who doubted it that if he walked out along the road to Ostia, he would find the trophies, the monuments, of the ones who founded that church: Peter's on the Vatican hill, and Paul's on the road to Ostia. And the great basilica that stands there now, Saint Paul Outside the Walls, was built over the place where Christians had always said he lay. You can still go there. It is very large, and very quiet, and full of light. There are long rows of round portraits all round the walls, one for every bishop of Rome. And under the altar there is a plain old stone with his name on it.

And the rented rooms above the wine shop are long gone. The islands fell down, as Juvenal said they would, or burned, and were built again, and fell again, many times over. The noisy street is a different noisy street. But the city is still full of people, more of them than anyone can count, and some of them, every day, are still climbing stairs to talk to somebody about Jesus. And some of them say yes. And some say no. And some say, not yet.

And the letters are still here. That is the thing I keep coming back to. The letter to the Philippians, full of joy. The letter to Philemon, with his own handwriting at the end. Written in a small room, with a chain clinking on the table, by a man who could not go anywhere. And they have gone everywhere. Every country in the world. Every language. Read aloud in churches this very week, somewhere, by somebody.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the last verses of the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twenty-eight, verses sixteen to thirty-one. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept. Paul's words to the Jewish leaders and theirs to him are in our own plain rendering, adding nothing. Verse twenty-nine is missing from the oldest manuscripts, and we have said so.

The visitors, Epaphroditus and his illness, the imperial guard, Caesar's household, the line about being content, and the whole story of Onesimus and the letter to Philemon are from Paul's own letters, which are Scripture too. Which prison each letter was written from is debated by scholars. We have followed the traditional view that they belong to these two years in Rome, and said that it is a view.

The Jews of Rome across the Tiber are in Philo. The expulsion under Claudius is in Suetonius and in Acts. The shared chain is in a letter of Seneca. The rents, fires and night-time carts are from the poet Juvenal, who wrote a little later.

Paul's release and later journey, his death on the Ostian road, and his tomb under the basilica are tradition, some of it very old. The later life of Onesimus is tradition too. The two Roman churches that claim his rented house each have their own local tradition.

The soldier Sextus, his shifts and his questions, the wine shop, the chickpeas and the room are our own invention.

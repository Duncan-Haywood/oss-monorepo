It is very early in the morning, so early that the sky is only just going grey, and a girl of about fifteen is standing at the door of a tent, holding the flap open with one hand and rubbing her eyes with the other.

She has come out because her little brother woke her. He is standing beside her now, in his nightshirt, with his bare feet in the sand, pointing.

All around the camp, as far as she can see, the ground has gone white.

It is not snow. It is far too warm for snow, and this is the desert. It is not frost, either, not quite. It is something small and fine and flaky, lying thin on the ground in every direction, between the tents, round the feet of the sleeping donkeys, on the stones, on the path, glittering a little as the light comes up.

Her brother looks up at her. What is it, he says.

And from the next tent, and the tent after that, and all along the row, she can hear other people coming out, and other voices, grown-up voices, saying the very same thing. What is it? What is it?

Nobody knows.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Let me tell you where we are.

If you were with us last time, you will remember Elim. After three days of walking through the wilderness of Shur with no water, and a pool at Marah that was too bitter to drink until the Lord showed Moses a piece of wood to throw in, the people of Israel came to a green place in the desert with twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees. And they camped there by the water. And Ziva's mother, at last, mended Ziva's tambourine, the one that had torn on the beach when Miriam was singing.

Well. You cannot stay at Elim for ever. It is a lovely place, but it is not where they are going. So the book tells us that they set out from Elim, and the whole congregation of the people of Israel came into the wilderness of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai.

Now, I know what you are thinking. The wilderness of Sin. It does sound rather like a place you would not want to take the children. But I promise you that it has nothing to do with the English word sin at all. It is a place name, in Hebrew, and nobody is quite sure what it meant. Some people think it is connected to the name of Sinai, the mountain they are heading for, which is a nice thought, because it would mean the wilderness of Sin is simply the wilderness on the way to the mountain. In any case, the people walking through it did not think of it as wicked. They thought of it as hot, and flat, and very large.

The book is also very exact about the date. It says they came there on the fifteenth day of the second month after they had left the land of Egypt. And the people had left Egypt on the fifteenth day of the first month, the morning after the Passover. So it is exactly a month. One month since the night they walked out of Egypt with their bread dough still in the kneading bowls, wrapped up in their cloaks on their shoulders, because they had been sent out in such a hurry that there was no time to let it rise.

A month is a long time to live on what you carried with you.

Think about it. You leave home in the middle of the night. You take what you can. Some flour, some grain, a bit of oil, a few dried things, the animals you own, and your bread, unleavened, flat, because there was no time. And then you walk. You cross the sea. You walk through Shur. You camp at Elim. And every day you eat a little of what you brought, and every day there is a little less, and nobody has had time to sow a field or harvest one, and there is nowhere to buy anything, because there are no shops in the wilderness. There are no farms. There is no market on a Tuesday. There are lizards, and thorn bushes, and an extraordinary amount of sky.

By the fifteenth day of the second month, the flour bags in Ziva's tent are nearly empty.

Her mother has been shaking them out into the bowl every evening, very carefully, getting every last bit out of the corners. Her father has been looking at the goats in a thoughtful way that the goats do not care for at all. And her little brother, who is four, has noticed that his bread is getting smaller, and has said so, at length.

So that is where we are. A month out of Egypt. Somewhere between the springs of Elim and the mountain of Sinai. A very great many people, and a very great many animals, and the food running out.

And you can probably guess what happened next.

* * *

What happened next is that people started to grumble.

The book's word is murmur. The whole congregation of the people of Israel murmured against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness. And I like the word murmur, because it is exactly the sound grumbling makes when a lot of people do it at once. Not shouting, not at first. Just a low, discontented hum, rising from all over the camp, like bees who have been told the garden is closed.

And what they said was this. I am going to give it to you just as the book does, because it is very human, and I think we have all said something like it.

Would that we had died by the hand of the Lord in the land of Egypt, they said, when we sat by the fleshpots and ate bread to the full. For you have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger.

Now, a fleshpot, in case you were wondering, is not anything exciting. It is just a pot with meat in it. A big pot on the fire, with a stew bubbling away, and people sitting round it with bread to dip in. That is what they were remembering. Sitting by the pot. Eating bread until they were full.

And I want to be fair to them, because it is easy to be hard on people in a story. They were hungry. Their children were hungry. That is a real and frightening thing, and anyone who has ever had to watch a child go hungry knows that it is not a small matter.

But I also want to point out, very gently, that they were remembering Egypt rather selectively.

In Egypt they had been slaves. They had made bricks all day under taskmasters. Pharaoh had told them to find their own straw and make just as many bricks as before. Their baby boys had been thrown into the Nile. And they had groaned under their slavery and cried out, and their cry had gone up to God. That was Egypt. That was the real Egypt.

But a month later, hungry in the desert, what did they remember? The stew. Sitting by the stew.

It is a funny thing, memory. It is a bit like those old photographs where everybody is smiling, and you look at them years afterwards and think, what a happy time that was, and you have quite forgotten that it rained the whole week and the car broke down and your aunt was not speaking to anyone. Memory keeps the stew and throws away the taskmasters. And when we are unhappy now, the past always seems to have been better than it was.

In Ziva's tent, her father has been listening to the murmuring from outside, and he is not joining in, but he is not exactly arguing with it either. He sits by the cold fire with his chin in his hands.

We did eat well in Egypt, he says. Some days.

Ziva's mother looks at him. Some days, she says. Some days we ate well, and some days you came home with your back bleeding.

Her father says nothing to that.

Her mother turns the empty flour bag inside out and shakes it over the bowl. A little white dust comes out. Not much.

My grandmother, she says, used to say that a hungry stomach has a very short memory and a very long tongue.

Ziva laughs, in spite of herself. Her father does too, a little. Her little brother, who does not understand the joke, laughs loudest of all, and then says, I'm hungry.

* * *

Now here is the thing about this story that I find most remarkable. It is not the bread from heaven, though we will come to that. It is that God did not tell them off.

You might expect him to. They had seen the plagues in Egypt. They had seen the sea open. They had seen the bitter water made sweet, only a few weeks before. And here they were, grumbling again, saying they wished they had died in Egypt. If I had been in charge, I think I might have said something rather sharp.

But listen to what the Lord actually says to Moses.

Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you. And the people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not. On the sixth day, when they prepare what they bring in, it will be twice as much as they gather daily.

That is all. No scolding. Just, I am going to feed them. And here is how it will work.

Notice that word, though. Test. That I may test them. It is not a trick, and it is not a trap. It is the sort of test a parent gives a child when they hand them a little responsibility. Here is enough for today. Do you trust me for tomorrow?

Moses and Aaron go and tell the people. And Moses, who has been on the receiving end of a great deal of murmuring, says something I think is rather wise. He says, at evening you shall know that it was the Lord who brought you out of the land of Egypt. And in the morning you shall see the glory of the Lord, because he has heard your murmurings against the Lord. For what are we, that you murmur against us?

And then he says it again, a little more plainly, in case anyone missed it. When the Lord gives you in the evening meat to eat, and in the morning bread to the full. Your murmurings are not against us, but against the Lord.

In other words, it was not Moses who brought them out of Egypt, and it was not Moses who was going to feed them. Moses was only the messenger. And it is a very great relief, I think, for anyone who is a leader of anything, a parent, a teacher, a person in charge of a parish fete, to remember that. You are not the one who makes the bread. You only pass it on.

Then Moses tells Aaron to call the whole congregation together. Say to the whole congregation of the people of Israel, come near before the Lord, for he has heard your murmurings.

And so they gather. The whole enormous crowd of them, out on the edge of the camp, Ziva and her family among them, her little brother up on her father's shoulders. Aaron begins to speak.

And as he is speaking, the book says, they looked toward the wilderness. And behold, the glory of the Lord appeared in the cloud.

I am not going to try to describe it. The book does not, and I do not think I could improve on its silence. There had been a pillar of cloud going before them ever since they left Egypt, by day, and a pillar of fire by night. And now, out over the empty desert, the cloud is shining with the glory of the Lord. And the whole crowd, which a few hours ago was murmuring like a hive of bees, has gone completely quiet.

Ziva's little brother, up on his father's shoulders, whispers, what is it?

And his father, who was sitting with his chin in his hands not long ago, whispers back, I think it is God.

* * *

And at evening, the book says, quails came up and covered the camp.

Now, a quail is a small, round, brown bird, a little bigger than a sparrow and a good deal plumper, with a short tail and a rather anxious expression. They live on the ground, mostly, and they run about in the grass, and when you startle them they whirr up into the air with a noise like someone shuffling a pack of cards very fast, and then come down again a little way off, as if they had not really meant to fly at all.

But twice a year, quails do something surprising. They migrate. Enormous numbers of them fly between Europe and Africa, in the spring and in the autumn, and a great many of them cross the very part of the world where our story happens, between the Mediterranean Sea and the deserts to the south. And because a quail is not, if we are honest, a very good long-distance flyer, they arrive exhausted. People who have watched them over the centuries have said that they come in low, just above the ground, and drop down wherever they can, too tired to go any further. People living along those coasts have caught them in nets.

So some people have suggested that what happened in the wilderness of Sin was something like that. A great flock of quails, coming in at dusk, tired from the sea, landing all over the camp.

That may well be how it happened. But I want to say clearly that the book is not telling us about a lucky coincidence. It is telling us that the Lord said, at twilight you shall eat meat, and at twilight there was meat. God made the quail, and God made the wind, and God made the evening. He can certainly use all three at once, at the moment he has promised. That is how he usually works, I think. Not by tearing up the world, but by using the world he made, at exactly the right time, for the people he loves.

In Ziva's part of the camp, the evening is full of brown birds.

They are everywhere. On the tents, between the tents, under the donkeys, running about on the sand in little panicked circles. Ziva's father, who had been looking so gloomy, is suddenly very busy indeed, and so is every other father in the row, and the children are running about with their arms out, and the whole camp is full of shouting and laughter and the whirr of small wings.

I will not tell you in detail what happens next, because quails are rather sweet birds, and this is a bedtime story. But I will tell you that that night, for the first time in a long while, there are fires burning all over the camp, and a smell of roasting drifting between the tents, and a great many people eating meat until their chins are shiny.

Ziva's little brother falls asleep with a drumstick still in his hand.

Ziva lies awake for a while afterwards, listening to the camp. It sounds quite different from the night before. There is still talking, but it is a different kind of talking. Somebody, a few tents away, is singing.

And she remembers that Moses said, at evening you shall know, and in the morning you shall see.

And she wonders what the morning will be.

* * *

And in the morning, the book says, dew lay round about the camp.

That in itself is a little unusual, in the desert. Deserts can have dew, on clear nights when the ground cools quickly, but it does not usually lie so thick and so wide. And when the dew had gone up, when the sun came up and the dew lifted off into the air, there on the face of the wilderness was a fine, flake-like thing, fine as hoarfrost on the ground.

That is what Ziva saw, at the beginning of this story, standing at the tent door with her brother pointing.

And that is when everyone said, what is it?

In Hebrew, that is man hu. What is it? And that, the book says, is where the name came from. The house of Israel called its name manna. Which means, more or less, the what-is-it. And I think that is one of the loveliest things in the whole Bible. The people were given the most wonderful food in the world, and they named it after the question they asked when they first saw it. For forty years, every time anybody said the word for their breakfast, they were really saying, what is this? It was a gift so strange that they never stopped being puzzled by it.

Moses tells them.

It is the bread which the Lord has given you to eat.

And then he tells them how to gather it, because there were rules, and the rules are rather wonderful too.

This is what the Lord has commanded, he says. Gather of it, every man of you, as much as he can eat. An omer apiece, according to the number of the persons whom each of you has in his tent.

An omer, I should explain, is a measure. A measure of dry things, like grain. The very last verse of this chapter, after all the drama, stops to tell us, in case we were wondering, that an omer is the tenth part of an ephah. Which is very helpful, if you happen to know what an ephah is. I find it rather endearing, that after the quails and the glory of the Lord and the bread from heaven, the book suddenly turns into a cookery book and gives you a conversion table. The usual guess is that an omer was a little over two litres. Roughly what a big jug holds. Enough to feed one person for one day.

So Ziva's family is four. Her father, her mother, Ziva, and her little brother. Four omers.

They go out with baskets and bowls, and so does everyone else, the whole camp spreading out over the white ground in the early morning light, stooping and gathering. The manna is small and light, like seeds, or little flakes. The book tells us elsewhere, in the Book of Numbers, that it was like coriander seed, and that it looked like bdellium, which is a sort of pale, pearly resin. And the Book of Exodus says it was white, and that the taste of it was like wafers made with honey.

Ziva tastes a little, right there on the ground. She cannot help it. It is sweet, and light, and it melts on her tongue, and it tastes, she thinks, like nothing she has ever had before and also like something she has always known.

Her little brother tries some too, and his eyes go very round, and he does not say I'm hungry for the whole rest of the morning, which in itself might count as a miracle.

* * *

Next to Ziva's tent there lives an old woman called Naamah. She is our own invention, so you will not find her in the book, but there must have been many women like her in that camp. She is a widow. She has no children with her. And she is so old and so bent that she walks with two sticks, and it takes her a very long time to stoop down, and a longer time to get up again.

Ziva sees her standing at the door of her tent, looking at the white ground with a basket on her arm, and not moving.

So Ziva goes over and says, shall I gather yours?

And Naamah says, oh, child, would you? I can do a little. But not much. And not fast.

So Ziva gathers for Naamah too. She is quick, and she is young, and she fills Naamah's bowl and brings it back.

Now here is the strange part, and this part is not our invention. This part is in the book.

Some of the people gathered more, the book says, and some gathered less. Some were quick and some were slow, some were young and strong and some were old and bent, some had big baskets and some had small bowls. But when they measured it with an omer, he that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack. Every person gathered according to what he could eat.

Everybody had enough. Exactly enough. Not too much, not too little.

In our telling, when Ziva's mother measures the family's manna into the omer measure, there are four omers, exactly, though Ziva was sure she had gathered more. And when Naamah measures the little bit she managed to scrape together with her two sticks, together with what Ziva brought her, there is one omer, exactly. No more. No less.

Naamah looks at it for a long time.

Well, she says at last. Well. I have lived for a very long time, and I have never been given exactly enough before. In Egypt it was always too little. She smiles, a slow, crinkly smile. And I suppose if it had ever been too much, someone would have taken it off me.

Hundreds of years later, Saint Paul remembered this verse. He was writing to the Christians in the city of Corinth, asking them to give money to help the poor Christians in Jerusalem, who were having a hard time. And he did not want anyone to give so much that they were left with nothing themselves. He only wanted it to be fair, he said. Your plenty at the present time should supply their want, so that their plenty may one day supply your want, that there may be equality. And then he quotes the Book of Exodus. As it is written, he who gathered much had nothing over, and he who gathered little had no lack.

I think that is a very beautiful use of the verse. Saint Paul looked at the manna and saw a picture of how Christians are meant to look after one another. The quick ones gathering for the slow ones. The young for the old. So that nobody has too much, and nobody goes without.

* * *

There was one more rule, and it is the one people found hardest.

Let no man leave any of it till the morning, Moses said.

Gather what you need for today. Eat it today. And tomorrow there will be more.

Now, you can see why that was hard. These people had been hungry. Some of them had been hungry for most of their lives. And when you have been hungry, and somebody gives you food, your first thought is not, how lovely, I will eat this today and trust that there will be more tomorrow. Your first thought is, I had better hide some, just in case.

And so some of them did. The book says so, plainly. They did not listen to Moses. Some of them left part of it till the morning.

And it bred worms and became foul.

I will not describe it any further than that, because you may be eating something as you listen. But you can imagine. A pot pushed into the back corner of a tent, a lid on it, and in the morning a smell, and something wriggling.

And Moses was angry with them.

In our telling, it happened in Ziva's tent too, in a small way. Not her father, and not her mother. Her little brother. Being four, and having been hungry, and having tasted the most delicious thing in his whole life, he had very quietly scooped up a small handful of manna at supper time and hidden it inside his blanket, rolled up in a corner of the cloth, for later.

In the morning there was a smell. And his mother unrolled the blanket. And there was the handful of manna, and it was not manna any more. It was something brown and sticky and wriggling.

The little boy looked at it in horror. Then he looked at his mother. Then he burst into tears.

And his mother, who might have been cross, and who did have to wash the blanket, which is no small thing in a desert, only picked him up and held him and said, oh, my love. You don't have to keep any. There'll be more. There'll be more in the morning. There'll be more every morning. That's the whole point.

And Ziva, watching, thought that perhaps the hardest part of the whole thing was not gathering the manna. It was believing that it would come again tomorrow.

* * *

Morning by morning they gathered it, the book says, each as much as he could eat. And when the sun grew hot, it melted.

So it came in the early morning, and it lay on the ground until the sun was up and warm, and then it was gone. Melted away into the sand, like frost does on a winter morning. If you wanted it, you had to get up early.

And here is a little thing I love. Many hundreds of years later, a wise Jewish writer, writing in Greek in the city of Alexandria, I think, put together a book that we call the Book of Wisdom, which Catholics and Orthodox read as Scripture. And in that book he looks back at the manna and wonders why it melted. Fire could not melt it, he says, for it was cooked and baked. But a single ray of the sun melted it away. And why? So that it might be known, he says, that we must rise before the sun to give you thanks, and must pray to you at the dawning of the light.

So the manna was teaching them something even by melting. Get up early. Say thank you. Before the day runs away with you.

I will be honest and say that I do not personally find this lesson easy. But I think it is a good one.

Then the sixth day came.

On the sixth day, the book says, they gathered twice as much bread. Two omers for each person. And all the leaders of the congregation came and told Moses, because I suppose they thought something had gone wrong. Everybody has gathered two omers, they said, instead of one. What does it mean?

And Moses said, this is what the Lord has commanded. Tomorrow is a day of solemn rest, a holy sabbath to the Lord. Bake what you will bake, and boil what you will boil, and all that is left over lay by to be kept till the morning.

So that afternoon, all over the camp, people were cooking.

The Book of Numbers tells us how they cooked manna, and it is very homely. They ground it in mills or beat it in mortars, and boiled it in pots, and made cakes of it. And the taste of it, says Numbers, was like the taste of cakes baked with oil.

In Ziva's tent, her mother is making cakes. Ziva is pounding manna in a little stone mortar with a stone pestle, and her brother is helping, in the sense that he is putting his fingers where the pestle is about to come down and having them moved out of the way about every ten seconds. Naamah has come over from next door, because she has more manna than her old arms can grind, and she is sitting in the shade of the tent giving instructions, which she is very good at.

Not so thin, she says. You'll burn them. A bit thicker. That's it. My mother made them just like that.

Ziva's mother says, your mother never made cakes from manna, Naamah.

No, says Naamah. But she would have done.

And the leftovers, this time, did not go bad. The book says so specially. It did not become foul, and there were no worms in it. Ziva's little brother checked several times during the night, just to be sure.

* * *

And then came the seventh day.

Moses said, eat it today, for today is a sabbath to the Lord. Today you will not find it in the field. Six days you shall gather it, but on the seventh day, which is a sabbath, there will be none.

This, by the way, is the first time the word sabbath is used anywhere in the Bible. God had rested on the seventh day at the very beginning, in the second chapter of Genesis, and blessed it, and hallowed it. But this is the first time that his people are told to keep it, and the first time it is called by that name. Not on a mountain, with thunder, but in the wilderness, over breakfast.

And some of the people, the book says, went out anyway, on the seventh day, to gather. And they found none.

In our telling, Ziva and her family are sitting outside their tent that morning, in the cool, eating the cakes that were baked the day before. And they watch a few people walking out past them, with baskets, looking at the ground. And they watch them walk further and further out, looking harder and harder, and then stand still. And then come walking back, with empty baskets, looking rather embarrassed.

The ground, that morning, is just sand. Plain, brown, ordinary sand.

And the Lord said to Moses, how long do you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws? See! The Lord has given you the sabbath. Therefore on the sixth day he gives you bread for two days. Remain every man of you in his place. Let no man go out of his place on the seventh day.

So the people rested on the seventh day.

And I want you to notice what the sabbath is here. It is not a burden. It is a gift. See, the Lord has given you the sabbath. For a month these people had walked and walked, and before that, in Egypt, they had worked every day of their lives, for masters who did not care if they ever stopped. And now God says, here. One day in seven, you will stop. I will feed you on that day too. You will not have to go out. You will not have to gather. You will not have to worry. Stay where you are. Rest.

In Ziva's tent, nobody quite knows what to do at first. Her father keeps getting up, as if he has forgotten something, and then sitting down again. Her mother picks up a piece of mending, and looks at it, and puts it down. They have never had a day like this before. None of them. Not once in their lives.

And then, in the afternoon, Ziva takes the mended tambourine out of its shawl. Not to dance. Just to hold. She taps it, very softly, with her fingertips, the way you might tap a table when you are thinking. And her little brother comes and sits beside her and listens. And Naamah, in the next tent, starts humming something old. And Ziva's mother lies down on the blanket with her eyes closed. And her father, at last, stops getting up.

That is the first sabbath. A whole people, in the middle of a desert, with nothing to do. Resting.

* * *

There is one more thing in the chapter, and it is a very small and very touching one.

Moses said, this is what the Lord has commanded. Let an omer of it be kept throughout your generations, that they may see the bread with which I fed you in the wilderness, when I brought you out of the land of Egypt.

And Moses said to Aaron, take a jar, and put an omer of manna in it, and place it before the Lord, to be kept throughout your generations. And Aaron placed it before the testimony, to be kept.

The testimony is the stone tablets of the law, which Moses had not been given yet, so the book is looking ahead here, just as it does in the very next verse, which jumps forward forty years at a stroke. But the point is simple. Keep some. One jar. Not to eat, not just in case, not because you do not trust God for tomorrow. Keep it so that your grandchildren can see it. So that they will know what he did.

It is the only manna that ever kept. Every other bit, if you left it overnight, went bad, except on the sabbath. But this one jar was kept, the book says, throughout the generations. Long afterwards, the Letter to the Hebrews in the New Testament remembers it, as one of the things kept in the Ark of the Covenant, a golden jar holding the manna, beside Aaron's staff that budded and the tablets of the covenant.

It is a bit like the way some families keep a christening gown, or a grandmother's wedding ring, or a little worn shoe. Something to bring out, now and then, and say: look. This is what happened to us. This is what we were given.

[pause]

And the people of Israel ate the manna for forty years, the book says, till they came to a habitable land. They ate the manna till they came to the border of the land of Canaan.

Forty years. Think of that. Every single morning, except the sabbath, for forty years, the ground around the camp went white. Children who were born in the wilderness grew up and had children of their own, and those children had never eaten anything else for breakfast. They would not have known what bread from a field was. To them, bread was something you went out and picked up off the ground in the early morning, and said thank you for.

And then one spring, forty years later, they crossed the river Jordan into the promised land, and kept the Passover there at a place called Gilgal, and the next day they ate the produce of the land, unleavened cakes and parched grain. And the Book of Joshua says that the manna ceased on the morrow, when they ate of the produce of the land. And the people of Israel had manna no more, but ate of the fruit of the land of Canaan that year.

It just stopped. Quietly. It had done what it came to do. I like to imagine some old woman, the last one who could remember Egypt, getting up early out of habit on that first morning in the new land, and going to the door of her tent, and looking out at the plain brown ground. And standing there for a while. And then saying thank you anyway.

Before they crossed, Moses spoke to the people one last time, in the long speeches of the Book of Deuteronomy. And he reminded them about the manna. He humbled you, he said, and let you hunger, and fed you with manna, which you did not know, nor did your fathers know, that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but that man lives by everything that proceeds out of the mouth of the Lord.

Those words were remembered for a very long time. Many hundreds of years later, a man was fasting in the wilderness, for forty days, and he was hungry, and the tempter came to him and said, if you are the Son of God, command these stones to become loaves of bread. And Jesus answered him with these very words, from Moses, about the manna. Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God.

The psalms remembered the manna too. He rained down upon them manna to eat, says Psalm seventy-eight, and gave them the grain of heaven. Man ate of the bread of the angels. And the Book of Wisdom, the same one that wondered why the manna melted, says that God gave his people the food of angels, bread ready to eat, providing every pleasure and suited to every taste. It says that the bread changed to suit the liking of whoever took it. Which is a lovely thought. That it tasted to each person like whatever they most loved. Wisdom says it as a reflection, looking back, and later Jewish teachers told it in many more ways besides. But I think anyone who has ever been given exactly what they needed, at exactly the right time, knows something of what it means.

And then there is a day, in the Gospel of John, by the Sea of Galilee, the day after Jesus had fed five thousand people with five loaves and two fish, when the crowd came looking for him again. And they said to him, our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness. As it is written, he gave them bread from heaven to eat.

And Jesus said to them, truly, truly, I say to you, it was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven. My Father gives you the true bread from heaven. For the bread of God is that which comes down from heaven, and gives life to the world.

And they said to him, Lord, give us this bread always.

And Jesus said to them, I am the bread of life. He who comes to me shall not hunger.

That is why Christians have always loved this story. Saint Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, explaining the Eucharist to the newly baptised in the fourth century, told them about the manna. It was a great and wonderful thing, he said, that God rained manna on the fathers, and they were fed every day with food from heaven. And yet, he said, those who ate it in the wilderness died. But the bread that Christ gives, whoever eats it shall never die. And Saint Thomas Aquinas, in a hymn he wrote for the feast of Corpus Christi, lists the old pictures of the Eucharist, the lamb of the Passover, and Isaac on the mountain, and the manna given to the fathers.

So it is not too much to say that, in a way, the story of the manna has never quite ended. Every morning, all over the world, somewhere, the bread is being given. And people are still gathering it. And still saying, what is it?

And there is one more echo, which you know already, whether or not you have ever thought of it this way. Give us this day our daily bread. A day's portion, every day. Not a cupboard full for the year. Not a pot hidden in the corner of the tent. Just enough for today. And trust for tomorrow.

Now, a few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Book of Exodus, chapter sixteen, told straight, in a plain rendering of the old words, with the look and cooking of the manna from the Book of Numbers, chapter eleven, and the day it stopped from the Book of Joshua, chapter five. Where the wilderness of Sin was is not known for certain, and depends on which way the people went, which is itself an open question. The date, the fifteenth day of the second month, and the sabbath, the double portion, the leftovers that went bad, and the jar kept before the testimony, are all in the book.

People have suggested natural explanations for both the quails and the manna. The quails, as I said, really do cross that part of the world in great tired flocks. And travellers have described a sweet, sticky substance that forms on certain desert shrubs, and have wondered whether it might be what the book means. That may tell us something about the kind of world God used. But it cannot account for what the book actually describes: enough for a whole people, every morning for forty years, exactly an omer each, nothing on the seventh day, and a double portion on the sixth that did not spoil. The book tells it as the Lord's gift, and so have we.

Moses's words in Deuteronomy are in chapter eight, and Jesus quotes them in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter four. Psalm seventy-eight calls the manna the bread of the angels. The Book of Wisdom's reflections are in chapter sixteen. Saint Paul's use of the omer is in his second letter to the Corinthians, chapter eight, and the golden jar in the Ark is in the Letter to the Hebrews, chapter nine. Jesus's words about the bread of life are in the Gospel of John, chapter six. Saint Ambrose's words are in his book On the Mysteries, and Saint Thomas Aquinas's hymn is the sequence Lauda Sion. They are told as their readings.

And Ziva, her mother, her father, her little brother and his hidden handful, old Naamah and her sticks, the baking, and every word they say, are our own invention.

So tonight, as you lie in the dark, I would like you to think about the manna, lying on the ground in the early morning, before anybody is awake.

It came while they slept. That is the thing. They did not do anything to make it come. They did not plant it or water it or pray for it with the right words. They went to sleep, grumbling, a good many of them, and in the night the dew fell, and the manna fell with it, and in the morning it was simply there. Enough for everyone. Enough for the quick ones and the slow ones. Enough for old Naamah with her two sticks. Enough for a little boy who had been hungry and was afraid of being hungry again.

And you do not have to keep any back. That is the other thing. You do not have to hide it in your blanket for later. There will be more in the morning.

So if you have been worrying tonight, about tomorrow, about what you will need and whether there will be enough, I hope you will remember the wilderness of Sin. Gather what you need for today. Let tomorrow look after itself. And go to sleep.

Somewhere in the desert, between Elim and the mountain, it is very late at night. The fires have burned down to red. The goats are asleep. Naamah is asleep, with her two sticks laid neatly beside her. A little boy is asleep with his blanket, clean now, pulled up to his chin, and nothing hidden in it at all. And a girl of fifteen is asleep beside him, with a mended tambourine wrapped up in a shawl by her head.

And outside, very quietly, on the sand, the dew is beginning to fall.

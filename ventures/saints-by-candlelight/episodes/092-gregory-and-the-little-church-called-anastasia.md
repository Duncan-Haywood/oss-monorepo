Imagine a city by the sea, a very long time ago, on a grey morning at the end of November.

The sky is low and heavy. The streets are packed with people, more people than you have ever seen in one place, standing on steps and walls and the roofs of porches, all of them looking the same way. Soldiers line the road. And down the middle of the road comes a procession.

At the front walks an emperor, a tall, strong, soldierly man in purple. And beside him, trying very hard to keep up, walks a small, thin, stooping man in a plain dark robe. He is about fifty. He is not well. He has a bad chest and bad joints and, at this exact moment, a very bad feeling about all of this.

Some of the crowd are cheering. A great many of them are not. The little man keeps his eyes on the paving stones. He is praying, mostly, and partly he is wishing he were somewhere else, somewhere with a garden and a bench and no crowds at all.

They reach the steps of the great church. And just as they go in through the doors, he will tell us later, the clouds over the city tear open, and the sun comes through.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

A few nights ago on this show we met Saint Basil the Great, who ran a soup kitchen in Cappadocia. Then we met his big sister, Saint Macrina, who baked the bread and told him to stop showing off. Tonight we meet the third of the three, the one who was not a member of their family but who might as well have been. Basil's best friend. Gregory.

There are, I should say straight away, a confusing number of Gregories in this part of the world at this time. Basil's younger brother was also called Gregory, Gregory of Nyssa, and he was a bishop and a great saint and a writer too. Tonight's Gregory had a father who was also called Gregory, and who was also a bishop. If people in fourth century Cappadocia had a shortage of anything, it was not wheat, or sheep, or bishops. It was names.

So we call our Gregory by his home town. Gregory of Nazianzus.

Nazianzus was a small town in the south-west of Cappadocia, which is in the middle of what is now Turkey. It is high country, dry and bright in summer and bitterly cold in winter, with wide bare hills and strange soft rocks that the wind has carved into towers and chimneys. Gregory's family had an estate just outside the town, at a place called Arianzus. They had fields, and vineyards, and servants, and enough money that nobody ever had to worry about the next meal.

His father, the elder Gregory, had not grown up a Christian. He had belonged to a small religious group of the time that worshipped one supreme God, but kept some odd rules about lamps and the sabbath, and did not believe in Christ. His wife Nonna was a Christian, and a very determined one. She prayed for her husband for years, patiently, and in the end he was baptised. And then, in the way of the fourth century, where things moved quickly, he was made a priest, and then bishop of Nazianzus, and he stayed bishop there for more than forty years.

Nonna, Gregory tells us, had prayed for a son before he was born, and promised that if God gave her one, she would give the boy back to God. Rather like Hannah in the Bible, who prayed for Samuel. And God gave her one, about the year three hundred and twenty-nine, and she told him so, often. You can imagine how that felt for a small boy. It is one thing to be told you are loved. It is another to be told, at breakfast, that you were promised to God before you were born, and please finish your porridge.

He had an older sister, Gorgonia, who grew up to be a wise and generous woman with a family of her own, and whom the Church remembers as a saint. And he had a younger brother, Caesarius, who was the practical one, and became a doctor, a very good one, and went to work at the court of the emperor. Gregory was the bookish one in the middle. He was clever, sensitive, easily hurt, funny when he wanted to be, and he loved, more than anything in the world, words. Poems. Speeches. Arguments. Books.

And he loved quiet. This is the important thing to know about Gregory, and it is the thing that makes his life so very funny, and so very touching. All he ever wanted was a quiet room and time to think and pray. And all his life, God and his friends and his father kept hauling him out of the quiet room and putting him in charge of things.

* * *

When Gregory was a young man, his parents sent him away to study, which in those days meant a long tour of the best schools in the Roman world. He went to Caesarea, the big city of Cappadocia. He went to the other Caesarea, on the coast of Palestine. He went to Alexandria in Egypt, the city of the great library. And then he set off for the most famous university town of them all, Athens.

He went by sea, in the wrong season, which was a mistake. Ancient sailors did not like to be at sea in late autumn, and they were right not to.

Somewhere off the island of Cyprus, a storm caught the ship. And this was not an ordinary storm. Gregory says it went on for twenty days. Twenty days of grey water and screaming wind, of the ship rolling so far over that the sailors thought every wave would be the last. The water casks were smashed, and they ran short of fresh water. Everybody on board was sure they were going to die.

And Gregory was frightened in a particular way. He had been brought up a Christian. He believed. But he had not yet been baptised. In those days it was quite common to put off baptism until you were grown up, sometimes until you were very old. Gregory, lying in the bottom of a heaving ship, suddenly realised that he might be about to drown without ever having been baptised at all.

So he prayed. He prayed harder than he had ever prayed. And he made a promise. If God brought him through this, he would give his whole life to God. Not part of it. All of it.

"You promised me already," you can imagine his mother saying, if she had been there. "Before you were born." But she was not there, and the promise this time was his own.

The storm blew itself out. The ship came limping through. And Gregory arrived at last in Athens, wet, thirsty, rather thin, and absolutely certain about what his life was for.

He did not forget the promise. That is one of the things I like best about him. He was a man of many moods, and many doubts about himself, and many complaints. But he never once wavered about that.

* * *

Athens in those days was a university town, and like all university towns it was full of young men who were very pleased with themselves. There were rival schools, with rival teachers, and the students of each school went down to the harbour to meet the new arrivals off the ships and try to recruit them, rather like clubs at a modern freshers' fair, only with more shouting. And new students were put through a sort of initiation. They were marched through the streets to the public baths, with everyone jeering and pretending to fight them, until they were finally let in and given their student gown. It was all meant in fun, mostly.

A little while after Gregory arrived, a new student came over from Cappadocia. A tall, serious, rather proud young man, who did not enjoy being jeered at, and whom Gregory already knew a little from home. His name was Basil.

Gregory, who had been in Athens long enough by then to know the ropes, quietly arranged for Basil to be let off the worst of the initiation. And Basil, who did not often let people help him, noticed. And they became friends.

They became, in fact, the kind of friends that people write about. Gregory, years later, wrote that it was as if they had one soul between two bodies. They shared rooms. They shared meals. They shared their books and their plans. Gregory said they knew only two roads in the whole of Athens. One road led to the church, and to the holy people they met there. The other led to their teachers. The roads to the parties and the theatres and the places where students went to get into trouble, they simply did not know. They had never learned the way.

I should say that Gregory wrote this many years later, in a sermon, and it is the kind of thing you say about the friends of your youth. It may have been a little more complicated at the time. But I believe the heart of it.

There was another student in Athens in those years, by the way, a young prince called Julian, a cousin of the emperor. A quiet, intense young man with a beard. He would later become emperor himself, and turn against the Christian faith, and try to bring back the old gods of Rome. Gregory said afterwards that he had not liked the look of him even then. Which is the sort of thing people say afterwards. But he may well have been right.

Basil left Athens first. Gregory stayed a while longer, and the students and teachers begged him to stay for good and teach. But he had made his promise in the storm, and in the end he too came home to Cappadocia, and was baptised at last.

And then he went to visit his friend.

* * *

Basil had decided to become a monk. He had found a place in Pontus, in the green hills of the north, on the river Iris, across the water from the house where his mother and his sister Macrina were living their life of prayer, which you heard about in Macrina's episode. He wrote to Gregory and described it in glowing terms. A mountain. A thick forest. A river full of fish. Wild flowers. Birdsong. Peace.

So Gregory came.

And Gregory, who had a wicked sense of humour, wrote back afterwards a pair of teasing letters, which we still have, describing the very same place as he had found it in fact. In our own words, it went something like this.

"My dear Basil. I remember your little paradise very well. I remember the hut, with no proper roof and no proper door. I remember the fire, which gave smoke but no warmth. I remember the walls, which we were always mending. I remember the garden, which was not a garden at all, but a heap of rocks and thistles. I remember the bread, which was so hard that our teeth slid off it. I remember hauling great stones about with our own hands, which are still blistered. Your river is very fine. Your mountain is very fine. But I would like to be quite clear that I nearly died there."

And then, in the second letter, having had his fun, he wrote quite differently. He said that what he missed was not the place. It was the psalms sung together through the night, and the hours of study, and the work done side by side, and the life they had shared. He said he would give anything to have those days again.

That, I think, is friendship. You can tease someone about their awful bread, and in the same breath tell them that those were the best days of your life.

The two of them, in those months, are said to have put together a collection of the best passages from the great Christian teacher Origen, which they called the Philocalia, which means the love of beautiful things. And they prayed, and argued, and walked, and were as happy as either of them would ever be.

It did not last. It never does. Gregory's father was getting old, and he needed help.

* * *

Here is where Gregory's life starts to go wrong, in the most wonderful way.

He went home to Nazianzus to help his old father. And one Christmas, or near it, without any warning at all, in front of the whole congregation, his father ordained him a priest.

In those days this sometimes happened. The people would see a good man in the church and simply grab him and make him a priest, whether he wanted it or not. It happened to Saint Augustine, and to Saint Ambrose, and to quite a few others. Gregory's father had decided that his son was needed, and that was that.

Gregory did not want to be a priest. He did not feel worthy. He was frightened of the responsibility. And above all, he wanted his quiet life.

So he ran away.

He ran all the way back to Pontus, to Basil and the hut with no roof. And for some months he hid there, and thought, and prayed, and tried to decide what to do.

And at last he understood something. He understood that his own wishes were not the most important thing. The people at home needed a priest. His father needed him. And God, who had brought him through the storm, had perhaps not done so in order that he could spend the rest of his life sitting comfortably in a hut.

So he came home. He came back at Easter. And on Easter day he stood up in his father's church and preached his first sermon as a priest. It begins, as near as we can put it in English, like this. "It is the day of the Resurrection, and a good day to begin."

And then he wrote a long, long letter, which we call his second sermon, explaining to everyone why he had run away, and why he had come back. In it he set out what a priest is for, and how frightening and wonderful it is to care for people's souls, which he called the art of arts and the science of sciences. It is one of the first books ever written on what it means to be a pastor, and priests have been reading it ever since, often, I suspect, while wishing they could run away to Pontus too.

* * *

Now, the next thing that happened is the sad bit in the middle of the story, and I am going to tell it quickly and gently.

Some years later, the emperor split the province of Cappadocia in two, for reasons of taxes and government, and that started a quarrel between Basil, who was by now the bishop of Caesarea, and another bishop, about whose churches were whose. Basil needed bishops he could trust in the little towns along the border. So he made his best friend Gregory bishop of one of them.

The town was called Sasima.

Gregory, who as you know was a writer, wrote a description of Sasima that I think is one of the finest pieces of complaining in all of ancient literature. Here it is, in our own words.

"There is a stopping place on the main road through Cappadocia, where the road splits three ways. It has no water. It has no grass. It has no proper people, only travellers passing through and strangers and vagrants. It is a miserable, cramped little village. The whole place is dust, and noise, and wagons, and wailing, and groaning, and tax collectors, and chains. That is my church. That is Sasima."

Gregory never went to live there. He could not bring himself to. He felt that his friend had used him, put him into a dusty hole as a move in a game of church politics, without asking him what he wanted. And Basil, who was a very great man but not always a very tender one, felt that Gregory was letting him down when he most needed help.

They wrote each other some sharp letters. The friendship was bruised. It was never quite as easy between them again.

I tell you this because it is true, and because it is a comfort. Saints are not people who never fall out with their friends. Saints are people who keep loving their friends after they have fallen out with them. And Gregory did. When Basil died, years later, Gregory gave a great sermon about him, the longest and most loving sermon he ever gave, and it is from that sermon that we know about the one soul in two bodies, and the two roads in Athens. He did not pretend the quarrel had not happened. But he did not let it have the last word.

* * *

And then, one after another, the people Gregory loved most began to go home to God.

His brother Caesarius died, still a young man. His sister Gorgonia died, peacefully, surrounded by her family. Gregory preached at both their funerals, and the sermons survive, and they are full of love and grief.

Then his old father died, at a great age, still bishop after more than forty years. And his mother Nonna died soon afterwards. She died, he tells us, in church, at prayer, holding on to the holy table. Which, if you have been listening, will not surprise you about Nonna at all.

Gregory was left alone. He was sick. He was tired. He was grieving. And he did the thing he always wanted to do. He went away to be quiet.

He went south, to a town called Seleucia, near the coast, where there was a famous shrine to an early Christian woman called Saint Thecla. And he lived there quietly for several years, praying, and resting, and writing. And for a while, it seemed, he had finally got his quiet life.

He had not.

* * *

Now we come to the great adventure of Gregory's life. The part that made him famous.

At this time, the capital of the Roman world in the East was the great city of Constantinople, on the Bosphorus, the narrow strip of sea between Europe and Asia. It was huge, and rich, and loud, and full of churches.

And almost every one of those churches was held by people who believed something about Christ that Gregory and Basil believed was badly wrong. They were called Arians, after a priest called Arius. They taught that the Son of God was not truly God in the way that the Father is God, but something less, a creature, the first and greatest of all creatures, but a creature. The great Council of Nicaea, more than fifty years before, had said no, the Son is true God from true God, one in being with the Father. But emperors had come and gone, and some of them had favoured the Arians, and in Constantinople the Arians had all the churches and all the bishops.

The Christians who held to the faith of Nicaea in Constantinople were a small, frightened little group. They had no church and no bishop. And they wrote to Gregory, at his shrine in the south, and asked him to come and be their shepherd.

He did not want to go. Of course he did not. But he went.

* * *

He arrived in Constantinople in the year three hundred and seventy-nine, a small, ill, shabby man from the provinces, with a bald head and a bent back and very plain clothes. The fashionable people of the capital took one look at him and laughed.

He had no church. So the little congregation met in a house, a big house lent by people who shared their faith. They made one room of it into a chapel. And Gregory gave that little house-church a name. He called it the Anastasia. It is a Greek word. It means the Resurrection. Because, he said, it was here that the true faith in that city would rise again from the dead.

Now let me introduce you to someone we have made up. I want you to picture an old woman in that house. We will call her Melitine. She is not in Gregory's poems. She is our invention. But houses like that always had someone like her, and I like to think the Anastasia did too.

Melitine has been sweeping the floor of that house for thirty years, since before it was a church. She has seen bishops come and go, and the Arians come and go, and she has opinions about all of them. She keeps the lamps filled. She knows which bit of the roof leaks.

On the first evening, she watches the new bishop come in, shaking the rain off his cloak, and she leans on her broom and looks at him for a long time.

"Is that it?" she says to the deacon beside her. "Is that what they sent us? He looks as if a good gust of wind would carry him off."

"He's supposed to be very clever," says the deacon, doubtfully.

"Clever," says Melitine, and sniffs. "We've had clever. What we need is a man who'll stay."

And Gregory, who was nearer than she thought, and had very good ears, turned round and smiled at her.

"Mother," he said, "I promise you this. I'll stay as long as they let me. Where does the roof leak? I'll stand somewhere else."

* * *

It was not easy. In those first months, the little congregation was mocked in the streets. One Easter night, Gregory tells us, as they were keeping the great vigil and baptising the new Christians, a mob broke in and threw stones, and people were hurt, and Gregory was hauled before the magistrates as if he had started the trouble himself.

And it was not only the Arians who caused him grief. There was a man called Maximus, who came to Constantinople and made great friends with Gregory. Maximus was a philosopher of the kind called a Cynic, who went about in a rough cloak with a staff and long hair, to show he did not care about worldly things. Gregory was charmed by him, and praised him in a sermon. And then one night, while Gregory was ill in bed, Maximus brought in some bishops from Egypt, crept into the Anastasia, and tried to have himself secretly made bishop of Constantinople in Gregory's place.

They were caught at it at dawn. The people came in and found them halfway through, and threw them out, and the bishops had to finish the job, Gregory says, in the house of a flute player, which I think he wanted us to understand was not a very holy place to make a bishop. And in his poem, many years later, Gregory, who was still a bit cross about it, spends a surprising number of lines making fun of Maximus's hair. Which had been, he hints, rather carefully curled, for a man who said he did not care about appearances. I do not think this is Gregory's finest hour. But it is a very human one, and I am fond of him for it.

* * *

And yet, in that little house with the leaking roof, something extraordinary happened.

Gregory preached. And people came to listen. At first a handful. Then dozens. Then hundreds, crammed into the rooms and out into the street, standing on tiptoe at the windows.

Because the funny little man from the provinces could preach like nobody they had ever heard. He was the best-trained speaker of his generation. He had spent ten years in Athens learning how to make words sing. And now he put all of it to work explaining the faith. Who God is. Who Christ is. What the Trinity means.

You need to understand that in Constantinople at this time, everybody had an opinion about theology. Everybody. Gregory's friend, Basil's brother, Gregory of Nyssa, has a wonderful description of it. In our own words, he says, if you go into a shop in that city and ask the price of a loaf, the baker will tell you that the Father is greater and the Son is less. If you ask whether your bath is ready, the bath-keeper will tell you that the Son was made out of nothing. Everybody was arguing about God. The bakers. The money-changers. The men who sold old clothes.

And into the middle of that, in the summer of the year three hundred and eighty, Gregory gave a set of five sermons that we still have, and that we call the Theological Orations. In them he explained, as clearly and beautifully as anyone ever has, the faith of Nicaea. That the Father is God, and the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God. Not three gods, but one God. One nature, three persons.

He did not pretend it was easy. He said again and again that God is far beyond anything our minds can hold. He said that the more you think about God, the more you realise how little you can say. But he also said that we must speak, and speak truly, because the faith matters. And because what we say about God shapes how we pray, and how we live.

There is one line of Gregory's, written a little later in a letter, that the Church has treasured ever since. He was writing about Christ, and about how Christ was truly God and also truly a man, with a real human body and a real human mind and a real human soul. And he said, very simply, what was not taken up was not healed. That is, if Christ did not become fully human, every part of us, then the parts he did not become are not saved. But he did become fully human. So all of us can be healed. Every bit.

The people of Constantinople listened. And they began to call him the Theologian. Which is what the Orthodox Church still calls him today. Saint Gregory the Theologian. It is a title given to only three people in all of Orthodox history. One is Saint John, who wrote the fourth Gospel. One is a monk called Symeon, who lived much later. And one is Gregory.

* * *

Melitine, our invented old woman, was there, of course, for every sermon. She always stood at the back, by the door, leaning on her broom, because she said that if she sat down she would not get up.

"Did you understand it?" the deacon asked her, after one of the great sermons, when the crowd had gone.

"Not all of it," she said. "Nobody understands all of it. That's rather the point, isn't it? He says so himself."

"Then why do you come?"

Melitine thought about it, sweeping a little heap of mud out of the door.

"Because when he talks about God," she said at last, "you can tell he's talked to Him. That's the difference. Clever, we've had. But that one prays."

* * *

Then, in November of the year three hundred and eighty, everything changed.

A new emperor had come to the throne. His name was Theodosius, a soldier from Spain, and he held to the faith of Nicaea. He rode into Constantinople, and he called in the Arian bishop of the city, and he told him, quite politely, that he could either accept the faith of Nicaea or leave. The Arian bishop, to his credit, did not pretend. He left.

And so, on that grey morning at the end of November, which you heard about at the beginning, the emperor came to fetch Gregory from his little house-church, and walked beside him through the crowded streets, to lead him into the great church of the city.

It was not a joyful walk for Gregory. He was ill. Half the city was angry. Soldiers lined the road. People glared from the rooftops. And Gregory says that he walked, bent and pale, praying all the way, half dead with fear.

And then, as he tells it in his poem, as they reached the church and went in, the clouds that had hung over the city all morning broke apart, and the sunlight came pouring down through the doors and the windows, and the people who had been sullen began to cheer, and shout that he should be their bishop.

That is Gregory's own account, many years later, and I tell it as his. He did not say the sun was a miracle. He said it felt like a sign. And I think we can let him have that.

* * *

There is another story Gregory tells from those days, and it is my favourite of all.

One day, not long after, when Gregory was ill in bed, a young man came to see him. A pale young man, who would not meet his eyes. Gregory's friends found out later that he had been sent to kill him. He had a weapon hidden in his clothes. He had come to the bishop's bedside to murder him.

And he could not do it.

He stood by the bed, looking down at this small, ill, kind old man, and he burst into tears. He fell on his knees and confessed everything, and begged to be forgiven.

And Gregory forgave him. Just like that. He did not call the guards. He did not have him punished. He blessed him and let him go. And all the city, Gregory says, heard about it, and some of the people who had hated him began to change their minds.

You can see why this is my favourite. It is the whole gospel in one small room.

* * *

In the next year, three hundred and eighty-one, the emperor called a great council of bishops in Constantinople, to settle the faith once and for all. It is counted now as the second of the great councils of the whole Church, after Nicaea. It is the council the Church remembers for the creed that we say at Mass and the Divine Liturgy every Sunday, with its words about the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life.

At first it was presided over by a gentle old bishop called Meletius of Antioch, a holy man much loved by everyone. But Meletius died, suddenly, while the council was still meeting. And Gregory, who was now the bishop of Constantinople, found himself in charge.

And it was a disaster. Not the creed. The creed was wonderful. But the bishops.

They argued about who should replace Meletius at Antioch. Gregory wanted to make peace, and suggested a way to heal a split there that had been going on for years. The bishops would not hear of it. Then more bishops arrived, from Egypt, who had never liked Gregory, and they pointed out that there was an old rule that a bishop should not move from one city to another. And Gregory, they said, was still officially the bishop of Sasima. That dusty crossroads he had never been to. So he could not be bishop of Constantinople at all.

Gregory looked at them all. A room full of bishops shouting at each other, like a flock of jackdaws, he said, or a swarm of wasps. And he was old, and ill, and tired. And he realised that he did not want to fight them. He had come to Constantinople to bring the faith back to life, and it had come back to life. He had never wanted to be important.

He said to them something like this. "If I am the cause of this storm, then do with me what the sailors did with Jonah. Throw me overboard, and let the sea be calm."

And he resigned.

* * *

Before he left, he gave one last sermon, in the great church, in front of the bishops and the emperor and the whole city. And at the end of it, he said goodbye. Not just to the people. To everything.

In our own words, it went something like this.

"Farewell, my Anastasia, my little church of the Resurrection, where the faith rose again. Farewell, great church, our new possession. Farewell, you churches all through the city, like jewels on a necklace. Farewell, Apostles, whose church I loved. Farewell, my bishop's chair, that I held for so short a time, and so many envied. Farewell, you choirs and singers of psalms, and you all-night vigils. Farewell, you lovers of my sermons, who crowded in, and the pens of those who wrote them down. Farewell, you emperor and your court. Farewell, great city, that loves Christ. Farewell, East and West, for whom I fought, and by whom I was attacked. And above all, farewell, you angels who guard this church, and who guard my coming and my going. And Holy Trinity, my study and my glory, may you be kept safe here, and may you keep these people safe. My children, keep what I gave you. Remember the stones they threw at me. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you all."

And then he walked out of the great church, and down the steps, and back to the little house.

* * *

Melitine was at the door of the Anastasia when he came to collect his few things. She had her broom.

"So they've thrown you over the side," she said.

"I jumped," said Gregory. "It's quite different."

She looked at him for a long time, the way she had on the first night.

"You said you'd stay as long as they'd let you," she said.

"I did. And I have. They won't let me any longer." He smiled at her. "And the faith is safe now, Mother. That's what I came for. It doesn't need me."

"Hmph," said Melitine. And then, because she was an old woman who had swept that floor for thirty years and did not care who saw, she put her arms around the bishop and held him, very tight, and told him to wrap up warm on the road, because his chest was terrible.

That bit, as I say, we made up. But I think it is true in the way that matters.

[pause]

Gregory went home.

Home to Cappadocia, to the high bare hills and the dry bright air, to the estate at Arianzus where he had been born. For a while he looked after the church at Nazianzus, because there was no bishop there, until a good man was found to take it over. And then, at last, after all those years of being hauled out of his quiet room, he was allowed to go back into it.

He had a garden there, with trees and a spring of water, and he could sit in the shade and think. He was often ill, and in pain. But he was where he had always wanted to be.

And he wrote. Oh, how he wrote. Poems, mostly. Thousands and thousands of lines of poetry, more than almost any other writer of the early Church. Poems about God, and about the Trinity, and about the soul. Poems of prayer. Poems about his own life, including that long one, On His Own Life, that we have been listening to all evening. Poems about his friends, and his family, and his illnesses, and his regrets. Little verses written as epitaphs for people he had loved. He wrote letters, too, hundreds of them, to friends and to strangers, warm and witty and sometimes a little sharp.

One Lent, he decided to keep complete silence. Forty days without speaking a single word. He said that he had spoken too much in his life, and too many words had come out of his mouth that would have been better left unsaid, and he wanted to learn to hold his tongue. And so the greatest speaker of his age spent forty days saying nothing at all, and writing little poems about how hard it was.

I find that very beautiful. A man whose whole gift was words, giving them back to God for a while, in the quiet.

[pause]

He died at Arianzus, at home, about the year three hundred and ninety. He was about sixty. We do not know very much about his last days. There was no Gregory to write his story the way he had written everyone else's. Which, perhaps, he would have liked. He had had enough of being the centre of things.

He was buried there in Cappadocia. Long afterwards, his body was carried to Constantinople, to the city he had served, and laid in the church of the Holy Apostles, the church he had said farewell to. And later still, in the Middle Ages, his relics came to Rome, and rested in Saint Peter's for many centuries.

And then, in the year two thousand and four, something lovely happened. Pope John Paul the Second gave a large part of the relics of Saint Gregory, and of Saint John Chrysostom, back to the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople, Bartholomew. They were carried in procession back to the city on the Bosphorus, and laid in the Patriarch's church. Gregory, who had spent so much of his life trying to make peace between quarrelling Christians, went home, carried by Christians who were trying to make peace with one another.

He is one of the Three Holy Hierarchs of the Orthodox Church, together with his friend Basil and with Saint John Chrysostom, and the Orthodox keep a feast for the three of them together at the end of January, as well as his own feast on the twenty-fifth. In the Catholic Church he is one of the Doctors of the Church, the great teachers, and his feast is on the second of January, which he shares with Basil. So the two friends, who fell out about a dusty crossroads, share a feast day for ever. I think they would both find that funny. I think Basil would pretend not to.

In icons Gregory is shown as an old bishop, with a white beard, a bald head, a gentle, rather tired face, and very often a book.

So if you have had a long day, and you have had to do things you did not want to do, and be in charge of things you never asked to be in charge of, and be brave when you only wanted to be quiet, you could think of him. The shy man who loved his books and his friends, who was promised to God before he was born, and promised himself again in a storm, who kept the promise all his life, even when it took him to dusty crossroads and crowded cities and rooms full of shouting bishops. And who at last was allowed to go home, and sit in his garden, and be quiet with God.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Most of tonight comes from Gregory himself. His long poem On His Own Life, written in his retirement, tells the story of his life up to his resignation, from his own point of view, and he is not always fair to his enemies. Carolinne White has translated it with his other poems about himself. His sermons tell us about his family, his flight after ordination, his farewell, and his friendship with Basil. His letters to Basil include the teasing ones about the hut in Pontus. The story of the bakers and the bath-keepers is from Basil's brother, Gregory of Nyssa. The Philocalia is traditionally credited to Gregory and Basil together. John McGuckin's biography is the best long modern account.

What is history. His family, his studies, the storm, Athens and Basil, Pontus, his ordination and flight, Sasima, the deaths of his family, Seleucia, the Anastasia, the Theological Orations, Maximus, Theodosius, the council of three hundred and eighty-one, his resignation and farewell, his retirement and death about three hundred and ninety, and the return of the relics in two thousand and four.

What is told as his own report. His mother's promise, his vow in the storm, the sun breaking through the clouds, and the young man who came to kill him and wept. These come from his own poems, and are told as his own account.

What we invented. Melitine, her broom, everything she says, the leaking roof, the conversations, and the wording of almost everything. Gregory's own words, from his poems, letters and farewell sermon, are given in our own plain paraphrase, and shortened.

That's the story of Saint Gregory of Nazianzus for tonight. His feast is on the second of January in the Catholic Church, together with his friend Basil, and on the twenty-fifth of January in the Orthodox Church, with the feast of the Three Holy Hierarchs on the thirtieth. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

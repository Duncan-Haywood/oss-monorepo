There is a crowd standing in front of a rock.

It is a big rock, the colour of a lion, with a crack running down its face, and there is nothing special about it at all, except that every single person in the crowd is looking at it. Thousands of them. Men and women and children, and goats, and a donkey who has wandered to the front because he thinks there might be something to eat.

In front of the rock stand two very old men. One of them is holding a staff.

He is angry. You can see it in his shoulders. He turns to the crowd and says something sharp, and his voice cracks a little on it, the way an old man's voice does when he has been shouting for days. And then he turns back to the rock, and he lifts up the staff, high, and he brings it down.

Crack.

And again.

Crack.

And for a moment nothing happens at all.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is Moses and Aaron at the waters of Meribah, from the Book of Numbers, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a woman who once danced behind Miriam at the sea, and the quiet words between the two brothers, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Last time on this show, we watched twelve sticks lie overnight in the dark of the tent of meeting, and in the morning one of them, Aaron's, had budded and blossomed and borne ripe almonds. And the Lord told Moses to put that staff back in front of the ark, and keep it there. Remember that staff. It may turn up again tonight. Or it may not. We will come to that.

Tonight we have moved on a long way. Not in miles, as it happens, but in years.

The people of Israel have been in the wilderness for a long time now. Nearly forty years. And a great deal has changed.

Most of the grown-ups who walked out of Egypt are gone. Years before, when the spies came back from the land of promise with a bunch of grapes so big it took two men to carry it on a pole, and the people were too frightened to go in, the Lord had said: very well. Then this generation will not go in. Your children will. And so the long years in the wilderness had become, among other things, a slow handing-over from one generation to the next.

Think of it like this. If you were a young man of twenty-five at the sea, with the water piled up on either side of you and the wind blowing all night, you would be sixty-five now. If you were a little child on your father's shoulders, you would be a grandparent. And a great many of the people in the camp tonight were not born when it happened at all. They have heard about Egypt the way you might have heard about your great-grandmother's village. A place in a story. Bricks and straw and a very bad king.

The people who remember are fewer every year. And three of them, in particular, are very, very old.

There is Moses. He was eighty when he first stood in front of Pharaoh, the Book of Exodus tells us, so he is somewhere around a hundred and twenty now, give or take. There is his brother Aaron, three years older, the priest, who still goes in and out of the tent of meeting in his blue robe with the little golden bells round the hem. And there is their sister.

Her name is Miriam.

* * *

I should tell you about Miriam, because tonight's story begins with her, and in a sense it is her story too, though she has only one sentence in it.

Long ago, when Moses was a baby, his mother hid him in a basket of reeds at the edge of the river Nile, because the king of Egypt had ordered that the baby boys of the Hebrews should be killed. And his big sister stood a little way off, the book says, to see what would happen to him. Readers have always understood that big sister to be Miriam.

So the first thing we know about her is that she watched over her little brother. She stood in the reeds with her feet in the mud and the flies buzzing and she kept her eyes on a basket. And when the king's own daughter came down to bathe and found the baby crying, it was the big sister who stepped out of the reeds, quite boldly, and said: shall I go and find you a nurse from the Hebrew women? And went, and fetched the baby's own mother.

I like that very much. It was brave, and it was clever, and it was a little bit cheeky, and it is exactly what big sisters are for.

The next time we meet her, she is grown up, and she is on the far side of the sea. The people have just walked through on dry ground and the water has come back over Pharaoh's chariots, and everyone is standing on the beach, wet and shaking and not quite able to believe it. And Miriam, the book says, Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, takes a tambourine in her hand. And all the women go out after her, with tambourines, dancing. And she sings to them:

Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea.

Much later, the prophet Micah, speaking for the Lord, remembers those years like this: I brought you up out of the land of Egypt, and I sent before you Moses, Aaron, and Miriam.

Three of them. Not two. The book does not forget her, and neither should we.

Now. I want to introduce one more person, and I want to be very clear that she is made up.

Among the women who went out dancing behind Miriam on the beach that day, let us imagine a girl of about fifteen. She had a tambourine that her mother had made her from a hoop of bent wood with a skin stretched over it, and she had not known she was going to need it, but she had grabbed it anyway when they ran out of Egypt, because you do not leave behind a thing you love.

The book does not tell us her name, because she is not in the book. We will call her Ziva, which is a modern Hebrew name and therefore entirely wrong for the period, but I like it, and she is not here to object.

Ziva is about fifty-five now. Her hair is grey. She has grandchildren. And she still has the tambourine, though the skin has been replaced three times and the hoop is mostly mended. It hangs on the pole of her tent. She does not play it much any more. But she has never once thought of throwing it away.

* * *

The place where tonight's story happens is called Kadesh.

It is in a stretch of country the book calls the wilderness of Zin, on the southern edge of the land the people have been walking towards all this time. Nobody is quite sure, now, exactly where Kadesh was. Many archaeologists think it was near a spring in the north-east of the Sinai, a place called Ein el-Qudeirat, where there is still water in a green little valley among the hills. Others point to a spring nearby. It is one of those questions where everyone agrees on roughly where and nobody agrees on exactly.

What we do know is that the people had been at Kadesh before. They had camped there for a long time, the Book of Deuteronomy says, many days. It was the place the spies had set out from, all those years ago. It was, if you like, a familiar camp. A place you might come back to and say, oh, Kadesh, I remember Kadesh, there used to be a good spot for the goats over by those rocks.

And the name Kadesh means holy. Keep that in your pocket too. It will matter by the end.

So. The book says that in the first month, the whole congregation came into the wilderness of Zin, and the people stayed at Kadesh. It does not say the first month of which year. Most readers have taken it to be the last year of the forty, because of what comes after, and that seems right to me, but the book does not say so here, so I will not pretend it does.

And then comes the sentence. It is very short.

And Miriam died there, and was buried there.

That is all. The book does not say how old she was. It does not say what she died of, or who was with her, or what anyone said. Just that. She died, and they buried her, at Kadesh.

[pause]

So let us imagine, quietly, the day they buried her.

It would have been soon after she died, because in that heat it had to be. Somewhere near the camp, in the rocky ground, a grave cut and lined with stones. The whole camp would have known. News like that goes round a camp of tents in about the time it takes to boil a pot of water.

And I imagine her two brothers there. Moses beside the grave, leaning on his staff. And Aaron, I think, standing a little way back, at the edge of the crowd, because the law that had been given at the mountain said that the high priest must not go near the dead, not even for his own father or mother, and he had kept that law for forty years and was not going to stop now, even for her. Two old men, white-bearded, who between them have seen the river turn to blood and the sea stand up in walls, and who are standing now near a hole in the ground, one close and one far, the way people have always stood near holes in the ground, not knowing what to do with their hands.

And then, at the back of the little crowd, not far from where Aaron is standing, a sound.

Tsh. Tsh-tsh. Tsh.

Ziva has brought her tambourine.

She is not playing it loudly. She is not dancing. She is just tapping it, very gently, against the heel of her hand, the old rhythm, the one from the beach, and her lips are moving. Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously.

A few of the other old women hear it, and they know it. One or two of them start to sing along, under their breath, and their voices are thin and cracked and not at all in tune. And the young people, who never saw the sea, look at one another, because they have heard the song a hundred times at festivals but they have never heard it sung like this, by people crying.

Aaron turns round and looks at Ziva. She stops, embarrassed.

"I'm sorry," she says. "I didn't mean."

"No," says Aaron. "Don't stop. She'd have liked it."

And so she goes on, tsh, tsh-tsh, until the song is finished. And then they put the stones back, and go home.

That is our invention, all of it, the tambourine and the song and Aaron's words, though the law about the high priest and the dead is real, in the Book of Leviticus. The book gives us only the one sentence. But I think it is the kind of thing that happens at funerals. Somebody does something small and slightly wrong, and it turns out to be exactly right.

* * *

And then, the very next sentence in the book:

Now there was no water for the congregation.

Just like that. Miriam is buried in one verse, and in the next one the water has run out.

There is an old Jewish tradition about this, written down by the rabbis a long time afterwards, and I want to tell it to you, as long as you remember that it is a tradition and not the book. The rabbis said that three good gifts were given to the people in the wilderness for the sake of three good leaders. The manna for the sake of Moses. The pillar of cloud for the sake of Aaron. And for the sake of Miriam, a well. A well that went with them, wherever they went, so that they always had water. And when Miriam died, the rabbis said, the well went away. And they pointed to these two verses, side by side. Miriam died there. And there was no water.

I find that a very lovely idea, the girl who watched over a basket in the river becoming, in the people's memory, the one who kept them supplied with water for forty years. But the Book of Numbers does not say it. The book only puts the two things next to each other and lets you notice.

Whatever the reason, the water was gone. Maybe the spring at Kadesh had dried in a bad year. Maybe there were simply too many of them, with too many goats. The book does not say. It only says what the people did next. And what the people did next was what people have done in every queue at every tap since the beginning of the world.

They gathered together against Moses and Aaron.

Now, I have to be fair to the people. This was not a small thing. In that country, without water, a child can get very ill in a day. Goats die. Old people die. If you have ever been really thirsty, properly thirsty, so that your tongue sticks to the roof of your mouth and your head aches and you cannot think about anything else, you will know that it does not make anyone their best self.

And these were not the same people as before. These were mostly the children of the ones who had grumbled at the first rock, years ago, at the other end of the wilderness. But it is remarkable, when you read the two stories side by side, how much the children sounded like their parents.

Here is what they said. I am giving it to you as the book has it, more or less word for word.

Would that we had died when our brothers died before the Lord!

That is a reference to the terrible day we told you about last time, when Korah and those with him rose up against Moses and Aaron and so many died. They are saying, it would have been better to go then, quickly, than to die slowly of thirst out here.

Why have you brought the assembly of the Lord into this wilderness, for us and our cattle to die here?

And why have you made us come up out of Egypt, to bring us to this evil place? It is no place for grain, or figs, or vines, or pomegranates. And there is no water to drink.

Figs. Vines. Pomegranates. I always notice that list, because those were exactly the things the spies had carried back from the promised land, all those years before. A great cluster of grapes, and pomegranates, and figs. It is as if the people are saying: you promised us figs and pomegranates, and look where we are. And they are not wrong that they are in a dry place. What they have forgotten, or perhaps never quite believed, is that they are in a dry place on the way to somewhere.

But that is the trouble with being on the way to somewhere. You cannot eat the somewhere. You can only eat where you are.

* * *

Moses and Aaron did not argue back. Not this time.

The book says that they went away from the assembly, to the door of the tent of meeting, and fell on their faces. It is not the first time in the Book of Numbers that they do this, fall flat on the ground in front of the Lord when the people turn on them. It is what they do instead of shouting. They lie down in the dust and wait.

And the glory of the Lord appeared to them.

The book does not describe it, and I will not try to. Only that it appeared, the way it always did, the way you know the sun has come out from behind a cloud even with your eyes shut.

And the Lord said to Moses:

Take the staff. And gather the people together, you and Aaron your brother. And tell the rock, before their eyes, to give out its water. And so you shall bring water out of the rock for them. And so you shall give drink to the congregation and their cattle.

Tell the rock. Speak to the rock. That is the instruction. Not strike it. Speak to it.

And if you were here a few nights ago, for the story of Aaron and Hur holding up Moses's hands at Rephidim, you may remember that this has happened before, or something very like it. Right at the start of the wilderness years, at a place called Rephidim, the people had no water and quarrelled with Moses, and the Lord told him then: take the staff, the one you struck the river with, and go to the rock at Horeb, and strike the rock, and water will come out of it. And Moses struck the rock, and the water came. And he called that place Massah and Meribah, which means testing, and quarrelling.

So this is the second time. Same people, or their children. Same complaint. Same old man, forty years older. Same staff, or perhaps not the same staff, and I will come to that in a moment. But a different instruction. The first time: strike. This time: speak.

And then the book says this. And Moses took the staff from before the Lord, as he commanded him.

From before the Lord. Do you remember where we left Aaron's staff, the one with the almonds on it? In front of the ark, in the tent. Before the testimony. Before the Lord. So a good many readers, Jewish and Christian, have thought that this is the staff Moses took out of the tent that morning. Aaron's staff. The one that had budded. The book does not quite say so, and a verse or two later it calls it his staff, meaning Moses's, so others have thought he took his own staff, the old shepherd's staff of the plagues and the sea, which may have been kept there too. I am not going to settle it. But I will say that if it was the almond staff, it makes tonight's story even sadder and even stranger, because it means he went out to the rock carrying a stick that had once flowered without anyone touching it.

* * *

So the two brothers came out of the tent, and they went to the rock.

Let me imagine the walk, because the book skips it and I do not want to.

It is not far. Nothing in a camp is far. But Moses walks slowly these days, and Aaron walks more slowly still, and behind them the word is going round the tents. Moses has been in with the Lord. Moses is going to the rock. Come and see. And so by the time they get there, the whole camp is following them, the way children follow a man with a ladder, and the noise of it is like the sea.

"You heard what he said," says Aaron, quietly, as they walk.

"I heard."

"Speak to it."

"I heard, Aaron."

They walk on a bit.

"I'm tired," says Moses, suddenly. "Do you know that? I'm so tired. Forty years. Forty years of this. Water. Bread. Meat. Water again. Why did you bring us out here. Why did you bring us out here. I didn't bring them out here. I didn't want to come out here. I told him at the bush, send somebody else. I said it in so many words. Send somebody else."

"I know," says Aaron. "I was the somebody else. He sent me as well."

And Moses almost laughs, but not quite.

"And she's gone," he says. "She's only been in the ground three days and they've already forgotten her. Listen to them."

Aaron does not answer that, because there is nothing to say to it.

I want to be careful here. Those words between the two brothers are our own invention. The book does not tell us what Moses was thinking on the way to the rock. But it does give us one hint, and it comes much later, in the Psalms, where the singer remembers this day. They angered him at the waters of Meribah, the psalm says, and it went ill with Moses on their account. For they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke words that were rash.

They made his spirit bitter. That is the psalm's word for what was going on inside Moses. Not wicked. Not faithless, exactly. Bitter. Worn through.

* * *

And so they came to the rock, and Moses and Aaron gathered the assembly together in front of it, as they had been told. And the crowd stood there, thousands of them, hot and thirsty and cross, with the goats bleating and the babies crying and the donkey nosing about at the front. And the rock stood there, lion-coloured, with its crack down the middle, saying nothing, the way rocks do.

And Moses turned round to face them.

And here is what he said. Again, I am giving you the book's own words.

Hear now, you rebels. Shall we bring forth water for you out of this rock?

[pause]

Hear now, you rebels.

You can hear the forty years in that. All the grumbling at the sea, and the grumbling about the bread, and the grumbling about the meat, and the golden calf, and the spies, and Korah. All of it in two words. You rebels.

And then: shall we bring forth water for you? We. As if it were the two of them doing it, the two old men, Moses and Aaron, against the people. As if the water were theirs to give.

And then Moses turned back to the rock, and lifted up his hand, and struck the rock with his staff.

Twice.

Crack.

Crack.

[pause]

And here is the extraordinary thing. The thing that I think is easy to miss.

The water came anyway.

The book says: and water came forth abundantly. A great deal of water. Pouring out of the crack in the rock, splashing down onto the stones, running away in little streams across the dust. And the congregation drank, and their cattle drank.

I like to imagine the noise of it. First a sort of gurgle from somewhere deep inside the rock, like a stomach. Then a trickle. Then a rush. And then the people surging forward, all at once, with jars and bowls and goatskins and cupped hands, and children shrieking and getting splashed, and the donkey, who was already at the front, getting the first and best drink of anyone and looking enormously pleased with himself.

And Ziva, I think, somewhere in the crowd, kneeling down at the edge of one of the little streams with her grandchildren, and letting it run over her hands, and thinking of her friend in the ground three days.

God did not withhold the water. That matters. The people were thirsty, and the children were thirsty, and the goats were thirsty, and whatever Moses did or did not do, God gave them water, a great deal of it, more than enough. The people were not punished for Moses's anger. They drank.

* * *

And then the Lord spoke to Moses and Aaron.

And here are the words, as the book has them.

Because you did not believe in me, to show my holiness in the eyes of the people of Israel, therefore you shall not bring this assembly into the land which I have given them.

[pause]

And the book adds, by way of a little label on the place: These are the waters of Meribah, where the people of Israel quarrelled with the Lord, and he showed himself holy among them.

He showed himself holy among them. And the place is called Kadesh. Holy. In Hebrew the two words sound alike, and the writer surely meant you to hear it. At the place called Holy, God showed himself holy, even though the two men he had chosen to show it did not.

Now. People have been arguing, for about three thousand years, about what exactly Moses and Aaron did wrong. And I want to tell you the main answers, because they are all interesting, and then I want to tell you that the book does not choose between them, and neither will I.

Some say: he was told to speak, and he struck. Simple disobedience. The instruction had changed, and he did the old thing out of habit, or out of temper.

Some say: it was the striking twice. As if once were not enough, and he had to hit it again to make his point.

Some say: it was the words. Shall we bring forth water for you. We. Not, the Lord will give you water. As if the miracle were Moses's and Aaron's to perform, and in a moment of anger he forgot whose it was.

Some say: it was the anger itself. You rebels. He had been told to show the people who God is, and instead he showed them who Moses was, on a bad day.

And the Lord's own words, in the book, are you did not believe in me, or you did not trust in me, to show my holiness. And later, in Numbers again, the Lord says to them that they rebelled against his word at the waters. And in Deuteronomy, near the very end, the Lord says to Moses that he broke faith at the waters of Meribah-kadesh and did not treat him as holy before the people. The book gives those words, and leaves it there. And I think the book is wise to leave it there. Because I suspect that most of us, if we are honest, have done all four of those things at once, on a tired day, and could not tell you afterwards which one it was.

But there is one more thing the book says, and it is the most tender. In Deuteronomy, Moses himself, as an old man, looking back on it, says to the people: the Lord was angry with me too, because of you. Not to blame them, I think. More as if to say: you wore me out. I loved you and you wore me out. And on the day it mattered, I was worn out.

* * *

So let us go back, for a moment, to the evening after the rock.

The camp is quiet. Quieter than it has been for days. Everyone has drunk, and the water jars are full, and the goats are lying down with their legs folded under them, chewing. Somebody, somewhere, is laughing. Somewhere else, somebody is singing. The pillar of fire is on the tent in the middle of the camp, as it is every night, glowing orange and gold against the dark.

And outside Moses's tent, two old men are sitting on the ground.

"So that's that," says Aaron, after a long time.

"That's that."

"I always thought," says Aaron, "that I'd see it. The land. I used to think about it. A fig tree. I'd have a fig tree. I'd sit under it in the afternoons and the grandchildren would bring me figs, and I'd say, in my day we had manna, and they'd roll their eyes."

"You'd have been unbearable," says Moses.

"I'd have been magnificent."

They sit.

"It was my fault," says Moses. "Not yours. You didn't strike it. I did."

"I was standing next to you," says Aaron. "I didn't stop you. I didn't say, wait, he said speak. I just stood there. When the calf happened, I made the calf. I didn't stop them then either." He shakes his head. "Forty years and I still don't know when to say no."

"You said no to Korah."

"That's different. That was holding a censer in the middle of the plague. That's easy. That's just running. It's the standing still that's hard."

And Moses puts his hand on his brother's knee, the way he might have done when they were boys in Egypt.

Again, I want to be clear that this conversation is ours. The book does not tell us what they said that night. It does tell us that the Lord's word came to both of them, and that both of them, in the end, did not go in. And it tells us something else that I find very moving, which is that the Lord did not stop speaking to them. Moses went on being Moses. Aaron went on being the priest. They had failed at the rock, publicly, in front of everyone, and the very next morning they went back to work.

Many years later, the book of Deuteronomy tells us, Moses asked the Lord to change his mind. Let me go over, he said, and see the good land beyond the Jordan. And the Lord said: let it be enough for you. Speak no more to me of this matter. And then told him to go up to the top of a mountain and look at it, all of it, as far as he could see. Which he did, and we told that story on this show a while ago, on the night of Mount Nebo.

* * *

I want to say a word now about how Christians have read this story, and then we will start to wind down.

Saint Paul, writing to the church at Corinth, remembers the wilderness, and says this. Our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all ate the same supernatural food, and all drank the same supernatural drink. For they drank from the supernatural Rock which followed them, and the Rock was Christ.

The Rock which followed them. That is the same old idea the rabbis had, of water that went with the people through the desert. And Paul says, more than that: the rock itself was a picture of Christ. Christ is the one from whom the water comes.

And Christian writers and preachers ever since have gone on noticing things. That Jesus, at the feast of Tabernacles in Jerusalem, stood up and cried out, if anyone thirst, let him come to me and drink. That at the cross, a soldier's spear pierced his side, and at once there came out blood and water. The rock struck, and water flowing out for everyone.

And some readers have gone further, and said: the rock was to be struck once, at the beginning, at Horeb, and after that only spoken to. That is one Christian reading of this story, and I find it beautiful, but I want to be honest that it is a reading. It is not in the Book of Numbers. The Book of Numbers is about two old men, and a rock, and a bad day.

But I will tell you one more thing, which is not a reading but a plain fact. Every single morning, all over the world, in monasteries and convents and parishes and at kitchen tables, when people who pray the Church's morning prayer begin their day, the psalm they most often start with is Psalm ninety-five. And in the middle of Psalm ninety-five there is this line. O that today you would listen to his voice. Harden not your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness.

That psalm is remembering the first quarrel, the one at Massah, at the start of the forty years. But the name is the same name, and the lesson is the same lesson. So the name of a quarrel by a rock in the desert is said, every morning, by an enormous number of sleepy people holding cups of coffee. As a warning, and a reminder, and, I think, a comfort. Even Moses had a bad day by a rock. Start again. Today. Listen to his voice today.

[pause]

And now let us go slowly.

The people did not stay much longer at Kadesh. The book tells us that, in the same chapter, Moses sent messengers from Kadesh to the king of Edom, which was the country to the east, the land of the descendants of Esau, Jacob's brother. And the messengers said, very politely: you know all the hardship that has come upon us. Let us pass through your land. We will not go through your fields or your vineyards, or drink water from your wells. We will go along the King's Highway, the great road, and not turn aside to the right or the left until we have passed through.

And the king of Edom said no.

So the messengers asked again. We will go by the highway, and if we drink any of your water, we will pay for it.

And the king of Edom said no again, and came out with a great many people to make sure of it.

And so Israel turned away from him, and did not fight. They went round. A long way round. The book does not make a fuss about it. It simply says: and Israel turned away from him. Brothers, after all. Jacob and Esau. You do not fight your brother if you can walk round him.

And from Kadesh they set out, and came to a mountain called Hor, on the border of Edom. And what happened on Mount Hor is a story for another night, and a very gentle one, and it is Aaron's. So I will save it.

* * *

But before they left Kadesh, let us imagine one last evening.

The water is still coming out of the rock. It has been coming out for days. The book does not say how long it lasted, but I like to think it was still going when they left, a little stream running down from the crack in the lion-coloured stone, across the gravel, and into a pool the children have dammed with pebbles.

It is late. The camp is asleep, nearly all of it. The stars are out, the enormous desert stars, so many that the sky looks dusty with them.

And Ziva has come down to the pool.

She sits on a stone beside it. She has her tambourine with her, though she does not play it. She just holds it in her lap, the old hoop, mended and mended again, with her thumb resting on the skin.

From here she can see the place where they buried Miriam, a little way up the slope, a low mound of stones, pale in the starlight.

She thinks about the beach. The wind. The wet sand. The girl in front of her, older, laughing, with her hair coming loose, holding up a tambourine and singing at the top of her voice. Ziva thinks about how she had followed her without thinking. Everybody had. When Miriam started to dance, you danced.

She thinks about the two old men in the tent up the hill, who will not see the land. And she thinks about herself. She is not sure whether she will see it either. She is not sure it matters as much as she used to think it did.

And then she listens.

Because the water is making a sound. Very small. A little splash where it comes off the edge of the rock, and a little trickle where it runs over the pebbles, and a sort of quiet chuckle where it goes into the pool. If you listen to it long enough, it is almost a rhythm.

Tsh. Tsh-tsh. Tsh.

Ziva smiles.

And she sits there a long time, in the dark, with the tambourine in her lap and the stars overhead, listening to the water keep time.

[pause]

Moses lived some while longer, and led the people all the way to the river Jordan, and saw the land from the top of Mount Nebo, and died there, and the Lord buried him, and no one knows his grave to this day. Aaron died first, on Mount Hor, as we will tell another time. And the people, the children and grandchildren of the ones who walked out of Egypt, crossed the river into the land without them, and drank from its springs, and ate its figs and its pomegranates.

And they never forgot the old men who brought them to the edge of it. They wrote down their failures, and their faithfulness, side by side. Which is, when you think about it, a kind of love.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty, verses one to thirteen, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, with the messages to the king of Edom from verses fourteen to twenty-one. Miriam's death and burial at Kadesh, the lack of water, the people's words, Moses and Aaron falling on their faces at the tent, the Lord's instruction to take the staff and speak to the rock, Moses's words to the people, the two blows, the abundant water and the Lord's words to the two brothers are all the book's own, told plainly.

The earlier water from the rock at Massah and Meribah is in Exodus, chapter seventeen. Moses's later words about this day, and the Lord's, are in Deuteronomy, chapters one, three and thirty-two, and the psalm about his bitter spirit is Psalm one hundred and six. Aaron's staff kept before the testimony is in Numbers, chapter seventeen; whether that is the staff Moses took is not settled by the book, and we have not settled it either. The book does not say which year of the forty this was, though Aaron's death in the fortieth year, in chapter thirty-three, suggests the last.

The tradition that a well followed the people for Miriam's sake is from the rabbis, in the Babylonian Talmud, written down long after; it is told as tradition. Paul's words about the rock are in the First Letter to the Corinthians, chapter ten. Where Kadesh was is uncertain. And the woman Ziva, her tambourine, and every word between Moses and Aaron on the way to the rock and that night, are our own invention.

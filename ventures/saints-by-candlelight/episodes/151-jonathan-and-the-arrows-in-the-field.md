The arrow goes up into the morning with a sound like someone tearing a very thin piece of cloth.

It goes up, and up, over the long grass of the field, over the head of a small boy who is running as fast as his legs will carry him. It goes over the boy, and past the boy, and a long way beyond the boy, and it comes down somewhere out in the stubble, and sticks there, quivering.

The boy skids to a stop. He looks back.

At the edge of the field, a tall young man is lowering his bow. He is a prince, and he is dressed like one. And he cups his hands to his mouth, and he calls out across the field, louder than he needs to, much louder than he needs to,

"Isn't the arrow beyond you?"

And then, even louder, "Hurry! Quickly! Don't stop!"

And the boy thinks, well, there's no need to shout. And he runs on, after the arrow.

But the words are not for the boy.

Behind a heap of stones at the side of the field, where nobody is looking, another young man has been lying very still for three days. And he has heard every word. And he knows exactly what they mean.

Tonight's story is about two friends. And the first thing I want to tell you is that, on paper, they should never have been friends at all.

One of them is a prince. His name is Jonathan, and he is the eldest son of Saul, the first king of Israel. He is tall, because his father is tall, and the Bible is quite pleased about how tall Saul is. He is brave. He is a soldier, and a very good one. And he is the heir. When Saul dies, everyone expects that Jonathan will be king.

The other one is a shepherd. His name is David. He is the youngest of the eight sons of Jesse, from a farming family in the little town of Bethlehem. He has red cheeks and fine eyes, the Bible says, and he plays the harp. And one day, not very long before our story, he walked out into a valley with a sling and five smooth stones and knocked down a giant, and since then, everybody has been singing about him.

Literally singing. The women of the towns came out dancing, with tambourines, and they sang a song that went, Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his tens of thousands.

You can imagine how much Saul enjoyed that song.

So here is the situation. There is a king, who is getting older, and more frightened, and who has been told by the prophet Samuel that God has chosen someone else to be king after him. There is a young hero whom everybody loves. And there is the king's son, the heir to the throne, who by all the usual rules of royal families ought to be the young hero's bitterest enemy.

And instead, the day Jonathan met David, the Bible says, the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.

He took off his own robe, the prince's robe, and gave it to David. And his armour. And his sword. And his bow. And his belt.

Hold on to the bow. We are going to need it.

Now, before we start, I want you to know a little more about Jonathan, because he does not get as much attention as he deserves. Everybody knows David and the giant. Not so many people know that Jonathan had his own giant moment, a few years before.

There was a Philistine garrison on top of a rocky crag, at a place called Michmash. And Jonathan, without telling his father, without telling anybody, said to the young man who carried his armour, come on, let's go over to them. Perhaps the Lord will act for us. For nothing can stop the Lord from saving, by many or by few.

And the two of them climbed up the cliff. On their hands and their feet. Just the two of them. And they won. The whole Philistine army fell into a panic. It was one of the great days in the history of Israel.

And later that same day, when he was hungry and tired, Jonathan found a honeycomb in a wood, and dipped the end of his staff in it, and put it to his mouth, and his eyes brightened. I love that detail. Of all the things the Bible could have told us about that great battle, it tells us that Jonathan liked honey.

There was trouble about that honey, as it happens. His father had sworn that nobody in the army was to eat anything until evening, and Jonathan, who was off climbing cliffs at the time, had never heard the order. When Saul found out, he was ready to punish his own son for it. And the soldiers stood up and said, no. Shall Jonathan die, who has won this great victory for Israel? Not a hair of his head shall fall to the ground. And they would not let it happen. It is worth remembering, for later tonight, that Saul had come close to hurting Jonathan once before, and that it was always other people who had to talk him out of it.

So this is our Jonathan. Brave. Generous. Fond of honey. A man who climbs cliffs because he trusts God. And a man who, when he meets the person who is going to take his crown, gives him his robe.

Things have been going badly at court.

Saul's jealousy has got worse and worse. Twice, while David was playing the harp for him, to soothe him on his dark days, Saul threw a spear at him. Twice David dodged. Which tells you something about David's reflexes, and also something about why harp players at Saul's court tended not to stay long.

Once, Jonathan talked his father round. He stood up for David, and reminded his father of all the good David had done, and Saul listened, and swore an oath. As the Lord lives, he said, David shall not be put to death. And for a while, things were better.

But it did not last. There was another spear. There was a night when Saul sent men to David's house to kill him in the morning, and David's wife, Michal, who was Saul's daughter, let him down through a window and put a household idol in his bed with a goat's-hair pillow, to fool the soldiers. That is a whole story in itself.

It is a very strange and rather wonderful chapter. And at the end of it, while Saul is lying on the ground at Ramah, David slips away. And he goes back, of all places, towards the king's own court. To find Jonathan. The book does not say where they met, but Jonathan lived at his father's house in Gibeah, so I think it was somewhere near there.

We do not know where they met. Somewhere quiet, I think. Somewhere out of sight of the king's house, which, in a hill town like Gibeah, was probably not much grander than a big, strong farmhouse.

And David comes to Jonathan, and he says, "What have I done? What is my guilt? What is my sin against your father, that he is trying to kill me?"

And Jonathan says, "Never! You won't die. Look, my father doesn't do anything, great or small, without telling me first. Why would he hide this from me? It isn't so."

You can hear how much Jonathan wants this to be true. He loves his father. He loves his friend. He does not want to believe that one of them is trying to kill the other. And he has, after all, heard his father swear an oath, as the Lord lives, that David would not die.

But David shakes his head. And he says something very clever, and very sad.

"Your father knows perfectly well that I'm your friend," he says. "So he's thinking, Jonathan mustn't know about this, or he'll be upset. That's why he hasn't told you. But truly, as the Lord lives, and as you live, there is only one step between me and death."

One step.

And Jonathan looks at his friend, and something in him gives way. "Whatever you want," he says. "Whatever you say, I'll do it for you."

So David makes a plan.

"Tomorrow is the new moon," he says. "I'm supposed to sit down with the king at the feast. Let me go instead, and hide in the field, until the evening of the third day. Now I should tell you about the new moon.

In ancient Israel, the start of each month was a holy day. The month began when the thin silver sliver of the new moon first appeared in the evening sky, and there were special sacrifices, and the trumpets were blown, and it was a feast. At the king's court, it meant a great supper, with everybody in their proper places. The king. The king's son. The king's general. And, these days, the king's son-in-law, the hero of Israel, David.

An empty place at that table would be noticed. That was the whole point.

And then David says one more thing. He says, "Deal kindly with your servant, because you have brought me into a covenant of the Lord with you. But if there is any guilt in me, kill me yourself. Why bring me to your father?"

And Jonathan says, "Never. If I find out that my father has decided to harm you, wouldn't I tell you?"

"But who will tell me," David says, "if your father answers you roughly?"

It is a fair question. If Jonathan sends a servant, the servant might talk. If Jonathan goes himself, he might be followed.

And Jonathan says, "Come on. Let's go out into the field."

* * *

So they go out into the field together. And I want to slow down here, because this is one of the most tender scenes in the whole Old Testament, and it happens in an ordinary field, on an ordinary afternoon, with nobody else there.

Let us picture it. A field outside the town, in the hill country, sloping a little. Stubble from the harvest, perhaps, or long grass going gold. A few olive trees along a terrace wall. Somewhere, a heap of stones, the kind farmers pile up at the edge of a field when they clear it, and which in a field that has been farmed for a few hundred years can get quite big. That part is our own picture. The Bible only gives us the field and the stone.

And Jonathan swears an oath.

"The Lord, the God of Israel, is my witness," he says. "About this time tomorrow, or the day after, I will sound out my father. If he is well disposed towards you, I will send and tell you. And if he means you harm, may the Lord do so to me, and more also, if I do not tell you, and send you away in peace. May the Lord be with you, as he has been with my father."

May the Lord be with you, as he has been with my father. Jonathan knows. He has known for a while, I think. He knows that the Lord's favour is passing from Saul's house to David. And he does not resent it. He blesses it.

And then Jonathan asks for something in return.

"If I am still alive," he says, "show me the faithful kindness of the Lord, so that I do not die. And do not cut off your kindness from my house, ever. Not even when the Lord has cut off every one of David's enemies from the face of the earth."

That word, kindness, is hesed in Hebrew, the word for faithful, promise-keeping love. The love that does not let go. Jonathan is a prince, asking a shepherd to look after his family. He is the heir, asking the man who will take his throne to be kind to his children.

And David promises. And then, the Bible says, Jonathan made David swear again, because of his love for him. For he loved him as he loved his own soul.

Then Jonathan explains the signal.

"Tomorrow is the new moon," he says. "You'll be missed, because your seat will be empty. On the third day, go down to the place where you hid before, and wait beside the stone there."

The Hebrew calls it the stone Ezel. The old Greek translation seems to call it something else, a mound, or a heap. Nobody is quite sure what Ezel means. It might be a name. It might mean "the stone of departing", or "the stone of going away", which, if it is right, is a rather beautiful name for what happens there. But we cannot be certain, and so I am only telling you that it might.

"I'll shoot three arrows to the side of it," Jonathan says, "as if I were shooting at a target. And I'll send a boy and say, go and find the arrows. If I say to the boy, look, the arrows are on this side of you, pick them up, then come out, because it's safe, as the Lord lives. Nothing is wrong. But if I say to the boy, look, the arrows are beyond you, then go. Because the Lord has sent you away."

And then he says, "And as for what we've promised each other, you and I, the Lord is between you and me for ever."

* * *

So the new moon comes.

And David hides in the field.

The Bible gives us this in five words. David hid himself in the field. But that is three days, and two nights, and I think we should spend a little time with him, so this part is our own invention.

David is good at being outdoors. He has spent his whole boyhood out on the hills around Bethlehem with his father's sheep, sleeping under the stars, keeping an eye out for lions and bears, which is not something many of us have had to put on our list of jobs. So a field with a heap of stones in it is not a hardship. He has a cloak. He has a skin of water. Jonathan, I imagine, has slipped him some bread and some cheese, and possibly, knowing Jonathan, a little honey.

On the first evening, he lies on his back behind the stones and watches the sky go from blue to gold to pink to a deep, clear violet. And there, low in the west, just after the sun goes down, he sees it. The new moon. A thin, curved line of silver, so fine you could miss it if you blinked.

Somewhere up the hill, in the king's house, the trumpets are blowing for the feast.

David is a poet. Many of the psalms are his, or are told in his name. And I like to think that he lay there, in the long grass, and looked at that thin silver line, and thought about the psalm that says, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is man that you are mindful of him? I do not know that he did. But he could have. And he had plenty of time.

The second day is longer.

There is nothing to do in a field when you are hiding in it. You cannot walk about, in case someone sees you. You cannot light a fire. You cannot sing, which for David, I suspect, was the hardest part. You lie in the grass, and you listen, and you watch the shadows of the olive trees go round like the hand of a very slow clock.

Late in the morning, in our telling, a farmer comes along the terrace wall with a donkey and a basket, and stops not twenty paces from the stones, and David lies so still that a beetle walks right across the back of his hand. The farmer is looking for a lost goat. He calls for it. He grumbles about it. He tells the donkey, at some length, what he thinks of goats in general and this goat in particular. And then he goes away again, still grumbling, and David lets out a breath he feels he has been holding since the new moon.

The goat, I am pleased to tell you, turns up in the afternoon, by itself, eats some of David's bread, and wanders off. Goats are like that.

And in the evening, the trumpets again, up the hill, for the second night of the feast. And David lies behind his stones, and thinks about the empty place at the table, and about Jonathan sitting next to it, and wonders what is being said.

He does not know, yet, about the spear.

Meanwhile, up the hill, the feast.

The Bible describes the scene very precisely, as if somebody who was there remembered exactly where everybody sat. The king sat in his usual seat, by the wall. Abner, the king's general, who was also his cousin, sat beside him. Jonathan was there. And David's place was empty.

Now picture it, in our way. A long room, with lamps on stands, and the smell of roast meat from the new-moon sacrifice, and fresh bread, and wine. Servants coming and going. The king's family, the king's officers, the king's household, all in their places. And one place, near the king, with nobody in it.

Everybody can see it. Nobody mentions it.

And Saul says nothing at all, that first night. Not a word. The Bible tells us what he was thinking. He thought, something has happened to him. He is not clean. That is, he thought David must have become ritually unclean somehow, which under the law would keep him from a holy meal until he had washed and waited till evening. Perfectly ordinary. Could happen to anyone. Nothing to worry about.

Jonathan eats his supper, I imagine, very carefully, and does not look at the empty place, and does not look at his father.

And the next day is the second day of the feast. And again, everyone sits down. The king by the wall. Abner by his side. Jonathan. And, again, an empty place.

And this time Saul speaks.

"Why hasn't the son of Jesse come to the meal," he says, "either yesterday or today?"

Notice what he calls him. Not David. Not my son-in-law. Not the hero who killed the giant. The son of Jesse. The farmer's boy. It is not a kind way to say it.

And Jonathan gives the answer they agreed. "David asked me very earnestly if he could go to Bethlehem. He said, please let me go, because our family is having a sacrifice in the town, and my brother has told me to be there. So if I have found favour in your eyes, let me slip away and see my brothers. That's why he hasn't come to the king's table."

And Saul's anger blazes up against Jonathan.

The Bible gives us what he said, and it is not nice. He says cruel things about Jonathan's mother, which is a very low thing to do, and the kind of thing people say when they are angry with somebody else and the person in front of them is easier to hit. And then he says the real thing. The thing that has been eating at him.

"Don't you see? As long as the son of Jesse lives on the earth, neither you nor your kingdom will be safe. Now send for him, and bring him to me. He must die."

And Jonathan, standing in front of his father in front of the whole court, says the bravest thing he has ever said. Braver than the cliff at Michmash.

"Why should he be put to death? What has he done?"

That is all. Two questions. He does not shout. He does not draw a sword. He just asks his father the question nobody else at that table would dare to ask. What has he done?

And Saul throws his spear at him.

At his own son. At the supper table.

He misses. Of course he misses. But the spear tells Jonathan everything he needs to know, more clearly than any words could. His father has made up his mind to kill David.

And Jonathan gets up from the table in hot anger, and he eats nothing at all that second day of the month. The Bible tells us why. Not because his father had thrown a spear at him. Because he was grieved for David. Because his father had shamed his friend.

I find that remarkable. His father has just tried to kill him, and the thing that upsets him is what his father has said about David.

* * *

That night, I do not think Jonathan sleeps very much.

The house is quiet. His father has gone to his room. Abner, I expect, has very carefully said nothing to anybody. The servants are clearing away the supper, and not looking at the place where the king was sitting when he threw the spear.

And Jonathan lies awake, and thinks about what he has to do in the morning. He has to send his best friend away. He has to do it with an arrow, and a shout, and a boy who must not know. And after that, he has to come back up the hill, and go on being his father's son.

And in the morning, early, at the time they agreed, Jonathan goes out to the field. He takes his bow. And he takes a boy with him.

The Bible calls him a little lad, a young boy, and does not give his name, and it says one thing about him that I love. It says the boy knew nothing about it. Only Jonathan and David knew.

So in our telling, we are going to call the boy Elon, and he is about nine, and he is one of the boys who run errands about the king's house, and he has absolutely no idea that he is about to take part in one of the most famous scenes in the Bible. As far as he is concerned, the prince has got up early to practise his archery, and wants someone to fetch the arrows, and Elon is the someone. He is thrilled. Nobody ever asks him to do anything with the prince.

They come out to the edge of the field. The air is cool. There is dew on the grass. Over at the far side, there is a heap of stones that Elon has never thought twice about.

And Jonathan says, "Run, and find the arrows I shoot."

And Elon runs.

And as he runs, Jonathan draws, and shoots. And the arrow goes up over the boy's head, and on, and on, and comes down far beyond him.

And when Elon gets to the place where the arrow fell, Jonathan calls out after him, "Isn't the arrow beyond you?"

And then, "Hurry! Quickly! Don't stop!"

Do you see what he has done? The arrow is beyond you. That was the signal. That means: go. The Lord has sent you away. And hurry, quickly, don't stop, that is not really for Elon at all. Elon is already running. That is for the man behind the stones. That is Jonathan saying, as plainly as he can without saying it: run, my friend. Run, and don't look back.

And Elon, puffing, picks up the arrows and comes trotting back to his master, very pleased with himself, and holds them out.

And Jonathan takes his bow, and his quiver, and gives them to the boy, and says, "Here. Take these back to the town for me."

In our telling, Elon looks up at him, puzzled. "Aren't you going to shoot any more, my lord?"

"Not today," says Jonathan. "Go on. Take them home. And Elon, thank you. You ran very well."

And Elon goes off up the path, carrying the prince's bow, which is nearly as tall as he is, feeling about ten feet tall himself, and never knowing, for the rest of his life perhaps, what he had really been part of that morning.

And as soon as the boy has gone, David gets up from beside the stones.

Three days in the field. Grass in his hair. Dew on his cloak. And he comes out, and he falls on his face to the ground, and he bows down three times.

To Jonathan. The prince bows to no one, but here is the man who will be king, bowing three times to the man who would have been.

And then they kiss each other, in the way that friends and brothers did in those days, and they weep together. The Hebrew says that David wept the most. It says it in a slightly strange way, and translators have puzzled over it, but the sense is clear enough. They both wept. David more.

And then Jonathan says to him,

"Go in peace. Because we have both sworn, in the name of the Lord, saying, the Lord will be between me and you, and between my children and your children, for ever."

And David got up and went. And Jonathan went back into the town.

That is the end of the chapter. Where David went next, to a small town called Nob, hungry, with no sword and no bread, and what a frightened priest gave him there, is a story for another night.

I have always wondered why, if they were going to meet and talk anyway, they needed the secret signal with the arrows at all. People have wondered about it for a long time. Perhaps Jonathan wanted to be sure nobody had followed him, and only came close once he had sent the boy away. Perhaps, by then, there was nothing left to be careful about. The Bible does not say, and I will not pretend to know.

But I am glad they got to say goodbye properly.

They saw each other once more.

Some time later, when David was hiding in the wilderness of Ziph, down in the dry hills of the south, with Saul hunting him, Jonathan came to find him. At a place called Horesh, which means a wood, or a thicket.

And the Bible says that Jonathan strengthened his hand in God.

It is a lovely phrase. He did not bring an army. He did not bring supplies. He came to strengthen his friend's hand in God. To encourage him. To remind him whom he trusted.

"Don't be afraid," Jonathan said. "My father's hand won't find you. You will be king over Israel, and I will be next to you. Even my father knows it."

And the two of them made a covenant before the Lord again. And David stayed in the wood. And Jonathan went home.

And that was the last time.

Jonathan did not get to be next to David when he became king. He died with his father, in a battle against the Philistines on Mount Gilboa. The Bible tells it quietly, and so will we. He went home to God, a soldier, beside his father, whom he never stopped loving, even after the spear.

And the promise held.

Years later, when David was king, and had rest from his enemies, he asked a question that no king of his time was supposed to ask. Is there anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness, for Jonathan's sake? And there was. A son of Jonathan, lame in both his feet. The kindness that was promised in a field, beside a stone whose name might mean departing, came home at a supper table. Where there had once been an empty seat, there was a full one.

Now, what do you do, in the ancient world, if an enemy is too strong for your army?

You hire a curse.

And now the wind-down. Let us go slowly.

I should tell you honestly that Balaam's story does not end as happily as tonight's episode. The Book of Numbers tells us, later on, that while Israel was still camped in Moab, at a place called Shittim, many of the people were led astray into the worship of the god of Peor. And when the book comes to list the reasons, it says, quietly, that this happened on the advice of Balaam. And it tells us, in one line, that Balaam died not long afterwards, in the war between Israel and Midian.

The New Testament remembers him the same way. The Second Letter of Peter speaks of Balaam the son of Beor, who loved the wages of doing wrong, and was rebuked for his own wrongdoing, when a speechless donkey spoke with a human voice and stopped the prophet's madness. The Letter of Jude, and the Book of Revelation, remember him in a similar way, as a warning.

So Balaam is not a saint. He is not a hero. He is a man who heard the word of the Lord, and spoke it, and spoke it beautifully, and then, it seems, went home with his heart still half on the silver.

And I think that is a very important thing for the story to tell us. That a man can say the right words, true words, words from God, words that will be read aloud for thousands of years, and still not be a good man all the way through. Knowing the truth is not the same as loving it. Balaam knew. The donkey simply obeyed.

[pause]

But the Bible remembers something else, too. Many centuries after Balaam, the prophet Micah wrote, speaking for God to the people of Israel. O my people, remember what Balak king of Moab planned, and what Balaam son of Beor answered him, so that you may know the saving acts of the Lord.

And the Book of Deuteronomy says it even more simply. The Lord your God would not listen to Balaam. Instead, the Lord your God turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the Lord your God loved you.

Turned the curse into a blessing. Because he loved you.

That is the real story, I think. Not really a story about a seer, or a king, or even a donkey, though I am very fond of the donkey. It is a story about a people asleep in their tents in a valley, who did not know that anybody was trying to curse them, and who woke up in the morning blessed instead.

There is one more thing to tell, and it is about the star.

Many, many centuries later, there were wise men in the east. Magi, Saint Matthew's Gospel calls them. And they saw a star rising, and they followed it west, across the desert, to Jerusalem, and from Jerusalem to Bethlehem. And there they found a child with Mary his mother, and they knelt down, and opened their treasures.

Matthew does not mention Balaam. But very early on, Christians noticed the connection. The great scholar Origen, writing in the third century, suggested that the wise men knew Balaam's prophecy, a star shall come out of Jacob, because it had been handed down in the lands of the east, where Balaam came from. That is an interpretation, not something the Gospel tells us. But it is a lovely one.

A seer from the east, who saw the star far off, and not yet. And then, centuries later, other wise men from the east, who saw the star near, and now, and followed it all the way to the manger.

And every year, in Advent, on the Monday after the third Sunday, the Catholic Church reads Balaam's words at Mass. How lovely are your tents, O Jacob. I see him, but not now. A star shall come out of Jacob. A foreign seer's words, read aloud in churches all over the world, while people wait for Christmas.

And there is one last, small thing.

In nineteen sixty-seven, archaeologists digging at a place called Tell Deir Alla, in the Jordan valley, found fragments of an old inscription, written in ink on a plastered wall. It had fallen down, long ago, perhaps in an earthquake, and it was broken into many pieces. And when the scholars put the pieces back together, as best they could, they found that the text began with the name of a seer of the gods, who saw a vision in the night. And his name was Balaam, son of Beor.

It is a different story from the one in Numbers. It is not about Moab, or Israel, or a donkey. But it is the same name, and the same father's name, and the same profession, written on a wall in the Jordan valley, not so very far from the plains of Moab, nearly three thousand years ago. It seems that people on that side of the river remembered Balaam too, and were still telling stories about him, centuries after the day he walked home beside his donkey.

Imagine a courtyard in winter, about sixteen hundred and fifty years ago.

It is grey and cold, and the ground is trampled into mud. Along one wall there is a row of huge clay pots, set over fires, and steam is rising out of them into the cold air like the breath of a row of sleeping cows. The smell is thick soup, the kind made of split peas and lentils and whatever else could be found, and it is the best smell in the whole city, because this year there is almost nothing to eat.

In front of the pots is a long, shuffling line of people. Farmers in from the country. Mothers with babies tied on their backs. Old men leaning on sticks. Children with very large eyes in very thin faces.

And behind the pots, with a big wooden ladle in his hand and a cloth tied round his middle like an apron, there is a tall, thin, tired-looking man with a long dark beard and a cloak that has been mended more than once.

A boy near the front of the line tugs his mother's sleeve.

"Mother," he whispers. "Is that a servant?"

"No," she whispers back. "That is the priest. The famous one. The one who preached about the barns."

The boy stares. The priest looks up, sees him staring, and, very slightly, winks.

Cappadocia is in the middle of what is now Turkey. If you have ever seen a photograph of hot-air balloons floating over strange rocky towers that look like giant mushrooms, or like very tall gnomes in pointed hats, that was Cappadocia. The towers are soft volcanic stone, carved by wind and rain, and people have been digging houses and churches into them for a very long time.

But our story is a little to the east of the famous towers, in the city of Caesarea, which today is called Kayseri, a big modern city of more than a million people. And above Caesarea, then and now, there stands a mountain. It is called Erciyes today, and the old Greeks called it Argaeus. It is an old volcano, nearly thirteen thousand feet high, and for most of the year it has snow on its top, so that it hangs over the city like a great white tent.

Cappadocia was high country. The winters were long and bitter, with snow that shut the roads. The summers were hot and dry. It was good land for horses, which were famous all over the Roman Empire, and for sheep, and for wheat and barley, as long as the rain came. When the rain came, everybody ate. When the rain did not come, things got very bad, very quickly, because there were no lorries and no supermarkets, and the nearest sea was a long way off, over the mountains.

The time is the three hundred and sixties. The Roman Empire is still standing, though it is beginning to creak. The emperors have been Christian for about fifty years, ever since Constantine, and you might think that would have made life easy for the Church. It had not. The Church was in the middle of an enormous argument, about whether the Son of God is truly God, the same as the Father, or something a little bit less, a sort of very high creature. That second view was taught by a priest called Arius, and the Council of Nicaea had said no to it, firmly, in three hundred and twenty-five. But the argument had gone on and on, and some of the emperors, who liked a quiet life, kept leaning towards the side that was easier to agree on, which was not the right side.

* * *

Basil was born about the year three hundred and thirty, in Cappadocia or in Pontus just to the north, into a family that, honestly, seems to have had more saints in it than is quite fair on the rest of us.

His grandmother, Macrina the Elder, had lived through the last great persecution, hiding in the forests of Pontus with her husband for years, and she had learned the faith from the disciples of a famous holy bishop called Gregory the Wonderworker. His mother, Emmelia, is honoured as a saint. His father, also called Basil, was a teacher of public speaking, which in those days was the job that got your sons into good careers. There were nine or ten children, the old writers count a little differently, and of those children, the eldest girl, Macrina, is a saint, and two of the boys besides our Basil became bishops and saints as well: Gregory, who became bishop of Nyssa, and Peter, who became bishop of Sebaste.

So at family dinners, you understand, it was quite difficult to get a word in.

Basil was clever. Very clever. He was sent off to study, first in Caesarea, then in Constantinople, the new capital, and finally in Athens, which was like being sent to the oldest and grandest university in the world. And in Athens, he met the friend of his life.

His name was Gregory, from a little town called Nazianzus, not far from home, and he was gentle and funny and a poet, and he would much rather have been reading than doing almost anything else. The two young men from Cappadocia found each other in that great foreign city full of showing-off students, and they became inseparable. Gregory wrote, many years later, that they seemed to have one soul between two bodies. He said that they knew only two roads in Athens, the road to the church and the road to their teachers, and that they did not even know where the parties were.

The narrator thinks this may be slightly polished for the occasion. But only slightly.

Basil came home, about the year three hundred and fifty-six, a brilliant young man with the best education money could buy, and he began to teach public speaking, like his father. And, according to his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa, who wrote it down later, Basil came home very pleased with himself indeed. Puffed up, his brother says. Looking down on the important people of the town. Rather full of his own eloquence.

And it was his sister Macrina who brought him down to earth.

Macrina had chosen a different life. She had turned the family's country house into something like a convent, where she and her mother lived simply, working and praying with the women who had once been their servants, all of them together, as sisters. And she looked at her clever brother, with his fine speeches and his fine friends, and she drew him, gently and steadily, towards something else.

Basil himself described what happened, years afterwards, in a letter. He said that he had wasted a lot of time on vain things, on the learning of a wisdom that God makes foolish. And then one day he woke up, as if from a deep sleep, and looked at the wonderful light of the truth of the Gospel, and saw how useless all the rest had been. And he wept, he said, over his miserable life.

So he gave it up. The career, the speeches, the admiration. He gave away a great deal of what he owned. He travelled to Egypt and Syria to see how the famous monks lived in the desert. And then he came home and went to live by a river.

* * *

The river was called the Iris, and it ran through a deep green valley in Pontus, close to his family's country estate at a place called Annesi. Basil wrote a letter to Gregory, inviting him to come, and in it he described the place like an estate agent who has truly fallen in love with a house. There is a high mountain, he said, covered with thick woods, and watered on the north by cool clear streams. At its foot there is a level plain. The river runs past, faster than any river he knew. The woods all round are like a wall. And the best thing of all, he said, is the quiet.

Gregory came. And Gregory, being Gregory, wrote him a teasing letter about it afterwards, which also still survives, in which he complains, at length and with great enjoyment, about the mud, and the cold, and the draughty lodging, and the food, and all the hard work they did digging the vegetable garden and hauling loads up and down the slopes like a pair of donkeys. And then he wrote another letter, saying he had only been joking, and that really, he missed it terribly, the psalms, and the work, and the quiet nights.

It was there, by the river, that Basil began to work out how Christians who wanted to give their whole lives to God should live together. And what he worked out was not quite what the famous hermits of the desert did. The hermits, many of them, lived alone. Basil thought that for most people, living alone was not the best way. And he gave a reason that I have always liked very much. If you live alone, he wrote, whose feet will you wash?

He meant the night of the Last Supper, when the Lord tied a towel round his waist and knelt down and washed his friends' feet, and said, do as I have done. You cannot do that on your own. You need other people. You need someone to serve.

So Basil's monks lived together. They prayed at set hours through the day and night. They worked with their hands, and the work was not only to keep themselves busy. It was so that they would have something to give away. They looked after children. They looked after the sick. They fed the poor who came to the door. Prayer and work and service, all woven together, so that you could not quite tell where one stopped and the next began.

The rules Basil wrote down for them are still read today. The monks of the Orthodox Church have followed them ever since. And in the West, a few generations later, when Saint Benedict wrote his own famous Rule for monks in Italy, near the end of it he tells his monks to read, among other good books, the Rule of our holy father Basil.

And Basil, who had wanted only the quiet by the river, was not allowed to keep it. The bishop of Caesarea needed help, and the Church needed clever men who would stand on the right side of the great argument. So, about the year three hundred and sixty-four, rather against his will, Basil was made a priest, and came back to the city under the white mountain. And a few years after that, the rain stopped.

* * *

Now I want you to meet a boy.

We have made him up. The old writers do not give us the names of any of the hungry people of Caesarea, which is a shame, because there were thousands of them. So we will call him Philo, and he is about eight years old, and he lives with his mother on a little farm a day's walk from the city, in the hills, where they grow barley and keep a few goats.

And the year is about three hundred and sixty-eight, and there has been no rain.

It did not rain in the winter, when it should have. It did not rain in the spring. Philo watched the barley come up thin and yellow and then simply stop, as if it had changed its mind. The streams went down to trickles and then to dry stones. The goats got bony. By the summer, the fields were bare and cracked, and the sky, every single day, was a hard bright blue with not a cloud in it.

Basil preached a sermon about that sky, and we still have it. He spoke, more or less, of a sky as hard as bronze, of clouds that gathered and made people hope and then drifted away without a drop, and of an earth cracked open and thirsty. And he looked at his congregation and asked them to look at themselves, and to pray, and to repent, and to share.

Because here was the terrible thing. There was not nothing to eat in Cappadocia. There was some grain. It was in the barns of the people who had a lot of land. And some of those people were not selling it. They were waiting. Because every week the famine went on, the price of grain went up, and if they waited just a little longer, they could sell it for even more.

And Basil, in the church, in front of everyone, preached about them.

He took the parable from the Gospel of Luke, about the rich farmer whose fields produced so much that he said to himself, I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones, and I will say to my soul, take it easy, eat, drink, enjoy yourself. And God said to him, you fool, this very night your soul will be required of you.

And Basil turned that parable round and round like a jewel, so that everybody listening could see their own face in it. He said, and here it is in our own plain words, the bread you are keeping belongs to the hungry. The coat hanging unused in your cupboard belongs to the person who has no coat. The shoes going mouldy in your house belong to the person with no shoes. The money you have buried in the ground belongs to the poor. And every person you could have helped, and did not, is a person you have wronged.

He said that wealth is like a well. If you keep drawing water out of it, it stays fresh and clean and goes on flowing. If you leave it alone and never draw from it, it goes stagnant and foul. So, he said, open your barns. Let your riches flow out, like a river into a dry land.

The narrator thinks that some of the rich people of Caesarea went home after that sermon in rather a bad mood.

But Gregory, Basil's friend, tells us what happened next. Basil could not make bread fall from the sky, Gregory says, like manna in the desert. He could not make the rain come. But by his words, he opened the barns of those who had them. And then he did something that nobody expected of a famous, learned, rather prickly priest. He gathered the hungry together in one place, and he fed them himself.

* * *

Philo and his mother walked to Caesarea in the autumn. They left the goats with a neighbour, who had promised to look after them, and who was probably going to eat them, and they all knew it, and nobody said so.

It was a long walk, and Philo was tired, and the white mountain grew bigger and bigger ahead of them until it filled half the sky. And when they came through the gates, his mother asked a woman at a well where the hungry were being fed, and the woman pointed, without even having to ask what she meant, because everyone knew.

So they came to the courtyard. And the pots. And the steam. And the line.

They stood in the line for a long time. Philo watched the man with the ladle. He was very tall and very thin, with sharp, deep-set eyes and a long beard going grey early, and he did not look well. He looked like someone who slept too little and ate too little and worked too much, which, we are told, he did. His health troubled him all his life. But he moved along the pots steadily, dipping and pouring, dipping and pouring, and he spoke to each person as they came, a word or two, and sometimes a hand on a shoulder.

Beside him, stirring the biggest pot with a paddle like an oar, was a broad, red-faced woman with her sleeves rolled up, and her name, we will say, was Domnina, and she was in charge of the kitchen, and everyone, the priest included, knew it.

"Not like that," she was saying to him, as Philo came near. "You are slopping. Every drop you spill is a drop somebody does not get. Dip, lift, pour. Dip, lift, pour."

"Yes, Domnina," said the priest, very meekly.

"Clever men," said Domnina to nobody in particular. "All books and no wrists."

And then it was their turn. The priest filled a bowl for Philo's mother, thick and hot, with a piece of salted meat in it, and then he looked down at Philo, and filled another, and held it out.

Philo took it in both hands. It was so hot it nearly hurt, and it was the most wonderful thing he had ever held.

"Thank you, sir," he said.

"Thank God, not me," said the priest. "I only hold the spoon. And Domnina tells me I hold it badly." He looked at Philo more closely. "Where have you walked from?"

Philo told him.

"That is a long way on an empty stomach," said the priest. "Eat slowly. Your stomach has forgotten how. Then come back, and there will be more."

That is our invention, the words, and Philo, and Domnina too. But the soup and the meat and the man serving it are not. Gregory tells us that Basil gathered the starving into one place, men and women, little children and old people, everyone in distress, and set before them pots of the thick pulse soup that poor people ate, and the salted meat that was the food of the poor. And he served them himself, with his own hands, and with the help of his servants, and he was not ashamed of it. Gregory says he was imitating the Lord, who tied the towel round his waist and did not think it beneath him to wash the feet of his disciples. And he fed their souls as well as their bodies, Gregory says, with kind words, so that they had two kinds of food at once.

Whose feet will you wash? Basil had asked, years before, by the quiet river. Well. Here they were. Hundreds of them. Muddy, and tired, and hungry. And here he was.

Philo and his mother stayed in the city all that winter. Philo helped. He was too small for the paddle, but he could carry wood for the fires, and he could fetch water, and he could take bowls to the old people who could not stand in the line, and by the end of the winter Domnina had decided, gruffly, that he was the only person in the kitchen who did not slop.

And then, at last, at long last, the rain came back. And Philo and his mother walked home to the hills. And the neighbour had not eaten the goats after all. Or not all of them.

* * *

In the year three hundred and seventy, the old bishop of Caesarea died, and Basil was chosen to take his place.

It was not a simple election. There were people who did not want him, because he was too strict, or too clever, or too much in favour of the poor, or simply because he was not their candidate. Gregory's old father, who was himself a bishop and very ill, had himself carried all the way to Caesarea on a litter, so that he could cast his vote for Basil, and Gregory said afterwards that the old man came like a corpse and went home like a young man, full of life, because he had got what he wanted.

So now Basil was a bishop, and an important one. And he was a bishop at a dangerous time. The emperor in the East, Valens, favoured the side in the great argument that said the Son was a little bit less than the Father. And Valens was coming through Cappadocia, and he wanted the bishops there to fall into line.

He sent ahead of him his chief official, the prefect Modestus, who was one of the most powerful men in the Empire, and who was used to people being frightened of him.

Gregory tells us about the meeting, and here we have to say clearly that Gregory wrote it down after Basil had died, in a speech meant to be admired, and he gives it a fine shape. But the heart of it is very likely true, and it went something like this.

Modestus summoned Basil and threatened him. He said, do you not fear my power? Do you know what I can do to you? I can take away everything you own. I can send you into exile. I can have you tortured. I can have you put to death.

And Basil said, in effect: Is that all?

You cannot take my property, he said, because I have none, unless you want this worn-out cloak and a few books. You cannot send me into exile, because the whole earth belongs to God, and wherever you send me, I will be at home. As for torture, my body is so weak that I will probably die at the first blow. And as for death, that would be a kindness, because it would only send me sooner to God, whom I have been longing to see.

Modestus was astonished. He said that nobody had ever spoken to him like that before.

And Basil said, perhaps you have never met a proper bishop before.

The narrator would like to point out that this is a very good line, and that it is the kind of line you think of on the way home, and that Basil apparently thought of it at the time, which is very annoying of him.

Then, Gregory says, on the great feast of the Epiphany, early in the year three hundred and seventy-two, the emperor Valens came himself to the church in Caesarea. He came in with his guards, and he found the church packed full of people, and they were singing the psalms, and the sound was like the sea, like thunder. And at the front, by the altar, stood Basil, perfectly still, facing God, as calm as a column of stone, with his clergy around him like angels, paying no attention to the emperor whatsoever.

And Valens, Gregory says, was so overcome that his eyes went dark and his legs went weak. When the time came for him to bring his gift to the altar, nobody came forward to take it, because nobody knew whether the bishop would accept a gift from such an emperor, and the emperor stood there holding it, and swaying, until Basil reached out his hand and took it.

Valens did not, in the end, send Basil away. Gregory tells some more remarkable stories about why, but we will leave those for another night. What matters for our story is that the emperor went away, and, according to a later historian called Theodoret, he did one more thing first. He gave some fine land for the poor whom Basil cared for.

* * *

And on that land, and on more land beside it, just outside the walls of Caesarea, Basil built a new town.

He had been thinking about it since the famine. Feeding people in a courtyard for a winter was good. But there would be other famines. And even in good years there were always people with nowhere to go: travellers, beggars, orphans, the old, the sick, and above all the lepers, who in those days were driven out of every town and lived in caves and ditches, and whom nobody would touch.

So Basil built a place for them.

He wrote about it himself, because some people complained. The governor of Cappadocia, a man called Elias, was told that the bishop was building something huge outside the city, almost a second city, and that it was getting above itself. So Basil wrote to him, and we still have the letter. Whom are we harming, Basil asks, by building a place for strangers to stay, for people passing through on journeys, and for those who need care because they are sick? And by providing them with what they need: nurses, doctors, animals to carry them, people to guide them? And he says there are craftsmen there too, for all the trades a place like that needs to live. And there is a church in the middle of it all. And he suggests, rather cleverly, that a governor ought to be proud to have such a place in his province, not cross about it.

The letter seems to have worked.

Gregory tells us what it was like. Go a little way out of the city, he says, and see the new city, the storehouse of goodness, where sickness is looked at with the eyes of faith and sympathy is put to the test. And he says that Basil himself would go there and greet the lepers as brothers, and kiss them, and that he did not think it beneath him, a nobleman and a bishop, to care for their sores with his own hands, so that the rich and the powerful of Caesarea, seeing him, were ashamed, and began to do the same.

People called the place after him. They called it the Basileias, Basil's place. And historians today think it may have been one of the first true hospitals in the world: not just a shelter, but a place with beds, and nurses, and doctors whose whole work was to make the sick well, free, for anyone who came.

Now I am going to tell you what happened to Philo, though you will remember that we made him up.

When he was grown, a young man with a beard of his own, Philo came back to Caesarea, and he went out through the city gate and along the road to the new town. It was not finished. It was never quite finished, the way good things seldom are. But there were long low buildings with rows of beds in them. There was a courtyard, and a well, and a little church with a cross on top. There was a workshop where a lame man was teaching two boys to make baskets. And there was a kitchen. And in the kitchen, older and broader and redder than ever, stirring a pot with a paddle like an oar, was Domnina.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Philo," she said. "The boy who did not slop."

"I have come to help," said Philo.

"Of course you have," said Domnina. "Wash your hands. Dip, lift, pour."

And he stayed.

Now I want to slow down.

Basil was not a bishop for very long. Not quite nine years. He was never strong, and he wore himself out, writing his long careful books about God, and hundreds of letters, and preaching, and travelling over the snowy roads to hold the Church together, and looking after his new city. He was often ill, and often cross, and often misunderstood, even by Gregory, his best friend, with whom he had one or two real quarrels, as best friends sometimes do. But they always came back to each other.

He died in Caesarea, in the winter, on the first of January in the year three hundred and seventy-nine. That is the date the Church has always kept. Some historians think it may really have been a year or so earlier, and they argue about it politely in journals. He was not yet fifty.

The whole city came to his funeral. Gregory tells us that the crowd was so great that the streets could not hold it, and that people climbed onto the roofs and the porches to see him carried past, and that people of every kind wept for him together. Gregory himself was ill, and could not come, and it grieved him very much. But a few years later, he stood up and gave the great funeral speech about his friend that we have been listening to tonight, and it is one of the most beautiful things anyone has ever written about a friend.

And the new city outside the walls went on. It went on for centuries. The bishops who came after Basil kept it going, and the idea of it spread, from Caesarea to Constantinople and to cities all over the Christian world: the idea that a Christian town should have a place where the sick are cared for, free, by people who see the face of Christ in them. Every hospital with a Saint in its name, the narrator thinks, owes a little bit to the man with the ladle.

His prayers went on too. In the Orthodox Church, and in the Eastern Catholic Churches, there is a Divine Liturgy that bears Basil's name, and it is prayed ten times every year: on the first five Sundays of Lent, and on Holy Thursday and Holy Saturday, and on the eves of Christmas and of the Epiphany, and on his own feast day. It is long and solemn and beautiful, and scholars think it grew from an older prayer that Basil himself reshaped.

And in Greece, and among Greek families everywhere, there is a custom on his feast day, which is New Year's Day. The family bakes a round sweet cake, called the Vasilopita, Basil's bread, with a coin hidden inside it. At midnight, or at New Year's lunch, the head of the family cuts it, with a slice for Christ, and a slice for Saint Basil, and a slice for the poor, and a slice for the house, and then a slice for each person, oldest to youngest. And whoever finds the coin is said to be lucky for the whole year.

There is a story told with the cake. It says that once, when a greedy governor had taken the people's money and jewels, Basil got them back, and did not know how to return each thing to its owner, and so he had them baked into loaves, and gave out the loaves, and every person found their own treasure inside. That story is a folk tale, told centuries after Basil, and nobody should think it is history. But the narrator thinks it understood him very well. A man who gives you back what is yours, hidden inside a loaf of bread.

So, somewhere in Kayseri tonight, under the white mountain, in the cold, there are hospitals with the lights on, and nurses walking the corridors, and somebody, somewhere, stirring a big pot of soup for people who have none.

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source is Gregory of Nazianzus's funeral oration on Basil, his forty-third oration, given a few years after Basil's death. It tells of their student days at Athens, the famine and the soup, the election, the prefect Modestus, the emperor Valens at the Epiphany liturgy, and the new city outside Caesarea. It is a speech of praise, so the scenes are polished, and we told the meeting with Modestus and the scene in the church as Gregory's report. The gift of land is from the later historian Theodoret. Basil's own letters give us the river Iris, his waking from sleep, and his defence of the poorhouse to the governor Elias in letter ninety-four.

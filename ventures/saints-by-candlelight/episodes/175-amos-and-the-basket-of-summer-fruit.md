It is the middle of the morning, in the great stone courtyard of the king's sanctuary at Bethel, and a man is standing in the middle of it with dust on his feet.

He does not look like much. He is wearing the sort of rough brown cloak that shepherds wear, and his hands are brown too, and stained, as if he has spent a long time handling fruit. He has a staff. He has no attendants. He is not wearing anything that says he is anybody.

In front of him is a priest, a tall man in fine linen, with the confident look of somebody who has a letter from the king in his pocket, or near enough.

Now. Where is Tekoa?

If you go to Jerusalem, and walk south, you come to Bethlehem, which is about six miles. And if you keep going, a few more miles south and a little east, up into the hills, you come to Tekoa.

It was a small town on a high hill, and it was right on the edge. To the west of it were the farmed hills of Judah, with their terraces and vines and olive trees. It was not a famous place. But it does turn up in the old books now and again. It was a wise woman of Tekoa that Joab brought to King David, to tell him a story about her two sons and her last coal, so that the king would bring Absalom home. We told that story too. It was proud of them, I expect, in the way small towns are proud of anyone who has done anything.

And it produced Amos.

The very first verse of his book tells us who he was, and it is so short and plain that I want to give it to you almost exactly. The words of Amos, who was among the shepherds of Tekoa, which he saw concerning Israel in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel, two years before the earthquake.

But notice, first, which words the book uses for him. Among the shepherds, it says. The Hebrew word there is an unusual one. It is used only once anywhere else in the Old Testament, and that is for Mesha, the king of Moab, who the Second Book of Kings says was a sheep breeder, and paid the king of Israel a tribute of a hundred thousand lambs. So the word may mean something more than a boy with a stick. It may mean a man who kept flocks, and bred them, and knew his business. Nobody can be completely sure. Later in the book Amos calls himself a herdsman, with a different word again. And he says he was a dresser of sycamore figs.

And that last one needs explaining, because it is my favourite thing about him.

* * *

First of all, a sycamore in the Bible is not the tree we call a sycamore in England, with the helicopter seeds, and it is not the American sycamore with the peeling white bark either. It is a kind of fig tree. A big, spreading, generous fig tree, with low branches, very easy to climb. It is the same tree that Zacchaeus climbed in Jericho, when he was too short to see Jesus over the crowd.

It grows in warm, low places. It does not like the cold. And this is the curious thing, because Tekoa is high up in the hills, where it gets cold in winter, and sycamore figs do not grow well there. They grew down in the lowlands, in the warm foothills to the west, which were called the Shephelah, and down in the deep hot valley of the Jordan. The First Book of Chronicles even mentions that King David had an official in charge of the olive trees and the sycamore trees in the Shephelah.

So many people who study the book think that Amos must have travelled, in the right season, down from his high cold hill to where the sycamores grew, perhaps with his flocks, perhaps for the work. That is a guess. But it is a sensible guess.

And what does a dresser of sycamore figs do?

The sycamore fig, left alone, gives a lot of small fruit that does not ripen very well, and is not very sweet, and is sometimes full of little wasps. But the farmers of the old world had found out a trick. If you went round the tree when the figs were young, and nicked or scraped each one, gently, with a little blade, the figs would swell up and ripen in a few days, sweeter and bigger and without the wasps. An old Greek writer on plants called Theophrastus, a few centuries after Amos, wrote that this kind of fig would not ripen unless it was scraped, and that they scraped it with iron claws.

Each one. By hand. On a big tree with a great many figs on it.

So that was Amos. A man who spent his days out with the animals, and some of his seasons up a fig tree, carefully pinching and nicking hundreds of small green figs, one at a time, so that they would become sweet. It is slow, patient, fiddly work. It is not grand work. It is the kind of work that teaches you a great deal about fruit, and about weather, and about waiting.

It was not, you would have thought, much of a training for speaking in front of a king's priest.

Ira thinks about this. Why did you go and look, he says, if the sheep was already eaten?

He knew about lions. He knew how a roar goes into you and you cannot help it. And when God spoke to him, it was a bit like that. He did not choose it. He could not help it.

That is all. It does not say how. There is no burning bush in the Book of Amos, no voice in the night calling his name three times, no angel. It just says that one day the Lord took him from following the flock, and said to him, go, prophesy to my people Israel.

Not to Judah, notice, his own country. To Israel. To the north. To the rich kingdom over the border, which had its own king and its own sanctuaries and was not, frankly, in the habit of taking advice from sheep breeders from the south.

I imagine it was a bit like being asked to go and preach in the next country, to people who think your accent is funny.

The first thing he was shown was locusts. A great swarm of them, forming up just as the late spring crop was beginning to come up, after the king's mowings. And Amos did not say, oh good, now they will listen. He did something much more interesting. He prayed for them. O Lord God, he said, forgive, I beg you. How can Jacob stand? He is so small.

Jacob, there, is the whole people of Israel, called by the name of their old ancestor. How can Jacob stand? He is so small.

I think that is one of the tenderest things in the whole book. This is the rich, proud, ivory-bed kingdom he is talking about. And when he sees what is coming to them, he does not see them as rich and proud. He sees them as small. Small, and in danger, like a lamb.

And the book says that the Lord relented about this. It shall not be, said the Lord.

Then he was shown a fire. A great fire, so great that it was eating up the great deep, the waters under the earth, and was about to eat up the land. And Amos prayed again, almost the same words. O Lord God, cease, I beg you. How can Jacob stand? He is so small. And again the Lord relented. This also shall not be, said the Lord God.

And then he was shown a third thing, and this one was different. He saw the Lord standing beside a wall that had been built with a plumb line, and in his hand there was a plumb line.

If you have never seen a plumb line, it is the simplest tool in the world. It is a piece of string with a weight on the end, a lump of lead or a stone. You hold it up beside a wall, and the weight pulls the string perfectly straight down, and you can see at once whether your wall is straight or whether it is leaning. Builders still use them. It does not argue. It does not flatter. It just hangs there, and shows you.

And the Lord said, Amos, what do you see?

And Amos said, a plumb line.

And the Lord said, behold, I am setting a plumb line in the midst of my people Israel. I will never again pass by them.

And this time, the book does not say that Amos prayed. Perhaps he did. Perhaps he could not find the words. It does not say. It just tells us what was going to happen, to the high places and the sanctuaries of Israel, and to the house of Jeroboam.

It was not a long way, as the crow flies. From Tekoa to Jerusalem, about ten miles. From Jerusalem to Bethel, about another ten or a little more, up the long ridge road through the hills. And Bethel was just over the border, the first big place you came to in the northern kingdom.

We are going to imagine Ira going with him part of the way, and carrying a basket of figs for the journey. The book says nothing about this. But somebody has to carry the figs.

And as they walk, let me tell you about Bethel, because it is one of the most important places in the whole Old Testament, and it has a sad story.

Bethel means the house of God. And it got that name a very long time before, from Jacob. You might remember that one too. Jacob, running away from home, from his brother Esau, lay down one night in the open with a stone for his pillow, and dreamed of a ladder, or a stairway, set up on the earth with its top reaching to heaven, and the angels of God going up and coming down on it. And the Lord stood above it and promised him that he would be with him and bring him home. And when Jacob woke up, he said, surely the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it. This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven. And he called the place Bethel.

That is a beautiful thing for a place to be named after.

But then, after Solomon died and the kingdom split, the first Jeroboam, the one a hundred and fifty years before our Jeroboam, had a worry. He was afraid that if his people kept going down to Jerusalem to the temple to worship, they would end up going back to the king in Jerusalem as well. So he made two calves of gold, and he set one up in the far north at Dan, and the other one here, at Bethel. And he said to the people, you have gone up to Jerusalem long enough. Here are your gods, O Israel.

And he made his own priests, and his own feast days, and his own altar, and Bethel became the king's sanctuary. A royal shrine. A temple of the kingdom, as the priest would put it.

So by the time of our story, Bethel was a busy, wealthy, important place, full of priests and pilgrims and sacrifices and music. It was where the king's people went on the great feast days. It was the house of God, in name. And it was also, very much, the house of the king.

I imagine Amos walking up into it. A man from a small town in the south, with figs in his basket and dust on his feet, coming into the big shining sanctuary on a feast day, with the crowds and the smoke of the offerings and the singers and the harps. I imagine Ira, who has never seen anything like it, with his mouth open.

And I imagine Amos standing for a little while at the edge of it all, quite still, the way you stand at the edge of cold water before you go in.

Now, I have to be honest with you here. The Book of Amos gathers together a great many of his words, and it does not tell us which of them he said where, or when, or in what order. Some of them he may have said at Bethel. Some at Samaria, which he mentions often. Some, perhaps, only to the people who later wrote them down. So what I am about to tell you is his words, from his book. But the crowd, and the order, and the feast day, are our picture.

And I want to start where the book starts, because it is very clever.

The first thing in the book, after that opening verse, is a series of speeches against the neighbours. Against Damascus, the capital of the Arameans to the north east, for their cruelty in war. Then against Gaza, down on the coast, in the land of the Philistines, for carrying off whole villages and selling them as slaves. Then against Tyre, the great sea city to the north, for the same thing, and for forgetting a covenant of brothers. Then against Edom, to the south east, for chasing his brother with the sword. Then against the Ammonites. Then against Moab.

And each one starts the same way. Thus says the Lord, for three transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not turn it back. For three transgressions of Gaza, and for four. For three transgressions of Tyre, and for four. It is like a drumbeat.

And you can imagine, if he said this to a crowd in Israel, how it went down. Very well indeed. Here is a prophet, people would think, who knows who the real villains are. Damascus! Yes! Gaza! Quite right! The Edomites, honestly, don't get me started on the Edomites.

And then he says, for three transgressions of Judah, and for four.

Judah! His own country, in the south! Well, the crowd would think, fair enough, he is being even-handed, we always said there was something odd about Judah.

And then he says, for three transgressions of Israel, and for four.

And it goes very quiet.

Because that is the one he came for. All the others were the way in. And what he says about Israel is not about cruelty in war, or slave raids on foreign villages. It is about how they treat their own people, at home, in the market and the law court, in the good years.

They sell the righteous for silver, he says, and the needy for a pair of sandals. They trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth, and turn aside the way of the afflicted.

For a pair of sandals. That is a phrase that stays with you. It seems to mean that a poor man might be sold into slavery to pay a debt as small as the price of a pair of shoes. Or perhaps that a judge would decide against him for a bribe that small. Either way, it is about how cheap a person had become.

He kept going, over whatever days and weeks he was there. And I should say that he was not, in the ordinary sense, polite.

He talked to the rich ladies of Samaria, who told their husbands to bring them something to drink, and called them cows of Bashan. Bashan was a region famous for its fat, sleek, well-fed cattle. So it was a bit like standing up in an expensive part of town and calling the people at the café prize heifers. I do not recommend it as a way of making friends. But it was memorable.

He talked about the winter houses and the summer houses, and the houses of ivory. He talked about the people who lay on beds of ivory and stretched out on their couches, and ate lambs from the flock and calves from the stall, and sang idle songs to the sound of the harp, and drank wine out of bowls, and anointed themselves with the finest oils. And were not grieved, he said, over the ruin of Joseph. Not grieved at all. They were having a lovely time, and their country was rotting underneath them, and they had not noticed.

And then, in the middle of all of this, he would say something completely different. Something so quiet and so lovely that you could almost miss it.

Seek me, and live, says the Lord.

Seek good, and not evil, that you may live, he said. Hate evil, and love good, and establish justice in the gate. It may be that the Lord, the God of hosts, will be gracious.

It may be. He did not say it was too late. He kept leaving the door open.

And he would look up, I think, at the sky. Because he was a shepherd, and shepherds look at the sky a great deal, at night, out by the fold. And he said, seek him who made the Pleiades and Orion, and turns deep darkness into the morning, and darkens the day into night, who calls for the waters of the sea, and pours them out on the face of the earth. The Lord is his name.

The Pleiades, the little cluster of stars like a handful of sugar. And Orion, the great hunter with his belt. They are both still there. If you go outside on a clear winter night, you can see the very same stars that Amos was pointing at, over the wilderness of Judah, nearly three thousand years ago.

And then he said the line that he is most famous for. People who do not know anything else about Amos know this line, even if they do not know it is his.

He said that God did not want their feasts and their offerings and the noise of their songs, while they treated the poor as they did. But let justice roll down like waters, he said, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.

Not a trickle. Not a stream that runs in the rainy season and dries up in the summer, which is what most streams in that country did. An ever-flowing stream. One that keeps on going all year round.

You will not be surprised to hear that the priest of Bethel was not pleased.

His name was Amaziah. And the book tells us that he did two things.

First, he sent a message to the king. To Jeroboam, in Samaria. And the message said, Amos has conspired against you in the midst of the house of Israel. The land is not able to bear all his words. For thus Amos says, Jeroboam shall die by the sword, and Israel must surely go into exile away from his land.

The land is not able to bear all his words. I find that quite a remarkable thing for him to say. As if words were a weight, and the country was buckling under them.

And then, without waiting, it seems, to hear what the king would say, Amaziah went to Amos himself. And that is the moment we began with.

O seer, go. Flee away to the land of Judah, and eat bread there, and prophesy there. But never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom.

Eat bread there. That is a little dig, I think. He is saying, you are a professional, are you not? You are doing this for a living. Go and do it for a living somewhere else. Go and be paid in Judah. We have our own prophets here, thank you, and our own priests, and they know which side their bread is buttered on.

And Amos answered him. And this is the line that has been quoted for nearly three thousand years.

I am no prophet, he said, nor a prophet's son. But I am a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore figs. And the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, go, prophesy to my people Israel.

I am not a professional, he means. I do not belong to a guild of prophets. I did not inherit this, like a family business. I was not looking for this job, and I am not being paid for it. I had a perfectly good job, with sheep and figs. I was taken off it. I am here because I was sent.

And then, because he had been sent, he gave Amaziah the hard word that had been given to him. It was a word about what would happen to Amaziah and his own family, when the land was taken, and about Israel going into exile away from its land. It is a hard word, and I will not tell it all to you at bedtime, but it is there in the book, and he did not soften it.

The book does not tell us what Amaziah said back. It does not tell us whether Amos went home that day, or the next day, or stayed a while longer. It moves straight on to the basket.

* * *

Because then, Amos says, the Lord showed him a fourth thing.

Thus the Lord God showed me. Behold, a basket of summer fruit.

The book is quite clear that this is another vision, like the locusts and the fire and the plumb line. Something he was shown. But I like to think that it was something he had seen a great many times with his own eyes, so that he knew exactly what he was looking at. A big woven basket, at the end of the summer, piled with ripe fruit. Figs, very likely. Perhaps the kind of basket that Ira had carried up the road, now with the last few figs at the bottom of it, soft and dark and almost too ripe.

The very end of the harvest. The last fruit of the year.

And the Lord said, Amos, what do you see?

And Amos said, a basket of summer fruit.

And the Lord said, the end has come upon my people Israel. I will never again pass by them.

Now, this one is a pun, and it only really works in Hebrew, so let me explain it. The word for summer fruit, the late fruit at the end of the season, is something like kayits. And the word for the end is something like kets. Kayits. Kets. The summer fruit. The end. They sound almost the same.

So when Amos looked at the basket and said what it was, he was already, without knowing it, saying the word for what was coming. Like looking at a calendar and saying, it's the last page.

And straight after the basket, the book gives us some of his sharpest words about the market. About the traders who said, when will the new moon be over, so that we can sell grain? And the sabbath, so that we can sell wheat? They could hardly wait for the holy days to finish, so that they could get back to business. And when they did, he said, they made the ephah small and the shekel great.

The ephah was the measure they used for the grain. The shekel was the weight for the silver. So making the ephah small meant giving short measure, a smaller basket of grain than you said. And making the shekel great meant using a heavy weight on your scales, so that you took more silver than the price. Both at once. Cheating on the way in and the way out. And they sold the refuse of the wheat, he said, the sweepings off the floor. And they bought the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals.

There it is again. A pair of sandals.

And I think you can see, now, why it was a fig dresser who was sent. A man who had spent years going round a tree, nicking each small fig by hand, one by one, so that it would ripen sweet. A man who knew exactly how much fruit was in a basket, because he had picked it. He knew what an honest measure looked like. He knew what work was worth. He knew that every single one of those small, needy people that the rich were selling for a pair of sandals had been made, carefully, by hand, by God.

How can Jacob stand? He is so small.

* * *

So, in our story, Amos went home.

We will imagine him and Ira walking back down the long ridge road, south, past Jerusalem, past Bethlehem, up the last hill to Tekoa, as the evening comes on. The book does not tell us he went back to his flocks. But it does not tell us he didn't, either, and I have always rather hoped that he did.

I imagine Ira's mother at the door, with the lamp lit.

Well, she says. How was Bethel?

And Ira thinks about it, and says, very big. Very shiny. And the priest told him to go home.

And did he? says his mother.

Ira looks at Amos, who is already taking off his sandals by the door, and banging the dust out of them, and looking at the sheep.

He has now, says Ira.

* * *

There is one more thing I want to tell you, before the wind-down, because otherwise you might go to sleep thinking the Book of Amos is only about the end.

It is not. Right at the end of the book, after all the hard words, there is a sudden change, like the weather turning. And the Lord says, in that day, I will raise up the booth of David that is fallen, and repair its breaches, and raise up its ruins, and rebuild it as in the days of old.

A booth is a little shelter, a hut made of branches, like the ones people put up in the fields at harvest time, to sleep in while they watched the crops. The great house of David, the kingdom, would one day be fallen down to a little broken hut. And God would raise it up again.

And then the book ends with the most beautiful picture of a harvest. Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, when the ploughman shall overtake the reaper, and the treader of grapes him who sows the seed. The mountains shall drip sweet wine, and all the hills shall flow with it. And I will restore the fortunes of my people Israel, and they shall rebuild the ruined cities and live in them. They shall plant vineyards and drink their wine, and they shall make gardens and eat their fruit. I will plant them on their land, and they shall never again be plucked up out of the land that I have given them.

The ploughman shall overtake the reaper. Think about what that means. The harvest is so big that they are still reaping it when it is time to plough for next year. The grapes are so many that they are still treading them when it is time to sow. There is no gap. No hungry season. No waiting. It all runs together into one long plenty.

It is a fig dresser's dream of heaven, really. Never-ending fruit.

[pause]

So what happened to Amos?

The honest answer is that we do not know. The book does not tell us. It tells us his words, and his visions, and the day at Bethel, and then it ends.

There is an old book, written much later, called the Lives of the Prophets, which collects traditions about where each of the prophets came from and where they were buried. It says that Amos was struck down by the son of the priest of Bethel, and that he made his way, still living, home to his own country, and died there a few days later, and was buried at Tekoa, in his own land. That is a later tradition, not Scripture, and we cannot know if it is true. But if it is, then he went home to God from his own hill, with the wilderness below him, which is where I think he would have wanted to be.

And the earthquake. Two years before the earthquake, the first verse said. It must have been a famous one, because more than two hundred years later, the prophet Zechariah could still say to the people, you shall flee as you fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah, king of Judah, and expect them to know exactly what he meant. Some archaeologists think they have found signs of a great earthquake from about this time, in walls that had cracked and fallen at old cities in the north. Other scholars are more cautious. Either way, the people remembered it.

And they remembered that Amos had spoken before it.

The good years did not last. Within about forty years of the days when Amos spoke at Bethel, the great empire of Assyria had come down from the north, and Samaria fell, and a great many of the people of the northern kingdom were carried away into exile, just as the hard words had said. Bethel, the king's sanctuary, was a ruin.

But the book of the herdsman from Tekoa was kept. Somebody wrote it down, and somebody kept it, and somebody copied it, and it went on being read.

Saint Stephen, in the Acts of the Apostles, quoted Amos, in his long speech before the council, just before he went home to God. And at the very first council of the Church, in Jerusalem, when the apostles were trying to decide whether people who were not Jews could become Christians without first becoming Jews, it was Saint James who stood up and settled the matter. And he did it by quoting Amos. The booth of David that is fallen, he said. God is raising it up again, so that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the nations.

Fig trees, and lambs, and a fallen hut. And the whole world coming home to it.

And in our own time, a great many people have quoted him, often without knowing it was him. Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. Martin Luther King said it, more than once, in his letters and his speeches. It is carved on a wall in a memorial in America. It is written on banners. It is a line that refuses to stop flowing.

The Orthodox Church remembers the prophet Amos every year on the fifteenth of June, at the very beginning of summer, a long time before the summer fruit.

[pause]

And now it is evening, back in Tekoa, on the last night of our story.

The sheep are in the fold. Ira has been sent to bed, and has gone, after only a little argument. His mother has put the lamp out.

And Amos is sitting outside, on a flat stone at the edge of the hill, with his cloak around him, because it gets cold up there at night, even in the summer.

Below him, the wilderness goes down and down in the dark, ridge after ridge, all the way to the Dead Sea. You cannot see it now. But you can feel it there, the great quiet emptiness, the way you can feel the sea at night even when you cannot see it.

Somewhere, very far away, a lion might roar. But tonight, none does.

Behind him, the town is asleep. Somewhere a dog is dreaming and twitching its feet. Somewhere a baby wakes up and is fed and goes back to sleep. In the fold, a ewe shifts her weight and sighs.

And above him, the sky is full of stars. More stars than you have ever seen, if you live in a town with street lights. And there, if you know where to look, is the little cluster of the Pleiades, like a handful of sugar. And there, rising over the wilderness, is Orion, with his belt of three stars.

He who made the Pleiades and Orion, and turns deep darkness into the morning.

And Amos sits and looks at them for a long time.

He is not a prophet, nor a prophet's son. He is a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore figs. And tomorrow, very likely, there will be figs to see to.

But the Lord took him from following the flock. And the Lord, he knows, has not finished with any of them yet. Not with Israel. Not with Judah. Not with the cows of Bashan or the priest of Bethel. Not with Ira, asleep with his mouth open. Not with him.

How can Jacob stand? He is so small.

But the one who holds the plumb line also made the stars. And the darkness, every single night, turns into morning.

[pause]

Now, a few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Book of Amos, told straight, in a plain rendering of the old words. The day at Bethel, Amaziah's message to the king and his words to Amos, and Amos's answer, are in chapter seven, verses ten to seventeen. The locusts, the fire and the plumb line are in the first half of chapter seven, and the basket of summer fruit, with the words about the market, is in chapter eight. The speeches against the nations are in chapters one and two, and the cows of Bashan, the beds of ivory, seek me and live, the Pleiades and Orion, and let justice roll down like waters, are all in chapters three to six. The booth of David and the ploughman who overtakes the reaper are at the very end, in chapter nine. The reign of Jeroboam and the word of Jonah come from the Second Book of Kings, chapter fourteen.

The book gathers Amos's words without saying which he spoke where, so the crowd at Bethel, the order of the speeches and the feast day are our picture, not the book's. Where the sycamores grew, and that Amos may have travelled to tend them, is a sensible guess by people who study the book, not something it says. That Amos was struck down and buried at Tekoa comes from the Lives of the Prophets, a much later book, and is a tradition, not Scripture.

Ira and his mother, the lion and the two legs and the piece of an ear as Ira heard it, the basket of figs for the road, and every word they say to one another are our own invention. We have tried not to add anything to what Amos, Amaziah or the Lord say or do. The whole Book of Amos is only nine short chapters, and it is a wonderful thing to read in one sitting, out loud if you can.

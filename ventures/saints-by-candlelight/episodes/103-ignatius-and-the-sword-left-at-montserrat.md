It is the middle of the night, and a young man is standing in a church on a mountain.

He has been standing for a long time. His legs hurt, especially the right one, which does not work quite as well as it used to. Every so often he kneels down on the stone floor, and then, when his knees hurt more than his legs, he stands up again.

In front of him, high up behind the altar, a small dark statue of the Mother of God sits with her Child on her knee, lit by a few lamps. And on the altar rail in front of her, where he put them a few hours ago, lie a sword and a dagger. Good ones. Expensive ones. The best thing he owns, or rather, owned, because he has just given them away.

He is wearing a robe made of sacking. It itches. One of his feet has a rope sandal on it. The other foot is bare.

A month ago, he would not have been seen dead dressed like this.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

After a long, happy stretch of nights in Constantinople, with emperors and icons and abbesses who answered back, we are going west tonight. A long way west. And a long way forward in time, too, about seven hundred years after Saint Kassia, to the Basque country, in the green, rainy, steep corner of the world where the mountains of northern Spain run down to the Bay of Biscay.

I should say at the start that Saint Ignatius is a Catholic saint. He lived long after the Churches of East and West had gone their separate ways, so he belongs to the Catholic calendar. But he is a man a great many people of every tradition have found worth listening to, and I think you will see why.

Now, the wonderful thing about tonight's story is where it comes from. Most of our saints come to us through other people. A friend wrote a life. A monk wrote it down three hundred years later. A chronicler heard it from someone who heard it from someone. But near the end of his life, in Rome, Ignatius was persuaded, very reluctantly, to sit down with a young Portuguese priest called Luis Goncalves da Camara and tell him the story of how God had led him. Goncalves listened, walked straight back to his room, and wrote down what he had heard, as close to word for word as he could.

And Ignatius told it in a very odd way. He never says I. He talks about himself as he. And very often, instead of his name, he calls himself the pilgrim. The pilgrim did this. The pilgrim thought that. As if he were telling the story of somebody he had known a long time ago and was a little embarrassed by. Which, in a way, he was.

So tonight, when I tell you what Ignatius thought, or felt, or noticed, it is usually because he told us himself. When I tell you what somebody said to him, it is usually me, making it up. I will try to make it clear which is which.

He was not called Ignatius to begin with. He was born Inigo, Inigo Lopez de Loyola, in about fourteen ninety-one, in a big square stone house with a brick top storey, at a place called Loyola, near the little town of Azpeitia. His family were minor nobility, the kind of family that owned a tower house and some farms and a mill and had a great many opinions about honour. He was the youngest of a big family. Thirteen children, by the usual count, though the old records disagree, which is what happens when there are that many children running about.

His mother died when he was small. His father died when he was a teenager. And Inigo was sent off, as boys of his kind were, to be a page in a grand household, in his case the house of the treasurer of the kingdom of Castile, at a town called Arevalo, a long way south.

And there, I am sorry to say, he learned all the wrong things. Or perhaps I should say, he learned all the things a young gentleman of his time was supposed to learn. He learned to ride. He learned to fence. He learned to dance and to write a pretty letter and to dress beautifully. He grew his hair long, down to his shoulders, which was the fashion. He read romances of chivalry, which were the adventure novels of the day, enormous books about knights who rescue princesses and fight giants and never seem to need lunch. He gambled. He got into fights. He was once taken to court in his home town for some brawl at carnival time, though the records do not say exactly what he had done, and his defence was that he was a cleric and so could not be tried by an ordinary court, which, as he had not the slightest intention of becoming a cleric, was very cheeky of him.

The first line of his own story sums it up. In our plain words, it goes something like this: until he was twenty-six years old, he was a man given over to the vanities of the world, and what he enjoyed most was the exercise of arms, with a great and empty desire to win fame.

I like that he told it straight. No polishing. He was vain, and he wanted to be famous, and he wanted, more than anything, to be admired.

Which brings us to Pamplona.

* * *

In the spring of fifteen twenty-one, Inigo was about thirty, and in the service of the Duke of Najera, who was the viceroy of the kingdom of Navarre for the king of Spain. And the king of France, who had his own ideas about who ought to rule Navarre, sent an army over the mountains.

The French army came down on the city of Pamplona. The city opened its gates to them, because, frankly, a lot of the people in it rather agreed with the French. That left the castle, or rather the fortress, and a small garrison inside it. The French guns were set up outside. And everyone in the fortress, looking out over the walls at the size of the army, came to the sensible conclusion that they ought to surrender.

Everyone except Inigo.

He argued. He persuaded. He went on about honour until the commander agreed to fight. And then, since there was no priest about, he made his confession to one of his fellow soldiers, which was an old custom in a pinch, and went up onto the walls.

And on the twentieth of May, fifteen twenty-one, a French cannonball came over the wall and went between his legs. It broke the right one badly, and it hurt the left one too. And when Inigo went down, the fortress surrendered almost at once, since he had been the only reason it had not done so already.

Now here is a thing I find rather touching. The French were very kind to him. Their own surgeons looked after his leg, and after a couple of weeks, they had him carried home, on a stretcher, over the hills, all the way to Loyola. Enemies, you understand. That is the sort of thing that the romances of chivalry said knights ought to do, and for once somebody actually did it.

* * *

At Loyola, things went badly.

The bones had not been set properly, or they had moved on the journey, and the doctors decided they would have to break the leg again and reset it. In those days, of course, there was nothing to put you to sleep for that. I am not going to describe it. Ignatius, in his story, says only that he said nothing during it, and showed no sign of pain except clenching his fists very tightly.

After that, he got worse. He got a fever. He could not eat. The doctors said that if he did not improve by midnight, he would die. He was told to make his confession, and he did, and received the last sacraments. It was the eve of the feast of Saint Peter and Saint Paul, at the end of June, and Inigo had always had a soft spot for Saint Peter. And that night, at midnight, the fever broke, and he began to get better.

So far, so good. But when the bones knitted together, one of them knitted wrong. There was a lump of bone sticking out below his knee. And his right leg was now shorter than his left.

And this was a disaster. Not because of the walking. Because of the boots. A gentleman of fashion, in those days, wore tight, fitted boots up to the knee, and a lump below the knee spoiled the line completely. So Inigo, and I want you to understand that he tells us this himself, with a sort of rueful honesty, Inigo asked the doctors whether the lump could be taken off.

They said yes, but it would hurt more than anything so far. He said go ahead. And they did. And then, to try to lengthen the leg, they put it in a sort of stretching contraption for days and days. All for a boot. He calls it, in his story, a martyrdom he chose for himself out of vanity.

Well. There he was, then. In bed, in the big stone house at Loyola, flat on his back, for weeks and weeks, with nothing to do.

This is where our invented friend comes in.

Let us imagine that in the kitchen at Loyola there was a boy of about ten called Joanes, which is a good Basque name, and that it was Joanes's job to carry soup up the stairs. Somebody must have carried the soup. We are just giving him a name.

And one afternoon, let us say, Joanes comes in with the soup, and Inigo, who is bored almost to tears, says to him, "Joanes. Go and find me a book."

"What sort of book, sir?"

"A good one. Knights. Giants. Battles. Amadis of Gaul, if they have it. Something with a dragon in it."

And Joanes goes all through the house, and comes back up the stairs some time later with two very large books in his arms and an apologetic face.

"There aren't any knights, sir," he says. "There's these."

That much is true. Ignatius tells us that he asked for romances of chivalry, which he was used to reading, and there were none in the house. Instead, they brought him two books in Spanish. One was a Life of Christ, a great big devotional work by a Carthusian monk called Ludolph of Saxony, which walks you through the Gospels scene by scene and asks you to imagine yourself there. The other was a book of the lives of the saints. There is a later tradition that it was his sister-in-law Magdalena, the wife of his older brother Martin, the lord of the house, who gave him these books. It may be so. Either way, they were the only books in the house, which tells you something about the house.

And Inigo, because he had nothing else to do, started to read.

* * *

Now here is the heart of tonight's story. It is a quiet thing, and it all happens inside one man's head, in a bed, in the Basque country, and it changed his whole life.

He read about Saint Francis giving away everything he had. He read about Saint Dominic preaching and praying through the night. He read about the hermits in the desert. And he found himself thinking, well. What if I did that? Saint Francis did this. So I should do it too. Saint Dominic did that. So I should do it too. It was almost like a competition. The same part of him that had wanted to be the bravest knight in Navarre now wanted to be a better saint than Saint Francis. Which, you will admit, is a very Inigo way to begin.

But he did not spend all his time on saints. He also spent a great many hours, he tells us, daydreaming about other things. About the deeds he would do in the service of a certain great lady. He never says who she was. Only that she was not of the ordinary nobility, which means she was very far above him. He would imagine the journeys he would make to her city, and the clever and charming things he would say to her, and the brave things he would do to impress her. He would lose himself in this for hours.

And then he would go back to thinking about the saints. And then back to the lady. Back and forth, back and forth, the way your mind does when you are stuck in bed.

And then, one day, he noticed something.

When he daydreamed about the lady, it was lovely while it lasted. But afterwards, when he stopped, he felt flat. Dry, he calls it. Empty. Discontented. Like the feeling after you have eaten a whole bag of sweets.

But when he daydreamed about going barefoot to Jerusalem, and eating only herbs, and doing all the hard things the saints did, it was not only pleasant while he was thinking about it. Afterwards, when he stopped, he was still happy. The gladness stayed.

He says that for a long time he did not notice this at all. And then one day his eyes were opened a little, and he began to wonder at the difference, and to think about it. And he came to see that one set of thoughts left him sad, and the other left him glad, and that this might be telling him something about where each set of thoughts came from.

That is all. That is the whole discovery. A man in bed noticing how his daydreams leave him feeling afterwards.

But Ignatius came back to it again and again for the rest of his life, and turned it into a way of praying that millions of people have used since. The idea is very simple and very hard. Pay attention to what goes on inside you. Not only to what you feel now, but to what you feel after. Notice what leaves you more at peace, more loving, more yourself. Notice what leaves you hollow. God speaks in that, too. Not only in thunder, but in the aftertaste of an afternoon.

I think that is a lovely thing to take to bed. You might try it tonight, if you like. What, in today, left you gladder afterwards? Just notice. You do not have to do anything about it yet.

Joanes, our Joanes, might have noticed something too. Let us say that he came up with the soup one evening and found the gentleman not reading, and not daydreaming, but sitting up in bed with a big book of blank paper, very good paper, polished and ruled with lines, and two little pots of ink.

"What are you doing, sir?"

"Copying out the best bits," says Inigo.

And this is true. He tells us that he started copying out passages from the Life of Christ and the lives of the saints, in a beautiful hand, because he had always had a beautiful hand. And he wrote the words of Christ in red ink. And the words of Our Lady in blue. He filled nearly three hundred pages like this.

"Why two colours?" asks Joanes.

"So that I can find them," says Inigo, which is our invention, but I think it is also probably true.

Around this time, Ignatius tells us, one night as he lay awake, he saw clearly an image of Our Lady with the holy Child Jesus. And it filled him with such consolation for a long time that afterwards he felt a great loathing for all his old vanities, as if they had been wiped out of his soul. That is his report of something that happened inside him, and I give it to you as he gave it. He says that from that night onwards, he never again gave in to the sort of thoughts he used to have.

He spent the long evenings now looking out of the window at the sky and the stars. He says it gave him great comfort, and a great desire to serve God. And his family began to notice. His brother Martin, the lord of the house, noticed most of all, and did not like it one bit.

* * *

By the end of February in fifteen twenty-two, his leg was healed enough to ride. He was still limping, and he would limp for the rest of his life. He told his brother he was off to visit the Duke of Najera, which was true as far as it went.

His brother took him aside, room by room, Ignatius says, and begged him not to throw his life away. Martin could see where all this reading was leading. You have such prospects, he said, more or less. Think of the family. Think of what people will say. Ignatius says he slipped away from him without exactly lying, which was a skill he had clearly not entirely lost.

So off he went, on a mule.

Now, I have decided that this mule was a very particular sort of mule. Nothing in the sources says so. But I like to think of her as a mule of firm views, who had carried a great many gentlemen in her time and was not impressed by any of them. She had a white nose and an expression of deep patience. And she was about to become an important person in the history of the Church, though she did not know it.

He went to the Duke's house and collected some money that was owed to him. He gave some of it to pay off debts he had in the town, and some of it to repair a statue of Our Lady that was in a bad way, and he rode on east towards Catalonia. He had decided to go to Jerusalem, to live where the Lord had lived. And on the way, he had heard of a great shrine of Our Lady, high up in a strange, jagged mountain near Barcelona, called Montserrat. He would stop there first.

And on the road, there happened one of the most extraordinary stories that any saint has ever told about himself.

He fell in with another man on the road, riding the same way. Ignatius calls him a Moor, which in Spain at that time meant a Muslim, or one whose family had been Muslim and had been made to become Christian. The two men rode along together and got talking, and the talk got onto Our Lady. And the other man said that he could believe she had conceived Christ without a man, but that he could not believe she had remained a virgin afterwards. And he gave his reasons. And Inigo, who was still very new at all this and did not know much theology, argued back as best he could. And the other man would not be persuaded. And eventually the man rode on ahead, rather quickly.

And Inigo rode on alone, getting crosser and crosser.

He tells us that he began to feel that he had not done his duty. That this man had insulted Our Lady, and that a proper knight, a proper servant of Our Lady, would ride after him and, in his own words, give him a few blows with his dagger. He went back and forth over it. His old self said, honour! Your new self is a coward! And he could not decide.

So he did what any sensible person would do. He left it to the mule.

The road went on to a fork. One way went into the village where the other man had said he was going. The other way was the main road. And Inigo decided: I will let the reins go loose. If the mule goes into the village, I will go after him. If the mule stays on the main road, I will leave him alone.

I would very much like to have seen the mule's face at this point.

The village was only thirty or forty paces off the road, he says, and the way into it was broad and good. It would have been the easiest thing in the world for a mule to wander down it.

And the mule, being a mule of firm views and deep good sense, took the main road.

Ignatius, telling this story thirty years later, as the head of a religious order of a thousand men, with nearly every word of it against himself, does not try to excuse it. He tells it, I think, so that we can see how far he had to go. He had given his heart to God. He had not yet learned what that meant. He still thought that serving Our Lady might mean hitting people. It would take God a long time to teach him otherwise, and the first lesson, it seems, was taught by a mule.

So, if you are ever unsure what to do, and you are cross, and you are about to do something you will regret, remember the mule. Take the main road. Sleep on it. It is very rarely the wrong thing to do.

* * *

In a town at the foot of the mountain, Inigo bought himself the things a pilgrim needed. A piece of rough sacking, to be made into a long robe. A pilgrim's staff. A little gourd for water. And one rope sandal. Only one, because his right leg was still swollen and bandaged at night, and he thought one sandal would do. You will remember that from the beginning.

And then he went up Montserrat.

If you have never seen it, Montserrat is one of the strangest mountains in the world. It looks as though it was made out of a huge heap of enormous grey loaves of bread, all standing up on end, or as if a giant had been playing with clay and left it out in the rain. The name means the saw-toothed mountain. And halfway up it, on a ledge, there was and still is a monastery of Benedictine monks, and in their church, a small, very old wooden statue of the Mother of God and her Child, gone dark with age and candle smoke, so that people call her the little dark one.

When he arrived, Inigo went to find a priest to hear his confession. And not just an ordinary confession. He wanted to make a confession of his whole life, everything he could remember, from the beginning. He wrote it all out first, to make sure he got it all in, and it took him three days. The monk who heard it was a Frenchman. And Inigo arranged with him that the mule would be given to the monastery.

So the mule, our mule, retired to the stables of Montserrat, where I like to think she lived a long and comfortable life, full of oats, and was quietly respected by the other mules for her good judgement.

And then Inigo did the thing from the beginning of the story.

He had read in the romances of chivalry about the night before a young man was made a knight. The young man would keep watch all night in a chapel, beside his armour, praying. It was called a vigil of arms. And Inigo, whose head was still full of those books, thought: I will do that. But for a different kind of knighthood.

So on the evening of the twenty-fourth of March, fifteen twenty-two, which was the eve of the feast of the Annunciation, when the angel came to Mary at Nazareth, he went out in the dark, and found a poor man, a beggar, and gave him all his fine clothes. Every stitch. The good doublet, the good hose, the lot. And put on his sacking.

And he went into the church, and he laid his sword and his dagger at the altar of Our Lady. And he kept watch, all night, standing and kneeling, with his staff in his hand, before the little dark statue.

I like to imagine that the monks, going in and out for the night prayers, noticed the odd figure in the sacking and simply let him be. Monks who live on a mountain with a famous shrine have seen a great many odd figures.

And at first light, he heard Mass, and went out of the church, and walked away down the mountain, with nothing at all. He did not go towards Barcelona, where people might know him. He took the other road, down to a little town called Manresa. He meant to stay a few days.

But before he had got very far, there is a little scene that I find one of the most beautiful in the whole story. Somebody came running after him down the road. A man from the monastery, I think, or from the town. And he said, did you give your clothes to a poor man last night? Because they have arrested him. They think he stole them.

Let us imagine it.

"He says a gentleman gave them to him," says the man, out of breath. "Nobody believes him. A beggar in a doublet like that? Is it true?"

And Inigo, who had not wanted anyone to know, had to say yes. It was true. He had given them away.

And Ignatius tells us that when he heard that the poor man was in trouble because of him, the tears came to his eyes. For the poor man, whom he had meant to help, and had got into trouble instead.

He does not cry, in his story, for his leg, or for his lost fame. He cries for a beggar he did not know, whose name he never tells us. And I think that is when you can really see that something had changed in him.

[pause]

* * *

He stayed in Manresa nearly a year.

I will not tell you all of it tonight, because a lot of it was hard, and this is a bedtime story. He lived very roughly. He begged for his food. He let his hair and his nails grow, because he had spent so long being vain about his appearance that he thought he ought to stop caring about it altogether. He fasted far too much. He prayed for seven hours a day. He looked after the sick in the town's hospital. There is a cave above the river, just outside the town, where tradition says he would go to pray, and you can visit it today, though Ignatius himself does not mention it.

And for a while he was very troubled. He was tormented by worry that he had not confessed his sins properly, that he had left something out, and he went back again and again, until he was in real misery. People who have suffered from scruples, as this is called, will know exactly what he means, and they have often found it a great comfort that a saint went through it too.

But slowly, God was teaching him. He says himself that God dealt with him in those months the way a schoolmaster deals with a child he is teaching. And slowly, he learned to stop. To eat properly. To cut his hair and his nails. To be sensible. He learned that the extremes did not make him holier. They only made him ill. And he took everything that he learned, everything he noticed going on inside himself, and he began to write it down in a little notebook, which over the next twenty years would become a small book called the Spiritual Exercises. It is not really a book to read. It is a book to do, like a book of exercises for the body, only these are for the soul. A month of prayer, step by step, with somebody to guide you. People still make them, all over the world.

And at Manresa, Ignatius tells us, God gave him some great graces. Once, while he was praying the office of Our Lady on the steps of a church, he says that his understanding was lifted up and he saw the Holy Trinity in the form of three keys of a keyboard instrument, three notes sounding together. That was how it came to him. Three notes, one chord. And it moved him so much that he cried and could not stop crying, and all day long he could talk of nothing else.

And once, he was walking along beside the river Cardoner, which runs below the town, on his way to a little church, and he sat down for a while facing the river, which runs deep there. And as he sat, he says, the eyes of his understanding began to open. He did not see a vision. But he understood a great many things, about the faith and about learning, all at once, with such a great light that everything seemed new to him. He says that if you added up everything he had learned in all the rest of his life, it would not come to as much as he learned in that one hour by the river. He gives us that as his own report, and there is nothing more to say about it than that.

When he got up from the river, he went and knelt before a wayside cross, to give thanks.

And I think, if you have ever sat by a river for a while and had something become suddenly clear, you will know at least a very small piece of what he means.

* * *

Now I am going to go a little faster, because there is a great deal of road ahead, and the candles are getting shorter.

In the spring of fifteen twenty-three, he set out for Jerusalem. He went by ship from Barcelona to Italy, walked to Rome and received the pope's blessing for his pilgrimage, as pilgrims did, and then walked to Venice, begging all the way, where he got a free passage on a ship to the Holy Land. He arrived in Jerusalem in September. He says that from the moment he first saw the city, he felt a great consolation, and so did everyone with him.

And he meant to stay forever.

The Holy Places at that time were looked after by the Franciscan friars, who had been there for a long time, under the rule of the Turkish sultan. And the head of the friars sent for him, and very kindly, very firmly, said no.

Let us imagine it.

"You can't stay," says the old friar. "I'm sorry. It's not that we don't want you. It's that we have pilgrims like you every year. They come with no money and no language and a great deal of holiness, and then they get themselves into trouble, and then they get themselves captured, and then we have to pay to ransom them, and we have no money. You are going home on the ship with the others."

And Inigo argued. Of course he did. He said that he was determined to stay, and nothing would make him change his mind, unless it were a sin to stay.

And the friar said, I have the authority to command you to go, and I am commanding you.

And Inigo stopped arguing. He tells us that once he understood that it was not God's will for him to stay, he did not want to stay. This is a man who, a year before, had made a whole fortress fight a French army rather than give in. It is a very big change.

But before the ship left, he did one more thing, which I love. He wanted, very badly, to see the place on the Mount of Olives from which, by tradition, the Lord ascended into heaven. There was a stone there with what people said were his footprints in it. And he had seen it, but he suddenly could not remember which way the footprints pointed. Which way the Lord had been facing. So he slipped away from the group, without a guide, which was strictly against the rules, and went back up the Mount of Olives, and gave the guards there a little penknife to let him in. And he had a good look.

And then, on his way back, he realised he had forgotten to check which way the right foot pointed, as opposed to the left. So he went back again, and this time gave the guards his scissors.

Meanwhile, the friars had discovered he was missing. And they sent one of the men who worked for them, a big, strong local Christian, after him, with a stout stick. And this man found him coming down the hill, and seized him by the arm, and marched him all the way back to the friary, looking very fierce.

And Ignatius says that the whole way, being marched along by the arm, he felt a great consolation, and it seemed to him that he saw Christ above him, all the way. He offers it as his own report. And then he went home on the ship, in a bitterly cold winter, in a very thin coat.

I like that story because it is so small. He did not need to see anything grand. He just needed to know which way the Lord's feet had been facing. And when he was dragged back by the arm, like a naughty schoolboy, he found the Lord there too.

* * *

So he came home. And he asked himself, what now?

And he decided that if he was going to be any use to anybody, he needed to study. He needed to learn theology properly. And to learn theology in those days, you had to learn Latin first.

So in fifteen twenty-four, at the age of about thirty-three, the former soldier and gentleman of fashion, Inigo of Loyola, went back to school in Barcelona, and sat down on the bench next to the little boys, and learned his Latin grammar.

I think this is one of the bravest things he ever did. Braver than Pamplona. Try to imagine it. A grown man with a limp, sitting among boys of ten and eleven, saying amo, amas, amat, and getting it wrong. He says he found it very hard, because every time he tried to learn a Latin verb, his mind kept wandering off to holy thoughts instead. So eventually he went to his teacher and promised him he would attend every lesson and pay attention, as long as he could find bread and water to keep himself alive, and the distractions stopped.

After two years, he went on to the university at Alcala, near Madrid, and then to Salamanca, in the west. And everywhere he went, he could not help talking to people about God, and teaching them his way of praying, and gathering a little group of friends around him, who all dressed in the same rough grey cloth. And in both places, the Church authorities, who were nervous at that time about people preaching without being trained, called him in and asked him a great many questions.

In both places he was put in prison for a few weeks while they looked into it. In Salamanca, he and a companion were chained together by the leg. And he tells us something wonderful about that. One night, all the other prisoners broke out and escaped, and the door was left open, and Inigo and his friends stayed exactly where they were. In the morning, when everybody found the prison empty except for them, it made a great impression in the town.

And in both places the judges found nothing wrong with his teaching at all. They just told him to stop teaching until he had finished his studies. And he took it with very good humour. When somebody visiting him in prison said how sorry they were to see him in chains, he said, more or less, there are not enough chains in Salamanca for how much I would gladly bear for the love of God.

So he decided that he needed a proper university, a long way off, where he could just get on and study. And in February fifteen twenty-eight, he walked into Paris. Alone, and on foot.

* * *

Paris was the greatest university in Christendom. And Inigo, now in his late thirties, did what he always did. He started at the bottom, and worked.

And he found friends. In fifteen twenty-nine, at a college called Sainte-Barbe, he was given a room with two young men. One was Peter Faber, a gentle, worried, holy young man from Savoy, in the Alps, who had been a shepherd boy before he went to school. And the other was Francis Xavier, a handsome, ambitious, athletic young nobleman from Navarre, who was clever and knew it, and wanted to be a great professor.

You may already know what happened to Francis Xavier. He became one of the greatest missionaries the Church has ever had, sailing to India and Japan, and we have told his story to the little ones on our sister shelf. But at the beginning, he did not like Inigo very much at all. He thought this limping old student was rather odd. And Inigo was patient with him. For a long time. Years. The early Jesuits told the story that Inigo kept saying to Francis, over and over, the words of the Lord from the Gospel: what does it profit a man to gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? And eventually, it got in.

Peter Faber was the first to be won over. Inigo helped him with his worries, and Faber helped Inigo with his Greek and his philosophy, which was a very good bargain on both sides.

There were seven of them in the end, all students. And on the fifteenth of August, fifteen thirty-four, the feast of the Assumption, the seven of them climbed up the hill of Montmartre, which in those days was outside the city, all fields and windmills, to a little chapel there. Faber was the only one of them who was a priest yet, so he said the Mass. And at the Mass, each of them made vows. To live in poverty and chastity. And to go to Jerusalem, all together. And if they could not get to Jerusalem within a year, to go to Rome instead, and offer themselves to the pope, to be sent wherever they were most needed.

Afterwards, the old accounts say, they went down to a spring on the hillside and ate together, and were very happy. A picnic, in other words, which I think is exactly the right way to start a religious order.

* * *

They never got to Jerusalem. They all met up again in Venice in fifteen thirty-seven, but there was a war on with the Turks, and for the first time in years, no pilgrim ships sailed at all. So in Venice, the ones who were not priests yet were ordained, Inigo among them, in the June of fifteen thirty-seven.

And here is a thing that tells you a lot about him. He did not say his first Mass straight away. He waited. For a year and a half. He hoped that perhaps he could say it in Bethlehem. And when it became clear that he would never get there, he said his first Mass on Christmas night, fifteen thirty-eight, in Rome, in the great church of Saint Mary Major, at the chapel where they keep what tradition says are pieces of the manger of Bethlehem. If he could not go to Bethlehem, he would go to the nearest thing to it.

On the road to Rome, the year before, at a little place called La Storta, he had stopped in a wayside chapel to pray. And there, he says, he felt such a change in his soul, and saw so clearly that God the Father placed him with Christ His Son, that he could not doubt it. That is his own report, and in the next centuries the Jesuits treasured it.

The friends could not go east. So they did what they had promised. They offered themselves to the pope, and decided, after a great deal of praying and talking, to stay together as one company, with one of them in charge. They called themselves the Company of Jesus. And on the twenty-seventh of September, fifteen forty, the pope approved them as a new religious order. The Society of Jesus. The Jesuits.

And then they had to choose who would be in charge. And of course they all voted for Inigo, or Ignatius, as he had started calling himself in Paris. Nobody is quite sure why. Perhaps he thought it was simply the Latin form of Inigo. Perhaps it was for love of Saint Ignatius of Antioch. He never told us. And he said no. He said they should pray for a few more days and vote again. They did, and voted for him again. And he still was not sure, and went to make a confession of his whole life to a Franciscan friar, and asked the friar to tell him what to do, and the friar said, accept. So he did.

And that was the end of the pilgrim's travels. For the rest of his life, he hardly left Rome. The man who had wanted to walk barefoot to the ends of the earth spent his last fifteen years mostly sitting at a desk, writing letters. Nearly seven thousand of them survive. Letters to kings, to bishops, to mothers worried about their sons, to young Jesuits in India and Brazil and the Congo, to a duchess, to a young man who could not decide what to do with his life. He sent his friends all over the world, and stayed behind himself, and missed them terribly. When letters came from Francis Xavier, from India or Japan, he would cry reading them.

[pause]

* * *

And now the candles are getting low, so let me tell you, quietly, how the story ends.

In Rome, Ignatius did not only write letters. He started a house for women who wanted to leave a hard life on the streets and needed somewhere safe to go. He started schools, and the schools spread everywhere, more quickly than anybody expected, until the Jesuits were best known as teachers. He taught the catechism to children in the squares of Rome himself, in Italian that was never very good, with a strong Spanish accent, and he did not mind at all.

He was often ill. His stomach had never recovered from the years of fasting. And his leg, of course, still limped.

In the summer of fifteen fifty-six, he was about sixty-five. He had been unwell for a while, but no more than usual, and the doctors did not think he was in danger. On the evening of the thirtieth of July, he asked his secretary to go to the pope and ask for his blessing, because he thought he was dying. The secretary, who had a great deal to do and did not think there was any hurry, said he would go in the morning.

And early in the morning, on the thirty-first of July, fifteen fifty-six, before the sun was properly up, Ignatius died, very quietly, with only a couple of his brothers there. No great speeches. No last words written down. Just a tired old man in a small room in Rome, going home to God, the way he had lived since Manresa. Without any fuss.

He was declared a saint in sixteen twenty-two, on the same day as his friend Francis Xavier, and Saint Teresa of Avila, and Saint Philip Neri, and Saint Isidore the farmer, which must have been one of the most crowded days in heaven's calendar. His feast is kept on the thirty-first of July. And three hundred years after that, in nineteen twenty-two, he was named the patron saint of retreats, because of his little book of exercises.

The house at Loyola is still there, inside a great church built around it. You can visit the room where he lay with his leg in the stretching machine, reading the only two books in the house. And up on Montserrat, the little dark statue of Our Lady is still there too, high behind the altar, and people still climb up to see her, and some of them still leave things before her that they have decided they no longer need.

[pause]

I said at the beginning that this was a story that happened mostly inside one man's head. And I think that is why so many people have loved it.

Ignatius did not start out holy. He started out vain, and hot-tempered, and very keen on boots. God did not change him with a thunderbolt. God changed him slowly, through a broken leg, and two books he did not want, and a long, boring summer, and a little bit of noticing. He noticed that some thoughts left him gladder and some left him emptier. He noticed it, and he paid attention to it, and he followed the gladness, step by step, limp by limp, for the rest of his life.

He used to say that we should find God in all things. Not just in churches. In all things. In a book you did not choose. In a mule that takes the main road. In a friar who says no. In being marched down a hill by the arm.

So tonight, as you fall asleep, you might just look back over the day, the way Ignatius taught. Not to judge it. Just to notice. Where was there a little gladness today? Where was there a little light? Say thank you for it. And then put the rest down. Hang it up, like a sword at the feet of Our Lady. You will not need it tonight.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source tonight is Ignatius's own story of his life, often called his Autobiography, though he never called it that. He dictated it in Rome between fifteen fifty-three and fifteen fifty-five to Luis Goncalves da Camara, who wrote it up after each session. It is told in the third person, and it stops in fifteen thirty-eight, so for the later years we rely on the early Jesuit records and on the first full biography, by Pedro de Ribadeneira, who knew Ignatius well from boyhood. There are good English translations by Joseph Tylenda, and by Joseph Munitiz and Philip Endean in the Penguin volume of his personal writings. For the history of the first Jesuits, John O'Malley's book The First Jesuits is the standard.

What is history. His birth at Loyola, his years as a page and soldier, the wound at Pamplona and the long recovery, the books he read, the journey to Montserrat, the general confession, the vigil and the sword and dagger left before Our Lady, the clothes given to a poor man, Manresa, Jerusalem, the schools and the questionings, Paris and his roommates, Montmartre, Venice, the first Mass at Saint Mary Major, the approval of the Society in fifteen forty, and his death in Rome in fifteen fifty-six. One small puzzle. His story says he lived for the world until he was twenty-six, but if he was born in fourteen ninety-one, he was nearly thirty at Pamplona. Nobody is quite sure why, and we have not tried to fix it.

What is his report of his inner life. The vision of Our Lady at Loyola, the three keys at Manresa, the light by the river Cardoner, Christ seen above him in Jerusalem and the vision at La Storta are all told as he told them, and nothing is added.

What is later tradition. That his sister-in-law Magdalena gave him the books, and the cave at Manresa.

What we invented. Joanes the kitchen boy, the soup, the mule's white nose and her excellent character, and nearly everything anybody says, including the friar in Jerusalem and the man who ran after him at Montserrat. Ignatius's own sentences are given in our own plain words. We have kept off the arguments of his century between Catholics and Protestants, which are not tonight's story.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Ignatius of Loyola for tonight. He is remembered on the thirty-first of July in the Catholic Church. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Here is a prayer from his little book of exercises, in our own plain words. Take, Lord, and receive all my freedom, my memory, my understanding and all my will, all that I have and call my own. You gave it all to me. To You, Lord, I give it back. All of it is Yours. Do with it what You will. Give me only Your love and Your grace, and that is enough for me. Goodnight, and God bless.

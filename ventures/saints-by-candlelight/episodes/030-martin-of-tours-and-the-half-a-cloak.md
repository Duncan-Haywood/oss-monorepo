There is a gate in a city wall, and it is the middle of a winter so cold that the birds have stopped complaining about it and simply sit very still. The gate is the gate of Amiens, in the north of Gaul, which is what the Romans called the country we now call France.

A young man is riding up to it. He is a cavalry soldier, no more than eighteen, in a good white cloak. And at the side of the road, in nothing at all except a few rags, there is a man holding out his hand.

Dozens of people have ridden past him this morning. They have not looked at him. They have not exactly been wicked, either. They were cold, and in a hurry, and each of them thought somebody else would do something.

The young soldier stops his horse. He has nothing in his pockets. He looks at his cloak. And then he does something with his sword that nobody at that gate is ever going to forget.

First, who is Martin?

He is a saint of the fourth century, which is the century when the Roman Empire stopped persecuting Christians and, quite suddenly, began to be run by them. That is an enormous change, and it happened inside one lifetime. The old people could remember churches being closed. The young people could remember bishops being invited to dinner with the emperor. And in the middle of that change, there were a great many people who had to work out what it meant to follow Christ when it was, for the first time, fashionable.

Martin is one of the people who worked it out. He did it quietly, and in a very practical way, and mostly with other people's problems.

He was born around the year three hundred and sixteen, in a town called Sabaria, in a province the Romans called Pannonia. If you want to find it today, it is in Hungary, in a town called Szombathely, and the people there are still rather fond of him. But he did not grow up there for long. His father was a soldier, a man who had risen through the ranks to be a military tribune, which is something like a colonel. And soldiers get posted. So the family moved to Pavia, in Italy, and that is where Martin grew up.

Now, his parents were not Christians. They were ordinary, comfortable, respectable pagans, the kind who take the gods for granted the way we take the postal service for granted. But Martin, Sulpicius tells us, was different. When he was ten years old, against his parents' wishes, he went to the church and asked to be made a catechumen.

A catechumen is someone who is preparing for baptism. In those days that was a long business. You were taught, you were examined, you were prayed over, and you were not baptised until you had shown that you meant it. A ten-year-old boy putting his name down for that, without telling his mother, is a very particular kind of child. I can only imagine the conversation at dinner.

Sulpicius says that by the age of twelve Martin wanted to go and live in the desert, like the monks of Egypt that everyone was talking about. He would have, too, says Sulpicius, if he had not been so young. Which is a gentle way of saying that a twelve-year-old with a plan is still a twelve-year-old.

[pause]

Here is what happened instead.

The emperor of the day had made a law that the sons of soldiers must themselves become soldiers, and the law was not interested in whether a boy would prefer to be a monk. When Martin was fifteen, he was rounded up, put in chains, the old Life says, and sworn into the army. It was not what he wanted. But he was a good soldier. Sulpicius tells us that he was obedient, and kind to the men who served beside him, and that he did not drink or swear or chase the things that soldiers chased. His comrades loved him for it. They said he was more like a monk than a soldier, which was true, and which he did not mind.

Martin was posted to Gaul, to a unit of the imperial guard. One of the duties of a young soldier on horseback in Gaul was to ride about on patrol. And on this particular winter, in this particular year, the cold was so terrible that, Sulpicius says, many people died of it.

* * *

So now we come back to the gate.

It is the gate of Amiens, on a day when the frost has turned the road to iron. Martin is riding in, wrapped up as warmly as any soldier can be. He is wearing his uniform and over it, the chlamys, a heavy military cloak. It is white. It is his own. It is in fact the only cloak he owns.

And at the gate there is a man with nothing on.

Sulpicius does not tell us who he was. He does not tell us his name, or how he came to be there, or what he had done to deserve it, and I think that is on purpose. He is only the man who needs a coat. He is the man every one of us has ridden past.

I want you to notice something about the scene. Nobody is being cruel. The people passing are not villains. Sulpicius says the beggar was begging passers-by to take pity on him, and that they simply went by. That is the whole sin of the story, and it is a small, ordinary, familiar one. It is not hatred. It is hurry.

And Martin, who has been trained since he was ten years old to be a person who notices, notices.

Now, here is the difficulty. He has no money. He has given away everything he carries, which is something he does all the time. All he has left is what he is wearing, and what he is wearing is the army's, in a sense, and his own, in another. He is a young man in the middle of a long cold ride, and if he gives the cloak away he will shiver too.

He thinks about it for about as long as it takes a horse to stamp.

Then he takes out his sword, and he takes hold of the cloak, and he cuts it in two.

Half of it, he wraps around the beggar. The other half, he puts back on himself.

[pause]

Why half? It is a thing I have always liked about this story. He could have given the whole cloak. A grander saint would have given the whole cloak. But the cloak, as the soldiers understood it, belonged partly to the army, and a soldier cannot give away what is not entirely his to give. And Martin, who is always honest, and always practical, gives exactly what is his. Half. And keeps half, so that he will not be a second beggar at the gate.

It is not a grand gesture. It is the right-sized one. He gives what he can, and he does not pretend to give what he cannot.

Some of the bystanders laughed, Sulpicius says. They thought he looked ridiculous: a soldier of the guard riding about with a stump of a cloak, like a man who had lost a fight with a pair of scissors. Others, he says, were ashamed, because they had far more to give and had given nothing.

I find that very true. If you do the kind thing in public, then half the people who see it will think you are a fool, and the other half will feel a little embarrassed, and nobody will thank you at the time. Martin does not seem to mind. He rides on.

* * *

That night, Martin goes to sleep.

And now we come to the part of the story that Sulpicius tells from Martin's own account, which is to say it is a vision that Martin himself reported, years later. We are not going to say more about it than Martin did. But this is what he said.

He saw Christ.

And Christ was wearing the half of the cloak that Martin had given away.

Martin was told to look closely, and to see that it was the very cloak. And then he heard Jesus say, in the hearing of the angels who stood around him, "Martin, who is still only a catechumen, has covered me with this garment."

That is the whole vision. There is nothing more to it, and it needs nothing more. It is a retelling, in a young man's dream, of the words of the Gospel of Matthew: "I was naked, and you clothed me." Martin had not been thinking of those words, and he had certainly not been trying to earn them. He had only seen a man who was cold. But Christ, as it turns out, had been standing at the gate in the man's place, and Martin had not known.

Sulpicius says Martin was not made proud by the vision. He did not go about telling everyone. What he did was to go straight away and be baptised, which he had been waiting to do for eight years. He was eighteen.

[pause]

Now I want to stop here for a moment, because there is a quiet joke in the vision that is easy to miss, and I think it is a good one.

Christ does not say, "Martin, who is a saint." He does not say, "Martin, who is a bishop." He says, "Martin, who is still only a catechumen." That is, a beginner. Someone who has not even been baptised yet. The Lord is showing off about him, in front of the angels, and the thing he points to is a thing that a not-yet-Christian, a not-quite-anything, has done on the road to Amiens with a sword and a bit of wool.

I think it tells us how much it matters to him. The whole thing is a kindness done by a beginner. And that is, a little, why we tell it at bedtime. You do not have to be finished to be able to do it.

* * *

For a while after this, Martin stayed in the army. He was baptised, but he was still a soldier, and he stayed a soldier for another two years, because he was bound by his oath and he did not know how to leave it honourably.

Then there came a day when the army was drawn up near the city of Worms, on the Rhine, facing the Germanic tribes across the river, and the Caesar in charge of that part of the empire, whose name was Julian, was handing out a bonus to the troops before the battle. It was the custom. The soldiers lined up and each of them was given a gift of money.

Martin went to the Caesar and asked to be released.

Sulpicius gives us the sentence, and it is plain and short. "I am Christ's soldier," said Martin. "I am not allowed to fight."

Now, you can imagine how that went down. The Caesar was not pleased. He said, in effect, that Martin was afraid, that he was looking for an excuse not to fight in the morning, and that it had nothing to do with Christ.

And Martin's answer is a very brave and very polite one. He said: "If you think I am a coward, and not a man of faith, then tomorrow I will stand unarmed in front of the line. In the name of the Lord Jesus, protected by the sign of the cross and not by a shield or a helmet, I will go in among the enemy without fear."

The Caesar said, very well, we shall see. And he had Martin put under guard, to be sent out unarmed in the morning.

[pause]

You are waiting for me to tell you how the battle went. Here is what Sulpicius says. The next day, before anything could happen, the enemy sent messengers. They surrendered. They gave up everything they had, and asked for peace, and the battle never took place.

Sulpicius draws the obvious conclusion, which is that God had spared the saint from being sent into battle, and that it was no wonder the fight was never fought, since there was no need to risk the life of a man who would not draw a sword. I will say only this. It is a story that a man told about his friend, written down some time after, and we tell it as he told it. It is the kind of thing that happened to Martin more than once. It may be that things simply tended to work out for people like him. Or it may be that God was keeping an eye on him. You may decide which you prefer. Both of them are good.

So Martin was discharged from the army, and he went away to find a teacher.

* * *

His teacher was Hilary, the bishop of Poitiers, who was one of the most learned and most stubborn men in the whole of Gaul. Hilary had been exiled once for defending the faith about who Jesus is, and had come back with a great deal to say about it. He wanted to make Martin a deacon, and Martin refused, saying he was not worthy. So Hilary asked him to be an exorcist, a humble minor office, which Martin could not refuse, since it sounded to him like the lowest job in the Church. He took it. I am told that Hilary was rather pleased with himself.

Martin spent some years travelling. He went to Pannonia, to see his parents, and he converted his mother, though his father stayed as he was. He went into Italy. He lived for a while as a hermit on a tiny island, on roots and wild herbs, until he made a mistake about which herbs and was very ill. He came back to Gaul. And in the end, around the year three hundred and sixty, he settled in a place near Poitiers called Ligugé, where he built a little community of men who wanted to live as he did.

That was the first monastery in Gaul, or so it is usually said. It was not grand. The monks lived in huts, and they prayed, and they copied books, and they grew a few vegetables, and, Sulpicius says, they did not trade, and they were quite poor, and it was very peaceful. People came from the villages and asked Martin to pray for them, and he did.

If you had gone to Ligugé in those days, I think you would have found a quiet place with a bell and a door that was usually unlocked, a smell of woodsmoke, and a tall, plain-looking man in a rough cloak who looked as if he had once been a soldier, but who talked to you as though you were the most important person who had called that day.

* * *

And then, in the year three hundred and seventy-one, something happened that Martin did not want.

The city of Tours needed a bishop. The old one had died. And the people of Tours, who had heard a great deal about the holy man at Ligugé, decided that they wanted him. There was only one difficulty, which was that Martin did not want to be a bishop, and he had said so, many times.

So they thought of a plan, the old Life says. One of the citizens, whose name was Rusticius, went to the monastery and said that his wife was very ill, and could Martin please come to Tours and pray for her? And Martin, who never refused anyone, set out. When he got to the city, the crowds were waiting in the road, and the bishops from the surrounding towns were there, and it was all arranged.

Not everyone was pleased. One of the visiting bishops, Sulpicius tells us, complained that Martin looked a mess: unkempt, badly dressed, with scruffy hair. This was not, he thought, how a bishop should look. But the people of Tours replied that he was exactly what they wanted. And in the end, Martin was made bishop of Tours, with the whole city cheering.

There is also a story, and it is a later one, and Sulpicius does not tell it, that Martin tried to hide. In that story, he crept into a barn full of geese, thinking nobody would look there. And the geese, being geese, began to honk. The people found him at once, and the geese have been blamed ever since. Now, I would not put that in a history book. It is a tradition, and a cheerful one, and it comes from long after Martin. But I like it very much, because it is exactly what you would expect. If you are a saint who does not want to be found, you will be found by the loudest creature in the room.

[pause]

So Martin was bishop. And what do you suppose he did?

He did not move into the bishop's house. He found it too grand, too full of people calling on him, and too much like a palace. So he built himself a little cell of wood nearby, next to the church, and then, when that was still too busy, he went to a place a couple of miles outside the city, along the river Loire, where there was a cliff with caves in it, and built a little monastery there. It became the abbey of Marmoutier. Soon there were eighty monks, living in caves and huts. They owned nothing. They wore camel-hair, and drank no wine unless they were ill.

And from there, Martin travelled. He walked, or rode a donkey. He went out into the countryside, where most people were still worshipping the old gods in their old groves, and he preached. He told them about Christ. He knocked down a few of their temples, and I will say honestly that this is a part of the story that modern people find uncomfortable, and I think we are right to. It was the way a fourth-century bishop did things, and we are not going to pretend it was gentle. But what Sulpicius mostly shows is that Martin did it by persuading, and by the sort of life he led, and that the people who followed him did so because they had seen how he behaved.

There is a small story of a pine tree. The villagers had a sacred pine beside a temple, and when Martin said it must come down, they said fine, we will cut it, if you will stand where it falls. And so they tied him up, and stood him in the place where the tree would land, and cut. And, says Sulpicius, as the great tree began to lean toward him, Martin made the sign of the cross, and it swerved, and fell in the other direction, so that the people who had been standing safe jumped and ran. And many of them were converted that day.

Now, I will say plainly what we are telling you. This is a miracle in the Life, and it is God who acts, not Martin, who was simply standing there with his hand raised. It is told by a friend, who may have been told it by someone else. You may take it as it is given.

I would rather tell you about a smaller thing, because it is the thing that I think is most like him.

[pause]

One day, the Life tells us, the emperor invited Martin to dinner.

You might expect that a bishop who lived in a cave would have said no. But he went, because he had a favour to ask for a prisoner. The emperor, who was called Maximus, was very pleased to have him at table, and treated him with great honour. At the end of the meal, a servant brought the cup of wine, and the emperor, instead of drinking from it himself, handed it to Martin, expecting that the bishop would take a sip and hand it back to him with a blessing.

Martin took a sip. And then he passed the cup, not to the emperor, but to his own priest, who was sitting beside him, because, he said, that priest was the person most worthy of drinking after him.

I am told that the whole court was astonished, and that the emperor, rather surprisingly, laughed, and was not at all offended. I do not know if I believe the emperor laughed. But I like that story, because it shows you something about Martin. He had been a soldier of the emperor's army. He had been a bishop in the emperor's empire. And he never, even for the length of a cup of wine, pretended that the emperor was the most important person in the room.

* * *

He lived to be old.

I do not want to tell you that Martin was always right. Sulpicius is a loyal friend, and he gives us the impression of a man who was very nearly always patient and kind. But there were bishops in Gaul who thought he was too gentle on sinners, and some who thought he was too stern, and he quarrelled, as honest people do, with a few who thought he was wrong. Even so, he kept going about the diocese until he was eighty or so.

In the year three hundred and ninety-seven, he set off to the village of Candes, at the far end of his diocese, because the clergy there were quarrelling, and he wished to make peace. He was very old. When he got there, he did what he had been going to do, and the quarrel was settled. And then, quietly, he told his monks that he would soon die.

They were distressed, and they begged him not to leave. And Martin, being ill, lay down, not in a bed, but on ashes and sackcloth, because, he said, a Christian ought to die in no other way than that. When his disciples tried to put straw under him, he refused.

Then he said a prayer, and the monks wrote it down, and it has been repeated ever since.

"Lord," he said, "if I am still necessary to your people, I do not refuse the labour. Your will be done."

That is all. He was ready to go, but he did not ask to go. He did not say, "I have done enough." He said, "If you need me, I will stay." And if you want a one-sentence description of Martin of Tours, I do not think you could improve on it.

[pause]

Now we are slowing down. The room is quiet. It is night at Candes, in the middle of autumn, and an old man is lying on a plain floor, with his eyes turned to heaven. His monks are around him. One of them, who is anxious, asks him to turn over, to ease his body. And Martin says, "Let me look towards heaven rather than at the earth, brother, so that my spirit may be set on its road to the Lord."

He died in the night of the eighth of November, the old Life says. He was about eighty-one. When the news reached Tours, the people hurried to Candes. And they say there were two thousand monks in the procession that carried him home, and that in the villages along the Loire, people stood on the banks, and nobody spoke.

He was buried on the eleventh of November. And that is the day that the Church remembers him.

* * *

It is a good day for a feast. The eleventh of November is a day when the autumn is nearly over and the winter is beginning. The leaves have come down. The evenings are dark and the fires are lit. And in many countries of Europe, people say that around this time there will be a few mild, bright days, a little warm spell before the real cold. They call it Saint Martin's summer. It is a folk custom, not a teaching of the Church, and I would not stake my life on the weather. But I think it is a sweet thing to say, that one of the kind of saints who gave away half a cloak should get a short summer named for him.

In many places, children carry paper lanterns on the evening of his feast and sing songs about him, and walk through the dark streets with their little lights. In others, there is a goose for dinner, which, if you remember the barn, seems to me a bit unfair to the goose.

And all over the world, there are churches named for him. The shrine at Tours was one of the great pilgrim places of the Middle Ages, and the half a cloak, a piece of cloth that his followers kept, was carried before the kings of France into battle and was so well known that the little room where it was kept got a special name. We will not go into that tonight. It is enough to say that a very small gift, given at a very cold gate, ended up being remembered for sixteen hundred years.

I think that is what I would like you to take to bed tonight.

You will probably never be asked to cut your cloak in half. But there is nearly always a gate. There is nearly always a person at the side of the road, and it is nearly always cold, and we are nearly always in a hurry. And the lesson of Martin is not that you must give away everything. He did not. He gave half. He gave exactly what was his to give, and no more, and kept the other half so that he would not need help himself. It is a lesson in giving the right-sized gift to the person in front of you, on the day that you meet them.

So close your eyes. Somewhere, in a very old story, there is a gate in a winter wall. A young man is on a horse. A man is holding out his hand. And the young man has just remembered that he has something that he can share.

Breathe out slowly. Let your shoulders go soft. If there is anything you were too busy to do today, or someone you rode past, put it down now. It is allowed to be dealt with tomorrow. Tonight, you are warm. Someone has put a cloak around you, and it is, perhaps, exactly the right size.

Now, notes for the curious, which we do at the end of every episode, so that you know what you have just been told.

Almost everything in the main story comes from the Life of Saint Martin by Sulpicius Severus, a wealthy lawyer from Gaul who became Martin's friend and disciple, and who wrote while Martin was still alive and finished after his death. That makes him an unusually close source. He is also a devoted one, and he wrote to praise the saint, so we have told you the stories as he told them, and said who is speaking. The vision of Christ in the half cloak is Martin's own account as Sulpicius gives it. The refusal to fight at Worms, the pine tree and the supper with the emperor are all in the Life, though the dating of the battle is uncertain, and we have not tried to fix it.

Some things we have told you are not from Sulpicius. The geese in the barn are a later tradition. Saint Martin's summer is a folk custom. The lanterns and the goose on the feast day are customs of later centuries. We have said so each time.

What the episode made up is the colour: the weather, the chatter of the passers-by, the narrator's guesses about why the cloak was cut in half, and some of the humour. We have not added any event or any meaning. And we have been honest that the destruction of temples is a part of the story that modern listeners find hard. We have not softened it, and we have not defended it.

It is early in the morning, in Rome, and a man from out of town is standing at the bottom of a flight of stone steps, holding a basket.

He has been in the city for nine days. His feet hurt. His Latin, which was perfectly good at home, has turned out to be the kind of Latin that makes Romans smile and answer him slowly. He has asked at more doors than he can count, and at most of them he has been told no, and at some of them he has been told to go away, and at one of them a man threw a cabbage, which missed.

At the top of the steps there is a soldier, leaning on a doorpost, eating a fig.

The man from out of town climbs the steps. He says, good morning. I'm looking for a prisoner. An old man, from Tarsus. His name is Paul.

And the soldier looks at him for a long moment. And looks at the basket. And then, to the man's great surprise, he does not say no.

If you have been with us on recent nights, you will know that we have been following Saint Paul to the end of his road. He reached Rome as a prisoner. He rented rooms of his own and welcomed everyone who climbed his stairs. And then, the Church has always believed, there was a second imprisonment, a harder one. And from it, he wrote one last letter, to his friend Timothy, who was looking after the Christians at Ephesus.

Last time, we told that letter from Timothy's side. Tonight, we are going to stay with one small paragraph of it, and tell it from the side of someone else entirely.

Here is the paragraph, in plain words. Paul writes:

You know that everyone in the province of Asia has turned away from me, including Phygelus and Hermogenes. May the Lord show mercy to the household of Onesiphorus, because he often refreshed me, and he was not ashamed of my chain. On the contrary, when he arrived in Rome, he searched for me eagerly, and he found me. May the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord on that day. And you know very well how much he served at Ephesus.

That's it. That's nearly all we know. And then, right at the end of the letter, in the greetings, Paul says, give my greetings to Prisca and Aquila, and to the household of Onesiphorus.

Four verses and a greeting. If you took every word the Bible says about this man and wrote it out on a card, it would fit on the back of a recipe. And yet I think it is one of the most beautiful little portraits in the whole of the New Testament. Because of what he did, and because of who did not do it.

Let's start with the name. Onesiphorus is a Greek name, and it means something like the one who brings profit, or the bringer of good things. It was a perfectly ordinary name. It is the sort of name a hopeful father gives a baby boy, in the way that people today call their children Felicity or Prosper. Whether he lived up to it, you can decide for yourself by the end of tonight.

Now, he had a household. Paul greets it twice. In those days a household was more than a family. It was the husband and wife and children, yes, but also the grandmother who lived in the back room, and the nephew who was learning the trade, and the people who worked for the family, and quite possibly a dog. When a household in the early Church became Christian, very often the whole house became a sort of little church, with people meeting in the courtyard to pray and break bread. So when Paul says the household of Onesiphorus, you should picture a house in Ephesus with its door open, and a lot of people coming in and out.

And Paul says to Timothy, you know very well how much he served at Ephesus. The Greek word is the one that gives us the word deacon. It means serving, waiting at table, running errands, doing the things that need doing. So whatever Onesiphorus did at Ephesus, it was the practical sort of help. The sort that nobody writes poems about. The sort that keeps everything else standing up.

There is one more thing I should tell you before the story starts, especially if you were listening on the night we told the story of Saint Thecla.

In that old story, the Acts of Paul and Thecla, which was written in the second century and which is not Scripture, there is a man called Onesiphorus. He lives in Iconium, in the middle of what is now Turkey, with his wife Lectra and his two boys, Simmias and Zeno. And when he hears that Paul is coming, he goes out to the crossroads to meet him, even though he has never seen him. All he has is a description from Titus. Paul is a short man, the description says, bald, with bowed legs, strong, with eyebrows that meet in the middle and a rather long nose, and full of grace, so that sometimes he looked like a man, and sometimes he had the face of an angel.

Is that the same Onesiphorus as the one in the letter? Nobody knows. Lots of people have thought so. It is quite possible that the writer of the Thecla story simply borrowed a name from the letter, the way novelists do. It is also possible that a man from Iconium moved to the big city on the coast, as people from small towns always have. So tonight our Onesiphorus lives in Ephesus, because that is where the letter puts him. But if you want to picture him as the same man, twenty years older, with his boys grown up, and still remembering the eyebrows, I won't stop you. I'll only say it's a picture, not a fact.

* * *

So. Ephesus. Some time in the middle of the sixties, in the first century.

Ephesus was a big, rich, noisy port city on the west coast of what is now Turkey. It had a huge theatre that could hold something like twenty-five thousand people, and a famous temple of Artemis, and a harbour full of ships, and a long marble street running down to the water. Paul had lived there for more than two years, years before, teaching every day in a lecture hall, and the Christians of Ephesus had become one of the biggest and liveliest communities anywhere.

And Onesiphorus, in our imagining, was one of the people who made it work.

I picture him as a man in the middle of life. Not young, not yet old. A man who knows where to buy good lamp oil cheaply, and which ship captains are honest, and how to find a bed for a traveller at an hour's notice. When Paul was in Ephesus, I like to think that it was Onesiphorus who made sure the hall was swept, and the lamps were filled, and the old ladies got a seat at the front. When Paul left, it was Onesiphorus who carried his bags down to the harbour. When Timothy arrived to look after the church, and was nervous, and too young, and had trouble with his stomach, I like to think it was Onesiphorus who said, come and eat with us, and gave him the soft bread.

None of that is in the letter. But the letter says Timothy knew very well how much he had done. That means there was a lot.

And then the news came from Rome.

We don't know exactly what news, or how it came. But the letter tells us the shape of it. Paul was in prison again. Not in the rented rooms this time. Somewhere much harder. He was in chains, he says, like a criminal. And the Christians of the province of Asia, the province Ephesus was the capital of, had turned away from him.

Now, I want to be gentle about this. Because we don't know what turning away meant. It may be that Paul had been arrested somewhere in Asia, and that people who could have spoken for him did not. It may be that when he wrote to them for help, they did not answer. It may be that they simply stopped coming. Paul names two of them, Phygelus and Hermogenes. That is all anyone knows about those two men. Two thousand years, and their only claim to fame is that they weren't there.

But I don't think it is hard to understand why people stayed away. These were frightening years for Christians. A few years earlier, in the summer of the year sixty-four, a great fire had burned for days in the city of Rome, and burned down whole districts. The Roman historian Tacitus, who was no friend of the Christians, wrote that people said the emperor Nero had started it himself. And to stop people saying that, Nero put the blame on the Christians. And many Christians in Rome were arrested, and many of them gave their lives.

So, in those years, to be known as a friend of a Christian prisoner in Rome was not a small thing. It was the sort of thing that got your name written down. And a lot of good, ordinary, frightened people decided, quietly, that they would pray for Paul from a safe distance.

And Onesiphorus went to Rome.

* * *

Why did he go? The letter doesn't say. It says, when he arrived in Rome. It may be that he had business there. A merchant from Ephesus might well have reasons to go to Rome, which was the biggest market in the world. It may be that he went on purpose, the moment he heard. It may be both. I like to imagine that he had been putting off a business trip for months, and when he heard the news about Paul, he said to his wife, well, I suppose I had better go to Rome after all, in the voice of a man who has already decided.

In the Thecla story his wife is called Lectra, so let's call her that tonight, with the warning that it's a borrowed name. And I imagine her packing his bag, and putting in a warm cloak, and a little jar of good oil, and a cheese, and a letter from the church at Ephesus, and then taking the cheese out again because it was too big, and then putting it back in.

And I imagine the household standing at the door to see him off. And somebody saying, you'll never find him. Rome is enormous. And Onesiphorus saying, well. I'll look.

How did you get from Ephesus to Rome? You could go most of the way by sea. You could take a ship west across the Aegean to Corinth, and cross the narrow neck of land there, and take another ship up the coast and across to Italy. Or you could go north and over by land through Macedonia, and cross to the heel of Italy, and walk up the great road. Either way it took weeks. Ships only sailed in the good months. Inns were dirty and expensive. Roads were long. It was not the sort of trip anyone made for fun.

When Paul himself had come to Rome the first time, years before, the Book of Acts says his ship landed at a port called Puteoli, on the bay of Naples. And from there he walked north up the Appian Way. And the Christians of Rome heard he was coming, and came out along the road to meet him, as far as a place called the Forum of Appius, and a place called the Three Taverns. And when Paul saw them, Luke says, he thanked God and took courage.

I like to think Onesiphorus walked that same road. Past the Three Taverns. Thinking about how, the last time, the whole church came out to meet Paul. And how, this time, nobody was coming out to meet anybody.

And then the walls of Rome. And the gate. And the city.

* * *

Here is something you have to understand about Rome in the first century. It was big. It was bigger than any city anyone from Ephesus had ever seen. Nobody knows exactly how many people lived there, but most historians think it was something like a million. Imagine a city of a million people with no street signs, and no house numbers, and no maps, and no one who could tell you where anyone was, and nobody very keen to help.

The rich lived in houses with gardens on the hills. Everybody else lived in apartment blocks, called insulae, five and six storeys high, built cheaply, with a shop on the ground floor and families stacked up above it like loaves on a shelf. The streets were narrow and steep and full of people shouting. And at night it got worse, because wheeled carts were only allowed into the city after dark, and so all night long the streets were full of wagons rumbling over the stones, and drivers cursing at each other when they got stuck. The poet Juvenal, who lived there a few decades later, complained that in Rome it was impossible to sleep unless you were rich. He said the sick died of insomnia. That was a joke. Mostly.

So that is the city. And somewhere in it there is one old man in a chain. And Onesiphorus has to find him.

How do you start? I imagine he did what you or I would do. He found somewhere to stay. He washed his feet. And he went out and started asking.

The letter uses a lovely phrase. It says he searched for me eagerly. The Greek word has a sense of hurry about it, of effort, of being in earnest. It is the word for a man who doesn't stroll. So let us follow him, eagerly, round Rome.

On the first day, I imagine, he went to the obvious place. The first time Paul was a prisoner in Rome, years before, he had been kept under guard by a soldier, and the soldiers who guarded prisoners for the emperor were the Praetorian Guard, who had a great walled camp on the edge of the city. So Onesiphorus walked out to the camp. And he stood at the gate, and he asked the guard on duty. And the guard on duty looked at him as if he had asked for directions to the moon.

A prisoner called Paul? Do you know how many prisoners there are in Rome? Do you know how many people are called Paul?

It is, to be fair, a very common name.

On the second day, he tried the prison by the Forum. Rome had an old, old prison right in the middle of the city, at the foot of the Capitol hill, partly cut into the rock, with a lower chamber underground that you could only get into through a hole in the floor. In later centuries, Christians came to believe that Peter and Paul had been held there, and today it is called the Mamertine prison, and you can go down into it, and there is a little chapel. That's a tradition, and a later one, so we won't build anything on it. But it is the kind of place Onesiphorus would have asked at. And at the door, in our imagining, a clerk with ink on his fingers ran down a list with his thumb, and said no, and then said, try the other one, and then would not say which other one.

On the third day he tried asking Christians.

* * *

This was harder than it sounds.

There were Christians in Rome. There had been for a long time. Paul had written them a whole letter years before. But after the fire, and Nero, and the arrests, the Christians of Rome had learned to be careful. They did not put signs on their doors. They did not answer questions from strangers with funny accents who turned up asking about prisoners. Because a stranger asking about a Christian prisoner might be a friend. Or he might be someone who wanted a list of that prisoner's friends.

So when Onesiphorus knocked at doors and said, I'm a brother from Ephesus, I'm looking for Paul, there were doors that did not open, and doors that opened a crack and shut again, and once, in our imagining, a door that opened and a woman looked at him and said, very loudly, there's nobody of that name here, and then, very quietly, as she shut it, God keep you.

And you have to imagine what that was like for him. Day after day. His feet sore. His money going on bread and lodgings and the small coins you gave to clerks to make them look at their lists. The cheese long gone. The rumble of carts outside his window all night. And nobody, anywhere, saying yes.

There must have been an evening, I think, when he sat down on the edge of a fountain, and put his head in his hands, and thought, they were right at home. Rome is enormous. I'll never find him. I should go back.

And I think it is worth saying, very plainly, that if he had gone home then, nobody would have blamed him. He had tried. He had come further than anyone else from Asia. He could have gone back to Ephesus and said, I looked, I couldn't find him, and everybody would have said, of course you couldn't, Rome is enormous, come and have some soup.

But the letter says he searched for me eagerly, and found me. And you don't find someone in a city of a million people by stopping on the evening you feel like stopping.

So in the morning, he went out again.

* * *

Here is the part of the story which is entirely our own invention, so hold it lightly. But it is built on something real.

About a hundred years after Paul, a Greek writer called Lucian wrote a satire, a mocking story, about a con man who pretended to be a Christian for a while, and got himself put in prison. Lucian thought Christians were gullible, and he wrote about them to make fun of them. But in making fun of them, he tells us something wonderful. He says that when a Christian was in prison, from the very first light of the morning you would see old widows and orphan children waiting outside the prison gate. He says the Christians brought the prisoner meals. He says they talked the guards round. And he says that people came from the cities of Asia, sent by the Christians there, to help him and encourage him.

Lucian meant it as a joke. Look at these silly people, he was saying, they'll do anything for one of their own. And I think if you had told those old widows at the prison gate that they were being laughed at, they would have said, well, that's all right, dear. Have a bit of bread.

So let us imagine that on about the ninth morning, Onesiphorus, out early, saw an old woman.

She was small, and bent, and she was carrying a basket covered with a cloth, and she was walking very slowly, with great determination, through a part of the city he had not been to yet. And something about her, the basket, the hour, the determination, made him follow her.

He followed her down one street and up another, and round a corner where a man was selling hot chickpeas, and past a fuller's shop that smelled terrible, the way fullers' shops did. And she stopped at the foot of a flight of stone steps, and she turned round, and she looked at him, and she said, young man, are you following me?

Onesiphorus was not a young man. But he took it as a compliment.

He said, I'm sorry. I'm from Ephesus. I'm looking for someone. A prisoner. An old man from Tarsus.

And the old woman looked at him for quite a long time. At his dusty cloak. At his tired face. At the funny accent. And she said, what's in the basket?

He didn't have a basket. So she gave him hers.

She said, I've got bad knees and those steps are very steep. You carry that up. The soldier at the top is called something I can't pronounce, and he likes figs, and if you give him one he'll let you in. Tell the old man my name. No, don't, he won't know who I am, and it's a long name. Tell him the bread is from the widows.

And then she sat down on the bottom step, in the morning sun, and folded her hands, as if she had done this a hundred times. Which perhaps she had.

* * *

And so that is how we get back to the beginning. A man from out of town, at the bottom of a flight of stone steps, holding a basket. At the top, a soldier, eating a fig.

Good morning. I'm looking for a prisoner. An old man, from Tarsus. His name is Paul.

And the soldier looked at him, and at the basket. And Onesiphorus, remembering, took a fig from under the cloth and held it out.

And the soldier took the fig. And jerked his head towards the door. And said, he's down there. Mind the step.

It was dark inside. It took a while for his eyes to adjust. There was a smell of damp stone, and old straw, and smoke from a little lamp. And there, against the far wall, sitting on a bench, with a chain on one wrist running to a ring in the wall, was a small, bald old man with eyebrows that met in the middle.

He looked up. He narrowed his eyes against the light from the door. And then his whole face changed.

Onesiphorus? he said. From Ephesus?

And Onesiphorus said, hello, Paul. I've brought you some bread. It's from the widows.

* * *

I would very much like to tell you what they said to each other. But the letter doesn't tell us, and it would be wrong of me to put words into the apostle's mouth and pretend they were his. So let me tell you instead what the letter says Onesiphorus did, and let you imagine the rest.

It says he often refreshed me.

Often. So this was not one visit. He came back. Again and again. For however long he was in Rome, he kept climbing those steps, and giving the soldier a fig, and going down into the dark.

And refreshed. The Greek word is a lovely one. It is related to the word for breath, or for a cool breeze. It means something like he let me breathe again. He was like cool air in a hot room. Think of what it is like, on a stifling night, when someone opens a window. That is what Paul says Onesiphorus was.

We can guess, from what we know about Roman prisons, some of the ordinary things that might have meant. Prisoners in Rome were not well looked after. Often the only food they got was what their friends brought them. The only clean clothes, the only blanket, the only lamp oil, the only paper and ink, came from outside. So perhaps it meant bread, and oil, and a clean tunic. Perhaps it meant water to wash in. Perhaps it meant someone sitting with him in the dark while he dictated a letter. Perhaps it meant news. How is Timothy? Is he eating properly? How is everyone at Ephesus? Who is still coming to the hall on Sunday?

And perhaps, more than any of that, it just meant somebody came. Somebody from home. Somebody who knew his name, and sat on the floor next to him, and talked to him like a friend instead of a prisoner. Jesus said, I was in prison, and you came to me. He didn't say, I was in prison and you got me out. He said, you came.

And then the letter says, he was not ashamed of my chain.

I think that is the bravest line in the paragraph. Because it was a shameful thing, in the eyes of Rome, to be in chains. Chains were for slaves and criminals. A respectable man from Ephesus did not want to be seen going in and out of a prison, giving his name to the guards, sitting next to a man in irons. People would talk. People would write things down. The soldiers would remember his face.

And he went in anyway. And sat down next to the chain. And didn't pretend it wasn't there, and didn't pretend he wasn't there either.

There is a line in the Letter to the Hebrews which says, remember those who are in prison, as though you were in prison with them. I think Onesiphorus did exactly that. For a few weeks in Rome, he chose to be the kind of person who is in prison with someone.

* * *

And then, one day, he went home.

We don't know when, or why. Perhaps his business was finished. Perhaps the sailing season was closing, and it was the last ship of the year. Perhaps Paul told him to go. Go home to Lectra, he might have said. Go home to your household. Tell Timothy to come.

I imagine that last visit. The soldier at the top of the steps, who by now had eaten an awful lot of figs, and had grudgingly started saying good morning. The old woman at the bottom of the steps, who by now had told Onesiphorus the names of all her grandchildren. And down in the dark, Paul, with his chain, and Onesiphorus kneeling for a blessing, and the old man putting his free hand on his head.

And then the long road home.

The letter does not tell us where Onesiphorus was when it was written. It only tells us what Paul thought of him. And it tells us that Paul, sitting in the dark, with so many people gone, wrote to Timothy and said: everyone in Asia has turned away from me. But Onesiphorus came. He found me.

And then, Paul does a very Paul thing. He makes a little play on words. In Greek, the word for he found me, and the word for may he find, are the same verb, in two forms. He found me, Paul says. May the Lord grant that he finds mercy from the Lord on that day.

Onesiphorus went looking for Paul, and found him. And Paul prays that on the last day, when Onesiphorus comes looking, he will find mercy waiting for him, the way Paul was waiting at the bottom of the steps.

I think that is about the kindest thing one person has ever written about another.

And there is one more scene I want to give you, because I think it is the one that matters most. And it is also imagined, so hold it lightly too.

Some time later, in the autumn perhaps, a letter arrives at Ephesus, for Timothy. If you were with us last time, you know what is in it. The fight he has fought. The race he has finished. Come before winter. Bring the cloak.

And I imagine Timothy, after he has read it, and cried, and read it again, walking across the city to a house with its door open. And going into the courtyard, where the household of Onesiphorus is having its supper. The children, and the grandmother, and the nephew, and the people who work there, and Lectra, if that was her name.

And Timothy says, there's a letter from Paul. There's a bit in it about you. And everybody stops eating.

And he reads it out. May the Lord show mercy to the household of Onesiphorus, because he often refreshed me, and he was not ashamed of my chain. When he arrived in Rome, he searched for me eagerly, and he found me.

And then, at the end. Greet Prisca and Aquila. And the household of Onesiphorus.

Imagine hearing your family's name read out like that. Not because you were famous. Not because you preached a great sermon or wrote a great book. Just because the man of your house went to a strange city and kept knocking on doors until he found a friend in the dark, and then kept going back.

The household of Onesiphorus. They have been greeted in that letter, in churches all over the world, every time it is read, for nearly two thousand years. That is a long time to be greeted.

[pause]

Now, let me tell you, gently, how the rest of it went.

Saint Paul did not come out of that prison. Saint Clement of Rome, writing only about thirty years later, says that Paul was in chains seven times, and preached in the east and in the west, and gave his witness before the rulers, and so went to the holy place. And the tradition of the Church in Rome is that he gave his life for Christ outside the city, on the road that runs down to the sea at Ostia, in the time of Nero. The great church of Saint Paul Outside the Walls stands there now. And the Church remembers him together with Saint Peter, on the twenty-ninth of June, in East and West alike.

Did Timothy reach Rome before the end? Nobody knows. We'd like to think so.

And Onesiphorus?

The Church in the West remembers him in the Roman Martyrology, in September. In the East, the Orthodox Church counts him among the Seventy Apostles, the wider circle of the Lord's disciples, and keeps his memory with others of that company. And later stories grew up about him, as later stories do. They say that he went on preaching, and that in the end he too gave his life for Christ. Those are legends. They may have something true at the bottom of them, or they may not. We don't know. The letter doesn't tell us how his story ended. It only tells us the part that mattered most to Paul.

Rome is still enormous. The old prison under the Capitol is still there, and you can go down the stairs into it, and there is a little altar, and people light candles. Whether or not Paul was ever in that particular room, it is a good place to say a prayer for anyone who is in prison now.

Because there are still people in prisons. And there are still people who go and visit them. Who give up their Saturdays to sit on plastic chairs in visiting rooms. Who write letters to people whose families have stopped writing. Who bring a book, or a phone card, or just their face. They do not get thanked very often. Their names are not usually read out in church.

But there is a name for them, I think. A household, you might call it. The household of Onesiphorus. The people who go looking. The people who are not ashamed of the chain.

And as for the rest of us, most of us will never go to Rome to look for an apostle. But most of us know someone sitting in some kind of dark. Someone everyone else has quietly stopped visiting. Someone ill, or old, or in trouble, or just very lonely. And most of us know where the steps are. And how steep they are. And that there is probably somebody at the top who would be glad of a fig.

He often refreshed me. He was not ashamed of my chain. He searched for me eagerly, and he found me.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Letter to Timothy, chapter one, verses fifteen to eighteen, and chapter four, verse nineteen. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read the letter as Scripture, and the Church has always received it as Saint Paul's last letter, written from his final imprisonment in Rome. Many modern scholars think it may have been written by a follower of Paul in his name. The Church has always read it as Paul's, and we have told it that way. Every word Paul says in tonight's episode is from the letter, in our own plain rendering, with nothing added. Whether Onesiphorus was still living when the letter was written, it does not say, and we have not guessed.

The road to Rome by Puteoli, the Forum of Appius and the Three Taverns is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twenty-eight. Nero blaming the Christians for the fire is in the historian Tacitus. The widows at the prison gate, and the helpers sent from the cities of Asia, are from Lucian's satire on Peregrinus, written about a century later and meant as mockery. The carts and the sleeplessness are from the poet Juvenal. Paul's seven chains are from the First Letter of Clement, and his death under Nero from Eusebius.

The Onesiphorus of Iconium, with Lectra, Simmias and Zeno, and the description of Paul's eyebrows, come from the Acts of Paul and Thecla, a second-century story that is not Scripture. Whether he is the same man is not known. The tradition about the Mamertine prison is a later one. The stories of Onesiphorus's later life are legend.

Everything else is our own invention: why he went, how he travelled, the cheese, the camp, the clerk, the doors, the old woman and her basket, the soldier and his figs, the dark room, and the supper at Ephesus.

That's the story of Onesiphorus, who went looking for Saint Paul in Rome. He is remembered in the Roman Martyrology in September, and among the Seventy Apostles in the Orthodox Church, and Saint Paul is remembered with Saint Peter on the twenty-ninth of June. And here is a blessing, from the same letter, the words Paul wrote just before he greeted the household of Onesiphorus. The Lord will rescue me from every evil, and bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom. To him be glory for ever and ever. Amen. The Lord be with your spirit. Grace be with you.

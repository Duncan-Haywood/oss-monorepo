Imagine a market in Rome, on an ordinary morning, about fourteen hundred and forty years ago.

It is loud. There are men selling olive oil out of jars as tall as a child, and men selling cloth, and men selling cheese that you can smell from three stalls away. There are donkeys complaining. There is a man who has been shouting the same price for an hour and has not sold a thing.

And walking through it all, slowly, in a plain dark habit, is a monk who has not come to buy anything. He is about forty-five. He has a thin face and a bad stomach and the sort of eyes that notice things.

He stops. He has noticed something.

Standing in a row near the end of the market, waiting to be sold, are three boys. They are pale as milk. Their hair is the colour of straw in August. They are looking at the stones of the street, the way children do when they are trying very hard not to cry.

The monk walks over to them. He kneels down, which is not something people did for boys in a market. And he asks them a question.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

First, the city.

If you had visited Rome in its great days, five hundred years before our story, you would have found the biggest city in the world. A million people, some say. Marble everywhere. Aqueducts striding in over the hills on their tall legs, bringing fresh water into fountains on every corner. Public baths the size of cathedrals. And the Senate, and the Emperor, and an army that went all the way from Scotland to Syria.

By the time of tonight's story, it was rather different.

The emperors had moved to Constantinople, far away in the East, and they did not come back to visit. The western half of the empire had fallen to pieces. Rome had been besieged, and captured, and besieged again, by Goths, and then by the armies coming to rescue it from the Goths, which was, if anything, worse. The aqueducts had been cut. The great baths were dry. A city built for a million people had perhaps a few tens of thousands left in it, rattling about among the ruins like peas in a very large jar. People grew cabbages in the old public squares. The marble was being taken away, slab by slab, to build other things, or burnt to make lime.

And now there was a new enemy in the north of Italy, a people called the Lombards, who came down every few years to see what they could take. And the river Tiber flooded. And there was plague.

It was, in short, not a cheerful time to be a Roman. It felt to a great many people like the end of the world. Gregory himself thought it probably was, and said so, more than once, in his sermons. I mention this because I want you to understand that what he is about to do is not the plan of a man who thinks everything is going well. It is the plan of a man who thinks everything is going badly, and has decided to do something kind anyway.

Now, the man himself.

Gregory was born in Rome, about the year five hundred and forty, into one of the old families. His father, Gordianus, was a wealthy official of the Church. His mother, Silvia, was a woman of great holiness, and she and two of Gregory's aunts are all honoured as saints too, which must have made family dinners rather intimidating for everyone else. They had a big house on the Caelian Hill, one of the seven hills of Rome, looking across to the ruins of the old palaces.

Gregory was clever, and well educated, and good at organising things, which in a falling city is a very useful kind of person to be. When he was about thirty-three, he was made prefect of the city, which was something like being mayor of Rome, and in charge of the grain supply and the walls and the police and the roads and the drains, of which there were a great many, mostly broken.

He was very good at it. And he did not like it at all.

* * *

Here is the first surprise in Gregory's life. At the height of his career, with everyone expecting him to rise further, he gave the whole thing up.

His father had died. Gregory took his share of the family fortune, which was very large, and he gave it away. He founded six monasteries on the family estates in Sicily. And he turned his own house on the Caelian Hill into a monastery, dedicated to Saint Andrew the Apostle, and he went inside, and became a monk in it.

I like to imagine the neighbours. One week the prefect of Rome lives next door, with his clerks and his guards and his messengers coming and going at all hours. The next week the gate is shut and there is singing at three in the morning. That is a guess, and I am fairly sure about the singing.

Gregory said, later, that the years in that monastery were the happiest of his life. He prayed. He read the Scriptures until he knew them almost by heart. He fasted, rather too hard, it seems, because his stomach was never right again, and for the rest of his life he suffered from indigestion, and later from gout, and complained about both of them in his letters, cheerfully and at length. If you have ever had a friend who answers the question how are you with a full medical report, you have a fair idea of one side of Saint Gregory the Great. He was a saint. He was also a man with a bad stomach who wanted you to know about it.

The happy years did not last. The Bishop of Rome, the pope, needed people like Gregory, and soon he was ordained a deacon, and sent off to Constantinople, to the court of the Emperor, as the pope's representative there. He spent about six years in the great city on the Bosphorus. He never learned to speak Greek well, which was the language of the court, and he did not much like the politics. He took some of his monks with him, so that he could keep living a monk's life in the evenings, and he gave them talks on the Book of Job, which went on for years and later filled thirty-five books. That is a great deal of Job. I think the monks must have been very patient, or very holy, or both.

And then, at last, he was allowed to come home, to Saint Andrew's, to his cell, and his books, and his bad stomach. And it was around this time, when he was a monk again in Rome and not yet anything more, that he went for a walk through the market.

* * *

Now, Bede, who tells us this story, was an English monk who lived in the north of England about a hundred and forty years later. He was a very careful historian, the most careful of his age, and he tells us plainly that this part was handed down by the tradition of the elders. In other words, he had not seen it in any letter. It was a story the English told about how they first came into Gregory's mind. I think it is a very good story, and it may well be true. But it is a story, and we will tell it as one.

Here is how it goes.

Some merchants had lately come to Rome with goods to sell, and a crowd had come to the market to look at them. Gregory came along with the rest. I have given him a friend for the walk, a younger monk from Saint Andrew's whom I am going to call Brother Felix, because it is a good Roman name and because I need someone for Gregory to talk to. Brother Felix is ours. Bede does not mention him.

"We are only buying lamp oil," said Brother Felix, firmly, as they came in under the arches. "And we are not stopping. You always stop."

"I never stop," said Gregory, and stopped.

Among the things for sale, Bede says, there were some boys. And here I must say something gently, because it is a hard thing to say at bedtime. In those days, people could be bought and sold. It was an old and very wicked custom, and it was very common, all around the world, and it was taken for granted, even by many people who were otherwise good. The boys had been brought a long way, from the other end of Europe, over the sea and across the whole of Gaul, and they were for sale.

Bede says they were fair skinned, with fine faces, and very beautiful hair. In Rome, where nearly everyone was dark haired and olive skinned, they must have looked as though they had come from the moon.

Gregory looked at them for a long time. Then he asked the merchant where they were from.

"Britain," said the merchant. "An island, a very long way north. At the edge of the world. It rains."

That last part is ours. But it does rain.

Gregory asked whether the people of that island were Christians.

"No," said the merchant. "Pagans, all of them."

And Bede says that Gregory sighed, from the bottom of his heart, and said something like this. "What a pity it is, that faces so full of light should belong to people who still live in darkness."

Then he asked what their people were called.

"Angles," said the merchant.

And here is the first pun. Gregory said, "That is right. For they have the faces of angels, and they ought to be fellow heirs with the angels in heaven."

In Latin, which is what they were speaking, this works much better. The word for the English was Angli. The word for angels was angeli. Not Angli, but angeli. You can hear how close it is.

Brother Felix, I think, closed his eyes for a moment. He knew where this was going.

Gregory asked the name of the province the boys came from.

"Deira," said the merchant. This is a real place. It was a kingdom in the north of England, roughly where Yorkshire is now.

And here is the second pun. Gregory said, "De ira. Out of wrath. Good. They shall be rescued out of wrath, and called to the mercy of Christ."

De ira is Latin for from wrath, or from anger. Deira. De ira. You can see that the man could not help himself.

And then he asked the name of their king.

"Aelle," said the merchant. And there really was a king of Deira called Aelle at about this time, so the story has its facts in the right place.

And here is the third pun, and it is the best one. Gregory said, "Alleluia. The praise of God the Creator must be sung in those parts."

Aelle. Alleluia.

Now, I have told you three puns, one after another, and you may be thinking that this is a strange way for a great saint to behave. I think it is a lovely way. Notice what he is doing. He is standing in front of three frightened boys in a foreign market, who do not understand a word he is saying, and everything they are, their faces, their country, their king, he turns into a promise. Angels. Mercy. Alleluia. It is the opposite of what a market does. A market looks at a person and asks what they are worth. Gregory looked at them and asked what God wanted for them.

The old story does not tell us what became of those three boys. I would like to think that Gregory bought them, then and there, and took them home to Saint Andrew's, and that they had a good supper. But the story does not say so, and I will not say so either. What we do know, from one of his own letters which still survives, is that a few years later, when he was pope, Gregory wrote to a priest called Candidus, who was looking after the Church's lands in Gaul, and told him to use the money from those lands to buy English boys of about seventeen or eighteen, so that they could be given to monasteries and brought up to serve God. Whether that letter has anything to do with the market, nobody knows. But it does tell you that the English boys stayed in his mind.

* * *

And here is where the story gets better, or sillier, depending on how you feel about locusts.

Bede says that Gregory went straight to the pope, the Bishop of Rome at that time, and begged him to send someone to preach to the English. And he offered to go himself.

He meant it. He was a man in poor health, in his forties, with a weak stomach, and he was offering to walk across the whole of Europe and get into a boat and go to an island at the edge of the world where it rained and nobody spoke Latin. The pope agreed. Bede says the pope was willing, but that the citizens of Rome would not allow it. Gregory was much too important to them. He was the man who knew how the grain supply worked.

But there is another, older telling, from a book written by a monk, or perhaps a nun, at the abbey of Whitby in the north of England, about a hundred years after Gregory died. It is the very first life of Gregory that was ever written, and it is a charming, muddled, loving book, and its writer says, quite frankly, that some of the things in it may not be exactly right. That writer tells us that Gregory did not only offer to go. He actually set off.

He slipped out of Rome with a few companions, and they walked north along the road, for three days. And on the third day they stopped to rest, and Gregory sat down by the road to read, as he always did whenever he sat down anywhere.

And a locust landed on his book.

It sat there, in the middle of the page. And Gregory looked at it for a while. And then he said, "Locusta." Which is the Latin for locust. And then he said, in the same breath, "Loco sta." Which is Latin for stay in your place.

Brother Felix, I am certain, would have groaned out loud. I am not sure the real companions did, but the Whitby writer does not tell us, so I am free to suppose it.

Gregory took the locust, and its pun, as a sign that he was not meant to go. And sure enough, says the Whitby Life, while they were still sitting there, messengers came galloping up the road from Rome. The people had found out he was gone. They had surrounded the pope in the street and shouted at him, and the pope had sent riders to bring Gregory back. And back he came.

Now, you may believe the locust or not. I find I would like to believe it, mostly because it is exactly the kind of joke that Gregory made, and it would be very like God to send him one back. But it is a legend, written down a long time later, and Bede, who was more careful, does not tell it. So keep it on the shelf where we keep the stories that are too good to check.

What is certain is this. Gregory did not go to England. He went back to Saint Andrew's. And the boys with the straw coloured hair went into a corner of his mind, and stayed there, like a letter waiting to be answered.

* * *

Then, in the year five hundred and ninety, the plague came to Rome.

It was a bad one. Among those who died was the pope himself, Pelagius. And the clergy and the people of Rome, all together, chose Gregory to take his place.

He did not want it. He wrote to the Emperor in Constantinople asking him to refuse to confirm the choice, which was how things were done in those days, and his friends in the city intercepted the letter. Another Gregory, the bishop of Tours, in Gaul, who heard the story from a deacon who had been there, says that Gregory planned to slip away and hide. But he was found, and brought back, and consecrated. And that was that. The monk who wanted nothing more than his cell and his books was now Bishop of Rome, in a city with no water, no army, a plague, a flood and the Lombards at the gate.

He was, as it turned out, extremely good at it.

He fed the city. He organised the Church's farms in Sicily and Italy to send grain to Rome, and kept lists of the poor, and sent food out to them every day, and a biographer writing a few hundred years later says that when he heard that a beggar in Rome had died of hunger, he took it so hard that for some days he would not say Mass, as though it were his own fault. He negotiated with the Lombards when nobody else would. He wrote letters, hundreds and hundreds of them, to bishops and kings and empresses and farm managers, about everything from doctrine to the price of horses. More than eight hundred of them survive. He wrote a book for bishops about how to look after their people, called the Pastoral Rule, which bishops were still reading a thousand years later. And he wrote a book of stories about the holy men of Italy, in the form of a conversation, called the Dialogues, which is why, in the Orthodox Church, he is lovingly known as Saint Gregory the Dialogist.

And through it all he called himself by a title which popes have used ever since, and which I think is one of the best titles anyone ever invented for himself. He called himself the servant of the servants of God.

But he did not forget the boys in the market.

* * *

It was six years into his time as pope, in the year five hundred and ninety-six, when he finally did something about it.

He could not go himself. So he sent the next best thing. He went back to his own old monastery, Saint Andrew's on the Caelian Hill, the house he had grown up in, and he chose the prior there, a monk called Augustine. Not the famous Augustine, the bishop of Hippo, who had died nearly two hundred years before. This was a different, quieter Augustine. We know almost nothing about him before this moment. He had been a good monk. He was trusted. And now he was to lead a company of monks to the very end of the world, to preach to the English.

I want you to imagine the evening that the news was given out, in the refectory at Saint Andrew's. This part is ours.

The monks are eating their supper, which is beans, because it is always beans. And the prior, Augustine, stands up, and clears his throat, and says that the Holy Father has asked for volunteers, and that it is not, strictly speaking, a request.

"Where to?" says someone.

"Britain," says Augustine.

There is a long pause.

"Is that the one with the rain?" says someone else.

I think it was a brave company. Bede says there were about forty of them, by the time they got to Kent, though that number may have included some who joined them on the way. They set off north, out of Rome, along the old road. And it went well for a while.

It went well until they got to Gaul, somewhere in what is now the south of France. And there, Bede tells us, they began to lose their nerve.

They had been talking to people along the road, I suppose, and the people had been telling them about the English. That the English were a fierce nation. That they were barbarians. That they did not speak a word of any language the monks knew. That nobody in his right mind went there. And Bede says that the monks were seized with a sudden fear, and began to think that it would be much better to go home.

I do not blame them. I think if I had walked out of a peaceful monastery in Rome, and got halfway across the continent, and was then told that the people at the other end were very large and very unfriendly, I would have thought about home too.

So they sent Augustine back to Rome, to ask Gregory, very politely, whether they might please be let off.

And Gregory wrote them a letter. And this letter we have. Bede copied it into his history, and it is dated the twenty-third of July, in the year five hundred and ninety-six. I will give it to you in our own plain words.

He said, more or less: It would have been better not to start a good work than to turn back from it once it has begun. So, my dearest sons, keep going. Do not be put off by the hardship of the journey or by the tongues of people who want to frighten you. The greater the labour, the greater the glory. I have made Augustine your abbot, so do as he says. And then he said something which I find very moving, for a man in charge of a whole city and a falling world. He said: I wish I could be working with you. Since I cannot, I hope that I may at least share in your reward, because I truly wanted to come.

And the monks read the letter, and looked at each other, and turned around, and kept walking north.

* * *

They came across the narrow sea in the spring of the year five hundred and ninety-seven, and they landed on the Isle of Thanet, on the eastern tip of Kent.

Thanet is not an island now, but it was then. There was a channel of water between it and the rest of Kent, which Bede says was about three furlongs wide, which is something under half a mile. It was a quiet place to land. And I think it was chosen on purpose, because it was near enough to the king to be noticed, but far enough away to be polite. They had brought some interpreters with them from Gaul, Franks who could speak something like the English tongue. And they sent word to the king that they had come from Rome, and that they brought the very best news there was.

The king of Kent at this time was Ethelbert. And here is the part of the story that I think God had been quietly arranging for some time.

Ethelbert had a wife called Bertha. She was a Frankish princess, from across the sea, the daughter of a king of Paris. And she was a Christian. When she had married Ethelbert, her family had made it a condition that she be allowed to keep her faith, and she had brought with her a bishop, called Liudhard, to be her chaplain. Outside the town of Canterbury, there was an old church, built in the time of the Romans, long ago, and fallen out of use, and Bertha had had it repaired, and dedicated it to Saint Martin of Tours, who, as you may remember from an earlier episode, was famous for cutting his cloak in half. And there she prayed.

So the king of Kent was not a stranger to Christians. He had been married to one for years. He did not know quite what to make of them, but he was used to having one about the house.

After some days, the king came down to the island to meet the monks. But he would not meet them indoors.

Bede tells us why, and it is one of my favourite details in all of English history. The king sat down in the open air, and made the monks come to him there, because he had heard an old superstition, that if you met magicians in a house, they could cast spells on you more easily, and he thought that out of doors he would be safer.

I like this enormously. He has been married to a Christian for years. He knows her bishop. And still, just in case, he would like to be outside, where the spells will blow away.

And so the monks came to him across the field. And this is how Bede describes them. They did not come with any magic power, he says, but with the power of God. They carried a silver cross in front of them, like a banner. And they carried a picture of our Lord and Saviour, painted on a wooden board. And they were singing litanies as they came, praying for themselves and for the people they had come to.

I want you to picture it. A grey sky. The wind off the sea. A king on a seat in the middle of a field with his warriors standing round him, trying to look as though they were not nervous of forty monks. And over the grass, slowly, a line of men in dark habits, with a silver cross catching what light there was, singing in a language nobody there could understand, and a painted face of Christ, carried high, looking at all of them.

The weather is ours. Bede does not mention it. But it was Kent, in the spring, so I think the odds are good.

Then Augustine sat down in front of the king, and through the interpreters, he told him the whole of the good news, from the beginning.

And when he had finished, the king answered him. And this answer, too, Bede has kept for us, and I want to give it to you nearly as he gives it, because it is one of the most honest and courteous things a pagan king ever said.

The king said: Your words and your promises are very fair. But they are new to us, and we do not yet know what to make of them. So I cannot agree to them yet, and give up the things which I and all the English have held for so long. But you have come a long way, into my kingdom. And I can see that you want to share with us the things that you believe are true and good. So we will not harm you. We will give you a welcome, and we will see that you have what you need to live. And we will not stop you from preaching, and winning over to your faith as many people as you can.

I think that is a wonderful answer. It is not yes. But it is the very best kind of not yet.

* * *

And so they came to Canterbury.

The king gave them a house in the town, and food, and leave to preach. And the monks walked from the coast towards the city, and as they came near to it, they formed up into a procession, the way they must have done on feast days in Rome, with the silver cross and the painted image of Christ carried in front. And Bede tells us the words they sang as they came down the road to the gates of Canterbury. It was an old prayer of the Church in Rome, used in processions in times of trouble. I will give it to you in plain words.

We beseech you, O Lord, in all your mercy, that your anger and your wrath may be turned away from this city, and from your holy house, for we have sinned. Alleluia.

Alleluia.

I want you to notice that word, at the very end of the prayer, because Bede noticed it too, and I am sure he meant us to. Years before, in a market in Rome, a monk had looked at three frightened boys and heard the name of their king, Aelle, and he had said, Alleluia must be sung in those parts. And now here it was, being sung, in those parts, at the gates of an English town, by his own monks, on an ordinary road, in the rain, I expect.

And the prayer itself asks for the very thing Gregory had said in the market, when he heard the name of Deira. That the people would be rescued out of wrath, and brought to mercy.

Some promises take a long time to keep. That one took about ten years. But it was kept, word for word.

The monks settled down in Canterbury. And Bede says they lived the way the first Christians had lived in the Book of Acts, praying, fasting, keeping vigils, preaching to whoever would listen, taking from the people only what they needed to eat, and living what they preached. They went to pray in Queen Bertha's little church of Saint Martin. And people began to notice them. Bede says they admired the simplicity of the monks' innocent lives, and the sweetness of their teaching.

And after a while, King Ethelbert himself believed, and was baptised. Bede does not give the date. Later tradition says it was at Pentecost, in that same year, but nobody can be quite sure. What is certain is that, being a good king, and having had a long think about it, he did not force anyone to follow him. Bede says he had learned from his teachers that the service of Christ must be willing, and not compelled. He only loved those who believed a little more, as fellow citizens of the kingdom of heaven.

* * *

Now I want to slow down.

Back in Rome, a man with a bad stomach and a great deal of paperwork was reading his letters. And in the year five hundred and ninety-eight, he wrote one to a friend of his, Eulogius, the patriarch of Alexandria, far away in Egypt, to tell him the news. And this letter survives too. In it, Gregory says, with what I can only describe as delight, that at the last Christmas, more than ten thousand of the English had been baptised by Augustine and the monks with him.

I do not know how anyone counted. But I like to imagine Gregory writing it down, and stopping, and reading it over again, and then going to the window, and looking out at the ruined city, and thinking of the boys in the market.

He went on writing to Kent for the rest of his life. He sent more monks, with books, and vestments, and the vessels for the altar, and relics of the apostles and martyrs. He sent Augustine long, patient answers to a great many questions, about how things ought to be done in a new Church in a strange country. He sent him a gentle letter warning him not to be proud of any wonders that God might work through him, because they were God's work and not Augustine's, and the only thing worth rejoicing in was that his name was written in heaven. Which is a very good thing for a holy man to be told, and Gregory knew it, because he had needed telling it himself.

And he sent, by way of one of the new monks, a man called Mellitus, a letter about the old temples of the English, which is one of the wisest letters he ever wrote. He said: do not pull the temples down. Take the idols out of them, and bless them with holy water, and set up altars, and let the people come to the same places they have always come to, so that they will feel at home there, and learn to worship the true God where they once worshipped false ones. And he said, about the people, the thing I would like you to take to sleep with you tonight. He said: it is impossible to cut away everything at once from hard hearts. A man who wants to climb to a high place goes up step by step, and not in leaps.

Step by step, and not in leaps.

I think that is how Gregory did nearly everything. It is how he fed a starving city, and how he wrote eight hundred letters, and how he got forty frightened monks across a continent. And it is how, slowly, over the next hundred years, the English became a Christian people. There were setbacks, and long gaps, and a great deal of rain, and other missionaries came too, from Ireland and Scotland, from the north, and met the ones from Rome in the middle. But it happened. And a century later, a monk in the north of England called Bede, sitting at a desk in the cold, wrote all of it down, so that we would know. And when he came to Gregory, he called him our apostle, the apostle of the English, because although Gregory never set foot in England, it was his heart that had brought the faith there.

Gregory died in Rome, on the twelfth of March, in the year six hundred and four, after a long and painful illness, worn out, still writing letters. On his tomb, Bede tells us, they called him God's consul, which is a very Roman thing to call a holy man, and which I think he would have found rather funny.

And Augustine died in Canterbury, only a little while later. He was buried in the monastery he had founded outside the walls of the city, near Queen Bertha's little church. And that church of Saint Martin, which was old even when Bertha prayed in it, is still there, on its hill, outside Canterbury. And people still pray in it, and sing in it, and on Sundays, I am told, there are still Alleluias.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Most of tonight's story comes from Saint Bede, The Ecclesiastical History of the English People, which he finished in the year seven hundred and thirty-one, about a hundred and thirty years after Augustine reached Kent. The mission is told in book one, chapters twenty-three to thirty-three, and Gregory's life in book two, chapter one. Bede is the best historian of his age, and he copied Gregory's own letters into his book, which is why we can be confident about the letter to the frightened monks in Gaul, the letter warning Augustine about pride in wonders, and the letter to Mellitus about the temples. Some of Gregory's letters, and the dates on them, have been argued about by scholars, and we have given them as Bede gives them.

What is history. Gregory's family, his time as prefect of Rome, his giving up his fortune and his monastery of Saint Andrew on the Caelian Hill, his years in Constantinople, his election in the plague of five hundred and ninety, his reluctance, his feeding of the city, his title, the servant of the servants of God, and his books are all well attested. So are the sending of Augustine in five hundred and ninety-six, the fright in Gaul, the landing on Thanet, Queen Bertha and Bishop Liudhard, the king's meeting in the open air, his answer, the procession with the cross and the painted image, and the prayer sung at Canterbury. The letter about ten thousand baptised at Christmas is Gregory's own, written to Eulogius of Alexandria. The letter to Candidus about buying English youths is also his own, and its connection with the market is a guess, and we said so.

What is tradition, or legend. The scene in the market and the three puns are given by Bede himself as a story handed down by the elders, and we have told it that way. The locust and the recall on the road come from the earliest life of Gregory, written at Whitby in the early seven hundreds, which is older than Bede but less careful. That the king was baptised at Pentecost is later tradition. The story of Gregory refusing to say Mass after a beggar starved comes from John the Deacon's life of Gregory, written in the eight hundreds, and is labelled as his report. And there is a famous later legend we did not tell: that during the plague procession, the people saw an angel on top of the great round tomb of the emperor Hadrian, putting his sword back in its sheath as a sign that the plague was over, which is why that building in Rome is still called the Castle of the Holy Angel. It is a beautiful story, but it appears only centuries later, and Bede and Gregory of Tours do not have it. Likewise, the tradition that Gregory composed the chant that bears his name is much later and doubtful, and we left it out.

What we invented: Brother Felix, and everything he said; the merchant's words beyond what Bede gives him; Gregory kneeling down; the supper of beans at Saint Andrew's and the talk about the rain; the weather on Thanet; the thoughts of the king's warriors; and Gregory at his window. None of it changes what happened or what it means.

One honesty note. We told the market scene gently, but it is about people being sold, which was a common and terrible wrong in Gregory's world. The Church grew slowly in her teaching against it, and today she names it, plainly, as a grave evil against the dignity of the human person. We did not want to pretend it was anything else.

That's the story of Saint Gregory the Great and Saint Augustine of Canterbury for tonight. The Catholic Church keeps Saint Gregory's feast on the third of September, and the Orthodox Church on the twelfth of March, the day he died; Saint Augustine of Canterbury is remembered on the twenty-seventh of May, and in England and Wales on the twenty-sixth. Holy Gregory, servant of the servants of God, and holy Augustine, who kept walking when he wanted to turn back, pray for us, that we may climb toward heaven step by step, and not be afraid of the road. Goodnight, and God bless.

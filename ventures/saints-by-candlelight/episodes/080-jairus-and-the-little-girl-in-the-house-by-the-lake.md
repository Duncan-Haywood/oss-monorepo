Picture a man running, and not enjoying it.

He is not young. He is a man of standing, the kind who is used to walking slowly down the middle of the street and being greeted, and there is no dignity at all in the way he is going now. His robe is hitched up in one hand. His sandals are slapping. Somebody calls his name, and he does not turn his head, because he does not have a single moment to spare for anybody. He is running along the shore of a lake towards a boat, where a crowd is gathering, and in the middle of the crowd is the one person he has come to find.

And when he gets there, this respectable man, he does not stop and clear his throat and begin with a polite remark about the weather. He goes down on his knees in the dust, in front of everybody, and he says, "Please."

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Gospel of Mark, with the tellings of Luke and Matthew beside it, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a few of the small things, like the walk home and the supper at the end, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let me tell you first where we are.

Jesus has been busy. In Mark's Gospel, which is the shortest of the four and the one in the greatest hurry, a great deal has happened already. He has called his first disciples. He has taught, and healed, and quieted a storm on the lake, and on the far side of the lake, in a country of hills and tombs and pigs, he has set a suffering man free and been asked, rather firmly, to leave. And so he has got back into the boat, and his friends have rowed him home across the water, and that is where our story begins. He is back on the western shore, among his own people, and word has gone ahead of him.

It always does. Mark tells us that a large crowd gathered around him as soon as he landed, and I think we can all picture that crowd. It is the sort of crowd you get at a railway station when somebody famous is expected. People are on tiptoe. People are holding up their children. People are saying "Is that him?" and "Where?" and "I can't see," and one man at the back has climbed a wall.

Mark does not tell us the name of the town. He just says that Jesus was by the sea. So we shall leave the town without a name too, a small place with flat-roofed houses, and a harbour with nets spread out to dry, and a synagogue.

I should say something about the synagogue, because the man who is running belongs to it.

A synagogue, in those days, was not quite what a church is to us. It was a meeting house. On the Sabbath, the community gathered there to pray and to hear the Scriptures read aloud and explained. In the week, it was something like a schoolroom, and a place where the town's business could be talked over. And it needed someone to look after it. Someone had to see that the building was kept up, and the scrolls were kept safe, and the services went in good order, and that on the Sabbath the right people read the right passage. That someone had a title, and the title was leader of the synagogue. It was not a priest's office. It was more like the job of a church warden, or the chairman of a small society, a respected neighbour that everyone relied on to be sensible.

His name, Mark tells us, is Jairus.

Now the name is worth a moment, as the Gospels do not often give us the names of the people Jesus helped. Mark gives us this one. Luke repeats it. Matthew, who tells the same story in a good deal fewer words, does not. And it may be that Jairus was a man the first Christians knew, and remembered, and I rather like to think so, though nobody can say.

What we do know about him is a short list. He has a daughter. In Luke's telling she is his only one, and she is twelve years old. And the daughter is very ill.

I want you to think for a moment about what it is to have a sick child, because that is all this story needs from you. There is a particular kind of tiredness that comes with it. You sit up. You listen to the breathing. You feel a forehead with the back of your hand a hundred times in the night, and you learn what the room sounds like at three in the morning, and you promise anything, to anybody, if only. Jairus has been through all of that. Whatever help the town had to offer, he has tried it. And now it is morning, and the girl is worse, and he has heard that the teacher from Nazareth has landed on the beach.

And here is the interesting thing. Jairus is a leader of a synagogue, and not everybody in his position was friendly towards Jesus. Some of them had strong opinions, and not good ones. Jairus has probably heard those opinions. He has, perhaps, agreed with them at a meeting. But he is not at a meeting now. He is a father, and he has run out of anything else to be.

[pause]

* * *

So here he is at the shore, on his knees.

Mark says that he fell at the feet of Jesus and begged him earnestly. And what he says is very short, and I think it is one of the most honest speeches in all of Scripture. "My little daughter is at the point of death," he says. "Come and lay your hands on her, so that she may be made well, and live."

Look at what he is not saying. He does not make a case. He does not list his qualifications or remind anyone of his position. He does not say, "I know you may have heard things about us." He says my little daughter, and he says come.

Matthew tells it a little more starkly, and has him say that the girl has just died, and that if Jesus will only come and lay his hand on her, she will live. The two accounts do not quite match, and I would not worry about that. Matthew is telling the story in a few lines, and he gets to the end of it fast, and the heart of the thing is the same in both. A father asks. And he believes that if the Lord's hand is laid on his child, something will change.

And Jesus goes with him.

That is the whole of his answer. Mark does not give us a speech. He says, "And he went with him." I find that very moving. There is no discussion, no question about whether this is the right moment, no sign of any other business. He gets up, and he goes, and the crowd goes with him, because of course it does. Mark says a great crowd followed, and pressed in on him from every side.

Now let us think about how this journey feels to Jairus. He is a man in a hurry, and he is trying to lead the most important visitor of his life through the narrow streets of a small town, and the streets are full. He is pushing, I expect. He is saying "Excuse me, excuse me, please," to people who have known him all his life, and who are, naturally, enormously interested in where he is going. Somebody has stepped on his foot. Somebody has asked him how his wife is. A man with a donkey is blocking the way, and the donkey, as donkeys will, has chosen this exact moment to have a view.

It is the longest short walk that Jairus has ever taken. And then, in the middle of the crowd, Jesus stops.

[pause]

* * *

Now we must meet someone else, because there is another person in the crowd, and Mark gives her almost as much room as he gives Jairus.

She has no name. Mark calls her simply a woman. And what he tells us about her could be written on a very small card. She has had a flow of blood for twelve years. She has been to many physicians, and has suffered a great deal under their care, and has spent all that she had. And she is no better. Mark says, with a dry plainness I always find touching, that she was rather worse.

So she is poor now, and tired, and has been ill since about the time Jairus's little girl was born. Twelve years. I should like you to hold that number in your mind, because Mark means us to, and Luke does too. A girl has lived for twelve years in a house with two people who love her. A woman has been ill for twelve years, on her own. They have never met. They are about to be brought into the same story, and the same number sits quietly on both of them.

There is something else to know. According to the Law of Moses, in the book of Leviticus, a woman with a long flow of blood was counted ritually unclean, and so was anything or anyone she touched. This did not mean that she was thought wicked. It was a matter of the rules for the temple, and for community worship. But it did mean that for twelve years she had been, in practice, kept at a little distance. She could not easily go to the synagogue. She might not be welcome at a neighbour's table. A great many doors, over twelve years, would have quietly closed. And I think we may fairly say, though it is a reading and not something Mark spells out, that this was a lonely thing as well as a painful one.

So she has come into the crowd without being noticed. She has heard about Jesus. She has made up her mind. And what she says to herself, Mark tells us, is this: "If I touch even his garments, I shall be made well."

She does not plan to ask. She does not want to make a speech, or to be seen, or to be a bother to anybody. She only wants to reach out, from behind, in a crowd of people all pushing, and touch the edge of his cloak. Matthew says it was the fringe. Pious Jewish men wore a tassel at the corner of their cloaks, on the command in the book of Numbers, as a reminder of the commandments of God. It would have hung low, at the edge, where a hand might reach it.

And she reaches, and she touches it.

Mark says that immediately the flow of blood dried up, and she felt in her body that she was healed of her trouble. Just like that. Twelve years, and it is over, in the space of one breath, in the middle of a shoving crowd on a hot day. And she does what I think most of us would do. She begins to slip away.

[pause]

But Jesus has stopped.

He has felt it. Mark puts it so simply. Jesus, perceiving in himself that power had gone out from him, turned about in the crowd and said, "Who touched my garments?"

Now I should like to defend the disciples for a moment, because they get a hard time over what happens next. They look at him, and they look at the crowd, which is pressing against him on every side like the sea against a pier, and they say, quite reasonably, "You see the crowd pressing around you, and yet you say, who touched me?"

It is exactly what you or I would have said. We would have said, "Lord, everyone is touching you. Look at them. We are practically carrying you."

But Jesus knows the difference between being jostled and being touched in faith, and he keeps looking. Mark says that he looked all around to see who had done it. And the woman knows. She understands that she cannot go on being quiet, and she comes forward, Mark says, in fear and trembling, knowing what had happened to her. She falls down in front of him. And she tells him the whole truth.

I should like to say something about that, too. She does not have to. She has been healed, and nobody knows but Jesus, and she could simply have gone home. But she comes forward, trembling, in front of the whole crowd, and tells her entire story, every year of it, out loud. And I think that when Jesus looks all around him, he is not looking for someone to blame. He is looking for someone to meet. He wants her to have more than a cure. He wants her to be seen, and spoken to, in front of all her neighbours, and set back in her place among them.

And he says to her: "Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be healed of your disease."

Daughter. It is the only time in the Gospels that anyone is addressed this way by Jesus, as far as we are told, and I find it very tender. She came to him as a woman nobody would touch. And she goes away called a daughter, by the Lord, in front of everybody.

[pause]

* * *

But now think of Jairus.

Think of him standing there, in the middle of all this, while it is going on. He has been waiting, and every moment is a moment his own daughter does not have. I do not for a second think that he begrudged the woman her healing. He was a decent man. But I think we may suppose he was standing there with his hands clenched, and his mind in a house some distance away, and a voice inside him saying, over and over, please, please, hurry.

And while Jesus is still speaking to the woman, Mark says, some people come from Jairus's house. They are his own people, neighbours or servants, and they have been running, and they have bad news, and they do not know how to say it, and so they say it plainly.

"Your daughter is dead," they say. "Why trouble the teacher any further?"

It is a dreadful sentence. And I do not think they mean it unkindly. They are telling him the truth as they understand it, and they are trying, in their way, to spare everybody a useless journey. It is over. There is no need to trouble the teacher any longer.

I do not think we should hurry past what that must have been. Jairus has run through the town, and got down on his knees in the dust, and walked through a thronging crowd, and watched the whole thing stop for a stranger. And now the thing he was most afraid of has happened, and he is standing in the street, in the sun, among people who are all looking at him, and he has no idea what to do with his hands.

And then Jesus speaks to him. Mark says that Jesus overheard what they said, or paid no attention to what they said, as some of the old manuscripts and translations have it. Either way, the effect is that he does not let the words stand. He turns to Jairus and says seven words. "Do not fear. Only believe."

Do not fear. Only believe.

It is not an explanation. It does not say what is about to happen. It does not promise anything, quite. It is only a hand put out, in the dark, for a man to hold. And what Jairus does, Mark does not say. He does not say that Jairus answered. He says only that they went on. The man who had run to the shore to ask for help goes back along the road with the one who has just told him not to be afraid, and I think that is the whole of his faith in that moment, to keep on walking.

[pause]

* * *

When they come near the house, Jesus stops the crowd.

Mark says that he allowed no one to follow him except Peter and James and John, the brother of James. These three will be with him on other great days, on the mountain, and in the garden. And so he leaves the crowd behind in the street, and the nine other disciples, and the curious neighbours, and he goes forward with the four of them and with Jairus to the door.

And the house is full of noise.

Mark says that he saw a commotion, people weeping and wailing loudly. Matthew adds the flute players, and I think that is a detail worth stopping for. It was the custom, in that country, for a family that had lost someone to hire mourners and musicians, and for the neighbours to come and cry aloud. It was how a community took part in a grief. It could be a real comfort, and it could be a performance, and probably it was a bit of both, as funerals are today. But whichever it was, the house is full of it, and the sound spills out of the door into the street.

And Jesus goes in, and he says something that must have seemed very strange.

"Why are you making a commotion and weeping?" he says. "The child is not dead but sleeping."

Now let me say something carefully, because this has been understood in different ways. Some have thought that Jesus meant the girl was not truly dead at all, that she was in a very deep sleep or a faint. Some have thought that he meant it as a gentle way of speaking, as people do, and Scripture itself often speaks of the dead as asleep. And the Church has generally read it in the second way, as the Lord seeing death in a different light than we do, as something he can wake a person from, like a morning. The Gospels say that she was dead. The messengers say so, and Luke says that her spirit returned. We shall tell it as they do. But I think "sleeping" is a word we may keep, and carry to bed with us, and be a little comforted.

And the mourners laugh at him.

I want you to notice that. Mark tells us they laughed at him, and Luke says they laughed at him, knowing that she was dead. They are not wicked people. They are sensible. They have seen a death before. They know what they know, and here is a visitor telling them that they are wrong, and so they do what people do when told something impossible in the middle of a sorrow. They laugh, a short and bitter laugh, because the alternative is to hope, and hope, just then, is too heavy to pick up.

Jesus does not argue with them. Mark says that he put them all outside. The flute players go, and the wailing stops, and the house, for the first time in hours, goes quiet. And the quiet is a sort of mercy.

[pause]

* * *

I should like you to picture the room now, because it is a small one.

There are only a few people in it. There is the Lord, and the three disciples who have come with him, standing near the door and keeping very still, the way big men do in a small room. There is Jairus. There is the girl's mother, whom Mark mentions only here, but who has been in the house all this time, and who is the one person in the story who has not left the bedside.

And there is the girl.

She is lying on her bed, in a plain white room, in the middle of a day with the sun still bright in the window. She is twelve years old. She has a little brown cloak hung over the foot of the bed, and a pair of shoes that are a bit too big for her, because her mother always buys them a size ahead. And she is very still. She looks, I think, like a girl who has fallen asleep over a book.

Now Mark does something here that is quite rare in his Gospel. He tells us the exact words that Jesus said, in the language in which he said them. The rest of the Gospel is in Greek, and the Lord would have been speaking, most of the time, in Aramaic, which was the language of ordinary life in that country. Most of the time we have a translation of a translation. But here, Mark keeps the sound. He gives us the words as they came out of Jesus's mouth, and then he adds, for his Greek readers, what they mean.

First, Jesus takes the child by the hand. It is a simple thing. It is what a parent would do. And I will tell you what I think is a quiet marvel. According to the Law, touching a dead body made a person unclean, and Jesus is touching it, taking hold of it, with his own hand. I think we may take that as something rather like his answer to the woman in the street. For him, uncleanness is not something that passes from the sick to the well. It is the other way round. When he takes hold of a hand, it is the life in him that passes into the hand.

And then he speaks. He says, "Talitha cumi."

It is two words. They are very small words, and I will say them again slowly. Talitha. Cumi. And Mark tells us what they mean: "Little girl, I say to you, arise."

Talitha means a little girl, a lass, a young one. It is the sort of word a mother would use at the foot of the stairs in the morning. "Come on, little one. Up you get." There is no thunder in it. There is no command to the heavens or to the powers. It is said quietly, in a small room, to a girl on a bed, by someone who is holding her hand.

And she gets up.

Mark says immediately the girl got up and began to walk about. That is all. She does not rise slowly, or dramatically, or with her hair streaming. She sits up, and looks around, and swings her feet off the bed, and begins to walk about the room. A girl of twelve, who has been ill for who knows how long, and is suddenly just well.

And now Mark gives us a line that I think I like better than any other in the whole story. He says, "And they were immediately overcome with amazement." The father and the mother, and the three big men by the door, who had all stood very still, are suddenly not still at all. I should think the mother had her arms round her before the girl had finished standing up. I should think Jairus, who had run along a shore and gone down on his knees and walked through a crowd, simply sat down on the floor, because his legs would no longer do what he told them.

[pause]

* * *

And now Jesus gives two orders. They are short, and I should like to tell you both, because they are both so entirely like him.

The first is a strict one. He tells them that no one should know about this. Mark says he gave strict orders that no one should know it. Which, as a practical matter, must have been a rather strange thing to ask of a family. There are a great many people in the street. They have just been put out of the house. They have seen the girl lying there. They will, quite soon, see her at the window. It is not a secret that is going to keep. And I do not think Jesus supposed it would. Mark's Gospel is full of this: the Lord asking people to say nothing, and people not being able to help themselves. I think he does not want a spectacle. He does not want the girl to be a wonder, passed from house to house. He wants her to be a child, in her own home, getting better.

And the second order is the one that I love.

"And he told them to give her something to eat."

That is the last thing in the story. After the great moment, after the words in Aramaic, and the amazement, and the laughter that turned into something else, the Lord of heaven turns round in a small room and says, in effect, "She'll be hungry. See that she has something."

It is the most ordinary thing in the world. And it is so completely right. A child who has been ill has not eaten, and the Lord of life notices that. He has just done something that no person could do, and the next thing on his mind is a plate of food. I do not think we should read that as a small point. I think it tells us a good deal about the sort of God we are being shown.

[pause]

* * *

I wonder what Jairus did.

Mark does not tell us. Luke does not tell us. Their story stops, and the three of them go on to other things. But we may allow ourselves a little imagination, as long as we say plainly that it is ours.

I think that for a while nobody quite knew what to do with the evening. I think the mother went into the kitchen and then came back, and went into the kitchen again, because she had gone to fetch bread and forgotten what for. I think a neighbour, who had been sent away with the others, knocked at the door very quietly, and came in, and stood looking, and went home without a word and returned ten minutes later with a bowl of something warm, because that is what neighbours do, and nobody had to ask.

And I think that in the end, a little late, the girl ate. Not very much. A little bread, and some broth, and a fig, perhaps, and the whole family sat on the floor round her and watched every mouthful as if it were a play. And the girl, who had been through all of this without quite knowing, looked at them one after the other and said, "Why is everybody staring?"

That is not in the Gospel. I will say so again. It is only what I think a girl of twelve would say. But I do think her father laughed, then, for the first time in a long while, and I think that laughing was something like a prayer.

[pause]

* * *

Now let us let the evening come down.

The crowd has gone home. The street is empty, and the dust has settled where all those feet stood. Down by the harbour, the boats are drawn up for the night and the nets are drying on their poles. The lake is going from gold to grey to blue, and a few early stars have come out above the hills on the far shore, the same hills, where only a day before, a different man was set free.

In a small house in a small town, a girl is asleep. It is a real sleep this time, the ordinary sort, the sort that is the most natural thing in the world. Her mother has pulled the blanket up. Her father is sitting on the floor by the bed, not saying anything. And the lamp is turned down low, so that it makes a little round of light on the wall.

Somewhere not far away is a woman who, that morning, woke up as she had for twelve years, and who is going to wake up tomorrow as she never has. She is sitting at her own table tonight with a neighbour, who has come, for the first time in a very long while, to sit with her. And somewhere, at the edge of the town, a teacher and his friends are walking out along the road to the next place.

There are two people in this story who are called daughter. Both of them were in trouble for twelve years, one on her own, and one in a house that loved her. And both of them were met by the same hand, in the middle of a day that had begun like any other.

I think that is a good thing to take to bed. We all have something that has gone on for a very long time, or something that has been dreaded, or a message that has come too late. We all have a noise in the house that we cannot quiet. And the Gospel does not say that such things never happen. It says that the Lord is on the road, and that he stops for the one who touches the hem, and that he goes on to the house where the message has said it is too late. It says: do not fear. Only believe. And it says that when everything has been put right, he remembers to ask whether anyone has fed the child.

Let the lamp go down now. Let the street be quiet. Let the lake lap softly at the boats. And let whatever you are carrying tonight be set down, for a few hours, at the foot of the bed, beside the shoes that are a size too big, and the little cloak on the hook.

[pause]

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Gospel according to Mark, chapter five, verses twenty-one to forty-three, with the parallel accounts in Luke, chapter eight, verses forty to fifty-six, and Matthew, chapter nine, verses eighteen to twenty-six. It is Scripture in the Catholic and Orthodox churches. Mark tells it with the most detail, and it is Mark's order, with the healing of the woman set inside the story of the girl, that we followed. The three accounts differ in small ways. Matthew has Jairus say that the girl has just died, where Mark and Luke have her still alive when he first asks, and we have told it in Mark's way, and said that Matthew is brief.

What we have kept from the Gospels: the crowd at the shore; Jairus, a synagogue leader, falling at the feet of Jesus; his request, in Mark's words, that the Lord come and lay his hands on his little daughter; the girl's age of twelve and her being an only child, from Luke; the crowd pressing round; the woman with the flow of blood for twelve years, her spending on physicians and her seeking the cloak; the touch, the stopped bleeding, the question who touched me, the disciples' answer, her coming forward in fear and the whole truth; the words daughter, your faith has made you well, go in peace; the messengers, and do not fear, only believe; the three disciples, and no others; the mourners and the flute players, from Matthew; the child is not dead but sleeping; the laughter; the room cleared; the hand taken; the two Aramaic words and their meaning; the girl rising and walking; the amazement; the strict order to tell no one; and the order to give her something to eat.

What we flagged as uncertain or as a reading. Mark does not name the town. The Church has understood the Lord's word sleeping in more than one way, and we have said that the Gospels themselves call the girl dead and Luke says her spirit returned. That the woman's condition made her unclean under the Law of Leviticus is plain from the text of Leviticus, and that this kept her at a distance in daily life is the usual way of reading the story, but it is a reading, and Mark does not say it. The same goes for the idea that Jesus took the girl's hand in spite of the rule about the dead in the book of Numbers. The word daughter is the only time the Gospels record Jesus using it to address someone, as far as we know, and we said it with that care. Later Christian tradition gives the woman a name, and Eusebius, the fourth century historian of the Church, reports that she came from the city of Paneas and that a statue in her honour stood there. We cannot check it, and the Gospels do not name her, and so we did not either.

What we invented: Jairus's thoughts as he ran; the donkey and the foot; the girl's room, her shoes and her cloak; the mother's trips to the kitchen; the neighbour with the bowl; the girl's remark about staring; and the evening at the end. None of it is in the Gospels, and none of it changes what happens or what it means.

For further reading, Mark chapter five is not long. It is a good chapter for a quiet evening, and I would suggest reading the last verses slowly, and noticing that the final thing in the story is a plate of food.

[pause]

That's the story of Jairus and his daughter for tonight. It has no feast day of its own, but the Catholic Church reads it at Mass on the Thirteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time in one year of its three, and the Orthodox Church reads it in its own cycles of Gospel readings. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Lord Jesus, who took a child by the hand and called her by a small word, take us by the hand tonight, and give rest to every house where someone is watching and waiting. Goodnight, and God bless.

Five women are walking across a camp.

It is early in the morning, and the camp is enormous. Tents in every direction, as far as you can see, in rows and blocks, with little lanes between them where children are already running and goats are already being difficult. Smoke from cooking fires goes straight up into a pale sky.

The five women walk in a line, one behind the other, down one of the lanes. They are not hurrying. They are not dawdling either. They walk the way you walk when you have decided something and you would rather not give yourself time to undecide it.

People look up as they go past. A man mending a sandal stops with the needle halfway through. Two old women at a fire stop talking. A small boy, holding a very unhappy chicken, stands and stares.

Because everyone knows where that lane goes. It goes to the middle of the camp. To the big tent with the cloud above it. To the door, where Moses sits, and the priest, and the leaders of the tribes.

And nobody, in forty years, has ever seen five sisters walk up to that door on their own and ask for anything.

Let me tell you where we are.

We are at the very end of a very long journey. For forty years the people of Israel have been walking, and camping, and grumbling, and walking again, through the wilderness between Egypt and the land that God promised to their fathers. It has not been a smooth trip. There have been quarrels about water. There has been, on more than one occasion, a very strong feeling that they should all just go back to Egypt, where at least the onions were good.

But now it is the fortieth year, and they have arrived. Not in the land itself, not quite. They are camped on the plains of Moab, on the eastern side of the river Jordan. And if you stand on a rock at the edge of the camp in the evening and look west, you can see across the river to the other side. You can see the green of the valley, and the hills going up behind it, and a walled town with palm trees round it, which is Jericho.

It is like the last morning of a long drive, when the children in the back seat have stopped asking "are we there yet", because they can see that they nearly are.

And when you are nearly there, you start to think about practical things. Where will we live? Which bit is ours? Who gets the house with the view?

So God told Moses and Eleazar the priest to count the people.

They had done this once before, at the beginning of the journey, forty years earlier at Mount Sinai. But almost none of the people from that first count were still alive. A whole generation had grown old and died in the wilderness. The people in this camp were their children and grandchildren. So it was time for a new count, and this one had a very particular purpose.

The land was going to be shared out.

The Book of Numbers is very clear about how. Each tribe would get a share, and the big tribes would get a bigger share and the small tribes a smaller one, and the exact places would be decided by lot, which means something like drawing names out of a hat, so that nobody could say the leaders had kept the best bits for their friends. And within each tribe, the land would be shared among the clans, and within each clan among the families. And each family's land would be passed down, from a father to his sons, generation after generation, and it would stay in the family for ever.

That was the important thing, for these people. Not just having land. Having land that had your family's name on it. A field your grandfather had ploughed, that your grandson would plough. For a people who had spent forty years in tents, that must have sounded like heaven.

The count was done tribe by tribe, and it is written out in full in chapter twenty-six of Numbers. It is a long list. I will not read it all to you, because you would be asleep before we got to Gad, and the story has not even started yet. But there is one line in it that matters very much for tonight.

It comes in the tribe of Manasseh. Manasseh was the elder son of Joseph, the Joseph with the coat of many colours, who went down to Egypt. The list goes down through Manasseh's family. Manasseh had a son called Machir. Machir had a son called Gilead. Gilead had a son called Hepher. And then the list says this:

Now Zelophehad the son of Hepher had no sons, but daughters. And the names of the daughters of Zelophehad were Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah.

That's it. One line. But in a list of fathers and sons, it sticks out like a daisy in a car park.

Let me say the names again, slowly, because you will want to know them. Mahlah. Noah. Hoglah. Milcah. Tirzah.

And a little note on one of them, before anybody writes in. Noah, the second sister, is not the Noah of the ark. It is a different name in Hebrew, spelt differently and said a little differently, and it is a girl's name. She did not, as far as we know, own a boat, or any giraffes.

Now, I want you to imagine a tent.

It is the tent of the five sisters, on the edge of the camp of Manasseh, on the evening after the count. The book does not describe it, so I shall describe it for us, and you can know that this part is mine.

It is a goat-hair tent, the dark kind, a little patched. Their father, Zelophehad, has been dead for some time. He died in the wilderness, like almost everyone of his generation. Their mother is not mentioned in the book at all, so we shall leave her in peace. The five of them live together, and they are, I think, good at it, in the way that sisters who have had to look after one another usually are, which is to say they argue constantly and would fight a lion for each other.

I imagine Mahlah, the eldest, as the one who keeps accounts. She knows how many goats there are and which one is limping and who owes them a jar of oil.

Noah is the one who talks. She has an opinion about everything and she shares all of them.

Hoglah is quieter. She is the one who listens at other people's fires and comes home knowing things.

Milcah is practical. She can mend anything, including, on one occasion, a tent pole and a cousin's broken heart, in the same afternoon.

And Tirzah is the youngest, and the others still treat her like the baby, and she minds very much.

None of that is in the book. The book gives us their names, and their family tree, and one speech, and one decision. But it does give us something about them, which is this: they noticed something that nobody else in the camp had noticed. Or if anybody else had noticed it, nobody had said it out loud.

So let us listen in.

It is Hoglah who comes home with the news. She has been at the fire of their uncle, their father's brother, and she has heard the men talking about the count, and the shares, and the lots.

"They're writing down the families," she says, sitting down. "Every man's name, and his sons. That's how they'll share the land. The name goes on the list, and the land goes to the name."

There is a pause.

"Well," says Mahlah, "Father's name is on the list. I heard them read it. Zelophehad son of Hepher."

"Yes," says Hoglah. "With a note under it. No sons."

Another pause. Longer.

"So what happens," says Milcah slowly, putting down the sandal she was mending, "to the share of a man with no sons?"

Hoglah looks at the fire.

"Nothing," she says. "There isn't one. The land goes to the sons. If there are no sons, there's no share. They'll divide it up among the cousins. In twenty years nobody will remember there was ever a Zelophehad. It'll be as if he never lived."

And here, I think, Tirzah, the youngest, says the thing everyone is thinking.

"But that's not fair. He did live. He was our father."

[pause]

Now, before we go on, I want to be careful about something, because it would be very easy to tell this story as if the people of Israel were villains for not thinking of daughters, and that would not be fair either.

In those days, all over the ancient world, land usually went from fathers to sons. It was not something anybody had sat down and decided to be unkind about. It was just the way things were done, the way the sun comes up in the east. A daughter would usually marry, and go to live with her husband's family, on her husband's family's land, and her children would belong to that family. So the land went to the sons, and the sons looked after their sisters and their mother, and that was how it was supposed to work.

It was a system with a gap in it. And the gap was exactly the shape of a family like this one. Five daughters. No sons. Nobody to carry the name.

And these five sisters were standing in the gap.

I imagine Noah wanted to go straight away, that night, and bang on the tent pole.

"You can't go in the middle of the night," says Mahlah. "Nobody makes a good decision in the middle of the night. Including you."

"Then we go in the morning."

"We?" says Milcah. "Women don't go to the door of the tent to argue about land. The men of the clan go. We should ask our uncle to go for us."

"Our uncle," says Hoglah quietly, "is one of the cousins who would get the share."

They all look at her.

That needs a little explaining, and it is important, so let me explain it.

Some years earlier, in the wilderness, there had been a rebellion. A man called Korah, with two others called Dathan and Abiram and a large crowd of supporters, had risen up against Moses and Aaron and said, more or less, who made you two the leaders? We are all holy. Why should you be in charge? It was a very serious business, and it ended very badly for Korah and his company, and I shall not tell it tonight, because it is not a bedtime story and this is.

But here is the point. Everyone in the camp remembered it. And if your father had been one of Korah's company, then people might feel that your family had lost its right to a share. Rebels against God and Moses do not usually get rewarded with fields.

So the sisters needed to say, very clearly, before anything else: our father was not one of those. He was an ordinary man. He died in the wilderness, the way all the men of his generation died, for his own sins, like everyone else, but not for rebellion. He did nothing to forfeit his name.

Tirzah, the youngest, had seen the hole in the argument before anyone else. And I like to think that after that, her sisters treated her a little less like the baby.

Count those. Moses. The priest. The leaders. The whole congregation. That is everybody. That is like walking into the middle of a parliament, a cathedral and a town meeting all at once. Eleazar the priest is new in his robes; his father Aaron has only lately died on Mount Hor. And five sisters walk up to it.

Now, from here on, I am going to give you their words just as the book gives them, as near as I can in plain English, because they are Scripture, and they are very good words, and they do not need any help from me.

They say this.

Our father died in the wilderness. He was not among the company of those who gathered themselves together against the Lord in the company of Korah, but died for his own sin. And he had no sons.

Why should the name of our father be taken away from his family, because he had no son?

Give to us a possession among our father's brothers.

[pause]

That's it. Three sentences. It is one of the shortest speeches in the Bible, and one of the best.

Look at what they do. First they say who their father was, and what he was not. They deal with the Korah question before anybody can raise it. Then they ask a question, a real question, not a complaint. Why should his name be taken away? And then they ask for something specific: a possession, a share of land, among their father's brothers. Not instead of the brothers. Among them. They are not asking to turn the whole system upside down. They are asking to be let into it.

Mahlah would have been proud. No shouting. No complaining. Nothing that wasn't true.

And then there is a silence at the door of the tent.

Now, what would you expect to happen next?

I think if I had been standing in that crowd, I would have expected one of two things. Either Moses would say, sorry, ladies, that's the rule, the land goes to sons, off you go. Or Moses would say, fair point, have a field, and everybody would grumble that he was making things up as he went along.

But Moses does neither.

The book says: Moses brought their case before the Lord.

I love that. It is such a small sentence. Moses, who is a hundred and twenty years old and has been leading this people for forty years and has heard every complaint there is about water and food and quails and onions, does not brush them off. And he does not just decide it himself either. He takes it seriously enough to take it to God. He goes into the tent, and he asks.

The book does not tell us how long the sisters waited outside. It does not tell us what Moses said in there, or how the answer came. The Scripture is very reserved about such things, and so shall we be.

But I imagine five sisters standing very still at the door of the tent, in front of everyone, not looking at each other, because if they looked at each other they might lose their nerve, or start laughing, which is what happens to sisters when they are very nervous. And I imagine Noah, who talks, saying nothing at all for once in her life. And I imagine the boy with the unhappy chicken, who has followed them all the way, sitting down in the dust at the edge of the crowd to see what happens.

And then Moses comes out.

The daughters of Zelophehad are right.

In some translations it says they speak rightly, or they have spoken well, or what they ask is just. But I like the plainest one. They are right.

Think about that for a moment. Of all the people in the Bible who come to God with a question, or a complaint, or an argument, there are not so very many who get an answer that begins: you are right. Abraham pleads with God for the city of Sodom and is listened to, which is not quite the same thing. But these five sisters, whom almost nobody has heard of, walk up to the door of the tent with three sentences, and God says: they are right.

I imagine the crowd. I imagine the man at the back saying, "Well, I never." I imagine the old women at the fire nodding to each other as if they had always known. I imagine the uncle, the one who would have got the share, looking at his sandals.

And I imagine the five sisters walking back down the lane. Not hurrying. Not dawdling. And at the corner, where nobody can see, all five of them hugging each other at once and Tirzah crying and Noah, finally, talking very fast.

But the story does not stop with the five sisters. And this is the part I think is cleverest of all.

Because the Lord does not just give an answer for this one family. He makes a law. Right there, the next thing in the book, he tells Moses to say this to all the people of Israel.

If a man dies, and has no son, then you shall cause his inheritance to pass to his daughter.

And if he has no daughter, then you shall give his inheritance to his brothers.

And if he has no brothers, then you shall give his inheritance to his father's brothers.

And if his father has no brothers, then you shall give his inheritance to his nearest kinsman in his family, and he shall possess it.

And it shall be for the people of Israel a statute and an ordinance, as the Lord commanded Moses.

[pause]

Do you see what has happened? Five sisters asked one question about one family, and the answer became the law for every family. For all the families who would ever have only daughters, in all the generations to come, there was now a way for the father's name to go on. The gap in the system had been closed. Not by tearing the system down, but by the five people standing in the gap having the courage to point at it.

That is quite something to do before breakfast.

And then, right after this, the book turns to something else, something very solemn. God tells Moses to go up a mountain called Abarim and look at the land across the river, the land that Moses himself will not enter. And Moses, being Moses, does not ask anything for himself. He asks God to give the people a new shepherd, so that they will not be like sheep without one. And God tells him to take Joshua, the son of Nun, and lay his hands on him, in front of Eleazar the priest and all the congregation.

So in the very same chapter, at the very same door, the five sisters are given their father's share, and Joshua is given Moses's place. Inheritance, both times. Something passing on, from one generation to the next, so that nothing good is lost. I don't think that is an accident. But that is a story for another night.

* * *

Now, there is a little sequel to this story, and I must tell it to you, because it is true and because it is very human.

Some time later, and the book tells it right at the very end of Numbers, in its last chapter, the leaders of the sisters' own clan come to Moses. These are the heads of the families of Gilead, the sisters' cousins and uncles. And they have been thinking.

And I imagine them coming up to Moses looking slightly uncomfortable, the way people look when they have to raise a point about procedure.

And they say, more or less: the Lord commanded that the daughters of Zelophehad should have their father's share. Very good. Quite right. Nobody is arguing. But.

But what happens if they marry men from another tribe? Then their land will go with them into their husbands' tribe. And it will be added to that tribe's share, and taken away from ours. And in the year of jubilee, when land goes back to its family, it will stay with the other tribe for ever. And our tribe, the tribe of Manasseh, will be a little bit smaller.

You can hear the worry in it. Every family had been given its land by lot. The tribes were all supposed to keep their shares. It was a fair question too.

And Moses, again, by the word of the Lord, gives an answer. And the answer starts in a way you might now be able to guess.

The tribe of the sons of Joseph is right.

Ha! Everybody is right in this story. I find that very cheering. It is a story full of people asking reasonable questions and being told they are reasonable.

And the answer is this. Let the daughters of Zelophehad marry whom they think best. Only, let them marry within the family of the tribe of their father. So that no inheritance shall pass from one tribe to another.

Let them marry whom they think best. I like that line very much. It is their choice. They are not being handed over to anybody. Only within the wider family, so that the land stays where the lot put it.

And the book says: the daughters of Zelophehad did as the Lord commanded Moses. And it lists them again. And here is a small thing that I think is lovely. In this last list, the names come in a different order. Mahlah, Tirzah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Noah.

Tirzah, the youngest, has moved up to second. And Noah has gone to the end.

Now the book does not say why, and scholars have wondered about it, and I am not going to pretend I know. But if you have sisters, you may have your own theory. I have decided that Noah is at the end because she was still talking when the list was written.

And they married, the book says, the sons of their father's brothers. Their cousins. And so their inheritance stayed in the tribe of the family of their father.

That is the last thing that happens in the Book of Numbers. The whole long book, with its counts and its camps and its quails and its quarrels, ends with five sisters and their weddings. I find that a very good way for a book to end.

And there is one more scene, and then we shall begin to wind down.

Years pass. Moses goes up his mountain and sees the land and goes home to God. Joshua leads the people across the Jordan, and we told that story too, with the twelve stones at Gilgal. There are battles and settlements and a great deal of moving in. And at last, the land is shared out by lot, just as Moses had said.

And when it comes to the tribe of Manasseh, the Book of Joshua tells us this. The daughters of Zelophehad came, and stood before Eleazar the priest, and before Joshua the son of Nun, and before the leaders. Again! Another doorway, another set of important men. And they said:

The Lord commanded Moses to give us an inheritance among our brothers.

Just one sentence this time. They do not need three. They have God's word on it and they know it.

I imagine they are older now. Some grey in their hair, perhaps. Children of their own running about. I imagine Mahlah with the same face she wore when she kept the accounts. I imagine Eleazar the priest, who was there at the door of the tent all those years before, and who remembers them, smiling a little in his beard. And Joshua, whose own story turned, in the very same chapter of the book, from Moses's helper into Moses's successor. They were there near each other's beginnings.

And the Book of Joshua says: so according to the commandment of the Lord, he gave them an inheritance among their father's brothers.

And then it adds a little note of arithmetic, and it is one of my favourite pieces of arithmetic in the whole Bible. Manasseh's land west of the river was divided into ten portions, it says, because the daughters of Manasseh received an inheritance along with his sons.

The daughters counted. They were in the sum. They were written into the land itself.

Now, then. Let us start to slow down. Let the fire burn a little lower.

What happened to the five sisters after that, the Bible does not tell us. They settled in the hill country of Manasseh, in the middle of the land, with its olive trees and its terraces and its little springs. They would have planted, and harvested, and pressed oil, and argued about the goats. Their children's children would have ploughed the fields that had their grandfather's name on them, Zelophehad's fields, which by all the rules of the time should never have existed.

There is something rather wonderful about that, about how long a name can last when somebody bothers to stand up for it.

And here is something that may be the sisters' names still on that hillside centuries later.

A long time afterwards, nearly eight hundred years before Christ, in the city of Samaria, the capital of the northern kingdom, a clerk in the royal storehouses kept records of jars of wine and oil coming in from the villages round about. He wrote them on broken pieces of pottery, which was the scrap paper of the ancient world. Archaeologists dug some of these pieces up, a little over a hundred years ago, and they are called the Samaria ostraca, which is the scholars' word for inscribed bits of broken pot.

And among the names of the districts the oil and wine came from, scholars have noticed two that look very familiar. Hoglah. And Noah.

Now, I must be careful here. A district with the same name as a sister is not proof that the sister lived there, and scholars argue about what it means, as scholars do. Some think the names in Numbers are the names of clans and places as much as of persons. But I like to think of an oil jar, centuries later, with a label scratched on it that says, more or less, "from Hoglah's". The quiet sister, who listened at other people's fires, still listened to.

And there is a third. Tirzah, the name of the youngest, is also the name of a town in those same hills. It became, for a while, the capital of the northern kingdom, before Samaria was built. And it was so lovely that the Song of Songs, the great love poem of the Bible, uses it as a picture of beauty. You are beautiful as Tirzah, my love, it says, comely as Jerusalem.

The baby of the family, ending up in the most beautiful poem in Scripture. She would have liked that, I think.

[pause]

There is one more echo, and it is the last.

Far away, at the end of a very different book, the Book of Job, after all his troubles, Job is given new children. Seven sons and three daughters. And the sons are not even named. But the daughters are. Jemimah, which means something like dove. Keziah, which is the name of a sweet spice, cassia, a cousin of cinnamon. And Keren-happuch, which means a little pot of eye-paint, which is a name I think would be very hard to live up to.

And the book says: in all the land there were no women so fair as the daughters of Job. And their father gave them an inheritance among their brothers.

Among their brothers. The very same words the daughters of Zelophehad had asked for at the door of the tent.

[pause]

The old Jewish teachers loved these five sisters. In the Talmud, the great book of rabbinic discussion written down many centuries later, the rabbis say that the daughters of Zelophehad were wise women. They knew the law, and they knew how to read it, and they were good women too. Wise, and learned, and righteous. That is not a bad thing to have said about you by people who argued about everything.

They were not queens or prophetesses. They did not part any seas or bring down any walls. They were five sisters in a patched tent at the edge of a camp, who noticed that something was not quite fair, and talked it over properly, and said it in three true sentences to the right people. And God said, they are right.

I find that a very comforting thought to go to sleep on. That a small person with a fair question, asked honestly, at the right door, is heard.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty-six, verse thirty-three, where the five sisters are named in the count, and chapter twenty-seven, verses one to eleven, where they come to the door of the tent of meeting, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Their three sentences, Moses bringing their case before the Lord, the words "the daughters of Zelophehad are right", and the law of inheritance that follows are all the book's own, told plainly. Moses on the mountain and Joshua chosen are the rest of chapter twenty-seven. The question from the leaders of Gilead, the answer that they may marry whom they think best within their father's tribe, and the second list of names in a different order are chapter thirty-six, the last chapter of Numbers. The sisters before Eleazar and Joshua and the ten portions are in Joshua, chapter seventeen. Korah's rebellion is Numbers, chapter sixteen, and the daughters of Job are in Job, chapter forty-two.

The Talmud's praise of the sisters is in the tractate Bava Batra. Rabbi Akiva's opinion that their father was the man found gathering sticks on the sabbath, in Numbers fifteen, is in the tractate Shabbat, and another rabbi rebuked him for it there, so we have not told it as fact. The Samaria ostraca are real, and the likeness of names is a scholars' observation, not a proof.

And the sisters' characters, their talk in the tent the night before, the boy with the chicken, the man at the back of the crowd, and the hug at the corner are our own invention.

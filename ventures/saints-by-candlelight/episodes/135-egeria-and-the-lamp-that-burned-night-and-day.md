It is late afternoon in winter, in a great round church, and somebody has just carried a light out of a tomb.

It is only a small flame. It has come from a lamp inside a little cave in the middle of the church, a lamp that has been burning there all day and all last night and all the night before that. Someone touches it to a candle, and the candle to another candle, and another, and another, until the whole round church is so full of light that you could read the smallest writing on the furthest wall.

Then a deacon stands up and begins to read out names. And after every single name, from somewhere near the front, a crowd of small boys shouts, as loudly as small boys can shout, which is very loudly indeed: "Kyrie eleison!"

One of the boys is shouting louder than all the others. And at the back, a woman from the far west of the world is smiling, and trying to remember how to spell it.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

If you have been with us over the last few weeks, you will know Egeria by now. She is the pilgrim who climbed Mount Sinai like a wall, looked out from Mount Nebo, and stood at the gate of Edessa listening to the bishop read a letter. Last time, we kept the Great Week with her, the week before Easter, in Jerusalem.

If you have not met her, here is all you need to know. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, very probably from the north-west corner of Spain. Some time around the year three hundred and eighty, she travelled east to see the places of the Bible with her own eyes, and she stayed in Jerusalem for about three years. And she wrote it all down in a long, careful, delightfully polite letter to a group of women at home, whom she calls her sisters.

She has a lovely way of addressing them. She does not say "you". She says "your affection", the way you might say "your honour" to a judge, or "your majesty" to a queen. "So that your affection may know," she writes, "what is done every day in the holy places, I thought I ought to tell you, because I know you would be glad to hear it."

And that is tonight's story. Not a journey. Not a mountain. Just an ordinary day in Jerusalem. And then an ordinary Sunday. And then two days that were not ordinary at all.

Now, I should warn you that this is the part of Egeria's letter that some people skip. It is the part about services. Who stood where, and who said which prayer, and at what hour the doors were opened. And I understand why people skip it.

But I would like to persuade you not to. Because if you read it slowly, the way she wrote it, it turns out to be one of the most beautiful things in the whole letter. It is a picture of a city that prayed all day long, and most of the night as well, and never quite let the light go out.

* * *

Let me give you the map first, very quickly.

In the middle of Jerusalem, near the western side of the city, stood a group of buildings round the place where Christ was crucified and buried. The emperor Constantine had built them, some sixty years before.

At the western end was a great round church over the tomb. They called it the Anastasis, which is Greek for resurrection. In the middle of the Anastasis was the cave of the tomb itself, with a screen of railings round it, which Egeria calls the cancelli. That is where we get our word "chancel", the part of a church behind the rail.

East of the round church was a courtyard, open to the sky, with a rock in one corner, and a cross on the rock. That was Golgotha. Egeria calls it simply the Cross. "Before the Cross" means in the courtyard in front of it. "Behind the Cross" means a little chapel on the far side.

And east of that was the great church, a huge basilica with columns and marble, which they called the Martyrium.

The tomb, the courtyard with the rock, the great church. Those three. That is all the map you need tonight, apart from a little town about six miles to the south, called Bethlehem, which we will come to later.

The other thing you need to know is how they told the time. There were no clocks on the wall. The day was divided into twelve hours from sunrise to sunset, and the night into twelve more, so an hour in winter was shorter than an hour in summer, which sounds very confusing until you remember that everybody simply looked at the sky. The third hour was mid-morning. The sixth hour was noon. The ninth hour was the middle of the afternoon. And the tenth hour, which is going to matter a great deal tonight, was late afternoon, round about four o'clock, when the light begins to go.

And before all the hours, in the dark, there was the cock. Every town in the ancient world had cockerels, and every cockerel had opinions about the dawn, and in Jerusalem, Egeria tells us, the doors of the holy places were opened at cockcrow and not a minute before. Which means, if you think about it, that the whole timetable of the greatest church in Christendom was being kept, every morning, by a chicken.

* * *

Now for some company.

Egeria does not tell us where she lodged in Jerusalem, or who she talked to. She mentions, just once, that after a long night everyone went home to their lodgings to rest. So I am going to give her a lodging, and I am telling you now that I am making it up.

Let us say that near the holy places there was a small lodging house for pilgrims, kept by a widow called Theodora. Theodora was a sensible woman with strong arms and very little patience for people who got up late. She cooked the bread, and swept the floors, and argued with the water-carrier, and she had a son called Zenon, who was nine.

Zenon was one of the boys who sang at the evening service. And he had a voice, I am afraid, that could have been heard in Bethlehem. He also had, thanks to the pilgrims who had stayed in his mother's house, exactly three words of Latin, which were "yes", "no" and "bread". He used them all the time, in every combination, and with enormous confidence.

Egeria arrived at Theodora's house on a cold evening, with her bags, and her writing things, and a great many questions. And Zenon met her at the door, looked her up and down, and said, very loudly, "Yes! Bread!"

Which, as it turned out, was exactly the right thing to say.

* * *

The next morning, Egeria was woken in the dark.

Not by the cock. Before the cock. By the sound of doors, and footsteps in the street, and people talking quietly as they hurried past.

She lay for a moment, listening. And then she got up, and wrapped her cloak round her, and went out, and followed them.

They were going to the Anastasis. And what she saw there, she wrote down that very day.

"Every day," she tells her sisters, "before cockcrow, all the doors of the Anastasis are opened, and all the monazontes and parthenae come down." Those are Greek words, and she explains them, a little proudly. Monazontes means the monks, the men who have given their lives to God. Parthenae means the virgins, the women who have done the same. "As they call them here," she adds.

"And not only they," she goes on, "but also lay people, men and women, who wish to keep vigil early."

And from that hour until daylight, she says, they sing. Hymns are sung, and psalms are answered, and antiphons too. And after each hymn, a prayer. Two or three priests take their turn each day, and two or three deacons, together with the monks, to say the prayers after each hymn.

Picture it, if you can. A round church in the dark, with lamps glowing here and there, and a few dozen people, most of them in plain dark clothes, singing back and forth to each other. One side sings a line of the psalm. The other side answers. Over and over, quietly, steadily, in the cold, while the city outside is still asleep.

Egeria stood at the back and listened. She did not know all the words yet, because they were in Greek. But she knew the psalms. Everyone knew the psalms. And so after a while she found that she could follow, and then, after a while longer, that she was very nearly singing.

Then, slowly, the windows began to grow grey.

"As soon as it begins to grow light," she writes, "they begin to sing the morning hymns. And behold, the bishop arrives with his clergy."

I love that "behold". She uses it again and again in this part of the letter, "and behold, the bishop comes", as though every single time it were a little surprise. As though the bishop might, one of these mornings, simply not turn up, and every day he did, and every day she was pleased about it.

The bishop goes straight into the cave, inside the screen. And from there, she says, he first says a prayer for everyone. He remembers by name whoever he wishes to remember. Then he blesses the catechumens, the people who are learning the faith and have not yet been baptised. Then he says another prayer, and blesses the faithful, the baptised.

And then he comes out from behind the screen. And everyone, Egeria writes, comes up to his hand. And he blesses them one by one as he goes out.

One by one. Every single person. Every morning.

I have been to services where it took a very long time to shake hands with the priest at the door afterwards. I imagine the bishop of Jerusalem had very well-blessed hands.

And by then, she says, it is daylight. And everyone goes home.

* * *

But not for long.

At the sixth hour, at noon, everyone goes down to the Anastasis again. Psalms and antiphons, until the bishop is sent for. And the bishop comes down, and this time he does not even sit. He goes straight in behind the screen, into the cave, as he did in the morning, and says a prayer, and blesses the faithful. And comes out. And again, everyone comes up to his hand.

"And at the ninth hour," Egeria says, "it is done just the same as at the sixth."

When Egeria came back to the lodging house at noon, Zenon was sitting on the step, eating, and his mother was standing over him with her hands on her hips.

"He is supposed to be at his lessons," Theodora said, in Greek, and then, seeing that Egeria had not understood a word, she said it again, louder and slower, which is what everybody does in every language when they are not understood, and which has never once helped anybody.

Zenon looked up at Egeria, and grinned, and held up his bread.

"Bread," he said. "Yes."

And Egeria, who had grown up in a house where children went to their lessons when they were told to, found that she was laughing, and could not entirely stop.

* * *

And so we come to the tenth hour. The lamp-lighting.

This was the service that Egeria loved best of all the ordinary ones, I think. You can hear it in the way she writes about it. The Greeks called it the lychnikon, she tells her sisters, from their word for a lamp. "We," she says, meaning the people back home in the West, "call it the lucernare." It was the moment, every day, when the light began to fade outside, and the church lit its lamps.

"At the tenth hour," she writes, "the whole crowd gathers again at the Anastasis. All the lamps and candles are lit, and the light is beyond counting."

And then she tells her sisters something which she clearly thought they would want to know, and which I think is the most beautiful thing in this whole chapter.

"The light is not brought in from outside," she writes. "It is brought out from inside the cave, from within the screen, where a lamp is always burning, night and day."

Night and day. Always. In the tomb where the Lord had lain, there was a lamp that was never allowed to go out. And every evening, when the sun went down, the light for the whole church was carried out from that one flame. Not from the kitchen fire. Not from the street. From the tomb.

You do not need me to explain that, I think. But I will just say this. If you wanted to show, without saying a single word, where the light of the world comes from, I do not think you could find a better way.

Then, Egeria says, the lamp-lighting psalms are sung, and the antiphons, for a long time. And behold, the bishop is sent for, and he comes down, and he sits in a high seat, and the priests sit in their places too, and more hymns are sung.

And when they have finished, the bishop rises, and stands before the screen, before the cave. And one of the deacons begins the commemoration. That is, he reads out the names of people to be prayed for, one by one, the way it was always done.

And here is the bit I promised you at the beginning. Because as the deacon reads each name, Egeria writes, "a great many little boys stand there and always answer, Kyrie eleison, which is what we say, Lord, have mercy. And their voices are beyond counting."

Their voices are beyond counting. She uses the same word for the voices of the little boys that she used a moment ago for the light. Infinite. Endless. As if the two things were somehow the same, the light coming out of the tomb, and the sound of a crowd of small boys shouting for mercy at the tops of their voices.

On Egeria's first evening at the lamp-lighting, she stood near the back, and the deacon began to read the names, and the boys began to answer.

"Kyrie eleison!"

And above all the others, clear and loud and very slightly ahead of everyone else, came one voice that she recognised.

"KYRIE ELEISON!"

Somebody near her winced. An old monk in front of her turned round, and caught Egeria's eye, and shrugged, as if to say: well, the Lord did ask for the little children to come to him. He did not say anything about volume.

Then the bishop prays for everyone, Egeria says, and everybody prays with him, the faithful and the catechumens together. And the deacon calls out for every catechumen, wherever they are standing, to bow their head, and the bishop blesses them. And then another prayer, and the deacon calls out again for every one of the faithful to bow their head, and the bishop blesses them too. And so the service at the Anastasis is ended.

But not the evening. Because then, she says, everyone comes up to the bishop's hand, one by one. And then the bishop is led with hymns out of the round church, across the courtyard, to the Cross, and all the people go with him. And before the Cross there is a prayer, and a blessing for the catechumens, and another prayer, and a blessing for the faithful. And then they all go round behind the Cross, and do the same again. And again, everyone comes up to the bishop's hand.

And all the while, Egeria tells her sisters, huge glass lamps are hanging everywhere, a great many of them, and there are tall candlesticks before the Anastasis, and before the Cross, and behind the Cross. So that the whole long procession, from the tomb to the rock and round behind it, is walking through light.

And it is all finished, she says, as the dusk comes down.

"This is done every day," she writes, "for six days of the week."

When they came out of the courtyard into the dark street that first evening, Zenon was waiting for her, hopping from one foot to the other, very pleased with himself.

"Kyrie eleison," Egeria said to him, smiling, and pointed at him, and then pointed at her ears, and pretended to rub them.

Zenon thought about this for a moment. Then he drew himself up to his full height, which was not very much.

"Yes," he said, with great dignity.

* * *

So that was six days of the week. And the seventh day was Sunday.

On Sunday, Egeria says, everything begins even earlier.

"On the seventh day, that is, the Lord's day, before cockcrow, the whole crowd gathers, as many as can possibly fit in the place, as if it were Easter." They gather, she says, in a basilica next to the Anastasis, outside it, where lamps are hung up for exactly this purpose. And the reason they come so early, she explains, is that they are afraid that if they do not, they will not be there in time for cockcrow. So they come ahead of time, and they sit, and they wait.

And while they wait, hymns are sung, and antiphons, with a prayer after each one. Priests and deacons are always there ready for the vigil, she says, because of the crowd. Because the custom here is that the holy places are not opened before cockcrow.

So there they all are, hundreds of them, in the middle of the night, sitting on the floor of a side building, wrapped in their cloaks, singing, with one ear always listening for a chicken.

Theodora went to the Sunday vigil every week of her life. She took a basket with her, with bread in it, and a blanket, and her son. Zenon, who could shout louder than any boy in Jerusalem at four in the afternoon, was, at two in the morning, a heavy, floppy, sleeping bundle, who had to be carried, and who fell asleep again the moment he was put down, with his head in Egeria's lap.

And Egeria sat with him, and sang the psalms in her bad Greek, and waited.

And then, somewhere out in the dark of the city, a cock crowed.

"And as soon as the first cock has crowed," Egeria writes, "the bishop comes down at once and goes into the cave at the Anastasis. All the doors are opened, and the whole crowd goes into the Anastasis, where countless lamps are already burning."

You can almost hear it. The scramble of people getting up off the floor, the rustle of cloaks, Zenon woken suddenly and very cross about it, and the great doors swinging open, and the round church beyond them already full of light, waiting.

Then three psalms, she says. One of the priests says a psalm, and everyone answers. Then a prayer. Then one of the deacons says a psalm, and another prayer. Then a third psalm, by one of the other clergy, and a third prayer, and a commemoration of everyone.

"And when those three psalms are said, and those three prayers," she writes, "behold, censers are brought into the cave of the Anastasis, so that the whole basilica of the Anastasis is filled with the scent."

Smoke. Sweet smoke, rising out of the tomb, and drifting up into the great dome, and filling the whole round church, so that hundreds of people, standing there in the lamplight at cockcrow, can smell it.

And then the bishop takes the book of the Gospels, and goes to the door of the cave, to the door of the tomb itself, and stands there, and reads.

He reads the Resurrection of the Lord.

Think of the women coming to the tomb at dawn with their spices. The stone rolled back. The young man in white, sitting inside, saying: he is not here, he is risen. Now think of hearing that read, at dawn, by the door of the place where, the church in Jerusalem believed, it had actually happened, with the smoke of the incense round you, like the smell of the women's spices.

Egeria tells us what happened next, and she does not dress it up.

"When the reading begins," she writes, "there is such moaning and groaning from everyone, and so many tears, that even the hardest heart could be moved to tears, that the Lord bore so much for us."

She does not say the people cheered. She says they wept. Every Sunday. At the reading of the Resurrection. Because they could not hear that he had risen without also remembering what it had cost.

Egeria looked down. Zenon was standing beside her, quite still, wide awake now, and holding a fistful of her cloak. He was not crying. But he was not shouting either. And she thought, later, that that was the first time she had seen him completely quiet.

Then the bishop came out, and was led with hymns to the Cross, and all the people with him. And there one psalm was said, and a prayer, and he blessed the faithful, and that was the dismissal. And as he went out, everyone came up to his hand.

Then the bishop went home, to rest. And the monks, she says, went straight back into the Anastasis, and went on singing psalms and antiphons until daylight. The priests and deacons took turns every day to keep vigil there with the people. And as for the lay people, Egeria says, men and women, if they wished, they stayed until daylight, and if they did not wish, they went home and went back to bed.

I like that. Nobody was made to feel bad for going back to bed. Some stayed and some slept. Both were allowed.

Theodora went back to bed. So did Zenon, over her shoulder. Egeria, who had walked up Mount Sinai and was not going to be outdone by a monk, stayed.

* * *

And then, when it was light, the main Sunday service, in the great church.

"With the daylight," Egeria writes, "because it is the Lord's day, they go in procession to the great church, which Constantine built, which is on Golgotha, behind the Cross. And everything is done that is done everywhere on the Lord's day."

Everything that is done everywhere. That is the Eucharist, the Mass, the Divine Liturgy, the same at its heart in Jerusalem as it was at home in Spain. Egeria does not describe it, because, she says, her sisters know it already. What she describes is the one thing that was different. And the one thing that was different was the preaching.

"The custom here," she writes, "is that of all the priests who are sitting there, as many as wish to may preach. And after all of them, the bishop preaches."

As many as wish to.

I want you to sit with that for a moment. Imagine a Sunday morning. Imagine every priest in the parish, one after another, each of them with something to say, and then, when they have all finished, the bishop.

Egeria knew exactly what this meant, and she explains it to her sisters with admirable patience. "These sermons are always given on the Lord's day," she says, "so that the people may always be taught in the Scriptures and in the love of God." Which is a wonderful reason. "And because of all these sermons," she goes on, in the very next breath, "there is a long delay before the dismissal, and so the dismissal does not happen before the fourth hour, or perhaps the fifth."

The fifth hour. Somewhere round about eleven in the morning. And they had been up since before the cock.

I do not think Egeria was complaining. I think she was simply reporting, accurately, the way she reports the height of mountains and the number of miles between towns. But I do notice that she says it twice in the same chapter, and I do wonder whether she sat down rather heavily that afternoon.

And after the dismissal in the great church, the monks led the bishop with hymns back to the Anastasis, and the doors were opened, and the faithful went in, and there was a thanksgiving, and a prayer for everyone, and a blessing. And everybody came up to the bishop's hand, one more time. So that, Egeria says, it went on nearly to the fifth or sixth hour.

And then, at the tenth hour, the lamp-lighting, just as on every other day of the week. And the little boys, and the Kyrie eleison, and one voice louder than the rest.

* * *

Now, before we leave the ordinary days, Egeria wants to tell her sisters one more thing. And she says it is the most remarkable of all.

"Among all these things," she writes, "this is especially fine: that the psalms and antiphons are always fitting."

The ones sung at night, and the ones sung in the morning, and the ones sung at the sixth hour or the ninth, or at the lamp-lighting. Always, she says, so fitting, and so well thought out, that they belong to the very thing that is being done.

That is all. That is the thing that impressed her most. Not the gold, not the crowds. That somebody had taken the trouble to choose the right psalm for the right moment. That when the lamps were lit, they sang about light. That when the day began, they sang about the morning. That the words and the hour and the place all fitted together, like a key in a lock.

I think that is what she meant, in the end, when she wrote all this down for her sisters. Not just: here is what they do. But: here is a city that has thought very carefully about how to pray, and has got it right. And perhaps, she was hoping, we could get it right too.

Egeria tried to explain this one evening to Theodora, with her hands, and her bad Greek, and a great deal of pointing. Theodora listened, nodding, while she stirred the soup. Then she said something in Greek that Egeria did not understand.

Zenon translated. "Yes," he said helpfully. "No. Bread."

The bread, it turned out, was burning.

* * *

And so we come to Epiphany.

Egeria was writing for women in the West, and she knew that they kept certain feasts in certain ways, and that Jerusalem did some things differently. And the first great difference came in January.

You see, in Rome, by Egeria's day, the birth of the Lord was already being kept on the twenty-fifth of December. But in Jerusalem, in those years, they kept the Lord's birth on the sixth of January, at the feast they called the Epiphany, the showing forth. It was not until some time later that Jerusalem moved Christmas to December. And the Armenian church, one of the most ancient churches of all, still keeps Christmas on the sixth of January to this day.

So for Egeria, in Jerusalem, the sixth of January was Christmas. And Christmas meant Bethlehem.

And here, I am very sorry to tell you, there is a problem.

There is a page missing.

The only copy of Egeria's letter that survives was made by a monk in Italy, about seven hundred years after she wrote it. And at exactly this point, in that copy, one leaf has fallen out. Just one. Two sides of one page. And it is the page that tells us what happened in Bethlehem on the night of the feast.

We know that the bishop was there, and a great crowd with him, because the very next page talks about them all coming back. We know it was at night. We can guess, from what came before and after, that there were psalms, and readings, and a vigil, at the place where, the church in Jerusalem believed, the Lord had been born. But the details are gone. Somewhere in the eleventh century, or later, somebody dropped a page, and the night in Bethlehem went with it.

I find that almost unbearably sad, and also, in a strange way, rather lovely. Because it means that of all the nights Egeria describes, the one we know least about is Christmas night. It is a little like the Gospels, which tell us that the shepherds came, and saw, and went away praising God, and then tell us nothing more about the shepherds at all. Some nights are not written down. Some nights are just kept.

And then the letter begins again. It begins in the middle of a sentence. In the middle, in fact, of a song.

"Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord," the next page begins, "and the rest of it that follows."

They are walking. All of them. The bishop and the clergy and the monks and the crowd, in the middle of the night, along the road from Bethlehem back to Jerusalem, six miles in the dark, singing. Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord.

"And because of the monks, who go on foot," Egeria writes, "they have to go slowly."

Slowly, for the monks. Not for the old, this time, or for the children. For the monks, who had kept vigil all night, and fasted, and went everywhere on foot, and were in no hurry. The whole crowd, the bishop included, walked at the pace of the monks.

"And so," she writes, "they arrive in Jerusalem at the hour when a person can begin to recognise another person. That is, close to daylight, but before it is light."

That hour. You know it. The hour when you are walking home very early, or very late, and you see a figure coming towards you in the grey, and you cannot tell who it is, and then, quite suddenly, you can. When faces come back. Egeria uses almost the same words, later in her letter, about the dawn of Good Friday. I think it was an hour she loved.

Let us imagine that Zenon walked the whole way. He had insisted, and his mother had said no, and he had insisted again, and then he had been allowed to go with Egeria and an old monk called, let us say, Brother Abraham, who walked very slowly indeed and therefore suited everyone. And for the first mile Zenon sang "Blessed is he who comes" louder than the whole procession put together. And for the second mile he sang it a little more quietly. And for the third mile he held Egeria's hand. And for the last three miles Brother Abraham carried him, fast asleep, over his shoulder, and sang very softly, so as not to wake him.

And when they came to the city, in that grey hour when you can just begin to see faces, they went straight into the Anastasis.

"Where," Egeria writes, "the lights are already burning beyond measure."

Of course they were. Somebody had stayed behind. Somebody had kept the lamp in the tomb alight all night, and lit all the others from it, so that when the crowd came in from the cold, out of the dark, after six miles on foot, there was light waiting for them.

One psalm. A prayer. A blessing for the catechumens, and for the faithful. And then, Egeria says, the bishop goes home. And everyone goes to their own lodging, to rest.

Except the monks. "The monks," she writes, "stay there until daylight, and sing hymns."

Of course they did.

* * *

And then, at the second hour of the morning, when everyone has had a little rest, they all gather again in the great church on Golgotha. And now, at last, Egeria lets herself go.

"How the church was decorated that day," she writes, "and the Anastasis, and the Cross, and in Bethlehem, it would be pointless to write."

And then, of course, she writes it anyway.

"You see nothing there but gold and jewels and silk. If you look at the hangings, they are all silk, striped with gold. If you look at the curtains, they are the same, silk, striped with gold. And every kind of sacred vessel brought out that day is gold, and set with jewels. And the number and weight of the candles, and the candlesticks, and the lamps, and all the other things, could anyone even guess at it, let alone write it down?"

I can see her trying. Sitting at Theodora's table that night, with her pen, starting to count candlesticks, and then giving up, and writing "could anyone even guess at it?" instead.

"And what shall I say," she goes on, "about the decoration of the building itself, which Constantine, with his mother there, adorned with gold and mosaic and precious marble, as far as the resources of his empire allowed?" The great church, and the Anastasis, and the Cross, and all the other holy places in Jerusalem.

"But to come back to the point," she says. Which is the most Egeria thing she ever wrote. She has just spent a whole paragraph on gold and silk, and suddenly she remembers herself, and gives a little cough, and gets back to the service.

The sermons are preached, the readings are read, the hymns are sung, "all of them," she says, "fitting to the day." That word again. And then they go with hymns to the Anastasis, as usual, and the dismissal comes at about the sixth hour. And at the tenth hour, the lamp-lighting, as on every day.

And here is the thing. The feast did not stop after one day. It went on for eight.

"For three days," Egeria writes, "all this joy is kept in the church that Constantine built." And then, on the fourth day, the feast moved to the church on the Mount of Olives, the Eleona, which is, she says, very beautiful. And everything is decorated there in just the same way. On the fifth day, to the Lazarium, the church at the tomb of Lazarus in Bethany, which is perhaps fifteen hundred paces from Jerusalem, she says. On the sixth day, to Sion. On the seventh day, to the Anastasis. And on the eighth day, to the Cross.

So for eight days, the whole feast, with all its silk and gold and candles, moved round the holy places of the city, one each day, like a family visiting all its relations at Christmas.

And in Bethlehem, meanwhile, she says, for all eight days, the same decorations and the same joy are kept every day by the priests and all the clergy of that place, and by the monks who belong there. Because, she explains, the bishop has to stay in Jerusalem for those days. And on that first night, when the whole crowd walked back to Jerusalem with the bishop, the monks of Bethlehem stayed behind, every one of them, and kept vigil in the church at Bethlehem until daylight, singing hymns and antiphons.

So even when the crowd went home, there was someone singing in Bethlehem all night. And someone singing in the Anastasis. The light was never left on its own.

"And because of the solemnity and joy of that day," Egeria writes, "endless crowds gather in Jerusalem from everywhere. Not only monks, but lay people too, men and women."

From everywhere. From Egypt and Syria and Mesopotamia, from Greece, from Gaul, from Spain. A woman from the end of the world, and a boy who knew three words of Latin, and a slow old monk, and thousands upon thousands of others, all crowded into one small city, for eight days, for the birthday of a child.

* * *

There is one more feast in this part of Egeria's letter. Only one short paragraph. But I have kept it till last, because I think it is the gentlest thing she ever wrote.

Forty days after the birth of a child, the Law of Moses said, his parents were to bring him to the temple, and present him to the Lord, and make an offering. And so, in Jerusalem, forty days after the Epiphany, they kept a feast for the day when Mary and Joseph brought the child Jesus to the temple.

Egeria does not give it a name. She just calls it "the fortieth day after the Epiphany". But it is the oldest description we have, anywhere, of the feast that is now kept on the second of February, forty days after Christmas. In the West it is called the Presentation of the Lord, or Candlemas. In the East it is called the Meeting, because of who the child met in the temple that day.

"The fortieth day after the Epiphany," Egeria writes, "is certainly celebrated here with the very highest honour. For on that day there is a procession to the Anastasis, and everyone goes in procession, and everything is done in order, with the greatest joy, just as at Easter."

Just as at Easter. For a small feast, a feast about an old man and an old woman and a baby, the whole city turned out as though it were the greatest day of the year.

"All the priests preach," she says, "and then the bishop, always about that place in the Gospel where, on the fortieth day, Joseph and Mary brought the Lord to the temple, and Simeon saw him, and Anna the prophetess, the daughter of Phanuel, and about the words they spoke when they saw the Lord, and about the offering itself, which his parents made."

So let me tell you that Gospel, as the priests and the bishop told it that day. I will tell it straight, the way Saint Luke tells it, because it does not need anything added.

When the days of Mary's purification were over, according to the Law of Moses, Joseph and Mary brought the child up to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord. It was written in the Law that every firstborn son should be called holy to the Lord. And they came to make the offering the Law asked for, a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons.

That was the offering of a poor family. A rich family would have brought a lamb. Mary and Joseph brought two small birds.

Now there was a man in Jerusalem called Simeon. He was a good man, and devout, and he was waiting for the consolation of Israel, for God to comfort his people. And the Holy Spirit was upon him. And it had been shown to him by the Holy Spirit that he would not see death until he had seen the Lord's Anointed, the Christ.

And that day, moved by the Spirit, he came into the temple. And when the parents brought in the child Jesus, Simeon took him up in his arms, and blessed God, and said:

"Now, Lord, you let your servant go in peace, as you promised. For my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the sight of all the peoples. A light to bring light to the nations, and the glory of your people Israel."

And the child's father and mother were amazed at what was said about him.

And Simeon blessed them, and said to Mary his mother that this child was set for the falling and the rising of many in Israel, and for a sign that would be spoken against, so that the thoughts of many hearts would be revealed. And, he said to her, a sword will pierce your own soul too.

And there was a prophetess, Anna, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher. She was very old. She had lived with her husband seven years after her marriage, and then she had been a widow, a very long time. Saint Luke says she was eighty-four, though the old Greek can also be read as saying she had been a widow for eighty-four years, which would make her older still. And she never left the temple, but worshipped there with fasting and prayer, night and day.

Night and day.

And coming up at that very hour, she gave thanks to God, and spoke about the child to everyone who was waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem.

* * *

I wonder whether Egeria noticed it. I think she must have.

A light to bring light to the nations, Simeon said. And Anna, who prayed in the temple night and day.

And there, in the Anastasis, a lamp that burned night and day. And monks who sang night and day. And little boys who shouted for mercy every evening at the lighting of the lamps. And a whole city that had decided, as a city, never quite to let the light go out, or the prayer stop, or the singing fall silent, in case the Lord should come into his temple, and find nobody waiting.

Simeon had waited his whole life. Anna had waited for longer than most people live. And they were there, on the right day, at the right hour, in the right place, and the child was put into their arms.

Let us imagine that on the evening of the fortieth day, at the lamp-lighting, Egeria stood in her usual place at the back of the round church. And the light was carried out of the tomb, and passed from candle to candle, until the whole church was full of it. And the deacon began to read the names.

And the little boys answered.

"Kyrie eleison."

Not so loud, tonight. Zenon had walked six miles from Bethlehem and back a few weeks before, and sung himself hoarse at the feast, and he had a cold. He was sniffing. But he was there, at the front, in his place, answering every name, as he did every night.

And Egeria thought of old Simeon, holding the child. And old Anna, who never went home. And all the people who had kept this light going, night after night, year after year, ever since Constantine's builders went away. And she thought of her sisters, a whole world away in the West, at their own evening prayers. And she decided that the very next morning, she would begin to write it all down.

So that your affection may know.

[pause]

We do not know how long Egeria stayed in Jerusalem after that winter. Long enough to keep Lent, and the Great Week, and Easter, and Pentecost, and the great feast of the dedication in the autumn. She wrote about all of them. And then, in the middle of describing that last feast, in the middle of a sentence, the old copy simply stops, and the rest is lost. We do not know whether she ever saw her sisters again.

But her letter got home. Or some of it did. Three hundred years later, a monk in Spain called Valerius read it and loved it, and wrote to his brothers in praise of the blessed Egeria, who had not been afraid to cross the whole world. And about four hundred years after that, a monk in Italy copied what was left of it, missing page and all. And in eighteen eighty-four, a scholar in the town of Arezzo opened that copy, and began to read, and the world found out, for the first time in centuries, how Jerusalem used to pray.

And some of it, you can still hear.

Every evening, in Orthodox churches all over the world, at the lighting of the lamps, the old hymn is sung that greets Christ as the gladsome light. Every Sunday, in a great many churches, East and West, somebody says or sings Kyrie eleison, in Greek, the very words the little boys shouted in the Anastasis, even in churches where every other word is in another language. Every year, on the second of February, people carry candles into church and remember old Simeon with the child in his arms, and sing his song. Now, Lord, you let your servant go in peace. And in a great many monasteries, East and West, every evening as the day ends, the monks and nuns sing that same song of Simeon's, quietly, the way Anna would have sung it in the temple.

And the lamp in the tomb? The building round it has been burnt, and broken, and rebuilt, more than once, in the centuries since Egeria stood there. But there are lamps burning at the tomb in Jerusalem tonight, as I tell you this. There almost always have been.

Night and day.

Slowly, slowly. At the pace of the slowest monk.

[pause]

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the part of Egeria's Travels that describes the daily prayer of the Jerusalem church, chapters twenty-four to twenty-six, in the long Latin letter written by a pilgrim woman to her sisters at home, probably in the early three hundred and eighties, and found in a single incomplete copy in Arezzo in eighteen eighty-four. The doors opened before cockcrow, the monks and virgins, the bishop at dawn, noon and mid-afternoon, everyone coming up to his hand, the lamp-lighting, the light brought out from the lamp that burns night and day inside the cave, the little boys answering Kyrie eleison, the blessings before and behind the Cross, the Sunday crowd waiting for the cock, the incense, the Resurrection read at the door of the tomb and the weeping, the many sermons, the fitting psalms, the missing page, the night walk from Bethlehem at the pace of the monks, the gold and silk, the eight days in eight churches, and the fortieth day kept as at Easter, are all in her letter. Her words are given in our own plain rendering, checked against the Latin and shortened.

That the Jerusalem church kept the Lord's birth on the sixth of January in her time is the view of historians, drawn from her letter and from later Jerusalem service books. That the bishop was Saint Cyril of Jerusalem is a historians' identification from the date, not something she says. That Constantine built with his mother present is what she was told. The Gospel passage is from Luke, chapter two, told straight and shortened.

The widow Theodora, her son Zenon and his three words of Latin, Brother Abraham, the burnt bread, the weather, every word anyone says apart from what Egeria reports, and Egeria's own thoughts, are our own invention.

[pause]

That's the story of Egeria and the lamp that burned night and day, for tonight. Egeria has never been made a saint and has no feast day, but the Presentation of the Lord, the feast she was the first to describe, is kept on the second of February by Catholics and Orthodox alike, and Saint Cyril of Jerusalem, who was very probably her bishop, is remembered on the eighteenth of March. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

And here is Simeon's prayer, which the monks and nuns of East and West still sing at the end of the day. Now, Lord, you let your servant go in peace, as you promised. For my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the sight of all the peoples. A light to bring light to the nations, and the glory of your people Israel.

Goodnight, and God bless.

It is early in the morning, before the heat, and a man of about forty is lying on a blanket outside a tent, with his foot propped up on a rolled-up cloak.

His ankle is swollen and hot. There are two small marks on it, close together, like the marks a pair of pins would make.

His sister is kneeling beside him. She is past fifty now, a grandmother, with grey in her hair, and she has known this man since the day he was born. She carried him on her hip out of Egypt. She gave him a pebble to suck once, at a place called Rephidim, when there was no water.

Now she has one hand on his shoulder, and she is pointing.

Look, she says. Not at your foot. Up there. Look up.

He does not want to. His foot hurts. He is frightened. Everything in him wants to look down, at the place where it hurts, and keep looking at it.

Look up, she says again, more gently. Please.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Let me tell you where we are, and, this time, when.

If you have been with us over the last few nights, you will know Ziva. We met her at the edge of the sea, a girl of fifteen with a tambourine whose skin had torn, dancing behind Miriam anyway. We followed her to the bitter water at Marah, and to the manna in the wilderness of Sin, and to the rock at Horeb, where the water came down the valley in the dark. She had a mother and a father and a little brother of four, and an old neighbour called Naamah who walked with two sticks.

Tonight we are going to jump.

Not a few weeks this time. Not a month or two. We are going to jump almost forty years.

I know. It is a big jump for a bedtime story. But the Book of Numbers jumps too. The people came to the edge of the land of promise in the second year, and sent spies in, and were too frightened to go. And the Lord said that the grown-ups of that generation, everyone who had been counted, from twenty years old and upward, would not enter the land. They would walk in the wilderness until that generation had passed away, and it would be their children who went in.

Their children. Do the sums with me for a moment. Ziva was fifteen at the sea. In the second year, when the spies came back, she was sixteen or so. Under twenty. And her little brother was five. So the two of them, in our story, are among the children who were promised the land.

But their mother and father were not. Nor was old Naamah, who walked with two sticks.

I am not going to make you sad about it. It is a long time ago, even in our story, and they all lived out their lives in the wilderness under the cloud, and ate the manna, and were buried in that country, one by one, the way people are. Naamah went first, very old, with her sticks laid neatly beside her. Ziva's father went some years later. Her mother lived a long time. And Ziva remembers all three of them every evening, when the pillar of cloud turns to fire.

So, forty years.

What happens to a girl in forty years? Well, she grows up. Ziva married a man from the tribe next door, a quiet man who mended sandals, and they had children, and the children had children. So there are little ones running round her tent now who call her grandmother. She still has the tambourine. The hoop of bent wood has been rebound three times, and the skin has been replaced twice, and her granddaughter is allowed to play it on feast days, carefully.

And her little brother, the boy with the pebble, is a grown man of about forty-three, with a beard and a wife and a son of his own, a boy of about twelve. He is a herdsman. He looks after goats. He has a deep slow voice and big hands, and he is quite unable to sit still for more than a few minutes at a time, which, his sister says, has been true since he was four.

Now, where are they?

This is the fortieth year. And it has been a hard year, even for a people who are used to hard years.

Early on, in the first month, which most readers take to be the first month of this same year, they came to a place called Kadesh, in the wilderness of Zin, near the southern edge of the land. And there Miriam died, the sister of Moses, the one who led the women with tambourines at the sea. Ziva was there. She played her tambourine very quietly at the burial, just once, a single shake. Then there was the second time the water ran out, at Meribah, and Moses struck the rock in anger, and the Lord told him he would not lead the people into the land.

Then Moses sent messengers to the king of Edom.

Edom was the country to the east and south, a land of red mountains, and the people of Edom were relatives, in a way. They were descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob. So Moses sent a very polite message. He said, this is your brother Israel speaking. You know all the hardship that has come upon us. Please let us pass through your land. We will not go through your fields or your vineyards, or drink from your wells. We will go along the King's Highway, the main road, and not turn aside to the right or to the left, until we have passed through. And if we drink any of your water, we will pay for it.

It is a beautiful, careful letter. And the king of Edom said no.

You shall not pass through, he said. And he came out with a great many people and a strong army to make sure of it.

So Israel turned away. They did not fight their relatives. They went round.

And then, at Mount Hor, on the border of Edom, Aaron died. We told that story a little while ago. Three men climbed the mountain in the sight of the whole camp, and two came down, and all Israel wept for Aaron thirty days.

So by the time our story starts tonight, the people have lost Miriam, and they have lost Aaron, and they have been told no at the border, and they have buried, over the years, almost every grown-up who came out of Egypt. Ziva, at fifty-four, is one of the older people in the camp now. That is a strange feeling. She keeps looking round for the old people, and finding that the old people are her.

* * *

And then the road.

The book puts it very simply. From Mount Hor they set out by the way to the Red Sea, to go around the land of Edom.

I want you to see what that means, because it is easy to read it and not notice.

The land of promise is to the north. They want to go north. And the road to the Red Sea goes south. Away from it. Down through a long dry valley that runs between the mountains of Edom on one side and the hills of the wilderness on the other, all the way down to the gulf of the sea at the bottom.

People today call that valley the Arabah. It is one of the hottest places in that part of the world. In summer, the middle of the day there is like standing in front of an open oven. The ground is gravel and sand and dry stream beds. There are a few thorny acacia trees, with the thin flat shade that acacia trees give, and not much else.

And Aaron died, the book tells us elsewhere, on the first day of the fifth month. Then thirty days of mourning. So this walk south is happening in high summer.

Imagine it. You are nearly there. After forty years, you are nearly there. You have come right up to the border. And instead of going in, you turn round and walk the other way, south, into the furnace, to go the long way round a country that would not let you through.

If you have ever been in a car on the way to somewhere you very much want to be, and the road was closed, and the man at the barrier waved you round a diversion, and the diversion went on and on, and the signs began to point back the way you came. Well. It is a little like that. Only on foot. In a desert. In August. With goats.

The book has a lovely, exact phrase for how the people felt. Most English Bibles say that the people became impatient on the way. But the old Hebrew words say something more like, the soul of the people grew short on the way.

Their souls grew short. I think that is very well put. Not their tempers. Their souls. Like a candle that has burned down and down until there is only a little stub left, and it gutters at the slightest breath.

In Ziva's part of the camp, at the end of a long day's walk, people are sitting in the strip of shade on the east side of the tents, where the sun has finally gone behind the mountains, and they are complaining.

Her brother is complaining loudest. I am sorry to say it, but he is.

We were at the border, he says. We were at the border. I could see the hills. And now look at us. Walking backwards. Every step today was a step further away.

His son, the boy of twelve, is sitting beside him, trying to look as cross as his father, the way boys do.

And there's nothing to drink, his son says.

There is a little, says Ziva. There is enough for tonight.

A little, says her brother. Always a little. Always just enough. And tomorrow, the same thing. He picks up a handful of something from a bowl beside him and lets it fall back. And this. Forty years of this. I can't look at it any more.

He means the manna.

Do you remember the manna? Small and white and sweet, like wafers made with honey, the book of Exodus said. It came every morning, except the sabbath, for all those years. Ziva's brother has eaten it for breakfast every day since he was four. He has eaten it boiled and baked. He has eaten it ground and made into cakes. His son has never eaten anything else for breakfast in his whole life.

And it has never once failed. Not once in forty years. I told you, on the night of the rock at Horeb, to hold on to that. That whatever else happened, breakfast was arriving every morning on time. It still is.

But that is exactly the problem, in a way, isn't it? When a gift comes every single day, and never fails, it is very easy to stop seeing it as a gift. It just becomes the thing that is there. The way you stop hearing a clock in a room. The way you stop noticing the air.

Ziva looks at her brother, and at the manna falling through his fingers. And she thinks of a little boy at Rephidim with a pebble in his mouth, leaning against their mother with his eyes half shut.

You used to say it was the best thing you had ever tasted, she says.

I was four, he says.

And it was not just Ziva's brother. It was all over the camp. The book tells us what the people said, and they said it out loud, and they said it, this time, against God himself as well as Moses.

Why have you brought us up out of Egypt, they said, to die in the wilderness? For there is no food and no water, and we loathe this worthless food.

Now let me say something about that.

There is no food. That is not true. There was food on the ground every morning. And there is no water. That was hard, it was always hard, but the Lord had given them water at Marah and at Elim and at Horeb and at Kadesh, every time.

And then the last part. We loathe this worthless food. Some translations say, this miserable food. Or, this light bread, this bread with nothing to it.

That is the manna they are talking about. The bread from heaven. The food that Psalm seventy-eight would call the grain of heaven, and the bread of the angels.

I do not want to be hard on them. I really don't. Forty years is a very long time. And grief does strange things to people. They had lost Miriam and Aaron in a single year. They had been turned back at the border. They were hot and frightened and walking the wrong way. When your soul is short, you say things you do not mean, about the very things you most depend on.

But it is worth noticing, all the same. That the worst thing they said, the thing that was most untrue, was about the gift they had been given most faithfully of all.

* * *

And then the serpents came.

I am going to tell this part gently, and quickly, because it is the frightening part, and this is a bedtime story. But I am not going to leave it out, because the book does not leave it out.

The book says, then the Lord sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people, so that many people of Israel died.

There. That is the whole of it, in one sentence, and the book does not say more, and neither shall I.

What were they? Snakes, certainly. The Hebrew word for them is the ordinary word for a snake, with another word beside it, which is usually translated fiery. And here is something curious. That second word is the word seraph. In the plural, seraphim.

You may know that word. It is the name of the burning angels that the prophet Isaiah saw around the throne of God, many centuries later, each with six wings, calling to each other, holy, holy, holy. The same word, burning, fiery, used for angels in one place and for snakes in another.

Why were the snakes called fiery? Nobody is completely sure. Perhaps because of their colour, a bright coppery red. Perhaps because the bite burned like fire. Perhaps both. The Book of Deuteronomy, looking back, calls that wilderness the great and terrible wilderness, with its fiery serpents and scorpions, and thirsty ground where there was no water. Whatever they were exactly, there are still vipers in that country today, small sandy-coloured ones that lie half buried in the sand in the heat of the day and come out in the evening. Nobody who lives there walks about barefoot after dark.

Now, I do want to say something about what this means, and I want to say it carefully.

The book says the Lord sent them. And some people stop there, and are troubled, and think, how could God do such a thing? That is an honest question, and the people who wrote the Bible were not afraid of honest questions.

But here is what I would say. The Book of Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and which thought about this story many centuries afterwards, says that the people were troubled for a little while, as a warning. A little while. And the Book of Deuteronomy says, about the whole forty years, that the Lord led them through the wilderness to humble them and to test them, to know what was in their heart, and to teach them that man does not live by bread alone.

And Saint Paul, in one of his letters, tells this very story to the Christians at Corinth, and says, these things happened to them as a warning, and were written down for our instruction.

So the serpents were not God losing his temper. They were a very hard lesson, in a very hard place, for a people who had forgotten, for a moment, who was keeping them alive. And as we are about to see, the Lord did not leave them in it for one moment longer than it took them to turn round.

* * *

It is evening on the first day of the serpents when Ziva's brother is bitten.

He is out at the edge of the camp, bringing the goats in, where the tents give way to the gravel and the acacia trees. It is the time of day when the heat goes out of the stones, and the things that hide from the sun come out. He does not see it. He feels a sharp sting at his ankle, like a thorn, and looks down, and sees something sandy-coloured slide away under a rock.

He walks back. He is a big strong man and he walks back on his own, with the goats, and puts them in their pen. And only then does he come to his sister's tent, and sit down rather suddenly on the ground, and say, Ziva. I think I've been bitten.

His son is behind him, white in the face.

Ziva does what any of us would do. She makes him lie down. She props up his foot. She washes the place with a little of the precious water and binds it with a clean cloth. She sends her granddaughter running for her brother's wife. And all the time she is talking to him, calmly, the way you talk to a frightened animal, or a small child at night.

And all over the camp, that evening, other people are doing the same thing. Kneeling beside someone they love, in the last of the light.

And it is then, I think, that the people realise what they have done.

* * *

Because here is the next thing the book says. And I think it is one of the most important sentences in the whole story.

The people came to Moses, and said, we have sinned. For we have spoken against the Lord and against you. Pray to the Lord, that he take away the serpents from us.

We have sinned.

Three words. They had said them once before, at Kadesh, after the spies came back, and then gone off to put things right in their own way, without the Lord, and it went very badly. This time is different. This time they say it straight out, without any excuses, and they do not try to mend it themselves.

Not, it was the heat. Not, it was the long road. Not, it was Moses's fault for bringing us here. Just, we have sinned. We spoke against the Lord and against you.

And notice something else. They say it to Moses. The very same Moses they have just been complaining about. They come to the man they were shouting at in the afternoon, and in the evening they say, we were wrong, and please, will you pray for us?

That is hard to do. You will know that if you have ever had to go and say sorry to someone you were cross with, and then, in the very same breath, ask them for a favour.

And Moses did.

That is all the book says. And Moses prayed for the people.

He did not say, well, I told you so. He did not say, you should have thought of that before. He did not make them wait. He did not remind them that he had been praying for them, and putting up with them, and walking at the front of them, for forty years. He just prayed for them.

I find that one short sentence very moving. Moses was an old man now, a hundred and twenty years old, or nearly, by the book's reckoning. His sister had died that year and his brother had died that year. He was tired, I should think, in a way none of us can imagine. And when the people who had spoken against him came and asked him to pray for them, he prayed for them.

That is what a saint does. That is what a prophet does. And it is what a mother or a father does, in the middle of the night, when the child who slammed the door at teatime comes in crying and says, I'm sorry, I'm frightened.

* * *

And the Lord answered.

And what he said is, I think, one of the strangest instructions in the whole of the Bible.

He did not say, I will take the serpents away. Not straight away, anyway. He said this.

Make a fiery serpent, and set it on a pole. And everyone who is bitten, when he sees it, shall live.

Make a serpent. The very thing that was hurting them. Make an image of it, and put it up high, where everyone can see it.

So Moses made a serpent of bronze, the book says, and set it on a pole.

There is a lovely little play on words in the Hebrew here, which I wish we could hear in English. The word for a serpent is nahash. And the word for bronze, or copper, is nehoshet. So Moses made a nahash of nehoshet. A snake of brass, you might almost say, to keep the jingle. Somebody, a long time ago, enjoyed telling it that way, and I think we can enjoy it too.

Where did he get the bronze? The book does not say. People in the camp had brought metal out of Egypt, and the tent of meeting was full of bronze, the great bronze basin and the bronze altar. And here is something interesting. The very next stops on the road, in the list in the Book of Numbers, are places called Zalmonah and Punon. And many scholars think Punon was in the country that is still famous for its ancient copper mines, on the eastern side of that long valley, at the foot of the mountains of Edom. People were digging copper out of those hills and melting it down three thousand years ago, and long before. So the serpent may have been made in copper country. We do not know. But it is a nice thought.

And the pole. The Hebrew word for it is nes. And it does not just mean a stick. It means a standard, a banner, the kind of pole you would put up high in the middle of a camp so that everybody could see it and gather round it.

And here is something lovely, if you have been listening to this show over the last few nights. That is the very same word that Moses used at Rephidim, forty years before, after the battle there. He built an altar, and he called it, the Lord is my banner. The Lord is my nes. My standard. My pole on the hill.

Ziva was there, at Rephidim, the first time he said that word. And here it is again.

And this, the book says, is what happened. If a serpent bit anyone, he would look at the bronze serpent, and live.

* * *

Now let us go back to Ziva and her brother.

It is the next morning, early, before the heat. The manna is on the ground as usual. Somebody has gathered it for them. Her brother is lying outside the tent on a blanket, with his foot on a rolled-up cloak, and his ankle is hot and swollen, and he has not slept, and neither has Ziva.

And word has come round the camp, tent to tent. Moses has prayed. Moses has made something. It is up on a pole, in the middle of the camp, near the tent of meeting. Anyone who has been bitten, look at it, and you will live.

Ziva's brother's son comes running back from the middle of the camp, out of breath.

It's a snake, he says. A big one. Made of bronze. On top of a pole. It's shining in the sun.

A snake, says his father. And he laughs, but it is not a very happy laugh. I've had enough of snakes, he says.

Ziva kneels down beside him.

You have to look at it, she says.

How is looking at a bronze snake going to help? he says. It's a lump of metal. It's not medicine. And he looks down, at his ankle, at the two little marks, the way you do when something hurts.

And Ziva, at that moment, does something that is our own invention, but I think it is the kind of thing a big sister would do.

She takes his face in her hands, the way she used to when he was small, and she turns it, very gently, away from his foot.

Do you remember Rephidim? she says.

He does not answer for a moment.

You had a pebble in your mouth, she says. You were four. You were so thirsty you couldn't speak. And Moses went up to the rock with the elders, and the water came. And afterwards you went up to the ridge to look for your pebble, and I told you, you don't need it any more.

I remember, he says.

You don't need to look at your foot, she says. Your foot isn't going to save you. Neither is the bronze. You know that. But the Lord told Moses to put it up, and he said, look at it and live. So look up.

And she points, over the tents, to the middle of the camp. And there, high up on its pole, catching the early sun, is something bright.

Look up, she says. Please.

* * *

Now, before I tell you what happened, I want to stop and think about that bronze serpent for a minute, because it is very easy to get it wrong. People got it wrong in the Bible itself. And I want us to get it right.

Remember that the people of Israel had been told, at Sinai, very firmly, not to make graven images to bow down to. And here is Moses, of all people, making an image of a snake and putting it on a pole. Was he breaking the commandment? Was the snake a magic charm?

No. And the Bible itself takes great care to say no.

The Book of Wisdom, which I mentioned before, tells this very story, and says it exactly. It says that God gave the people a sign of salvation, to remind them of the command of his law. And then it says this, and I think it is one of the wisest sentences anyone ever wrote about a miracle.

For he who turned toward it was saved, not by what he saw, but by you, the Saviour of all.

Not by what he saw. Not by the bronze. Not by the shape of the snake, or the height of the pole, or the shine of the metal in the sun. By God. The serpent was a sign, pointing beyond itself. Like a signpost on a road. You do not get to the town by hugging the signpost. You get there by going the way it points.

And the Book of Wisdom goes on. It says, neither herb nor poultice cured them, but it was your word, O Lord, which heals all people.

So what was the looking for, if it was not magic?

I think it was turning round. That is what the Book of Wisdom says: he who turned toward it. The people had spent days looking down. Looking at the road going the wrong way. Looking at the manna and despising it. Looking at Moses and blaming him. Looking at their own complaints. And now, to be healed, they had to lift up their heads, and turn, and look at the thing God had set up for them, and trust that he meant what he said.

It was the smallest thing in the world. It cost nothing. Anyone could do it. A child could do it. An old woman could do it from her tent door. You did not have to be strong, or clever, or good. You just had to look where God told you to look.

And yet it was not nothing, because you had to believe enough to do it. Everything in you wanted to look down at the place where it hurt. And you looked up instead.

And as it happens, the Bible does tell us what happened to that bronze serpent, a long time afterwards, when people did get it wrong. If you listened to our episode about King Hezekiah, you may remember. The people of Israel kept the bronze serpent for hundreds of years, and carried it all the way to Jerusalem. And at some point, they started burning incense to it, as if the bronze itself were holy. As if it were the snake that had done the healing. So good King Hezekiah, many hundreds of years after Moses, took it down and broke it into pieces, and called it by a name that means, more or less, a mere bit of bronze.

I like that very much. The same Bible that tells us God told Moses to make it also tells us that a good king was right to break it, once people had started looking at it instead of through it. A sign is wonderful while it points. When it stops pointing, and people start worshipping the signpost, you take it down.

And now, back to the tent.

Ziva's brother looks up.

He has to lift his head off the blanket, and it is an effort, because his whole body is tired and sore. And his son helps him, with an arm under his shoulders. And he looks out over the tops of the tents, over the smoke of the morning fires, to the middle of the camp, where the bronze serpent is shining on its pole.

He looks at it for a long time.

I do not know what he felt. The book does not tell us what anybody felt. It just says that they looked, and lived. So I am not going to tell you that his ankle stopped hurting at once, or that the swelling went down while they watched, because the book does not say how it happened, and I don't want to add a miracle the book doesn't tell. All it says is, they looked, and they lived.

But I can tell you, in our story, what happened next, because it is the sort of thing that happens with brothers.

After a while, he lies back down. And he takes a long breath. And he says, without opening his eyes:

Is there any manna left?

And Ziva laughs, and it comes out as half a laugh and half a sob, and she says, yes. There is always manna. There has been manna every single morning since you were four.

I know, he says. I know. And he takes the little cake his son brings him, and eats it slowly, and says, it's not bad, actually.

It is the best thing you have ever tasted, says Ziva.

He does not argue.

[pause]

The camp did not stay long in that place. The book tells us that soon afterwards the people of Israel set out again, and camped at Oboth, and then on, round the end of the land of Edom, and up the far side, the east side, north again at last. Towards the river Jordan. Towards the land.

And Ziva walked that road with them, with her grandchildren, and her brother walking beside her, a little slowly at first, and then not slowly at all, because, as she says, he has never been able to stand still for more than a few minutes at a time, and why should a snake change that.

And the bronze serpent went with them, wrapped up, I expect, and carried carefully, the way the people carried everything that mattered.

That is the end of the story in the Book of Numbers. But it is not the end of the story.

* * *

Because many hundreds of years later, a very learned man came to see Jesus one night in Jerusalem, in the dark, when nobody else would see him. His name was Nicodemus. He was a teacher of Israel, and he knew the Book of Numbers very well indeed. He would have known all about the serpents, and the pole, and the looking.

And Jesus said to him, in the Gospel of John:

As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.

And then, straight after that, come some of the most famous words in the whole of the New Testament. For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life.

Lifted up. Like the serpent on its pole. And later in the same Gospel, Jesus says it again, and Saint John explains what he meant. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself. He said this, John tells us, to show by what death he was to die.

So Christians, from the very beginning, have looked at the bronze serpent on its pole in the wilderness and seen, in it, a picture of the Cross. Saint Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, a little over a hundred years after the time of Jesus, spent a long time on it, in a book where he argued with a Jewish teacher called Trypho. He pointed out exactly what the Book of Wisdom had pointed out. That God, who had forbidden images to be worshipped, told Moses to make this one, not to be worshipped, but as a sign. A sign pointing ahead to something that had not happened yet.

The people in the wilderness were bitten by the very thing that was killing them, and were healed by looking at a likeness of it, lifted up. And Christians have always seen in that a picture of what happened on Good Friday, when the Son of God took on the likeness of our human nature, and our death, and was lifted up on a tree, so that anyone who looks to him, and believes, might live.

I want to say it carefully, because it is a deep thing. Jesus is not a serpent. The serpent is a sign, and the Cross is the thing it pointed to. And just as the people in the wilderness were not healed by the bronze but by God, so Christians are not saved by a piece of wood, but by the One who hung on it.

But the shape of it is the same. Something lifted up, high, in the middle of the camp. And the only thing you have to do is turn round, and look up.

* * *

So that is why, if you go to Mass in a Catholic church on the fourteenth of September, the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, the first reading you will hear is this very story. The serpents, and the pole, and the looking. And then, a little later, the Gospel, the words of Jesus to Nicodemus. As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness.

And in the Orthodox Church, on the Sunday just before that same feast, the same words from the Gospel of John are read, about the serpent and the Son of Man lifted up. Two great traditions, setting the bronze serpent and the Cross side by side, at the same time of year.

Before I let you go, one small thing that people often wonder about.

You may have seen a snake wound round a staff on the side of an ambulance, or above the door of a chemist's shop. And many people assume that it comes from this story, from Moses and the bronze serpent. In fact, as far as anybody can tell, it does not. It comes from an old Greek story, about a god of medicine with a staff and a single snake. A different story altogether. People mix the two up all the time, and I suppose it is an easy mistake. But I thought you would like to know.

And one more. In the copper country of the south, at a place called Timna, not far from that long hot valley, archaeologists digging in the remains of a small ancient shrine found, among other things, a little copper snake, no longer than your hand, with a gilded head. Some people have wondered whether it has anything to do with tonight's story. Nobody knows. It may have nothing to do with it at all. But it is a strange and lovely thing to think of, a little copper snake lying in the sand for three thousand years, in the very country where the people of Israel walked.

[pause]

So let us leave Ziva where we found her, on the road north at last.

It is evening, in a new camp, and the heat has gone out of the stones. Her brother has brought the goats in, walking perfectly well, and is complaining, mildly, about one of them that will not stay with the others. His son is helping him. Her granddaughter is sitting at the door of the tent with the old tambourine on her knees, tapping it very softly, the way Ziva taught her. Not playing. Just tapping. Keeping time with nothing in particular.

Ziva is sitting beside her, with her hands in her lap.

She has lived a long time in the wilderness. She has seen the sea part and the water come from the rock and the manna fall every morning of her life. She has buried her mother and her father and old Naamah and Miriam the prophetess. She has watched Aaron go up a mountain and not come down. And now she has seen her little brother look up at a bronze serpent in the sun, and live.

And she thinks, as she watches the cloud over the tent of meeting turn slowly from grey to gold to fire, that perhaps that is what the whole forty years has been. Learning, very slowly, to stop looking down.

And over the camp, the pillar of fire is shining, as it has shone every night since Egypt.

And somewhere, very quietly, the dew is beginning to fall. And in the morning, there will be manna.

[pause]

Now, a few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty-one, verses four to nine, told straight, in a plain rendering of the old words. The refusal of the king of Edom is in the chapter before, chapter twenty, and Aaron's death on Mount Hor at the end of that chapter. The stages of the road, Zalmonah and Punon and Oboth, come from the list in chapter thirty-three. Where exactly on that road the serpents came is not known, and the suggestion that Punon was in the old copper country is a suggestion made by scholars, not something the book says. Why the serpents are called fiery is uncertain too; it is the same word as seraphim, which Isaiah uses for the angels he saw.

What the people said, what the Lord said, Moses praying for the people, the bronze serpent on the pole, and those who looked and lived are all in the book. The reading of the serpent as a sign, and not a charm, is from the Book of Wisdom, chapter sixteen. Hezekiah breaking it is in the Second Book of Kings, chapter eighteen. Saint Paul's warning is in his First Letter to the Corinthians, chapter ten. The words of Jesus to Nicodemus are in the Gospel of John, chapter three, and Saint Justin Martyr's reading is in his Dialogue with Trypho. The copper snake from Timna is a real find, but nobody knows whether it has anything to do with this story.

Ziva, her brother and his son, her granddaughter with the tambourine, the bite at the edge of the camp, the conversation outside the tent, and every word they say to one another are our own invention. So is the fate of Ziva's parents and Naamah, though the book does say that almost all of that generation died in the wilderness. We have tried not to add anything to what Moses, the Lord or the people do in the story, and we have not said how the healing happened, because the book does not.

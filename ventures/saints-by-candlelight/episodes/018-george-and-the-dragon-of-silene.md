There is a dragon on the edge of a lake, and it has a problem.

It is not the problem you might expect. It is not that anyone has come to fight it. Nobody has come to fight it for years. The problem is that it is, frankly, a little bored. The lake is cold and the reeds are damp. Every few days a sheep is led down to the water by a worried man in a good coat, and tied to a post, and left there. And the dragon eats the sheep, because that is what dragons do, and the man in the good coat goes home and writes something in a book.

That is the whole arrangement. It has gone on for some time. And it is about to be interrupted by a man on a horse, who has been riding since dawn and would very much like a drink of water.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is of Saint George, a real martyr of the early Church, and of the old legend of the dragon, which was told about him many centuries after he died, and which we will tell as a legend and nothing more, and a few of the small conversations are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let's begin with a question, because it is the kind of question that a good bedtime story answers slowly. Who was Saint George?

You know him, even if you think you do not. He is the knight in shining armour on the white horse. He has a lance, and a red cross on his shield, and a dragon at his feet, looking sorry for itself. He is on flags. He is on coins. He is on a very large number of pub signs. He is the patron saint of England, and also of Georgia, and also of Portugal, and of Catalonia, and of Ethiopia, and of Moscow, and of scouts, and of soldiers, and of farmers, and, for reasons that I think are mostly historical accident, of a great many towns in countries that have never seen a dragon.

That is a lot of people to belong to. So it may surprise you to hear that nearly everything we are sure about Saint George would fit on a single index card. Let me read you the card.

George was a Christian who was put to death for his faith. This happened in the early centuries of the Church, and the tradition says it was in the time of the Roman emperor Diocletian, around the year three hundred and three. Within a generation or two he was loved. A church was built over his tomb in the town of Lydda, in what is now Israel, and pilgrims came to pray there. By the fourth and fifth centuries, Christians from Egypt to Gaul were keeping his feast day. In the East, the Orthodox Church calls him the Great Martyr and the Trophy-Bearer, and he is one of the best-loved saints it has. In the West he was kept just as warmly.

That is the card. That is all the solid ground there is. Everything else, the soldier's career, the long list of tortures in the old Greek Acts, the dragon, comes from stories written later, some of them much later.

And the Church has always been honest about this. About the year four hundred and ninety-four, a document attributed to Pope Gelasius listed certain writings the Church did not accept as reliable, and among them was an account of the sufferings of Saint George, with a note, in effect, that his name was rightly honoured among people, but that his deeds were known to God. I find that rather beautiful. It means that fifteen hundred years ago, somebody looked at the pile of wonderful stories and said: we do not have to believe every word of these, and we do not have to stop loving him either.

So tonight we are going to do both. We are going to tell you the dragon story, the way the old book tells it. And we are going to tell you, along the way, which part is the story and which part is the man. Agreed? Good. Settle in.

[pause]

* * *

The oldest versions of the dragon story do not belong to the early Church at all. As far as anyone can tell, it grew up in the Middle Ages, in the East first and then in the West, and it reached the form we know best in a book called the Golden Legend. This was written by a friar, an Italian Dominican named Jacobus de Voragine, who became an archbishop, and who in the thirteenth century collected the lives of the saints for the whole year into one enormous volume. It was one of the most popular books in Europe for about three hundred years, which is a good run for anything, and it put Saint George on the map of the imagination.

Jacobus tells it like this. Near a city called Silene, in the province of Libya, there was a lake as big as a sea. The city is not on any map we can check, and the historians have wondered a long time where it was meant to be, and I think the honest answer is: it is in the story, and that is where we will leave it.

Now, in the lake lived a dragon. The book does not make a great deal of its appearance, and neither shall we. It is enough to say that it was large, and that it lived in the water, and that the people of Silene did not like it. They had tried, in the early days, to deal with it by force. The soldiers had gone down to the lake. The soldiers had come back, fewer, and had said that perhaps there was another approach.

So the people tried something else. They gave it sheep.

You will have noticed, if you have ever tried it, that giving a large creature something to eat does not usually make it go away. It makes it come back. And at first the arrangement worked well enough. The dragon was fed. The town was left alone. The dragon, we may suppose, felt that this was a very reasonable settlement, and had no complaints beyond the damp.

I would like you to meet the town clerk.

[pause]

His name was Philo, and he is our invention, but I am quite sure that somebody exactly like him existed in Silene, because somebody exactly like him exists in every town. Philo was a small man with a large ledger. He had ink on his fingers and a pen behind each ear, in case the first one failed. And it was Philo's job, from the day the arrangement began, to keep the book of sheep.

It was a good book. Every sheep was entered. The date was entered. The weight, roughly, was entered. The owner was entered, and the compensation owed, and the town committee that had approved the compensation, and a note on the committee's minutes in case anyone ever asked about it. Philo loved his ledger the way some men love a garden. He ruled the columns with a straight edge. He used a different colour of ink for the totals. And as the weeks went by, he took a quiet pride in the fact that, whatever else might be going wrong in Silene, the sheep were accounted for.

Which is why Philo was the first person in the city to notice the trouble.

He noticed it on a grey morning in the market, while adding up a column for the third time, because the total would not come out right. It was not that the sums were wrong. It was that they were right. The number at the bottom of the page was simply a very large number, and it was larger this month than last month, and larger last month than the month before. And Philo, who understood sheep better than anyone alive, sat down on an upturned bucket and thought: we are going to run out.

He took the matter to the king. The king, who was a kind man and a tired one, said that he had been thinking much the same thing. The flocks of the whole district were thinning. The shepherds had begun to look at their own lambs the way a man looks at a rising river. And so, one evening, the king called the people together in the square and said, as gently as he could, that the town would have to try something different.

I am sorry to tell you that the something different was worse. The old book says that the people decided to draw lots. Every day, they would draw the name of a person, and that person would be the dragon's meal in place of the sheep. I want to tell you that I find this plan appalling, and that the story does too, and that it is the reason the story exists. A town that begins by giving its sheep to the lake will, if nothing stops it, give its children. This is a thing that has been true in a good many places and times, and it is the reason the old storytellers hated dragons so.

So let us not stay in that square any longer than we must. The lots were drawn. For some time, the old book says, it was done as the king had decreed, and the people of Silene wept and said very little. And then came a day when the lot fell on the king's own daughter.

[pause]

* * *

I will tell you what the old book says about the king, because it is the most human part of the tale.

He did not hide behind his crown. He offered all that he had. He offered the gold in the treasury and half the kingdom besides, if the people would let his daughter go and take something else instead. And the people, who loved their king and also loved their own children, said, with great sorrow, that they could not. They said: you made the law. You have seen us give up our own. We cannot break it for you and keep it for ourselves.

And the king, I think, knew they were right. That is what makes it such a hard moment. Nobody in Silene was wicked. Everyone in Silene was frightened and decent and had done the best they could with a bad idea, and the bad idea had finally come to the palace door.

The king asked for eight days. The people gave him eight days. And on the eighth, with the whole town watching and weeping, the princess was dressed in royal clothes, and walked down to the lake.

Now, the old book is gentle at this point, and so shall we be. She was not alone for long.

Because at that same hour, along the road from the north, there was a man on a horse. And he had stopped, as I said, because he was thirsty.

[pause]

Let us stop for a moment and look at him.

The legend says his name was George, a soldier from Cappadocia, which is a region in what is now central Turkey, a land of rock and wind and hillsides carved into churches. The Church tells us that this is where George's family came from, and it is as good a start as any. He is a tribune in some tellings, a soldier of rank, which means a sort of senior officer. He rides a horse. He has no great army with him. He has a spear, a shield, and, according to the Golden Legend, the sign of the cross, which he makes before he does anything of consequence.

And he looks up from the dusty road and he sees something odd. A young woman in fine clothes is walking toward a lake, and behind her, at a distance, are crowds of people on the city walls, and none of them is cheering.

George is not a stupid man. He gets down from his horse. He walks to the girl. He says, in effect: excuse me, but what is going on?

And the girl says, and this is the part I like best, that he should go away. She says: good young man, you must not stay here. You will die with me. Ride on.

It is a very brave thing to say. She is about to be eaten by a dragon, and what she is concerned about is the stranger's safety. And she says it kindly, and she means it. And George, who has heard many things in his life, says: Fear not.

[pause]

Now we come to the dragon.

It comes out of the lake, as dragons do. The old book says it lifted its head from the water, and the girl, who had been very brave until then, trembled. But George did what he always did. He made the sign of the cross, he commended himself to God, and he rode toward it. I should tell you that Saint George, in this story, does not talk very much. He is not a wisecracker. He is not a wizard, and he certainly does not wave a magic sword. He goes forward in faith, he strikes with his spear, and the dragon is wounded and falls to the ground.

Remember, please, rule number two of this show, which is that miracles are God's. The old book is quite clear about whose victory it is. George does not claim it. He says a prayer, and then he does something very unusual for a dragon-slayer. He turns to the princess and says: take your girdle, your belt, and throw it around the dragon's neck. Do not be afraid.

She does. And the dragon, which a moment ago was the terror of a kingdom, follows her back to the city on the end of her belt, as meek as a little dog on a lead.

I would like to say that I don't know exactly what that is supposed to mean. I think what it means is this. The old storytellers wanted to say that what had held the town in fear was something that, once faced with faith and courage and a clear heart, was not so strong as it seemed. A monster that has been fed for years is only big because everybody keeps feeding it. And a girl with a belt can lead it home.

[pause]

* * *

Now picture it from the town's point of view.

Philo the clerk is on the wall. He has been there all day, with his ledger, because he did not know what else to do with himself, and because there was a ledger entry he did not want to write. He has his pen out. He has the page open. And he sees, coming up the road from the lake, a princess, a soldier on a horse, and a dragon on a belt.

It is the only time in his career that Philo is unable to think of a column to put it in.

He stands there with his mouth open. All around him people are shouting, and running, and hiding behind each other. The cry goes up that the dragon is coming. And George, from the road, raises a hand and calls out, in a voice that carries across the whole square: do not be afraid. God has sent me to deliver you from this plague. If you will believe in Christ, and be baptised, I will kill the dragon.

The old book says the king and all his people were baptised that day, more than twenty thousand, not counting women and children. I should say that the number is probably a storyteller's flourish, and so is a great deal in the next part. I would not put it in the ledger. But I would say that what the old book wants us to see is the real heart of the tale, which is that the people of Silene came in from their fear into something larger. They had spent years being afraid together. Now they were glad together, and it turned out to be a very different thing.

And then George drew his sword and killed the dragon, and it was carried out of the city on four ox-carts, and that was the end of it.

I will not describe it further. It is not that kind of story. The dragon was a danger to the town, and the danger is over. Let that be enough.

[pause]

Now the king, naturally, is overjoyed. He wants to give George everything. He opens the treasury. He offers gold, and jewels, and a house, and a title, and, in some versions, half of the kingdom, which in a story is the usual price of a favour and in real life is the usual start of a lawsuit.

George says no.

He does not want any of it. He asks the king only to do three things: to take care of God's churches, to honour the priests, and to remember the poor. And he takes what the king has offered and gives it away on the spot to the people who need it, and then he mounts his horse, and he kisses the king's hand, and he rides out of the city, and that is the last they see of him.

I think that is the loveliest thing in the whole story. It is not the dragon, I promise you. It is the horse riding quietly away down the road with nothing to its name.

[pause]

* * *

I have had a thought, and I am going to share it, because it is bedtime, and bedtime is for thoughts.

When we hear stories about dragons, we tend to think the dragon is the point. But in this story, I wonder if the dragon is the least important creature in the whole city. It is the clerk, and the king, and the people, who are the interesting ones. They are the ones who made a bad choice for good reasons and then could not see how to get out of it. And the story says that the thing which finally got them out was not a clever scheme from within. It was a stranger who turned up, did not ask for anything, and showed them that the fear was smaller than they had been told.

Philo, I like to think, understood this. In the days after George rode away, the book says the whole city was changed. They built a church. They built it, in fact, in honour of the Blessed Virgin and Saint George, and at its altar, the old book says, a spring of water flowed that healed the sick. I will leave that where the book left it. But I will tell you that I have a little picture in my mind of Philo, in that first week, taking down his great ledger of sheep and turning to the last page. And under the final total, in the red ink he used only for important things, he wrote a single new line.

It said: no further entries.

And he closed the book, and he put the pens back in their box, and he went out and sat in the sun for the first time in years.

[pause]

* * *

So that is the dragon. Now let us go back to the man.

The Church remembers George, as I said, as a martyr, and here the history gets thin but it does not go away. In the early fourth century, the emperor Diocletian, who had ruled the Roman world with considerable skill and considerable cruelty, decided that the Christians were a danger to the empire. In the year three hundred and three he issued the first of a series of edicts. Churches were to be pulled down. Holy books were to be burned. Christians who held office were to lose their positions. In time, those who would not give up their faith were to be put to death.

This was the worst persecution the Church had known. It lasted for years, in different forms, in different places. And somewhere inside it, according to the tradition, a Roman soldier named George was asked to deny what he believed.

The old Greek accounts say he was a young officer of good family, who had risen quickly in the army. They say that when the persecution began he gave away what he owned, stood up before the court, and declared himself a Christian. After that come the long lists of sufferings, wheels and swords and fires, and in the oldest versions the saint is brought back to life three times, in a way that even the old copyists seem to have found a little much. I will not tell you those parts. They are not true to the best of what we know, and they are not for bedtime.

What I will tell you is the simple thing the tradition keeps. George would not give up Christ. He was a soldier, and he knew about obedience, and he knew that there was one thing he could not obey. And so, the tradition says, in the city of Nicomedia, he was put to death, and went home to God.

His friends, we are told, took his body to Lydda, where his family had land, and buried it there. There was a church over the grave before long. And that, I think, is the oldest and best thing to say about him: the people who loved him did not forget where he was.

[pause]

* * *

Let me tell you how the soldier and the dragon got joined together.

Nobody knows quite how it happened. It seems to have been the work of storytellers in the East, who told of Saint George as the great protector of the weak, and who in time found the picture they needed. There was an old pattern in the ancient world of a hero rescuing a king's daughter from a sea monster. The Greeks told it of Perseus, and then a good many other peoples told it in their own way. When the Christian storytellers took up the pattern, they changed it in one important respect. In the old myths, the hero won by strength and cleverness. In the Christian tale, he won by faith, and then gave the credit to God and the treasure to the poor.

And then the tale went west. The knights of the Crusades heard it. The kings of England took the saint as their patron, and in the fourteenth century King Edward the Third put him at the head of a new order of knighthood, the Order of the Garter, which still exists. English soldiers marched under the red cross. English children were told the dragon story in their cradles. And all the while, in the East, the Orthodox faithful were painting icons of the Great Martyr on horseback, with the dragon at his feet, and a small princess holding a small belt at one side.

I find one thing particularly good about this. The dragon in the icon is not really a dragon at all. In the Eastern tradition it is understood to stand for the old enemy, the evil that every Christian has to face. The princess, in some readings, stands for the Church, or for the soul. And the saint is there to say: you do not have to be afraid. This is not a story about killing animals. It is a story about the courage to meet what we are most afraid of, with God on our side, and to find it smaller than we thought.

I do not think that is a bad thing to take to bed.

[pause]

* * *

It is a very quiet evening now.

The knight has ridden away. The dragon is gone. The sheep are back in the field, where there are many more of them than there used to be, and they are standing about in the way that sheep do, thinking about nothing. The lake is still. A little mist is coming up off the water, and the reeds are all leaning one way in the evening wind.

In the city, the lamps are being lit. In a small house near the market, a man with ink on his fingers has taken off his shoes. In the palace, a king is sitting beside his daughter, and neither of them is saying very much, because there is not very much that needs to be said. They are alive. They are together. That is enough for one night.

And the road beyond the city is empty, and runs on, as roads do, into the dark.

George is a long way off now. He is riding to another town, I expect, where somebody else is in trouble. Or he is riding to the city where his own long road will end, in the quiet way the tradition tells it. We do not know, and we do not have to know. The soldier has a good horse, and a clear conscience, and nothing at all in his saddlebags, and he is not afraid of the dark.

You do not have to be afraid of it either.

The Church keeps his feast on the twenty-third of April, in the West and in the East, though the Orthodox calendar counts the day a little differently. On that day, Christians in a hundred countries remember a soldier who stood up for what was right when it was dangerous, and a legend about a lake, and a town that learned to stop feeding its fear.

[pause]

* * *

Now, a few notes for the curious, before we say goodnight.

What is history, and what is legend? The history is the plain one: George was a real Christian martyr, honoured very early, buried at Lydda, and loved by the whole Church. The tradition says he was a soldier who died under Diocletian around the year three hundred and three. The details of his life are late and mixed up, and the Church itself has said so since the days of Pope Gelasius.

The dragon is legend. It is a medieval story, which reached its most famous form in the Golden Legend in the thirteenth century, long after the saint's death. We have told it as the old book tells it, and we have said so, and we have left out the parts that are not for bedtime. The princess's name is not given in the earliest telling, and we have not given it either.

Philo the clerk, his ledger, his pens, and his line of red ink are our own invention. So are the shepherds' worries and the conversations. They are told in the spirit of the old tale, which was always about a town learning not to be afraid, and I hope they do it no harm.

If you would like to read more, look for the Golden Legend itself, in a good modern translation, and for the study of the warrior saints by Christopher Walter. The sources are listed on the page for this episode. And if you ever go to a church with a stained-glass window of a man on a horse, have a look at the dragon. It always looks a bit surprised.

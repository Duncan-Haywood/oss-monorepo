It is the middle of a warm afternoon, in a valley full of vines, and a woman in a dusty travelling cloak is following an old priest along a path between fruit trees.

The priest is walking quite fast for an old man. He has done this walk before. The woman is walking quite fast for a woman who has been on a donkey since dawn. She has done a lot of walks before, too, all over the eastern half of the Roman Empire, and she is not going to be outpaced by anybody.

"How far?" she asks.

"Two hundred paces," says the priest. "We are nearly there. Listen."

And she listens. And under the hum of the bees and the creak of the branches, she can hear it. Water. A great deal of water, running clear and fast over stones, somewhere just ahead, in the green.

"The people here have a name for this orchard," says the priest. "In Greek they call it cepos tu agiu Iohanni."

"Which means?"

"The garden of Saint John."

If you have been with us the last two nights, you know Egeria already.

If you haven't, here is everything you need. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, very probably from the north-west corner of Spain, though we cannot be certain, because the beginning of her letter is lost. Some time around the year three hundred and eighty, she travelled east to see the places in the Bible with her own eyes. She lived in Jerusalem for about three years, and from there she went out on journey after journey. And she wrote all of it down, in a long, chatty, wonderfully polite letter to a group of women back home, whom she calls her sisters, and her ladies, and once or twice, rather grandly, her light.

Only part of the letter survives. It turned up in eighteen eighty-four, in a single old copy in a library in the Italian town of Arezzo, with pages missing from the front and the back and here and there in the middle. But what is left is a treasure. And all the way through it there is Egeria: curious, tireless, or at least refusing to admit she is tired, and forever asking the locals one more question.

We have followed her up Mount Sinai, straight up, like climbing a wall. We have followed her up Mount Nebo, to stand at the door of a small church and look at the land Moses saw and never entered. And tonight, she is back in Jerusalem, and she has been talking to monks.

That is how most of her ideas began. Jerusalem in those years was full of monks. They came from the deserts of Egypt, from the hills of Syria, from caves and cells and little communities scattered over the whole of the east, to pray at the holy places. And they talked, as travellers do, about where they had come from.

And some of the monks Egeria met came from a country she had read about, but never thought of visiting. A country far to the north and east, beyond the Jordan, on the edge of the desert. The Bible calls it the land of Uz. And it is where, the monks said, a man named Job was buried.

This is how Egeria puts it. "Some time later," she writes, "I wanted to go to the country of Uz, to visit the tomb of holy Job, to pray there. For I saw many holy monks coming from there to Jerusalem, and as they told us about those places, my wish to go grew stronger." And then she adds something that I think tells you everything about her. She says she wanted to take on the toil of going there, "if indeed," she writes, "one can call it a toil, when a person sees their wish fulfilled."

Which is a lovely thing to say. And also, if you have ever ridden a donkey for eight days through the Jordan valley, a very generous one.

Now, who was Job?

You may know the story, or you may know the phrase. People still talk about the patience of Job, though if you read the book, you find that Job was actually not very patient at all, and that this is rather the point.

The Book of Job begins like a story told by a fire. There was once a man in the land of Uz, it says, whose name was Job. He was good, and he was upright, and he feared God and turned away from evil. And he was very rich. He had seven sons and three daughters, seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred yoke of oxen, five hundred donkeys, and a great many servants, and he was the greatest man of all the people of the east.

And then, in the space of a single day, he lost all of it. The oxen and the donkeys were stolen. The sheep were struck by fire from the sky. The camels were carried off. And his sons and daughters, who were having a feast together in the eldest brother's house, were killed when a great wind struck the house and it fell.

And Job tore his robe, and shaved his head, and fell on the ground, and worshipped. And he said some of the most famous words in the whole of the Bible. "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I shall return. The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord."

After that, things got worse. He was struck with sores from head to foot, and he went and sat among the ashes outside the town, scraping himself with a piece of broken pottery.

And three of his friends heard about it, and came from their own countries to comfort him. And when they saw him from far off, they did not recognise him. And they wept, and tore their robes, and sat down on the ground with him, for seven days and seven nights. And nobody said a word, because they saw how great his suffering was.

That, I think, was the best thing they did in the whole book. Because after the seven days, they started talking. And they talked, and talked, and talked, for about thirty-five chapters. And most of what they said came down to this: you must have done something to deserve it.

And Job, who had not done anything to deserve it, argued back. He did not curse God. But he did not sit quietly either. He complained. He wept. He said he wished he had never been born. He demanded that God come and explain himself. And in the middle of all that, in the darkest part of the book, he said something astonishing. "I know that my Redeemer lives," he said, "and that at the last he will stand upon the earth. And after my skin has been destroyed, yet in my flesh I shall see God."

Hold on to that line. We'll come back to it.

So that was Job. And somewhere in the land of Uz, the monks told Egeria, there was a tomb, and a church being built over it. And Egeria, being Egeria, decided she had to go.

So she packed. Again.

* * *

The holy men who went with her were a mixture of priests and monks who knew the road and knew the Scriptures. That is how she always travelled. And she tells us the distance. From Jerusalem to the city of Job, she writes, was eight staging posts, which means eight days of travelling, with a stop each night at an inn or a monastery or a village along the road.

Eight days is a long way to go to pray at a tomb. But Egeria never thought anything was a long way, if there was a place in the Bible at the end of it.

And there were donkeys, of course. There were always donkeys. And since our donkey-boy Malchus from last time was entirely made up, we may as well make him up again.

You may remember Malchus. He is about twelve, from a village near Jericho, and he names his donkeys the opposite of what they are. There is a grey one called Patience, because she has none, and a big brown one called Thunder, because he is very quiet.

He turned up at the door of the house where Egeria was staying the evening before she left, with both donkeys, and another boy, a bit older and a bit taller, standing behind him.

"This is my cousin," said Malchus. "His name is Zacchaeus."

Egeria looked at Zacchaeus, who was very tall indeed. "Zacchaeus," she said, "like the little man in the Gospel who climbed the tree?"

"Yes," said Malchus. "Because he doesn't need to."

"Your family has a system."

"It's my aunt's system," said Malchus. "She's very funny. Nobody notices."

Zacchaeus said nothing at all. It turned out over the next eight days that Zacchaeus hardly ever said anything at all, which suited Malchus very well, because Malchus said enough for three.

"Do you know where we are going?" Egeria asked.

"To see a dead man," said Malchus. "Uncle says it's a very long way."

"It's a very long way," Egeria agreed. "We are going to visit the tomb of holy Job."

Malchus thought about this. "Was he the one with the whale?"

"No, that was Jonah."

"Was he the one with the lions?"

"That was Daniel."

"Was he the one who lost all his camels?"

"Yes," said Egeria. "That's him."

Malchus looked at his two donkeys with deep sympathy. "Poor man," he said.

* * *

The road went down out of the hills to the Jordan valley, past Jericho, green with its palm trees and its springs. And then, instead of crossing the river, as they had for Mount Nebo, they turned north and followed the valley upstream.

Now, the Jordan valley is a strange and wonderful place. It is a long, deep crack in the earth, lower than the level of the sea, with brown hills rising steeply on either side. Down at the southern end, near the Dead Sea, it is hot and dry and almost bare. But as you go north, it changes. The hills draw back a little. Streams come down from both sides. There is more green, and then a great deal more green. Palm trees. Fields. Little villages with water running beside the road in stone channels.

Egeria noticed it. She notices everything. A few days up the road, she writes, they came to a valley that was extremely beautiful, full of vines and trees, with a great deal of very good water. And in the middle of it, there was a village. The people called it Sedima.

And in the middle of the village, there was a hill.

Not a big hill. A hillock, really. A round, steep little mound, the kind of shape that looks almost made rather than grown. And on the very top of it, there was a church.

Egeria, of course, wanted to know about the hill.

* * *

They were met by the priest of the village, an older man, a clergyman of the kind Egeria loved best, the kind who knew every stone of his own place and the Scripture that went with it.

"What is this place?" she asked him.

And he told her. This, he said, was the city of King Melchizedek. In the old days it was called Salem, and now, with the years and the way people speak, the name had worn down into Sedima. And the church on top of the hill, he said, the people called in Greek "opu Melchisedech," which means "where Melchizedek." Because this, they said, was where Melchizedek offered to God his pure sacrifice of bread and wine, as it is written in Scripture.

And the stones she could see all round the foot of the hill, the old foundations poking up through the grass, those, he said, were the foundations of Melchizedek's palace.

Malchus, standing at the back with the donkeys, leaned over to Zacchaeus. "Who's Melchizedek?" he whispered.

Zacchaeus shrugged. It was his longest speech of the day.

So Egeria, who had been taught as a girl to read the Bible in the very place where things happened, asked for the passage to be read. And it was. And because you might be wondering too, here is the story she heard.

* * *

It comes from the fourteenth chapter of Genesis, and it is one of the strangest little stories in the whole of the Bible. It comes out of nowhere, and it goes back into nowhere, and in between, it is gone in a few lines.

Abraham was still called Abram then. He was living as a stranger among the oaks of Mamre, with his tents and his flocks. And his nephew Lot was living down in the plain, in the city of Sodom.

And four kings from the east came marching down, and fought five kings of the plain, and beat them. And they carried off everything from Sodom, all the goods and all the food, and they carried off Lot too, and everything he had.

And somebody escaped, and ran to tell Abram. And Abram, who was not a king, and not a soldier, but a herdsman with a large household, gathered three hundred and eighteen men who had been born in his own house, and went after the four kings. He chased them all the way north, past Dan, and fell on them by night, and beat them. And he brought back all the goods, and his nephew Lot, and the women, and the people.

And as he was coming home, the king of Sodom came out to meet him. But before the king of Sodom could say a word, somebody else came out to meet him too.

"And Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine," says the Book of Genesis. "He was priest of God Most High. And he blessed him, and said, Blessed be Abram by God Most High, maker of heaven and earth. And blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand." And Abram gave him a tenth of everything.

And that is all. Melchizedek is never mentioned again in Genesis. We are not told who his father was, or his mother. We are not told where he came from, or what happened to him. A king of peace, from a city called Salem, which sounds like the Hebrew for peace, comes out of the hills with bread and wine, blesses the father of the faithful, and goes back into the silence.

But the Bible never quite forgot him. Hundreds of years later, a psalm said of the king God had chosen, "You are a priest for ever, according to the order of Melchizedek." And hundreds of years after that, the Letter to the Hebrews took up that very line and said it was about Jesus. A priest without a list of ancestors in the book, it says, without beginning of days or end of life written down, made like the Son of God, who remains a priest for ever.

And so, for Egeria, standing on a little hill in a green valley, this was not just an odd old story. Every time she went to Mass, the bread and wine were brought to the altar. And in the great prayer of the Roman Mass, which Saint Ambrose was already quoting in Milan within a few years of her journey, and which is still prayed today, the priest asks God to accept the offering, as he once accepted the gifts of the righteous Abel, and the sacrifice of Abraham, and the holy sacrifice of his high priest Melchizedek.

Bread and wine. On a hill. In a valley full of vines.

Egeria stood there for a long time, I imagine. And they prayed. And then, because it was what she always did, they read the reading, and sang a psalm suited to the place, and the priest gave a blessing, and they came down.

Now, I should say something honest here, because Egeria was always honest, and we try to be too.

The priest of Sedima was quite sure his village was Salem. But not everybody, then or now, agrees. A great many readers, Jewish and Christian, ancient and modern, have thought that Salem was another name for Jerusalem. There is a psalm that seems to say so: "His tent is in Salem," it says, "his dwelling place in Zion." Other people, like the priest of Sedima, pointed to a place in the Jordan valley. Scholars still argue about it, and they will probably go on arguing until the end of the world.

We don't have to settle it tonight. Egeria didn't either. She wrote down what she was told, by a kind old man who loved his village, and she prayed where he showed her. And I think there are worse ways to visit a holy place.

* * *

But Egeria had been thinking. She had been thinking all the way up the hill and all the way down again. Because she knew her Gospels. And she remembered something.

In the Gospel of John, in the third chapter, there is a short line about John the Baptist. "John also was baptizing at Aenon near Salim," it says, "because there was much water there. And people came and were baptized."

Aenon near Salim. Salim, Salem. Sedima.

So she turned to the priest, and she asked him, with that politeness of hers that was really a way of not letting anyone off the hook, where that place was. Where was Aenon? Where did John baptize?

And the old priest smiled, I'm quite sure, because people must have asked him this a hundred times. And he said, more or less, "It is right here. It is two hundred paces away. If you like, I will take you there now, on foot."

And then he told her something else. All that water, he said, all that fine clear water she had seen running through the village, in the channels beside the road, watering the vines, all of it came from that one spring.

So off they went. And this is where our story began.

* * *

The path ran out of the village, between the vines, and into an orchard. Egeria says it was a fruit garden, very pleasant, and I think she means she was enchanted with it, because she does not often use that sort of word. It was full of trees. And in the middle of the orchard, there was a spring.

Not a trickle. A spring that came out of the earth all at once, in one great rush, very clear and very pure, and ran away down a channel between the trees. And in front of it there was a pool, where the water gathered, deep enough to stand in.

"Here," said the priest. "This is the place where John baptized."

Malchus, who had been running ahead and back again, like a dog on a walk, the whole two hundred paces, came to a halt at the edge of the pool and stared.

"There's so much water," he said.

"Yes," said Egeria.

"Where I live, if there were this much water, people would fight about it."

"Where you live," said the priest mildly, "people do fight about it. I have been to Jericho."

Malchus could not argue with that, so he took his sandals off and put his feet in. And then he took them out again very quickly.

"It's cold," he said, with deep betrayal.

"It comes from under the mountains," said the priest. "It is always cold. In summer, that is the best thing about it."

And this was the garden. The people of the village, the priest said, called it in Greek cepos tu agiu Iohanni, the garden of Saint John. And a great many monks came here from all over, he said, from far-off places, just to wash in this water.

Then he told her the thing I like best in this whole chapter. Every year, at Easter, he said, every person in the village who was to be baptized, every one of them, was baptized here, in this spring. They came out to this garden, and they were baptized in the cold, clear water, the same water John had baptized in, as the village believed. And then, all together, the newly baptized with the clergy and the monks walked back through the orchard and the vines to the church in the village, carrying candles in the dark, and singing psalms and antiphons as they went.

Think of it. A line of little flames moving through the trees, at Easter, and the sound of singing, and the smell of wet leaves, and the water still running in the dark behind them.

Egeria wrote it all down for her sisters. I think she wanted them to see it as clearly as she did.

* * *

Now, while they stood by the pool, someone read the passage from the Gospel, the way Egeria liked it done. And because John is one of the great figures of the night sky of the Bible, and because this is his garden, let us stay with him for a moment.

John was the son of Zechariah, an old priest, and of Elizabeth, his wife, who was a cousin of Mary, the mother of Jesus. He went out into the wilderness when he was young, and lived there. He wore a coat of camel hair, with a leather belt, and he ate locusts and wild honey, which is the kind of diet that sounds terrible until you have tried the honey.

And he preached. Turn back to God, he said. Get ready. Someone is coming. And people came out to him from everywhere, from Jerusalem and from all Judea and from the whole country round the Jordan, and he baptized them in the river, washing them as a sign that they were turning back to God.

And one day, Jesus came to be baptized too. And John knew who he was, and said, "I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?" But Jesus said, Let it be so, for now. And when Jesus came up out of the water, the heavens opened, and the Spirit came down like a dove, and a voice said, This is my beloved Son.

That was at the Jordan, a long way to the south. But the chapter of John that tells of Aenon, near Salim, tells us something from later on. By then Jesus had disciples of his own, and they were baptizing too. And John's disciples came to him, and they were a little put out. Teacher, they said, that man who was with you on the other side of the Jordan, the one you told everybody about, he is baptizing now. And everyone is going to him.

And John was not put out at all. He said this:

"No one can receive anything unless it is given from heaven. I am not the Christ. I have been sent ahead of him. The bridegroom is the one who has the bride. But the friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, rejoices greatly at the bridegroom's voice. So this joy of mine is now complete. He must increase. But I must decrease."

He must increase. I must decrease.

If you were going to choose one sentence to say at a spring that has been pouring out water for thousands of years, and asks for nothing back, you could do worse than that one.

* * *

Before they left the garden, the old priest did something kind.

He picked fruit from the trees, and he gave it to Egeria, and to the monks who were travelling with her, as a blessing. Egeria calls it by a Greek word, eulogia, which means a blessing, and which people used for any gift given in God's name: a loaf of bread, a bit of oil, some fruit from a holy garden. You were supposed to eat it gratefully, or take it home to share. Egeria was given these little gifts all over the east, from monks and bishops and hermits, and she always mentions them, and you can tell she was always pleased.

The letter does not say what kind of fruit it was, which I think is a shame. So I will not tell you either. But I will tell you that Malchus got some too, because I am in charge of Malchus, and I say he did.

"Can we come back at Easter?" he asked, through a mouthful.

"I don't think so," said Egeria. "It's eight days' journey from Jerusalem, and I need to be in Jerusalem at Easter."

"Why?"

"Because of everything that happens there. The palms, and the night watches, and the Cross. I want to see all of it. I want to write all of it down for my sisters."

Malchus considered. "You write everything down for your sisters."

"Yes."

"Do they write back?"

Egeria was quiet for a moment. "Sometimes," she said. "It is a long way to Spain. Letters take a long time." And then, more cheerfully, "But when they do, they always ask for more."

That part, at least, I am almost sure is true. You do not write a letter that long for people who don't want to read it.

* * *

They left the green valley of Sedima, with its vines and its hill and its spring, and went on.

The road kept to the Jordan valley for a while. And Egeria, who saw a story in every bend of the road, saw two more.

First, they came to a town. And they told her its name was Tishbe. And from this town, she says, came the prophet Elijah, whom the Bible calls Elijah the Tishbite. There was a cave there, where the holy man sat, they said. Egeria does not tell us much more about it, and I won't invent any.

But you may want to know who Elijah was, if you have forgotten.

Elijah was a prophet in the days when Israel had a bad king called Ahab, and an even worse queen called Jezebel. They had turned the people away from the Lord to worship an idol called Baal. And Elijah walked into the king's court one day, from nowhere, the way Melchizedek walks into Genesis, and said: "As the Lord God of Israel lives, before whom I stand, there shall be neither dew nor rain these years, except by my word." And then he walked out again.

And the rain stopped.

Which made Elijah, very quickly, the most unpopular man in the kingdom.

And that led to Egeria's next stop. A little further on, she writes, they passed a valley with a stream running down it. And this, they told her, was the brook Cherith, where Elijah hid, in the time of King Ahab.

Because after Elijah told the king there would be no rain, God told Elijah to go and hide himself by the brook Cherith, east of the Jordan. You shall drink from the brook, God said, and I have commanded the ravens to feed you there. And so Elijah did. And the ravens brought him bread and meat every morning, and bread and meat every evening, and he drank from the brook. Until at last the brook dried up, because there had been no rain, and God sent him on to a widow in a town far away, who had a jar of flour and a jug of oil that never ran out.

And Egeria tells us that in that valley, by the brook, a holy monk was living now, in a cell. A hermit, in Elijah's old hiding place, on his own, praying.

"Do the ravens bring him his supper?" asked Malchus.

One of the monks travelling with them laughed. "No," he said. "The villagers bring it. On a donkey."

Malchus looked at Thunder, the big brown donkey who was very quiet, and Thunder looked back, and I think something passed between them, some sense of the dignity of their calling.

"Ravens are not very reliable," said Malchus, with satisfaction. "Donkeys are better."

Egeria wrote nothing about that conversation, because it never happened. But I like to think she would have agreed.

* * *

And then, at last, after eight days of road, they came to the city of Job.

Egeria calls it Carneas. It lay out beyond the Jordan, to the north and east, in a wide high country of black stone and good soil, on the far side of the hills, on the edge of the desert. She tells us that it was in the country of Uz. Today, many scholars think the place was near a village called Sheikh Saad, in the south of Syria, where people have pointed out places connected with Job for many hundreds of years.

And there was a church. Or rather, there was half of one.

The monks who lived there told Egeria how the tomb had been found. There was a holy monk, a hermit from the desert, they said. And he had been shown, by a revelation, that he should dig in a certain place. So he dug. And he and the people with him found a cave. And going down into it, they found a stone. And on the stone, carved into it, was a name.

Job.

And so, over that place, someone was now building a church. A tribune, Egeria says, an officer of the Roman army, was paying for it, and the altar was being set so that the place of the tomb was underneath it, the way the altars of the martyrs were built in those days. But it was not finished. It was still going up, Egeria tells us, when she was there.

I like that the church was not finished. I like that she says so. She could have just said, I went to the tomb of Job, and there was a church. But she was honest. She came eight days' journey, and there was a building site.

"It's not finished," said Malchus.

"No," said Egeria.

"We came eight days for a church with no roof."

"We came eight days," said Egeria gently, "to pray where holy Job is laid. The roof is not the important part."

Malchus looked up at the sky, where the roof would be, and thought about it. "It's quite a nice sky," he admitted.

* * *

And there, in the church with no roof, they did what Egeria did everywhere.

They prayed. And they read from the Book of Job. And since this is the last place we will go with her tonight, let us finish Job's story, sitting on the half-built walls, in the late sun, with the black stones going warm and the swallows coming out.

Remember where we left him. Job, who had lost everything, sitting in the ashes, arguing with his friends, who were sure he had done something wrong. And Job, who was sure he hadn't, demanding that God come and answer him.

And at last, God did.

He came out of a whirlwind. And he did not explain anything. Instead, he asked Job some questions.

"Where were you," God said, "when I laid the foundations of the earth? Tell me, if you understand. Who set its measurements? Who stretched a line across it? On what were its foundations sunk, or who laid its cornerstone, when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?"

And on he went. Have you commanded the morning, since your days began? Have you walked in the depths of the sea? Have you seen the storehouses of the snow? Can you send out the lightning? Who feeds the young ravens, when they cry to God? Do you know when the mountain goats give birth? Do you give the horse its strength, or teach the hawk to fly?

It goes on for four chapters. It is some of the most beautiful poetry ever written. And all through it, God never once tells Job why he suffered. He shows him, instead, how big the world is. How full of wonders Job had never seen. How every one of them is held, and fed, and known.

And Job, who had demanded answers for thirty-five chapters, found he did not need them any more. "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear," he said, "but now my eye sees you."

And then something rather wonderful happened. God turned to the three friends, the ones who had talked so much, and said that he was angry with them. Because they had not spoken rightly about him, as his servant Job had. Job, who had wept and complained and argued, had spoken more truly than the men who had defended God with tidy explanations. And God told the friends to go to Job, and ask him to pray for them. And Job prayed for his friends. And it was when he prayed for his friends that God restored his fortunes.

He had sons and daughters again, seven sons and three daughters. And the book tells us the daughters' names, which is unusual. The first was called Jemimah, which means something like dove. The second was Keziah, which is a kind of sweet spice. And the third was Keren-happuch, which means, roughly, a little jar of eye-paint. And there were no women in all the land as beautiful as Job's daughters, the book says. And their father gave them an inheritance along with their brothers, which in those days was very unusual too.

And Job lived a hundred and forty years after this, and saw his children, and his children's children, four generations. And he died, an old man, and full of days.

Egeria does not tell us how long she stayed at Carneas. A day, perhaps two. Long enough to pray, and to read, and to look at the stone, and at the walls going up. And then she turned round and made the long journey home, back down through the valley, past Cherith and Tishbe, past the green hill of Sedima and the garden of Saint John, past Jericho, and up, and up, and up into the hills, to Jerusalem.

She tells us so in a single line. And then the next chapter of her letter begins with a new decision. It had been three full years, she writes, since she had come to Jerusalem, and she had seen all the holy places she had come to see, and it was time to go home.

But she did not go straight home. She never did anything straight. She went the long way, north, all the way to Edessa, to see the shrine of the apostle Thomas, and then round by the shrine of Saint Thecla, where she found an old friend waiting, and on to Constantinople. And that is a story we have told on another night.

We don't know what happened to her after that. The letter breaks off. We don't know if she ever reached home again, or saw her sisters. We only know that, three hundred years later, a monk in Spain read her letter and loved it so much that he wrote one of his own, praising the blessed Egeria, who had gone out from the very end of the world to see the wonders of God.

And the places she saw?

Egeria never tells us whether the church at Carneas was finished. But people kept coming to the place they called the city of Job for many hundreds of years, Christians, and later Muslims too, who also honour Job as a prophet.

The hill of Sedima is quiet now. People still argue about where Salem was. They will probably always argue.

And the garden of Saint John? Nobody now can say for certain which spring the old priest showed her. There are a number of good springs in that part of the valley, and the experts have their favourites. The water still comes out of the ground there, cold, from under the mountains, and runs away between the fields, the way it did in John's day, and in Egeria's.

I like to think of it on the night of Easter. The priest, with his lamp. The monks, singing. The newly baptized, shivering a little, with wet hair, carrying their candles back through the orchard to the church, in a long line of small lights between the trees. And the water running on, in the dark, behind them.

He must increase. I must decrease.

The water does not mind being forgotten. It just keeps flowing.

And Job, who lost everything, sat in the ashes and said, I know that my Redeemer lives. He did not know how it would come right. He did not know why. He only knew who. And it turned out that was enough.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from chapters thirteen to sixteen of Egeria's Travels, the long Latin letter written by a pilgrim woman to her sisters at home, probably in the early three hundred and eighties, and found in a single incomplete copy in Arezzo in eighteen eighty-four. Her wish to see the tomb of Job after meeting monks from that country, her remark that a toil is no toil when your wish is fulfilled, the journey with holy men, the eight staging posts, the beautiful valley with its vines and water, the village called Sedima with its small hill and church, the priest's account of Melchizedek, the Greek name of the church and the foundations round the hill, her question about Aenon, the two hundred paces, the orchard and spring called the garden of Saint John, the monks who came to wash there, the Easter baptisms with candles, psalms and antiphons, the fruit given as a blessing, the town of Tishbe, the valley of Cherith with a monk living in it, Carneas the city of Job, the holy monk who was shown where to dig, the stone with Job's name, and the tribune's church, not yet finished, are all in the letter. Her own words, and the priest's, are given in our plain rendering, shortened.

That the hill was Salem, that the spring was Aenon, and that the cave was the tomb of Job are the local traditions she was told, and we have told them as traditions. Many readers place Salem at Jerusalem instead.

The stories of Melchizedek come from Genesis fourteen, Psalm one hundred and ten and Hebrews seven; John's words from John three; Elijah from First Kings seventeen; and Job from the book that bears his name, told straight.

The boys Malchus and Zacchaeus, the donkeys, and every word anyone says apart from what Egeria herself reports, are our own invention.

That's the story of Egeria, and the garden where John baptized, for tonight. Egeria has never been made a saint and has no feast day, but John the Baptist, whose garden she visited, is remembered on the twenty-fourth of June, the day of his birth, and on the twenty-ninth of August, and holy Job, in the Orthodox calendar, on the sixth of May.

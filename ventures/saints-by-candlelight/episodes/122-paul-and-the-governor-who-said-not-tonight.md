It is evening, in a palace by the sea.

The sun has gone down behind the water, and the sky over the harbour is the colour of a peach. Somewhere below the windows the waves are coming in against the rocks, slowly, the way they always do. There are lamps lit in the room, and the smell of the oil, and a breeze coming in off the sea that makes the flames lean over and then stand up again.

On a couch, in very good clothes, sits the Roman governor of Judea. Beside him sits his wife, who is young, and beautiful, and a princess, and knows it.

And standing in front of them, with a soldier at the door behind him and a light chain on his wrist, is a short man with a tired face, talking.

He has been talking for some time. They asked him to. And at first the governor was interested, and leaned forward, and nodded. But the short man has moved on now, from what he believes to what it means. And he is talking, quite gently, about justice. And about keeping hold of yourself. And about the day when everybody will have to give an account.

And the governor has stopped nodding. He has gone a little pale. He is looking at the floor.

And all at once he holds up his hand, and says, that's enough. Go away for now. When I have a convenient moment, I'll send for you.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Last time, if you were with us, Saint Paul was in Jerusalem, under arrest, in the Roman fortress at the corner of the temple. He had stood before the council, and been struck on the mouth, and set the two parties of the council arguing with each other about the resurrection. And that night, in the barracks, the Lord had stood beside him and said, take courage.

And then more than forty men had sworn an oath not to eat or drink until they had killed him. And Paul's nephew, his sister's son, had heard about it, and walked into the fortress, and told the commander. And the commander, whose name was Claudius Lysias, had decided that this prisoner was more trouble than he was worth. And at nine o'clock that night he had sent him off down the hill, on a horse, in the middle of four hundred and seventy soldiers, to the governor at Caesarea, with a letter.

And the governor had read the letter, and asked Paul which province he was from, and said, I will hear your case when your accusers arrive. And he had him kept in Herod's palace.

And that is where we left him. In a palace by the sea, waiting.

I should tell you at the start what kind of a story this is. It is a story with a trial in it, with a professional speaker who has been hired for the occasion and gives very good value for money. It has a prisoner who does not raise his voice once. It has a governor who knows more than he lets on, and a young wife with a past. And it has a lot of waiting. Two whole years of it.

And it has, at the heart of it, one of the saddest little sentences in the whole book. Not because anything terrible happens. But because nothing does.

* * *

Let me set the scene.

Caesarea is not an old city. It is about eighty years old, which, in that part of the world, makes it practically brand new. King Herod the Great built it, on a flat bit of coast where there was nothing much but an old tower and a lot of sand. And he built it to show off.

He wanted a harbour, and there was no harbour, so he made one. His engineers poured enormous blocks of concrete into the sea, in great wooden frames, and the concrete set under water, which is a thing Roman concrete can do and which still rather amazes the people who study it. And they built two long arms of stone out into the sea, curving round, with towers on them and a lighthouse, and statues at the entrance, and a quay where the ships could tie up in any weather. Josephus says it was bigger than the harbour at Athens. He may be boasting on Herod's behalf. But divers have been down to look at what is left, and it was very big indeed.

And round the harbour, Herod built a city in the Roman style. Straight streets. Drains that the sea flushed out. A theatre facing the water. A great temple on a platform above the harbour, to the goddess Rome and to the emperor Augustus, which is where the city got its name. Caesarea, the city of Caesar. And on a rocky point that sticks out into the sea, he built himself a palace, with a pool in the middle of it, and the waves breaking on three sides.

That is where Paul is being kept. Not in a dungeon. In the governor's palace, which the Romans have taken over as their headquarters. I imagine a small room somewhere near the soldiers' quarters, with a window, and a guard outside, and the sound of the sea all night long.

Now, let me tell you about the governor.

His name is Antonius Felix, and he is, I think it is fair to say, one of the more surprising people ever to be put in charge of a Roman province. Because Felix was born a slave.

As far as we can tell, he and his brother Pallas were slaves in the household of a great Roman lady called Antonia, who was the mother of the emperor Claudius. And at some point they were both set free. And Pallas rose and rose, until he was one of the most powerful men in Rome, in charge of the emperor's money. And he used his influence to get his brother made governor of Judea.

The Roman historians did not like this at all. Tacitus, who was a senator and a great snob, says that Felix used the power of a king with the mind of a slave. Which is unkind. But Tacitus also says that he behaved very badly, and Josephus, who was there, agrees with him.

Felix's time as governor was not a peaceful one. There were bandits in the hills, and he hunted them down, and put a great many of them to death. There were men who went about in the crowds at festivals with daggers under their cloaks. There were prophets who led people out into the desert promising signs, and Felix sent the cavalry after them. And according to Josephus, when a former high priest called Jonathan kept telling Felix how to do his job better, Felix arranged for him to be quietly got rid of. So, not a gentle man. And not a man you would want to owe money to.

And Felix had a gift for marrying well. The Roman writer Suetonius calls him, in passing, the husband of three queens. Which is not a phrase you hear very often. One of his wives, according to Tacitus, was a granddaughter of Antony and Cleopatra, and she was called Drusilla. And his wife now, the one sitting beside him on the couch in our cold open, is a different woman, who was also called Drusilla.

I do not know how he kept them straight. Perhaps he did not have to. Perhaps they were not both in the house at once. The historians do not say, and I think on the whole we had better not ask.

* * *

Five days after Paul arrived in Caesarea, his accusers came down from Jerusalem.

It is about a two day journey, so they did not waste any time. And they came in some style. The high priest Ananias came himself, the same one who had ordered Paul struck on the mouth. And some of the elders of the council came with him. And they brought with them an orator. A professional. A man called Tertullus.

Now, you may wonder why the high priest needed to bring an orator. Surely the high priest of Jerusalem could speak for himself?

And the answer is that this was a Roman court. A Roman governor, sitting in judgement, under Roman law. And Roman courts had their own way of doing things, and their own way of talking, and there were men who made a living from knowing exactly how it was done. You can think of Tertullus as a sort of barrister. A hired voice. The kind of man you bring in when you want to win, and you are not sure that you can do it on your own.

Luke does not tell us anything about him except his name and what he said. So I am going to imagine him for you, and you can imagine him differently if you prefer. I see a man in a beautifully folded toga, with very clean hands. He has a voice like warm honey. He has given this sort of speech a hundred times, in a hundred courtrooms, and he knows exactly where to pause, and where to lower his voice, and where to look sadly at the floor.

The court met in the hall of the palace. The governor on his seat. The soldiers at the walls. The high priest and the elders on one side, in their robes. And then they brought Paul in, and he stood on the other side, on his own.

And Felix nodded to Tertullus, and Tertullus stepped forward, and drew a breath, and began.

And the first thing he did was butter up the governor. This was quite normal. Every speech in a Roman court began with a little polite flattery of the judge. There were textbooks on how to do it. But Tertullus, I think it is fair to say, laid it on with a trowel.

Here is what he said, more or less, in our own plain words.

Most excellent Felix. Because of you, we enjoy a great peace. Because of your wise care, many improvements have been made for this nation. In every way, and everywhere, we welcome this, with all our gratitude.

Now. You will remember what I told you about Felix's time in office. The bandits, and the hangings, and the daggers in the crowd, and the cavalry, and the high priest Jonathan. And here is a man standing in front of the current high priest, saying, because of you, we enjoy a great peace.

I like to imagine that there was a very small silence after that, in which one of the elders looked hard at the ceiling.

And then Tertullus went on. But I do not want to take up too much of your time, he said. So I ask you, in your kindness, to hear us briefly.

Which is what people always say, just before they speak for a long time. But to be fair to Tertullus, he did not. He got to the point.

We have found this man, he said, to be a plague. A pest. He stirs up riots among the Jews all over the world. He is a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes. He even tried to defile the temple, and so we seized him. And if you examine him yourself, you will be able to find out from him all the things we are accusing him of.

Some old copies of Acts have a few more lines in the middle of that speech, where Tertullus complains that they would have judged Paul by their own law, if the Roman commander had not come along with great violence and snatched him out of their hands. Which is an interesting way to describe a man rescuing somebody from a crowd that was trying to beat him to death. But most of the oldest copies do not have those lines, so we will leave them out.

And then the elders all joined in, and said, yes, yes, it is all true.

So there were three charges, if you were counting. One, that Paul was a troublemaker, stirring up riots everywhere he went. Two, that he was a ringleader of the Nazarenes, which is what some people called the followers of Jesus of Nazareth. And three, that he had tried to defile the temple.

And the clever thing about the speech, if you know what to look for, is that the first charge is the one aimed at the Roman. A Roman governor does not care very much about the fine points of the Jewish law, or who is allowed into which court of the temple. But a Roman governor cares very much about riots. Riots are his whole job. Tertullus did not say, this man has strange ideas. He said, this man is dangerous to the peace. Your peace. The peace you have so wisely given us.

It was a very good speech. Whoever paid for it got their money's worth.

* * *

And then the governor nodded to Paul. Your turn.

And Paul did something that I think we should notice. He also began politely. But he did not flatter. He just said something true.

I know, he said, that you have been a judge over this nation for many years. So I am glad to make my defence before you.

Which, if you think about it, is a compliment of a sort. It means, you have been here long enough to know how things work. You have seen a few riots. You know what this city is like at festival time. You will not be fooled.

And then he answered the three charges, one at a time, in order, without a single wasted word.

The first charge was that he stirred up riots. And Paul said, you can check this for yourself. It is not more than twelve days since I went up to Jerusalem to worship. And they did not find me arguing with anybody in the temple, or gathering a crowd, either in the synagogues or anywhere in the city. And they cannot prove to you the things they are accusing me of now.

Notice the word, to worship. Paul did not go up to Jerusalem to cause trouble. He went to pray. And twelve days is not long. It is hard to start a world-wide rebellion in twelve days, especially if you have spent most of them in the temple, finishing a vow, and the rest of them in Roman custody.

The second charge was that he was a ringleader of the Nazarenes. And here Paul did not deny anything. He said, this I do admit to you. That according to the Way, which they call a sect, I worship the God of our fathers. I believe everything that is laid down in the law, and everything that is written in the prophets. And I have a hope in God, which these men here also hold, that there will be a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous. And so I always do my best to keep a clear conscience before God and before everybody.

Now, I want to stop on that for a moment, because it is a beautiful little piece of honesty, and it is also very clever.

He does not say, I am not a Christian. He says, yes, I follow the Way. That was one of the earliest names for the followers of Jesus, the Way. Not a sect, says Paul, gently. They call it a sect. We call it the Way. And it is not a new religion. It is the old one. The same God our fathers worshipped. The same law, the same prophets, the same hope.

And the hope he names is the resurrection. The very thing he had shouted out in the council in Jerusalem, a few days before. The dead will rise, the just and the unjust together. You can find that hope in the Book of Daniel, which says that many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake. And you can find it in the Gospel of John, where the Lord says that the hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out.

And Paul says, these men believe that too. Or some of them do. The Pharisees certainly did. Ananias the high priest, who was probably a Sadducee, almost certainly did not. And I suspect Paul knew exactly who in the room he was talking to.

And then the third charge. That he had tried to defile the temple. And here Paul told the governor what had actually happened. After several years away, he said, I came to bring alms to my nation, and to offer sacrifices. And while I was doing that, they found me in the temple, having been purified, and there was no crowd and no disturbance.

This is the only place in the whole of the Acts of the Apostles where this collection is mentioned. You may remember that for years, all round Greece and Asia, Paul had been collecting money from the churches, for the poor believers in Jerusalem. He writes about it in his letters to the Romans and the Corinthians. Put something aside every Sunday, he tells them. Not grudgingly. God loves a cheerful giver. And he carried that money all the way to Jerusalem, at real risk to himself. And here, in a Roman courtroom, almost in passing, he mentions it. I came to bring alms.

So. He had gone to the temple to pray, purified, quietly, with a gift for the poor. And somebody had started a riot round him. It was not he who made the riot. It was the riot that found him.

And who started it? Paul said, some Jews from the province of Asia. They were the ones who saw me in the temple and shouted. And here is the point. They ought to be here, in front of you, making their accusation, if they have anything against me.

And they were not there. The people who had actually seen what happened, the eyewitnesses, had not come. They had gone home, I suppose, after the festival. And in Roman law, that mattered. Accusers who did not turn up to accuse were a serious problem for a case.

And then Paul finished. Or else, he said, let these men here say what wrong they found in me when I stood before the council. Except for this one thing, which I shouted out as I stood among them. It is about the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial before you today.

And then he stopped.

No flattery. No raised voice. No clever words. Three charges, three answers. I did not start a riot, and nobody can prove I did. I do follow the Way, and the Way is the faith of our fathers. I did not defile the temple, I was there to pray and to give, and the men who said so have not come. And if there is anything left, it is that I believe the dead will rise.

I have always thought that if I were ever in trouble, I would rather have Paul speaking for me than Tertullus. Even though Tertullus had the better toga.

* * *

And now Felix had to decide.

And Luke tells us something very interesting here. He says that Felix had a rather accurate knowledge of the Way.

How did he know? Luke does not say. He had been governor for some years by now, and there had been followers of Jesus in Judea for more than twenty years. There was a church in Caesarea itself. Philip the evangelist lived there with his four daughters, and Paul had stayed in his house on the way up to Jerusalem. And the household of the centurion Cornelius, the first family of Gentiles that Saint Peter baptised, had been baptised in Caesarea. A Roman officer, one of Felix's own sort. So perhaps he had simply heard a lot about it. Perhaps his wife had told him things, since she was Jewish, and had grown up hearing about the followers of Jesus. We are guessing. But he knew enough to know that the Way was not a gang of rebels.

And he knew enough, I think, to see what this case really was. It was not a riot. It was an argument about religion, between people of the same religion, and one of them had brought along a very expensive orator to make it sound like a riot.

So did he let Paul go?

No. He did what officials in a tight corner have always done. He put it off.

When Lysias the commander comes down, he said, I will decide your case.

Which sounds reasonable. Lysias was the man who had arrested Paul, after all. But Lysias had already written Felix a letter, and Luke has given us that letter, and in it Lysias says quite plainly that he found Paul accused of nothing that deserved death or chains. Felix knew what Lysias thought. And as far as we know, Lysias never came down. Or if he did, nothing changed.

And then Felix gave the centurion his orders. Keep him in custody, he said. But let him have some freedom. And do not stop any of his friends from looking after him.

Which is, if you think about it, a very odd thing to do with a dangerous man who stirs up riots all over the world. You do not give a dangerous man some freedom and let his friends come and go. You do that with a man you know is innocent, and cannot quite bring yourself to release.

And so the high priest and the elders and Tertullus packed up, and went back up the road to Jerusalem, having not exactly won and not exactly lost. And Paul went back to his room by the sea.

* * *

Now, I want to tell you about the friends. Because Luke says that Felix let Paul's friends look after him, and then he does not tell us one single thing about them. So this part is ours. But I do not think we are far wrong.

There was a church in Caesarea. And at the heart of it, as far as we can tell, was Philip.

Philip was one of the seven men chosen, long before, to look after the widows and the poor in the church at Jerusalem, so that the apostles could get on with preaching. He was the one who met the Ethiopian official on the desert road, reading the prophet Isaiah in his chariot, and explained it to him, and baptised him in the first water they came to. And then, Luke says, he went preaching from town to town all up the coast, until he came to Caesarea. And there he seems to have settled down, and had a family. And he had four daughters, unmarried, who prophesied.

And Paul had stayed in their house, only a few weeks before, on his way to Jerusalem. So I think that when the gate of the palace opened, and the guard said, your friends are here, it was very often Philip's family.

I imagine one of the daughters coming in with a basket. Bread. Olives. A bit of cheese wrapped in a vine leaf. Some figs. And I imagine the guard at the door looking into the basket in the way guards do, and taking a fig, in the way guards also do, and nobody saying anything about it.

And I imagine Paul being very pleased to see them. He was a man who loved his friends. His letters end with long lists of them. Greet Prisca and Aquila. Greet my dear friend. Greet the mother of Rufus, who has been a mother to me as well. He was not, whatever people think, a lonely man. He was a man who was always being sent away from people he loved, which is a different thing.

And I like to think that Luke was there too. Luke had come up to Jerusalem with Paul. Luke tells us so, because for that part of the story he writes we, we did this, we went there. And two years later, when Paul is put on a ship for Rome, Luke is on the ship, because he says we again. In between, he does not say where he was. But a great many people have guessed that he stayed somewhere near, in Caesarea, or in the country round about, for those two years. And some have wondered whether he used the time to go about Judea, asking questions, of the people who had known the Lord, and his mother, and the apostles. Gathering what he would one day write down.

That is only a guess. We do not know. But if you ever wondered when Luke had the time to talk to everybody, it is a nice thought that it might have been here, while his friend was waiting by the sea.

So Paul had his friends, and his food, and his letters, and his prayers, and the sound of the waves. And he waited.

* * *

And then, some days later, the governor sent for him again. Not to the court this time. Just to talk.

And the governor's wife was there.

Her name was Drusilla. And she was, I am sorry to say, not just anybody. She was the youngest daughter of King Herod Agrippa the First.

You may remember him, if you have been with us for a while. He was the king who had the apostle James put to death, and threw Saint Peter into prison, and then an angel came in the night and let Peter out. And not long after that, the king came here, to this very city, to Caesarea. And he put on a robe of silver, Josephus says, that shone in the morning sun. And he sat in the theatre, and made a speech, and the crowd shouted that it was the voice of a god and not of a man. And he did not stop them. And he was taken ill, and within a few days he died.

And Josephus says that when he died, his little daughter Drusilla was six years old.

So the young woman sitting next to Felix, in the palace by the sea, had lost her father in this same city, when she was a little girl. I do not know whether she ever went past the theatre and thought of him. Josephus does not say. But she would have been about twenty now. Not much more.

And she had a past, even at twenty. She had been married, very young, to a king of a little country to the north, called Emesa. And then Felix had seen her, and wanted her. And Josephus says that Felix sent a friend to persuade her to leave her husband and marry him instead, and promised to make her happy. And she did. Josephus says she did it partly to get away from her sister, who was jealous of her. So she left her husband, and married the governor, who was not a Jew, and was not free to marry her by her own people's law, and who was a former slave with a sharp sword and a bad reputation.

I tell you all this not to be unkind to her. She was very young, and nobody in her family had ever taught her much about faithfulness. I tell you because it helps us to understand what happened next.

So, Luke says, Felix came with his wife Drusilla, who was Jewish. And he sent for Paul. And he heard him talk about faith in Christ Jesus.

And I can imagine how it began. A pleasant evening. The lamps lit. Perhaps there were figs and wine. And Felix, who was curious, and who already knew a fair bit about the Way, says to Paul, tell us about this Jesus. And Drusilla, who had grown up hearing about him, for her father had persecuted his followers, perhaps leans forward too.

And Paul tells them.

He tells them, I should think, what he told everybody. That Jesus of Nazareth was the Christ, the anointed one, the one the prophets had promised. That he was crucified, under Pontius Pilate, who had governed from this very city. That God raised him from the dead on the third day, and that Paul had seen him, on the road to Damascus. That through him, God forgives sins. That anyone can come to him, Jew or Greek, slave or free, man or woman.

Slave or free. I wonder if Felix noticed that.

And then Paul went on. Because he never told the good news as a pleasant story. He always told what it meant.

And Luke says that Paul talked to them about three things. About justice. About self-control. And about the judgement to come.

* * *

Now, think about who he is talking to.

Justice. To a governor who hanged people in rows, and arranged for a high priest to be murdered, and was known to take bribes.

Self-control. To a man and a woman who had married each other by breaking up her marriage, because he wanted her and she wanted to get away.

The judgement to come. To a man who, by the standards of this world, had never had to answer to anybody. Who had climbed all the way up from slavery to a palace, and was not used to being told that one day he would have to give an account.

Paul was not being rude. I do not think he was even being pointed, exactly. He was simply telling the truth, the whole truth, the way he always did. Faith in Christ is not only a thing you believe. It is a door you walk through, and on the other side of the door, your whole life has to change. And he said so. To a governor. Who had the power to have him killed in the morning.

And Luke says that Felix became afraid.

Not angry. Afraid. That is the word Luke uses. Something in what Paul said went past all of Felix's armour, past the palace and the soldiers and the clever brother in Rome, and touched the place where he knew, the way we all know, deep down, that what he had done was wrong. And for a moment the most powerful man in the province sat on his couch and was frightened.

And then he did what we all do, when that happens. He held up his hand.

Go away for now, he said. When I have a convenient time, I will send for you.

An old English translation puts it, when I have a convenient season. And that phrase, a convenient season, has been in preachers' mouths for four hundred years, because nearly every one of us has said it at some point. Not tonight. Not now. Later. When things are less busy. When I am ready.

And the guard opened the door. And Paul went back to his room.

* * *

And here is the sad little sentence I promised you.

Luke says that Felix did send for Paul again. Often, in fact. He sent for him often, and talked with him.

But Luke also tells us why. At the same time, he says, Felix was hoping that Paul would give him money.

Which tells you a great deal, I am afraid. Felix had heard that Paul came to Jerusalem with alms. A bag of money for the poor. And he had seen Paul's friends coming and going. And he thought, perhaps, this man has friends with money. Perhaps if I keep him long enough, somebody will offer me a little something to let him go.

And Paul never paid. Not a penny. He would not buy his freedom with a bribe, any more than he would buy his life by keeping quiet about the judgement.

So I imagine many evenings, over two years. The guard at the door. The governor on his couch. The lamps. Paul talking, and Felix listening, and Felix asking questions, and perhaps even liking him a little. And all the time, the convenient season never came. Felix had faith in Christ explained to him, in person, by Saint Paul the Apostle, again and again. And every time, he heard it, and every time, he let it go past him, and every time he was really thinking about something else.

That is the sad sentence. Nothing terrible happened. The governor just never quite got round to it.

And then, Luke says, when two years had passed, Felix was replaced. A new governor came, called Porcius Festus. And Felix, wanting to do the Jewish leaders a favour on his way out, left Paul in prison.

Two years. Think of that. Two whole years, in a room by the sea, for a man who had done nothing wrong.

And I wonder what Paul thought, all that time. He was a man who had spent his whole life on the move. Ships and roads and markets and synagogues. And the Lord had told him, in the barracks in Jerusalem, that he would bear witness in Rome. And here he was, stuck, with the ships coming in and going out of Herod's harbour under his window, every day, and none of them for him.

I think he prayed. I think he wrote letters, though we do not know for certain which ones. I think he taught the friends who came to see him. I think he talked to the guards. He had a habit of talking to guards. Once, at Philippi, his jailer ended the night being baptised with his whole family, which must have been very confusing for the jailer's officers.

And I think he trusted. Not because it was easy. But because he had learnt, by then, the thing that he once wrote to the Romans. That in everything, God works for good with those who love him. Even in a room. Even for two years. Even when the man who could let you go keeps saying, not tonight.

[pause]

And what happened to them all?

Felix went back to Rome. And Josephus says that the leading Jews of Caesarea went after him, and accused him in front of the emperor Nero, and he would certainly have been punished, if his brother Pallas had not been standing by the emperor, and asked him to let it go. So Felix escaped, one more time. After that, he drops out of history. We do not know when he died, or where, or whether he ever thought again about the prisoner he used to send for in the evenings. I hope he did. I hope that one day, somewhere, the convenient season came after all. It is never too late, until it is.

Drusilla had a son by Felix, and Josephus says they named him Agrippa, after her father. And that is nearly all we know of her.

Tertullus, the orator, vanishes entirely. I expect he gave a great many more speeches. I expect they were all very good. Nobody remembers any of them except this one, and he only lost it a little.

The high priest Ananias went back to Jerusalem, and kept on being rich, for a while.

And Paul? Paul waited. And when the new governor came, Paul's case was opened again, and before long Paul said four words that changed everything, which are, I appeal to Caesar. And so, in the end, he got his ship to Rome after all. But that is a story for another night.

And Caesarea is still there. Not the city, quite. The city is mostly ruins now, golden stone among the sand and the wild flowers, by the edge of the sea. But you can walk in Herod's theatre, where Drusilla's father made his last speech, and where they still hold concerts on summer evenings. You can see the outline of the palace on the rocky point, and the pool in the middle, with the sea coming in over the rocks. And if you put on a mask and swim out into the water, you can see Herod's great harbour lying on the sea floor, the concrete blocks still there after two thousand years, with little fish going in and out.

And if you stand on that point in the evening, when the sky is the colour of a peach, and the waves are coming in slowly, the way they always do, I think you can imagine a small window somewhere above you. And a man at the window, with a chain on his wrist, watching the ships go out. Waiting. Praying. Not in a hurry. Not afraid.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twenty-four. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept. The words of Tertullus, of Paul and of Felix are in our own plain rendering, adding nothing to what Luke reports. Some old copies of Acts add a few lines to the speech of Tertullus about the commander Lysias; we have mentioned them and left them out, as most modern translations do.

Felix being born a slave, his brother Pallas, the troubles of his time, the death of the former high priest Jonathan, his marriage to Drusilla, her first husband, and his recall are from Josephus. The line about the mind of a slave is from Tacitus, who also names the other Drusilla. The three queens are from Suetonius. The death of King Agrippa at Caesarea is told both in Acts and in Josephus, who gives Drusilla's age. The harbour is in Josephus and has been studied by divers and archaeologists.

How Felix knew so much about the Way, and whether Luke stayed near Caesarea for the two years, are guesses. Tertullus's toga, the palace room, the visits of Philip's daughters, the basket, the guard and the fig, and what Paul and Felix said on the other evenings are our own invention.

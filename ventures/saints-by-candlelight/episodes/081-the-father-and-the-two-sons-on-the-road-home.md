Picture a doorway, and a man who keeps looking through it.

It is an ordinary doorway in an ordinary house, at the edge of an ordinary village, and nothing about it is special. There is a worn step, and a bit of road beyond the step, and beyond the road a field, and beyond the field the long pale line of the way that leads out into the world. And every evening, when the work is done and the light has begun to go yellow, an old man comes out and stands in that doorway with one hand on the frame. He does not say anything. He does not do anything. He just looks along the road for a while, as if he were reading it, and then he goes in again.

Nobody in the house asks him why. They all know.

But let us start where the story starts, a good deal earlier than that.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is one of the best known in the whole Bible. It is a parable that Jesus told, from the fifteenth chapter of the Gospel of Luke, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a parable is a story, not a report, so we have taken care to keep what Jesus said and to say plainly when we are adding colour of our own, like the road, the farm and the kitchen. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

First, why was it told?

Luke gives us the reason, and it is worth hearing, because it changes how the story sounds. At the start of the chapter, he says that tax collectors and sinners were all gathering round to listen to Jesus. These were people that respectable folk crossed the street to avoid. And the Pharisees and the scribes, who were serious, careful people who had spent their lives trying to keep the Law, stood at the edge of the crowd and grumbled. "This man," they said, "welcomes sinners and eats with them."

Now that was meant as an insult. And Jesus, who could have answered with an argument, answered with stories instead. First he told them about a shepherd with a hundred sheep, who loses one, and leaves the ninety-nine, and goes looking, and when he finds it puts it on his shoulders and calls his friends in to rejoice. Then he told them about a woman with ten silver coins, who loses one, and lights a lamp, and sweeps the whole house, and when she finds it calls the neighbours in to rejoice. Something lost, something found, and a party at the end of each.

And then he said, "There was a man who had two sons."

Remember that, as the story goes on. There were two. Jesus did not say there was a man who had a son who went wrong. He said two sons, and he said it at the start, and as we shall see, the second one matters as much as the first.

[pause]

* * *

So. There is a man with two sons, and he is a man of some means. He has land, and animals, and people who work for him, and a house with a flat roof and a good well. We are not told his name, and we are not told the name of either boy, so we shall do as Luke does and call them the father, the younger son and the elder.

The younger son is, I should think, about the age at which a young man feels that he has got to the end of his village. He knows every lane. He knows every neighbour's opinion of him. He has heard about the cities down the coast, and what is to be found there, and the more he thinks about it the more the farm seems to him like a very small box. And one day he goes to his father and says something that no son in that time and place would have said lightly.

"Father," he says, "give me the share of the property that will belong to me."

Let us pause on that, because it is startling. In the ordinary way, a son received his inheritance when his father died. To ask for it while the father was alive and well was, to put it mildly, to say that you could not wait. It is a bit like saying, I should like to treat you as though you were already gone.

And what does the father do? Luke says that he divided his property between them.

He does not argue. He does not shout, and he does not say what he might very well have said, which is, "Over my dead body." He does not make the boy wait, or earn it, or promise to behave. He simply hands it over. I want you to notice that, because it is the first of several moments where the father does something that nobody in the audience would have expected. He lets his son go. He lets him have what he asks for, even though he must have known, in his heart, what a young man with a full purse and no experience is likely to do with it.

Now a word about the share. The usual way of reading this, going by the law in the book of Deuteronomy, is that a father with two sons divided the property so that the elder had twice as much as the younger. So the younger son would be getting one part in three. That is a reading, and not something that Luke says in so many words, but it is a reasonable one, and it means that what the younger son takes away is a good deal, and what the elder son keeps, we shall see, is more.

Luke tells us the next part with a great economy. "Not many days later," he says, "the younger son gathered all he had and took a journey into a far country."

Gathered all he had. He turned it all into money, you see, or into something he could carry. He did not keep a corner of it back for a rainy day. And off he went along the road, with the dust on his sandals and the sun on his face, and I expect that for the first mile or two he felt wonderful. Nobody had told him what to do. Nobody knew who he was. The whole world was lying out in front of him like a table laid for a feast.

And behind him, in a doorway, stood a man who watched him go until he could not see him any more.

[pause]

* * *

What happened in the far country, Luke tells in a single sentence. He squandered his property in loose living.

That is all. We are not given the details, and I think that is a mercy. Whatever the days were like, and they were probably very merry, and very full of new friends, they went by quickly. A young man with money is never short of company. And Luke does not tell us the day it ran out. He just says, "when he had spent everything," as though it were a thing that happened all at once, the way a candle goes out, with no warning except a flicker.

And at exactly that moment, Luke says, a severe famine arose in that country, and he began to be in need.

Now I find that detail very true. It is never the case that one thing goes wrong at a time. The money was gone, and then the crops failed, and then the prices rose, and the friends, who had been so fond of him in the good weather, were suddenly not at home. I imagine him going from door to door, in that town, with his good coat gone and his good sandals gone and his good manners wearing a bit thin, being told one after another, "Come back another day."

And so he did the thing he had sworn he would never do. He went and hired himself out. Luke says that he attached himself to one of the citizens of that country, who sent him into his fields to feed pigs.

You need to know what that meant. To the people listening to Jesus, who were Jewish, pigs were unclean animals. The Law forbade them to eat pork, and a good Jewish family would not have kept pigs, nor handled them, nor gone near them if they could help it. So when Jesus says that the young man was sent to feed pigs, every person in the crowd would have winced. Of all the jobs in the world, this was the one at the very bottom. It was as far from his father's house as a man could get, not only in miles but in everything he had been brought up to be.

And it was not even a good job. Luke tells us that he would gladly have filled his stomach with the pods that the pigs were eating, and nobody gave him anything.

I should like to say something about those pods. The Greek word is *keratia*, which most readers take to mean carob pods, the long dark curved ones that grow on the carob tree. They are food, of a sort, for animals, and in hard times people have eaten them too. So we are not to imagine him gazing at something disgusting. We are to imagine him gazing at something that was just barely food, being eaten by an animal, with a rumbling stomach and no one to give him a share. The whole of his fall is in that one picture. He looks at the pigs, and he envies them.

[pause]

I think there must have been an evening, out in the field, when he sat down on a fence rail, and the pigs were rooting about in the mud, and the light was going. And he was very tired, and he was very alone, and he had nothing left to be clever about.

And Luke says the thing that seems to me the turning point of the whole story. He says, "But when he came to himself."

Came to himself. It is such a lovely phrase. It means, I think, that all the time he had been away he had not been quite himself. He had been somebody else, a person with a purse, a person with friends, a person who was having a wonderful time. And now, in a field, at dusk, with nothing, he found himself again, and the first thing he saw when he did was something he had not thought about in a long time.

"How many of my father's hired servants," he said, "have bread enough and to spare, but here I am perishing with hunger!"

Notice what he thinks about. He does not think, I am a wicked son. He is not yet that far along. He thinks, my father's hired men are better fed than I am. It is a very practical sort of thought. It is a hungry man's thought. And I do not believe Jesus is embarrassed by that, because that is where the road starts. We do not always begin our journey home with a great spiritual insight. Sometimes we begin it with a stomach.

But then he goes a little further, and the thought turns into a plan. "I will get up," he says, "and go to my father, and I will say to him, Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Treat me as one of your hired servants."

He is composing a speech. You can feel him doing it. He is saying it over under his breath, the way you rehearse a difficult conversation on the way to it. Father, I have sinned. I am no longer worthy. Make me as one of your hired men. He has got it all worked out, and it is a good speech, and it is honest. He is not asking to be taken back as a son. He knows he has thrown that away. He is asking for a job.

And Luke says, "And he arose and came to his father."

[pause]

* * *

Now let us think about that road.

It is the same road that he left on, and it is going the other way. And I do not suppose it was any shorter. He has no money, so he is walking. He has one cloak, and it is not a good one. He has no friends on the way, and nothing to trade, and he probably sleeps in barns and under bridges and eats whatever he is given by people who do not much like the look of him.

And all along that road, I think, he is saying the speech. Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. Over and over, in time with his feet. It is the only thing he has, and he holds on to it. And as the country begins to look a little familiar, as he starts to see hills he knows, and a well, and a particular crooked tree that he used to climb, I think that the speech begins to get harder to say. Because now there is a face in it. Before, it was a speech to an idea of a father. Now, with every mile, it is a speech to a real man, with a real voice, whom he has hurt.

I wonder if he slowed down, as he got near. I wonder if he stopped at the last bend and sat on a stone and thought, what if the door is shut? What if the dogs are set on me? What if he looks at me, and says, "I have no son by that name?" It is what he deserves, after all. He knows that.

But he gets up, and he goes on. And this is the part of the story that I love best, and I want you to listen to it carefully, because every word of it is Luke's.

"But while he was still a long way off," Luke says, "his father saw him."

While he was still a long way off.

Do you see? The father is looking. All those evenings in the doorway, all those long looks along the road, were not the idle looks of an old man with nothing to do. He was watching. He has been watching every day since the boy left. And so, when a thin figure appears at the far end of the road, in a bad cloak, walking slowly with his head down, the father knows him at once. Not by his face. He is much too far away for that. He knows him by the way he walks.

And what does the father do? A dignified old man in that world did not run. It was not done. A man of his age, and his standing, would walk, slowly, and let others come to him. But Luke says, "he had compassion, and ran."

He ran. I should like you to picture it. The old man gathers up his robe in one hand, just as Jairus did in another story, and he goes down the road at a speed that nobody in the village has seen him go for years, with his knees hurting and his breath short and not caring in the least who sees him. He runs, and the boy is still working out where to put his feet, and before he has said a single word of his speech, his father's arms are around his neck.

Luke says, "and embraced him and kissed him."

[pause]

* * *

Now the son, who has been rehearsing for days, does start the speech. He is not going to be talked out of it. "Father," he says, "I have sinned against heaven and before you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son."

You will notice that he gets through those two sentences. But there is a third one that he had planned, the one about hired servants, and Luke does not give it to us. Perhaps he said it. Perhaps it never came out, because the father had already turned to the servants. Luke does not say. All we are told is that the father does not seem to be listening to the end. He is already calling for things.

"Quickly," he says to the servants, "bring out the best robe, and put it on him. Put a ring on his hand, and sandals on his feet. And bring the fattened calf and kill it, and let us eat and make merry. For this my son was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found."

I want to go through those, one by one, as though we were unpacking a bag.

The robe. The best one, not a spare. The one that would have been kept for the most honoured guest in the house. The boy has come up the road in rags, and the first thing the father does is cover him.

The ring. A ring on the hand was a sign of belonging to the family, of authority, of being trusted with the household. Whatever a hired man gets, it is not a ring.

The sandals. Servants and hired hands often went barefoot. Sons wore shoes. The father is not waiting for the speech to be finished. He is saying, with a pair of sandals, you are my son.

And the calf. A fattened calf was not for an ordinary Tuesday. It was kept for a great occasion, and it fed a great crowd. The father is not going to welcome his son home with a quiet cup of tea. He wants the whole village to come, and to know.

And over all of it, that sentence. This my son was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found. It is the same pair of words as the shepherd and the woman, lost and found, but now there is a new one, dead and alive, and I do not think that is by accident. The Church has always heard in it something greater than a family story. But for tonight, let us just stay in the courtyard, with the servants running, and the smell of roasting beginning to drift out over the wall.

"And they began to make merry."

[pause]

* * *

I wonder what the servant boy thought.

Luke does not give us a servant boy, but there must have been some, and I like to imagine a young one, who was sent running with the message, and who had been in that house only a year or so, and who had always heard the master spoken of as a quiet, careful man. And now he was being told to fetch the best robe, and he could not quite believe his ears.

That is not in Luke, and I say so plainly. It is only what I think a boy of that age might have felt, seeing a calm old man go suddenly out of his mind with joy. But I think that the servants, who had been in the house for the whole of the long waiting, were glad too. They had seen the doorway. They had seen the evenings. They knew exactly what this day was.

And so the house fills with noise. Someone is lighting fires. Someone is running to the neighbours. A woman is rolling out dough, and complaining happily about the short notice. Somewhere a pipe has begun, and a drum, and there is the sound of feet on the courtyard stones, which is the beginning of a dance.

And the younger son sits at the table, washed, in a clean robe, with a ring on his finger that he is looking at as if it belonged to somebody else, and a plate in front of him that is piled so high he does not know where to begin. And he is, for the first time in a very long while, not hungry. I think he is crying a little, and I think his father pretends not to notice.

That should be the end. It would be a perfectly good ending. Lost, found, party. We have heard the same shape twice already in this chapter, with the sheep and the coin.

But Jesus does not stop there. Remember what he said at the beginning. There was a man who had two sons.

[pause]

* * *

Now we must go out into the fields.

"Now his elder son was in the field," Luke says. Of course he was. He has been there all day. He is the son who stayed, and whose life, for all these years, has been work: early mornings, and the weather, and the accounts, and the animals, and doing the share of two people because one of them had left. And at the end of the day, tired, and probably looking forward to his supper, he comes walking back towards the house.

And as he draws near, he hears music and dancing.

It is a strange thing, to come home to a party you were not told about. He stops, I think, at the edge of the courtyard, and listens, and does not like it. And he calls over one of the servants, and asks, rather stiffly, what is going on. And the servant, who is full of the news, says, "Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fattened calf, because he has received him back safe and sound."

Because he has received him back safe and sound. I do not think the servant meant any harm. He thought it was wonderful news, and he wanted to share it. But you can see it landing on the elder brother like a stone.

Luke says, "But he was angry, and refused to go in."

Now I do not want you to be too hard on him. Let us be fair. He has a point, or he thinks he has. He has done everything right. And now the one who did everything wrong is being given a ring, and the calf that he, the elder, helped to raise, and perhaps could have had one small goat from, on any day of the last ten years, for a party with his friends. It feels unfair. And it is very, very human to feel that way.

And the father comes out to him.

I hope you caught that. We have already seen the father run down the road to the younger son. Now, when the elder will not come in, the father goes out. Luke says, "his father came out and entreated him." Entreated: begged, pleaded, spoke gently. He leaves his own feast, with all the guests waiting and the music playing, to stand in the dark of the field edge and plead with the son who stayed. He is as tender with the one who is angry as he was with the one who was lost.

And the elder son lets it all out.

"Look," he says, "these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command, yet you never gave me a young goat, that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours came, who has devoured your property with prostitutes, you killed the fattened calf for him!"

Listen to the words he uses. I have served you. Not, I have worked beside you. Served, like a slave. And your command, as if the farm were a barracks. And this son of yours, not my brother. He cannot bring himself to say it. For all those years in the house he has been, in his own heart, a hired man, working for wages that were never paid, and he has been quietly keeping the account. And the bitterest thing is that he is not entirely wrong.

And the father's answer is the most tender speech in the whole parable.

"Son," he says. Child. "You are always with me, and all that is mine is yours."

All that is mine is yours. He did not need to ask for a goat. He did not need to earn a ring. It was his already, every day, all along. The elder son had been living beside a treasure and had thought of himself as a servant. And the father goes on, gently: "It was fitting to make merry and be glad, for this your brother was dead, and is alive. He was lost, and is found."

Your brother. He gives the boy back his brother, in a single word, just as, a minute ago, he gave the other one back his name.

[pause]

* * *

And then Jesus stops.

I want you to notice that, because it is surprising. Luke does not tell us what the elder son did. Did he go in? Did he stand in the dark and fold his arms? Did he turn, and see his brother through the lit doorway, and take a step? The parable simply ends, with the father's hand held out and the music still playing in the house, and with the answer left open.

And I think that is deliberate. Remember who Jesus is talking to. He is talking to the Pharisees and scribes, who are standing at the edge of the crowd, grumbling that he eats with sinners. They are the elder brother. And Jesus does not tell them how the story comes out, because they have not finished it yet. The door is open, and the father is pleading, and it is for them to decide whether to go in.

And I suspect it is for all of us, in a way. Many of us have been the younger son, at one time or another, on a road we wished we had not taken. And many of us have been the elder, standing outside a kindness we thought was unfair. And the father in the story is the same in both. He runs to the one and he goes out to the other. In neither case does he wait for them to be good enough.

[pause]

Let me say what I think happened after, and I shall say plainly that it is mine, not Luke's.

I think that the music went on until quite late. I think the neighbours stayed and sang, and that the calf was eaten, down to the last bone. I think that at some point the elder brother, who had been standing a long way back in the shadows, was seen to come in. Perhaps not at once. Perhaps he stood there a long time. But I like to think that his father took him by the arm, and that at some point in the evening the two brothers found themselves in the same corner of the courtyard, with nothing to say. And I like to think that one of them said, "Pass the bread," and the other did.

That is as far as I dare go. The Gospel does not tell us. But I think a story that ends with someone passing the bread is not a bad one to take to bed.

* * *

Now let us let the evening come down.

The guests are going home. The last of the singing is fading down the lane. The lamps in the courtyard are being put out one by one, and the dogs, who have had a very good day, are asleep by the wall. The servants have swept the floor. The fire is a heap of red.

In the house, a young man is asleep in a bed he did not think he would see again. He has the ring on his finger still, and he has not taken it off, and he will wake once in the night to check that it is there. His clean robe is folded on the chest. His old cloak is in a corner, where somebody will quietly take it away in the morning. The sandals stand together at the door, side by side, a good pair.

In the next room, an older brother lies awake for a while, looking at the ceiling, thinking about a word his father said, and turning it over and over like a stone in his pocket. Your brother. He is not at peace yet. But he is in the house.

And on the roof, in the cool of the night, an old man sits for a moment under the stars, with his hands in his lap, and for the first time in a long time he is not looking at the road. There is nobody on it. Both his sons are indoors, and the door is shut, and the whole world seems quiet.

I think that is the picture Jesus wants us to take away. A father with nothing left to wait for, because everyone is home. And it is an invitation, I think, to each of us, whichever son we feel like tonight. There is a door, and there is a light on, and there is someone who has been watching the road a long while. There is nothing to be rehearsed. The speech is not needed. There is only the road, and the first step on it, and then a sound of running.

Let the lamp go down now. Let the house be quiet. Let whatever you are carrying tonight be set down at the door, with the old cloak and the dusty shoes, and let it be taken away, quietly, in the morning.

[pause]

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Gospel according to Luke, chapter fifteen, verses eleven to thirty-two, which is Scripture in the Catholic and Orthodox churches. It is a parable, a story that Jesus told, and it is the third in a row, after the lost sheep and the lost coin, in a chapter that Luke opens by saying that tax collectors and sinners drew near to listen and the Pharisees and scribes complained. We have kept that frame, because it shapes the meaning of the story, especially its ending.

What we have kept from Luke: the man with two sons; the younger son's request for his share; the father dividing the property; the journey to a far country; the loose living; the famine; the work feeding pigs; the longing for the pods; coming to himself; the rehearsed words, Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; the father seeing him a long way off; the compassion and the running; the embrace and the kiss; the best robe, the ring, the sandals and the fattened calf; this my son was dead and is alive again, he was lost and is found; the music and dancing; the elder son in the field, his question to a servant, his anger and refusal to go in; the father coming out and pleading; the elder son's complaint about the young goat; the father's answer, you are always with me and all that is mine is yours; your brother; and the ending, which Luke leaves open.

What we flagged as a reading or as later tradition. That the younger son's share was one third and the elder's two thirds follows the usual understanding of Deuteronomy twenty-one, but Luke does not give the sums. That the pods were carob pods is the common reading of the Greek word *keratia*, not certain. That the pigs would have been unclean to the hearers follows the Law in Leviticus eleven. The father's running is described as undignified for a man of his age and standing, which is a common observation about the customs of the time, and is not in Luke. The Church Fathers, among them Saint John Chrysostom, Saint Augustine and Saint Gregory of Nyssa, read the parable in many ways, as a story of every sinner's return, and as a story of the elder brother's own need, and we have only touched on that. The name the prodigal, which means wasteful, is a later label and not Luke's. Luke names no one in the story, and neither did we. The Orthodox Church keeps the Sunday of the Prodigal Son as the second Sunday of preparation before Great Lent, and the Catholic Church reads the parable on the Fourth Sunday of Lent in Year C.

What we invented: the particular road, the thoughts of the son on it, the stone at the last bend; the doorway and the father's evenings; the farm and the fence rail; the servant boy; the woman rolling dough; the kitchen and the dogs; the elder brother lying awake; the passing of the bread; and the night at the end. None of it is in Luke, and none of it changes what happens or what it means.

For further reading, the whole of Luke chapter fifteen is short, and is best read at one sitting, so that the sheep, the coin and the sons come one after another, as Jesus meant.

[pause]

That's the story of the father and his two sons for tonight. The Catholic Church reads it at Mass on the Fourth Sunday of Lent in one year of its three, and the Orthodox Church keeps a Sunday for it before Lent begins. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Lord Jesus, who told us of a father who runs, bring home everyone who is far off tonight, and open the door in every heart that is standing outside. Goodnight, and God bless.

It is night in Jerusalem, in the spring, and a man is standing at the bottom of a flight of stone steps with his hand on the wall.

He is not young. His beard has grey in it. His cloak is good wool, the kind a person wears who sits on councils and is listened to when he speaks. Behind him, a young man is holding a little clay lamp, cupping it with one hand, because there is a wind tonight, coming in from somewhere, and it keeps trying to put the flame out.

At the top of the steps there is a door, and light under the door, and the sound of voices.

The older man does not go up.

He stands there with his hand on the wall for a long time. Long enough that the young man with the lamp shifts from one foot to the other, and clears his throat very quietly, the way you do when you want to say something to your master but would never dream of saying it.

Then the wind gusts, and the flame flattens, and nearly goes out, and comes back.

And the older man takes a breath, and puts his foot on the first step.

Let me tell you where we are.

We are in Jerusalem, at Passover. It is early in the story of Jesus, in the Gospel of John, not long after the wedding at Cana, where the water became wine. If you were with us for that one, some while ago, you will remember the six stone jars.

Jerusalem at Passover is full. It is more than full. People have come from all over the country, and from far beyond it, from Egypt and Babylon and the cities of the Greeks, walking for days or weeks to be at the temple for the feast. Every house has guests. Every roof has someone sleeping on it. The streets smell of woodsmoke and roasting lamb and sheep, a great many sheep, and the noise in the daytime is tremendous. If you have ever tried to find a parking space near a cathedral at Christmas, you have some idea. Now take away the cars and add the sheep.

And a few days before our story, something has happened in the temple that everybody is talking about.

The Gospel of John tells it in the chapter just before ours. In the great outer court of the temple, there were people selling oxen and sheep and pigeons, for the sacrifices, and money-changers sitting at their tables. And Jesus made a whip out of cords and drove them all out, the sheep and the oxen with them, and poured out the coins of the money-changers, and overturned their tables. And he said to the people selling pigeons, Take these things away. You shall not make my Father's house a house of trade.

You can imagine the talk. You can imagine the talk among the people who looked after the temple most of all.

And the Gospel says one more thing, right at the end of that chapter. It says that while Jesus was in Jerusalem at the Passover feast, many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he did. We are not told what all the signs were. Only that there were signs, and that people saw them, and that people were talking.

Now let me tell you about our man.

His name is Nicodemus. It is a Greek name, which was not unusual among Jews at the time; plenty of people had a Greek name, or two names, one for the family and one for the wider world. It means something like victory of the people, which is a grand name, and I hope he lived up to it. I think, in the end, he did, though not in the way anybody would have expected.

The Gospel tells us three things about him in one sentence. He was a Pharisee. He was a ruler of the Jews. And, a little later, Jesus calls him the teacher of Israel.

Let us take those one at a time, because they matter.

A Pharisee. People sometimes hear that word and think it simply means a bad person, a hypocrite. That is not fair, and it is not what the word meant. The Pharisees were a movement of devout, serious, learned Jews who wanted to keep God's law faithfully in every part of ordinary life, not just in the temple but at the kitchen table, at work, in the market, at the well. They studied. They argued. They loved the Scriptures. Saint Paul was one, and said so. Jesus argued with Pharisees a great deal, it is true, but you mostly argue with the people who take the same things seriously that you do. Nobody bothers arguing with people who do not care.

A ruler of the Jews. This very probably means that Nicodemus was a member of the great council in Jerusalem, the Sanhedrin, the body of elders and priests and teachers that met near the temple and decided serious matters. So he was not just a learned man. He was an important one. He had a seat. People stood up when he came into a room.

And the teacher of Israel. When Jesus says it, there is a little smile in it, I think, as we shall see. But it tells us what Nicodemus was known for. He taught. Younger men sat at his feet and learned the law from him. He was the person other people came to with their questions.

So you have to picture this. Here is a man who has answers. Here is a man whose whole life is answers. And he has a question of his own, and nobody to take it to.

Except the young teacher from Galilee who has just turned over the tables in the temple.

* * *

The Gospel does not tell us anything about the evening before Nicodemus went out. So this part is ours.

Let us imagine a house in the upper part of the city, a good house, built round a little courtyard, with a fig tree in a corner of the courtyard. Let us imagine a young man of the household, about nineteen, whose name is Tobiah. He is not a scholar. He looks after the lamps and the doors and runs errands, and he is extremely good at knowing where everybody in the house is at any moment, which is a talent all of its own.

And tonight, after the evening meal, when the guests have gone to their places on the roof and the house is quiet, Tobiah finds his master standing in the courtyard under the fig tree, in his cloak, as if he is about to go out.

Which is strange, because it is dark, and nobody important goes out in Jerusalem after dark if they can help it.

Master, Tobiah says. Is something wrong?

No, says Nicodemus. Then, after a moment: Fetch a lamp. A small one. And your cloak. You'll come with me.

Where are we going?

To a house in the lower city. I have been told where he is staying.

Tobiah does not have to ask who he is. Everybody in Jerusalem this week knows who he is.

Now, why at night? This is the first great question about Nicodemus, and people have been asking it for a very long time, and I want to be honest with you that the Gospel does not say.

Many of the old writers thought it was fear. Saint Augustine, preaching on this chapter in North Africa sixteen hundred years ago, thought Nicodemus came by night because he was not yet ready to be seen coming, that he was still in the dark himself, and was coming towards the light. That is a lovely reading, and the Gospel itself plays with light and dark all the way through this chapter, as we will hear.

Others have suggested something gentler. Perhaps he simply wanted a proper conversation. In the daytime Jesus was surrounded by crowds. At night, the crowds went home. And teachers in that time and place often did their deepest studying at night, when the day's work was done. Perhaps he wanted to talk teacher to teacher, without a hundred people listening and taking sides.

It may have been a bit of both. Most things we do are a bit of both.

So they go out. Tobiah walks in front with the lamp, and Nicodemus walks behind him, with his hood up.

The streets of Jerusalem at night are steep, and stepped, and narrow, and very dark. There are no streetlamps. There is a little light from the moon, because Passover always falls near the full moon, so there is that at least, silver on the tops of the walls and black in the alleys below. And there are sounds. A baby crying somewhere. Somebody singing a psalm on a rooftop, a long way off. Sheep, of course. There are always sheep.

And there is the wind.

It comes in gusts, up the narrow streets, from no direction in particular. It tugs at Nicodemus's hood. It sets a loose shutter knocking somewhere above them. And every time it comes, Tobiah has to stop and turn his back and cup his hand round the lamp, and every time, the flame lies down flat, and every time, somehow, it stands up again.

Where does it come from, Tobiah says, at one corner, half to himself, sheltering the flame. There was no wind at all at supper.

Nicodemus does not answer. He is thinking about other things.

And then they come to a house, and a flight of stone steps on the outside of it, going up to a door, with light under the door. And you know what happens next, because I told you at the very beginning. He stands at the bottom for a long time. And then he goes up.

Now. Here is the conversation, from the Gospel of John, chapter three, told straight, in a plain rendering of the old words. I am not going to add anything to what is said. Tobiah, you will be glad to know, waits at the bottom of the steps, wrapped in his cloak, with the lamp beside him in a crack in the wall out of the wind. He does not hear a word of it. Nobody but the two of them, and perhaps the disciples, heard it that night. We have it because it was remembered, and written down, and given to the whole Church.

We are not told where they sat. Painters like to put them on a rooftop, under the stars, with a lamp between them, and I think that is a fine guess, but it is a guess.

Nicodemus begins very politely. He begins, in fact, with a compliment, which is what a learned man does when he is not quite sure how to begin.

Rabbi, he says, we know that you are a teacher come from God. For no one can do these signs that you do, unless God is with him.

Notice the we. We know. He has not come only for himself. Perhaps there are others on the council, or among his students, who are wondering the same things. Or perhaps it is just the way a member of a committee talks. I have known people who say we when they mean I. It feels safer.

And Jesus does not answer the compliment at all. He goes straight past it, to the thing underneath.

Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born anew, he cannot see the kingdom of God.

Now here is something that is very hard to bring across into English, so let me take a moment over it. The word that Jesus uses, the one we translate born anew, can mean two things in the Greek of the Gospel. It can mean born again, a second time. And it can mean born from above. Both at once. Some translations say one, and some say the other, and some put the other one in a little note at the bottom of the page. Jesus, I think, meant both. Born a second time, and born from above, from God.

But Nicodemus hears only the first meaning. And he answers like a teacher who has been handed a riddle and is determined to take it apart.

How can a man be born when he is old? he says. Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb, and be born?

I like Nicodemus very much at this moment. He sounds, to me, like a man who is a bit puzzled, and a bit stubborn, and also, just a little, sad. How can a man be born when he is old. He is old. He knows he is old. He has spent his whole life learning, and here is someone telling him that what he needs is to start again from the very beginning. Can that even be done, at my age?

And Jesus says it again, more fully.

Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Do not marvel that I said to you, You must be born anew.

And then he says something that, if you only remember one thing from tonight, I hope you will remember.

The wind blows where it wills, and you hear the sound of it, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with every one who is born of the Spirit.

Here is another thing that is hard to bring into English. In the Greek of the Gospel, the word for wind and the word for spirit are the same word. And in Hebrew, the language of Nicodemus's Scriptures, it is the same again: one word, ruach, that means wind, and breath, and spirit, all together. So when Jesus says the wind blows where it wills, every word of it is also saying, the Spirit blows where it wills. You can hear it. You can feel it on your face. You can watch what it does to the trees. But you cannot hold it in your hand, or say where it started, or tell it where to go.

And Nicodemus, of all people, would have known his Scriptures well enough to hear the echoes. There is a verse in the Book of Ecclesiastes that says, As you do not know the way of the wind, or how the bones grow in the womb of a mother, so you do not know the work of God, who makes everything. The wind, and being born, in one verse, and the same word there can be read as wind or as spirit. And there is the prophet Ezekiel, who saw a valley full of dry bones, and was told to say, Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live. And there is that same prophet's promise from God: I will sprinkle clean water upon you, and you shall be clean. A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you. Water, and a new spirit. Nicodemus had taught these verses. And now here they were, sitting across a lamp from him.

How can this be? says Nicodemus.

Just that. How can this be. Not an argument any more. Just a question. I think it is the most honest thing he says all night.

And Jesus answers, with that little smile I promised you.

Are you the teacher of Israel, and yet you do not understand this?

I do not think this is unkind. I think it is the kind of thing a friend says. You, of all people. You, who know every page. And yet here you are, and here I am, and you came. You did come.

Then Jesus goes on, and speaks for some time, and what he says is some of the most famous words ever written. Let me give you the heart of it.

He says that he speaks of what he knows, and bears witness to what he has seen. He says that no one has gone up into heaven except the one who came down from heaven, the Son of Man.

And then he says this. And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.

If you were with us last night, you will remember the bronze serpent on the pole. The people bitten in the camp, and told to look up, and those who looked up, lived. Nicodemus would have known that story since he was a little boy. And here is Jesus, telling him that it was a sign of something still to come. Something that would be lifted up. Nicodemus did not know yet what that meant. Neither did anybody else. Keep it in your pocket. We will come back to it.

And then come the words that perhaps more people know by heart than any other verse in the Bible. You may know them yourself.

For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life. For God sent the Son into the world, not to condemn the world, but that the world might be saved through him.

Not to condemn. To save. Hold onto that one too.

And then the chapter turns, at the very end, to light and darkness. Which, for a conversation held at night, by lamplight, with a man who came in the dark, is rather pointed. The light has come into the world, it says, and people loved the darkness rather than the light. But whoever does what is true comes to the light.

Now I should tell you, quietly, that scholars are not all agreed on where exactly Jesus stops speaking in this chapter and the Gospel writer starts reflecting on what he said. The old manuscripts did not have quotation marks. Some think Jesus's words go all the way to the end of the passage. Others think that from For God so loved the world, it is the Gospel writer speaking, drawing out what the conversation meant. Either way, it is Scripture, and the Church reads it all as the word of God. I only mention it so that, if you ever see a Bible with the red letters stopping in a different place from another Bible, you will know why.

And here is the strangest thing about the whole scene. We are not told how it ended.

Nicodemus does not say, I believe. He does not say, I do not. He does not ask another question. The Gospel simply stops telling us about him, and moves on to Jesus going out into the countryside of Judea with his disciples. Nicodemus just fades out of the scene, back into the night where he came from.

As if, perhaps, he had something to think about.

So, back down the steps. This part, again, is ours.

Tobiah has been sitting at the bottom for what feels like most of the night, though it is probably about an hour. He has heard voices through the door, now and then, but not words. He has watched the moon move from one rooftop to the next. He has relit the lamp twice from a neighbour's brazier, very politely, when the wind got the better of it.

When the door opens at last, and his master comes down, Tobiah stands up quickly, and picks up the lamp, and waits to be told it is time to go.

But Nicodemus does not say anything at all. He just nods, and they set off.

They go back up through the city the way they came. Up the steps, along the narrow streets, past the doors and the dark windows. And the wind is still there, coming and going in gusts, from no direction at all.

At one corner it catches them full on, and Tobiah stops and turns his back and shelters the flame with his hand, as he has done all night. And he says, without thinking, the same thing he said on the way down.

Where does it come from?

And his master, who has not said a word since they left the house, stops beside him in the street.

And for a moment Tobiah thinks he is going to be told off, for chattering.

But Nicodemus only stands there, with the wind pulling at his hood, and his face turned up, a little, towards the rooftops. And then he says, very quietly, I don't know. I don't think anybody knows.

And then, after a while: But you can hear it. Can't you.

Yes, says Tobiah, rather puzzled. You can hear it.

And they walk home.

I want to be honest that this is the part of our story where we have guessed the most. The Gospel tells us nothing about what Nicodemus thought on the way home, or whether he slept, or what he said to anybody. All it tells us is what he did later. And what he did later tells us, I think, that the conversation stayed with him. That he carried it with him, the way you carry a seed in your pocket, for a long time, without being sure what it is going to grow into.

Because the next time we see him, it is about six months later. And he is about to do something brave.

* * *

It is autumn now, and Jerusalem is full again, this time for the Feast of Tabernacles, the Feast of Booths.

This is one of the happiest feasts of the whole year. It comes at the end of the harvest, when the grapes are in and the olives are coming. And for seven days, everyone lives in little shelters made of branches, on rooftops and in courtyards and in the streets, to remember the years in the wilderness when the people of Israel lived in tents, and the Lord was with them. You can see the stars through the roof of a booth. That is meant to be part of it.

The old Jewish writings, in the part called the Mishnah, describe two great ceremonies of this feast as they were done at the temple. Every morning, a priest went down with a golden pitcher to the pool of Siloam, at the bottom of the city, and drew water, and carried it up, with trumpets, and poured it out at the altar, praying for the rains of the coming year. And at night, in the Court of the Women, great golden lamps were lit, so tall that young priests had to climb ladders to fill them, and the old writings say there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that was not lit by their light. And holy men danced with torches in their hands, and the Levites sang the psalms, all night long.

Water, and light. Do you hear the echoes? Keep them in your pocket too.

Because on the last day of that feast, the great day, the Gospel of John tells us that Jesus stood up in the temple and cried out, If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. And the Gospel adds, so that we cannot miss it, that he said this about the Spirit.

Now, by this time, the mood in Jerusalem has changed. People are arguing about Jesus everywhere. Some say he is a good man. Some say he is leading people astray. Some say he is the prophet, or even the Christ. And some of the leaders have decided that something must be done. So they send the officers of the temple, the temple guard, to arrest him.

And the officers go. And they find Jesus teaching in the temple courts. And they stand at the edge of the crowd, the way people who have been sent to arrest someone tend to do, waiting for the right moment.

And they listen.

And then they come back to the chief priests and the Pharisees without him.

Why did you not bring him? they are asked.

And the officers say, No man ever spoke like this man.

I love those officers. They are not theologians. They are not on the council. They are the men who are paid to stand at doors and keep order. And they went out to bring in a prisoner, and stood at the back of the crowd, and listened, and came back empty-handed, and they cannot entirely explain why. No man ever spoke like this man. That is all they have got. It is a very good thing to have got.

The council is not pleased. Are you led astray, you also? they say. Have any of the authorities, or of the Pharisees, believed in him?

Have any of the authorities, or of the Pharisees, believed in him.

And there, in the room, sitting in his place, is a man who is both an authority and a Pharisee. A man who went up a flight of stone steps by night, six months ago, and sat across a lamp from Jesus, and heard about the wind.

The Gospel says it very carefully. Nicodemus, who had gone to him before, and who was one of them, said to them,

Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing and learning what he does?

That is all he says.

It is not a speech. It is not, I believe in him. It is a question, a teacher's question, a lawyer's question, about fairness and the law. Surely, he says, we ought to hear the man before we judge him. Surely that is what our own law requires.

But I want you to think about how much it cost. He was in a room full of the most important men in his world, his colleagues, his friends perhaps, and they were angry, and they had just asked, out loud, whether anybody in their own number was foolish enough to be taken in by this Galilean. And into that silence, Nicodemus, alone, said, we should give him a hearing.

And they turned on him at once.

Are you from Galilee too? they said. Search and see, no prophet is to rise from Galilee.

Which is the kind of thing people say when they have no answer to your question, so they make fun of where you come from instead.

And that was all. The council did not change its mind. Nicodemus did not win the argument. The Gospel does not tell us that he said anything else. He asked one fair question, and was laughed at for it, and the meeting moved on.

But he asked it.

And somewhere, perhaps, a seed that had been sitting in a pocket for six months moved, a very little, in the dark.

Now we come to the last scene, and I am going to slow down, because it is getting late, and because this is a quiet scene, and it should be told quietly.

It is spring again. It is Passover again, about two years after that night with the lamp. And it is Friday afternoon.

I am not going to tell you about the trial, or the Cross itself, tonight. You know what happened. Jesus was lifted up, as he had said, on a hill outside the city wall. And in the middle of the afternoon he said, It is finished, and bowed his head, and gave up his spirit.

And now it is late in the day, and the sun is going down towards the hills, and the Sabbath begins at sunset. And it is a special Sabbath, the Sabbath of the Passover. There is very little time.

The Gospel of John tells us what happened next.

There was a man called Joseph, from a town called Arimathea. The other Gospels tell us more about him. Saint Mark says he was a respected member of the council, who was himself looking for the kingdom of God. Saint Luke says he was a good and righteous man, who had not agreed with what the council decided. And Saint John says he was a disciple of Jesus, but secretly, because he was afraid.

So here is another one. Another member of the council. Another man who had been coming towards the light quietly, without letting anyone see.

And Joseph, Saint Mark says, took courage, and went to Pilate, the Roman governor, and asked for the body of Jesus. And Pilate gave him leave.

And then the Gospel of John says this.

Nicodemus also, who had at first come to him by night, came bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds' weight.

Who had at first come to him by night. That is how the Gospel remembers him, even here, at the end. The man who came by night. As if to say, you remember him. You remember where he started.

And now look where he has come to.

He is not coming by night any more. He is coming in the daylight, the last of the daylight, to the foot of a Roman cross, in front of anyone who cares to look, to claim the body of a man the council has condemned. Any member of the council would know exactly what that meant, and what people would say.

And he does not come with a little. A hundred pounds. The pounds they used then were a little lighter than ours, so it is perhaps seventy of our pounds, about thirty-three kilograms. It is still an enormous amount. It is more than one man could easily carry; he must have had servants with him. Perhaps, I like to think, a young man of his household, now a little older, with a strong back.

Myrrh is a sweet, bitter resin from a thorny tree in Arabia and Africa, the same myrrh, you may remember, that the wise men brought to Bethlehem. Aloes here is probably not the plant you put on a sunburn, but a fragrant wood, ground fine, though scholars are not completely sure. Together, in that quantity, it is the kind of burial you would give to a king.

Nobody spends that much on a man they think was simply a mistake.

And the two of them, Joseph and Nicodemus, two members of the council, two men who had been afraid, took the body of Jesus down, and wound it in linen cloths with the spices, the Gospel says, as is the burial custom of the Jews.

I am not going to describe it any more than that. Only that it was done gently. Only that it was done by men who had sat in their places on the council and listened to others decide, and who now, at the very end, when it was too late to change anything, did the one thing that was left to do, and did it with their own hands.

Now in the place where he was crucified, the Gospel says, there was a garden. And in the garden a new tomb, where no one had yet been laid. And because it was the day of Preparation, and the tomb was close at hand, they laid Jesus there.

A garden. Of course there was a garden. The whole story of the world started in a garden.

And Joseph, the other Gospels tell us, rolled a great stone against the door of the tomb, and went away. And some of the women who had followed Jesus from Galilee were sitting nearby, opposite the tomb, watching, so that they would know the place.

And then the sun set. And the Sabbath began. And all over Jerusalem, in every house, the Sabbath lamps were lit.

And what became of him after that, the Gospel does not say. Not a word. He is never mentioned again in Scripture.

But people wondered, of course, as people always do. And stories grew up.

About four hundred years later, in the year four hundred and fifteen, a priest called Lucian, from a village not far from Jerusalem, wrote a letter saying that he had seen an old man in a dream, who told him that he was Gamaliel, the great teacher of the law who appears in the Acts of the Apostles. And the old man in the dream said that he had buried the first martyr, Saint Stephen, on his own land, and that Nicodemus was buried there too, and his own son as well. And when they dug, the letter says, they found tombs. That is the story behind the Orthodox Church remembering Nicodemus on the second of August, with Saint Stephen. It is an old and much-loved story, but it rests on a dream told in a letter, three and a half centuries after Nicodemus lived, and we should hold it gently, as a story, not as history.

There is also an old book called the Gospel of Nicodemus, written down some centuries after the Gospels, with a long account of the trial of Jesus, and of his going down to the dead. It was very popular in the Middle Ages and gave painters a great many pictures. But it is not Scripture, in either Church, and nobody thinks Nicodemus wrote it.

And in the old Jewish writings, in the Talmud, there is a very wealthy man of Jerusalem called Nakdimon son of Gurion, who is remembered for a story about water, of all things. It is said that one year, when the pilgrims coming to the feast had nothing to drink, he borrowed twelve wells of water for them from a foreign official, and promised to pay it back, and that when the day came to repay it and the wells were still empty, he went into the temple and prayed, and the rain came. Is he our Nicodemus? Some people have wondered. Nobody knows. It could be a different man entirely. But it is a lovely thing to think about on a night about water and wind.

And the garden? The garden is still there, in a way.

That tomb, and the hill where the Cross stood, are inside one great old church in Jerusalem now, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the church of the Resurrection, which the Catholic and Orthodox Churches look after together, along with other ancient Christian communities. Pilgrims have gone there for many centuries, holding candles. If you go round to the back of it, in a small, dark chapel, there are some very old rock-cut tombs, much older than the church, which by long tradition are called the tomb of Joseph of Arimathea. Whether that is right, nobody can say for certain. But it is a good place to stand for a moment and remember the two men from the council who came, at the very end, to do the last thing that could be done.

And every year, in the Orthodox Church, on the third Sunday of Easter, counting Easter Day itself, there is a Sunday for the women who brought spices to the tomb. And on that Sunday, Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus are remembered alongside them. The men who carried the myrrh on Friday, and the women who came with more of it on Sunday morning, and found they did not need it.

And whenever a baby, or a grown-up, or an old man, for that matter, is baptised, in a Catholic or an Orthodox church, the words that Jesus said to Nicodemus that night are close by. Born of water and the Spirit. Both Churches have always read them that way. So in a sense, that conversation by lamplight, which only two people heard, has been going on ever since, in every font in the world.

Now, a few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Gospel of John. The night conversation is in chapter three, verses one to twenty-one. The scene in the council at the Feast of Tabernacles is in chapter seven, verses forty-five to fifty-two, and Jesus crying out about living water is a few verses before, in the same chapter. The burial is in chapter nineteen, verses thirty-eight to forty-two, and the linen cloths in the empty tomb are in chapter twenty. What Saint Mark, Saint Matthew and Saint Luke say about Joseph of Arimathea and the stone and the women watching comes from their own accounts of the burial. All of that is told straight, in a plain rendering.

The Gospel does not say where the night conversation happened, or why Nicodemus came by night. Saint Augustine's reading, that he came in the dark because he was still in the dark himself, is from his sermons on this chapter, and Saint John Chrysostom preached on it too. The Feast of Tabernacles ceremonies, the water from Siloam and the great lamps, are described in the Mishnah. Exactly where Jesus's words to Nicodemus end and the Gospel writer's reflection begins is not agreed, and we have said so. The weight of a hundred of their pounds is an estimate, and so is what the aloes were.

The story of Nicodemus's burial beside Saint Stephen comes from Lucian's letter about a dream, and is told as legend. The Gospel of Nicodemus is a much later book that neither Church reads as Scripture. Nakdimon son of Gurion is in the Talmud, and nobody knows if he is the same man.

Tobiah, the lamp, the walk through the streets, the waiting at the foot of the steps, the wind on the way home, the fig tree in the courtyard, and every word that Tobiah and Nicodemus say to each other are our own invention. The Gospel does tell us there was night, and Passover, and later a garden; we have not added anything to what Jesus, Nicodemus, Joseph or the council say or do.

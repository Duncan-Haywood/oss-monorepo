Here is how the book puts it, and it does not mince its words. And David says no.

You shall not do so, my brothers, he says, with what the Lord has given us. He kept us safe, and he gave the raiders into our hands. Who is going to listen to you about this? * * *

Let us think about that, because it is a very good rule, and a very surprising one.

First, notice what David says it is. It is what the Lord has given us. Not what we won. Not what we fought for. What God gave. If it is a gift, then nobody earned it, and if nobody earned it, then nobody can say they earned more. That is the whole argument, in one sentence.

There was an older rule, in the Law of Moses, that said something like it. In the Book of Numbers, after one battle, the spoil was split in two halves, one for the soldiers who went out and one for all the rest of the people. So David was not making it up out of nothing. But he made it plainer and more generous. Not half and half. Alike.

And second, notice who the two hundred were. They were not shirkers. They were men who gave everything they had, and their legs gave out. And they did not just sit there. They guarded the baggage. Somebody had to. If everyone had gone on, the army's supplies would have been left lying in the desert for any passing raider to pick up.

There is a poet, John Milton, who went blind in the middle of his life. And he wrote a sonnet about how frustrating it was, not to be able to work the way he used to. And the sonnet ends with a line that has comforted a great many people since. They also serve who only stand and wait.

The two hundred at the brook Besor would have understood that line.

Saint Paul said something similar, about the Church being like a body. The eye cannot say to the hand, I don't need you. And the parts of the body that seem to be weaker are the ones we cannot do without.

And Our Lord told a story about workers in a vineyard, where some were hired at dawn and some at the very last hour of the day, and the owner paid them all the same. And the ones who had worked all day grumbled. And the owner said, am I not allowed to be generous with what is mine?

David, at the brook Besor, was generous with what was not even his. He said it was the Lord's. And he gave it away alike.

Nobody is sure, now, where Ziklag stood. There are some old mounds out on that southern edge, where the farmland meets the desert, and archaeologists dig in them and write papers and disagree. Perhaps one day someone will find a burnt layer, and a bit of an oven, and say, this is it.

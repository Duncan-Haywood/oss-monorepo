Imagine a small stone room on an island, a long time ago.

It is night. There is one oil lamp on the table, and a window with the shutter half open, and outside the window you can hear the wind going through the olive trees. Somewhere far off, a dog is barking at nothing in particular, the way dogs do.

At the table sits a young man with ink on his fingers. In front of him is a long strip of parchment, covered in small careful writing. Across the table, in a chair with a blanket over his knees, sits an old man with a white beard. The old man is reading aloud from a wax tablet, slowly, one line at a time, and the young man is writing the lines down.

After a while the young man stops. He puts his pen down. He counts something on his fingers. Then he counts it again.

"Father," he says. "Do you know how many verses this is now?"

"I have a rough idea," says the old man.

"It is more than two hundred. And we are still only in the Old Testament."

The old man smiles. "Then you had better sharpen another pen," he says.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. Last time, we were in Constantinople with Saint Romanos, the deacon who could not sing very well, and who ended up writing the hymns the whole city sang. If you missed that one, here is the short version. In the five hundreds, the great sung sermon in the churches of the Greek-speaking world was called a kontakion. It was a long poem in many verses, with a refrain the people sang back. Romanos was the master of it.

Tonight we are going to move on about a hundred and fifty years, and meet the man who is often called the first great writer of the thing that came next. It is called a canon. Do not worry. I will explain what a canon is. There will be no test at the end. Although if there were, I think you would do very well.

Our man is called Andrew. Today he is known as Saint Andrew of Crete, because that is where he was a bishop for most of his later life. But he was not born on Crete. He was born a long way east, in the city of Damascus, in Syria, in about the year six hundred and sixty.

Damascus is one of the oldest cities in the world that people have never stopped living in. It sits on a green plain beside the desert, watered by a river that comes down out of the mountains. It had orchards and gardens and markets and a great many very old streets. One of them, you may remember from another episode, is the street called Straight, where Saint Paul waited, blind, for three days, until a man called Ananias came to lay hands on him.

The world had changed a great deal in Damascus not long before Andrew was born. A few decades earlier, the city had been part of the Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople. Now it was ruled by the new Arab caliphs. In fact, just about the time Andrew was a baby, Damascus became the capital of the whole caliphate, which made it, for a while, one of the most important cities on earth.

But the Christians of Damascus did not vanish. They were still there, in large numbers, with their churches and their bishops and their monks, and many of them had jobs in the government. One of the most famous Christian families in the city worked for the caliphs as officials. And a boy from that family, born a few years after Andrew, would grow up to be Saint John of Damascus, another great writer of hymns. So if you had been walking through the streets of Damascus in about six hundred and seventy, you might, without knowing it, have passed two little boys who would both one day write songs that people still sing in church every year. I find that a very nice thought. I like to imagine them as boys, arguing about whose turn it is with a ball. But the sources do not say they ever met, so I will leave it there.

Andrew's parents, the old Life says, were called George and Gregoria. They were Christians, and good ones. And they had a problem.

* * *

The problem was that their son did not speak.

I should say at once that this part of the story comes from the old Greek Life of Andrew, which was written some time after he died, by a man called Niketas. It is the kind of thing that later writers loved to tell about saints, and we cannot check it. So we will tell it as the Life tells it.

The Life says that the little boy Andrew could not speak at all. Not a word. Not when he was one, not when he was three, not when he was five. He could hear. He could understand. He could, I imagine, point very firmly at things he wanted. But no words came out.

Now, you can picture how his parents must have felt. Every parent waits for the first word. Some children say "mama". Some say "dada". I have heard of a child whose first word was "tractor", which I think is very sensible. And his parents waited, and waited, and nothing came.

And then, the Life says, when he was about seven years old, his parents took him to church, and he received Holy Communion. And after that, he spoke.

That is all the old story says about it. It does not say what his first words were, which I think is a great shame. But it does say what it thought the story meant. The boy who would one day give the Church one of its greatest prayers was given his voice at the altar. His first real words came after he had received the Lord.

It is a lovely story. Whether it happened just like that, we do not know. But what we do know is that once Andrew started talking, he did not stop. He went to school, and he learned to read and write very well, and he learned Greek grammar and rhetoric, which was the art of speaking beautifully, and he learned the Scriptures. And by the time he was a teenager, it was clear to everybody that this was a young man who loved words, and who loved God, and who was going to put the two together.

* * *

When Andrew was about fourteen or fifteen, his parents took him to Jerusalem.

Jerusalem, in those days, was also under the caliphs. But it was still a city full of Christian churches and monasteries, and at the heart of it was the great church of the Holy Sepulchre, built over the place where the Lord was crucified, and buried, and rose again. Pilgrims came from everywhere to pray there. And the church had its own clergy, and its own monks, and its own offices full of people writing letters.

Andrew was taken into the service of that church. The old Life says he was given the tonsure there, the little cutting of the hair that marked a young man as dedicated to God. And because he could write so well, he was given a job that suited him perfectly. He became a secretary.

At that time, the church of Jerusalem did not have a patriarch on the throne. The office was being looked after by a priest called Theodore, who kept the place, as it were, until a new patriarch could be chosen. And Theodore needed someone to write his letters, and keep his records, and copy out documents, and generally be the clever young person in the office who knows where everything is.

That person was Andrew.

I like to picture him there. A young man in a black robe, in a small room near the great church, surrounded by scrolls and inkpots. Outside, pilgrims from every country under the sun are shuffling past, speaking every language you can think of. Inside, Andrew is writing a careful letter about grain supplies for the monasteries, or the repair of a roof, or a dispute about which monk gets which cell.

It was not glamorous. But it was a very good place to learn. He was surrounded by the old prayers of Jerusalem, sung every day at the holiest places in the world. And he was surrounded by stories.

One of those stories, I think, he must have heard. A generation before Andrew, there had been a patriarch of Jerusalem called Sophronius. And to Sophronius, tradition gives a famous little book: the Life of Saint Mary of Egypt. If you have heard our episode about her, you will know the story. A woman who lived a very wild life in Alexandria, who came to Jerusalem on pilgrimage almost by accident, who could not get through the door of the church until she had prayed to the Mother of God, and who then went out across the Jordan into the desert, with three loaves of bread, and stayed there for nearly fifty years, praying.

Hold on to Mary of Egypt. She is going to come back at the end of our story, in a way that I think Andrew would have liked very much.

* * *

Now we come to Constantinople.

To explain why Andrew went there, I am going to have to tell you about a church council. Please stay awake for this part. I promise it is short, and I promise it matters.

In the year six hundred and eighty and six hundred and eighty-one, the bishops of the Church met at Constantinople for what we now call the Sixth Ecumenical Council. Both the Catholic and the Orthodox Churches count it as one of the great councils of the whole Church.

The question they were meeting about was this. We believe that Jesus Christ is truly God and truly man. Fully divine, and fully human. But does that mean he has a human will as well as a divine will? A human wanting, a human choosing, as well as God's?

Some people had been saying no. They said Christ only had one will, the divine one. It sounded tidy. It sounded respectful. But the council said no to that, and said yes, he has both. A divine will and a real human will, and the human will freely and perfectly says yes to the divine.

And the place they pointed to, among others, was the garden of Gethsemane, on the night before the Lord died. When he prayed, Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. Yet not as I will, but as you will.

There, the council said, you can hear a real human will, a will that does not want to suffer, freely choosing to say yes to the Father. If Christ did not have a human will, then that prayer would not mean what it seems to mean. And if he did not take on every part of being human, including our wanting and our choosing, then he would not have healed every part of us.

That is the whole council, in a nutshell. I hope it did not hurt.

Now, the church of Jerusalem had not been able to send its own people to the council. And so, a few years afterwards, in about six hundred and eighty-five, Theodore in Jerusalem decided to send a small delegation to Constantinople to say, officially, that Jerusalem agreed with what the council had decided. And among the people he sent was his young secretary. Andrew.

So off he went. Damascus, then Jerusalem, and now the greatest city of the Christian world. He was about twenty-five years old.

And he never went back.

* * *

We do not know exactly why he stayed in Constantinople. Perhaps someone important heard him speak and said, we need this young man here. Perhaps he simply loved the city. Perhaps the road home was difficult. But stay he did, and he was ordained a deacon, and not just any deacon. He became a deacon of Hagia Sophia, the great church of the Holy Wisdom, the enormous domed church that the emperor Justinian had built in the time of Romanos.

If you have ever seen a picture of Hagia Sophia, you will know how big it is. Even today, more than fourteen hundred years after it was built, people walk in and go very quiet and look up and forget to close their mouths. Andrew would have served there, in the great services, with the light coming down through the windows around the dome.

But that was not his main job. His main job, the old sources tell us, was running an orphanage.

The church in Constantinople ran a great many charitable houses. There were hospitals, and hostels for travellers, and homes for the old, and homes for children who had nobody. And Andrew was put in charge of an orphanage, and of a home for old people as well.

Now, this is where I would like to introduce you to two people who did not exist.

The first is a woman called Anna. She is the orphanage cook. She is about fifty, she has forearms like a blacksmith, and she has been feeding orphans for thirty years. She has seen a great many deacons come and go, and she is not easily impressed.

The second is a boy called Leo. He is eight years old when Andrew arrives. He has been at the orphanage since he was a baby. Nobody knows who his parents were. He is small and quick, and he is the sort of boy who is always found somewhere he is not supposed to be.

The first time Leo meets Andrew, it is in the kitchen. Leo is supposed to be at his lessons. Instead he is sitting under Anna's table, eating a piece of bread he has not been given.

Andrew comes in, looking for the cook, to ask about the accounts. He sees a pair of small feet sticking out from under the table.

"Good morning," he says to the feet.

The feet go very still.

"I am the new deacon," says Andrew. "I am told I am in charge here. Which means, I think, that I am supposed to tell you to go back to your lessons."

There is a pause under the table. Then a small voice says, "Are you going to?"

Andrew thinks about it. "Is the bread good?" he asks.

"Very good," says the voice.

"Then finish the bread first," says Andrew. "And then go to your lessons. And tomorrow, come and find me, and I will teach you your letters myself. I have a feeling you will be faster at it than your teachers."

Anna, who has been listening from the doorway with a ladle in her hand, says nothing at all. But that night, there is an extra piece of honey cake on the deacon's plate. Which, if you knew Anna, was the closest thing to a speech of welcome anybody had ever got out of her.

* * *

Andrew stayed in Constantinople for more than twenty years. He served at the great church. He looked after the orphans and the old people. He preached. He wrote.

And Leo, in our story, grew up. He learned his letters, just as Andrew said, and then he learned to write them very beautifully, and by the time he was a young man he had the neatest hand of anyone in the orphanage. Andrew used to say that Leo could copy a whole psalm without a single blot, which is more than he could say for most bishops.

Then, in about the year seven hundred and eleven or twelve, when Andrew was about fifty, he was made a bishop. And not a bishop of somewhere in the city, either. He was sent to be the archbishop of Gortyna, on the island of Crete.

So he packed up his books. And in our story, he went to say goodbye to Anna in the kitchen, and she gave him a large parcel of honey cakes for the voyage and told him not to eat them all on the first day. And then he went to find Leo.

"I need a secretary," he said. "I used to be one. I know how useful they are. Would you like to come to Crete?"

Leo had never been on a ship. He had never seen a mountain. He had never been anywhere except the orphanage and the streets around it.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, please."

* * *

Crete is a long, mountainous island at the bottom of the Aegean Sea, about halfway between Greece and Africa. It has high white mountains, and deep green gorges, and olive trees that are older than anyone can remember, and wild goats that stand on the edges of cliffs and look at you as if you are the strange one.

Gortyna was in the south of the island, in a wide plain. In Roman times, it had been the capital of Crete. It was very, very old. It had a famous wall, covered in old laws carved into the stone, so old that the lines go back and forth, left to right and then right to left, the way an ox ploughs a field. They are still there today, if you go and look, and they are some of the oldest written laws in Europe.

And Gortyna was a Christian place with a long memory. The Church of Crete traces itself back to Saint Titus, who was one of Saint Paul's companions. In the New Testament, there is a short letter from Paul to Titus. In it, Paul says that he left Titus behind in Crete, to put things in order and to appoint elders in every town. And the tradition is that Titus became the first bishop of Crete, and that his church was at Gortyna. There was a great church of Saint Titus there in Andrew's time, and you can still see its ruins.

In that same letter, by the way, Paul quotes an old Cretan poet who said that Cretans are always liars. I want to say very clearly that I do not hold with this. Every Cretan I have ever read about has seemed perfectly honest to me. And I notice the poet was a Cretan himself, which makes the whole thing rather confusing. If a Cretan tells you that all Cretans are liars, is he telling the truth? Philosophers have been arguing about that for a very long time. I suggest we let them.

So Andrew came to Gortyna, and became its archbishop. And from what we know, he was a good one. He built churches, and he built a home for the poor, because he had been running homes for the poor for twenty years and he did not see why he should stop now. He preached on the great feasts. Some of his sermons still survive, especially beautiful ones on the feasts of the Mother of God: on her birth, and on her falling asleep at the end of her life, the feast that the Orthodox call the Dormition. People still read them today.

But before we go on, there is something I have to tell you, because this show tries very hard to tell the truth. And the truth is that soon after Andrew became a bishop, he made a serious mistake.

* * *

In the year seven hundred and eleven, a new emperor came to the throne in Constantinople. His name was Philippikos. And Philippikos had a strong opinion. He was on the side of the people who said that Christ had only one will. He did not like the Sixth Council at all.

So in seven hundred and twelve, he called a synod of bishops, and he had them reject the council. He had its decisions set aside. And a good many bishops went along with it. Some of them, I am sure, were frightened. Some of them were confused. Some of them perhaps thought that it would all blow over.

And Andrew, the old sources tell us, was one of them.

This is a strange thing to think about. Andrew, who as a young man had carried Jerusalem's agreement with that very council all the way to Constantinople. Andrew went along with an emperor who undid it.

We do not know why. The sources do not tell us what he was thinking. I will not pretend to know.

But we do know what happened next. Philippikos did not last long. In seven hundred and thirteen, he was overthrown. A new patriarch, Saint Germanos, came in, who held to the council. And Andrew turned back. He returned to the faith of the Sixth Council, publicly, and stayed in it for the rest of his life.

Now, I could have left this out. Many of the old lives of saints do leave out bits like this. But I wanted to tell you, because of what Andrew is most famous for. And it is this.

He wrote the greatest hymn of repentance in the whole of the Eastern Church.

We do not know when he wrote it. We do not know where. Some people have guessed it was late in his life. Some have guessed earlier. Nobody knows, and I will not pretend that he wrote it because of the synod of seven hundred and twelve. That would be making things up.

But I will say this. Whenever he wrote it, it was written by a man who knew what it was to get something important wrong. And to turn around. And to come home.

* * *

Now I have to keep my promise and tell you what a canon is.

A canon, in the Eastern Church, is a long hymn sung at the morning service. It is built on a very old foundation. For centuries, at the morning prayers, the monks had sung the great songs of the Bible. Not the psalms, but the other songs. The song Moses and the people sang after they crossed the Red Sea. The song of Moses near the end of his life, in the book of Deuteronomy. Hannah's prayer, when she brought little Samuel to the temple. The prayer of the prophet Habakkuk. A song of Isaiah. Jonah's prayer from inside the great fish. The prayer of the three young men in the fiery furnace in the book of Daniel, which was long enough to count as two. And at the end, the songs from the beginning of Saint Luke's Gospel: Mary's Magnificat, and the song of Zechariah.

Nine songs. And at some point, around Andrew's time, people began to write new verses to go with each of those nine songs. Each set of verses was called an ode. Each ode picked up an echo of its old song, and then went off and sang about the feast of the day. Nine odes together made a canon.

So a canon is a bit like a house with nine rooms, where each room has an old window in it from the Bible, and the new verses are the furniture you put inside.

Andrew was one of the first to write canons, and some people would say he was the very first great one. And the one he is most famous for is so long that it is simply called the Great Canon.

How long is long? It has about two hundred and fifty verses. Most canons have perhaps thirty or forty. It is so long that some people think it is the longest hymn still regularly sung in any church anywhere. In our story, it is very long indeed for a young scribe with a sore hand.

* * *

So let us go back to that little room on Crete, where we started.

It is late at night. Leo, who is not a boy any more but a young man of thirty or so, is sitting at the table with his pen. Andrew, who is old now, is sitting with a blanket over his knees, reading from his wax tablet. In our story, he has been working on this hymn for a long time, in the evenings, after the day's work of being an archbishop is done. And now he is reading it out, so that Leo can make a fair copy.

Out in the kitchen, there is a Cretan woman whose name I have decided is also Anna, because, Leo says, all good cooks are called Anna, and he will not hear otherwise. She has left them some bread and olives and a jug of watered wine, and gone to bed.

Andrew reads the first line. In our own plain words, it goes something like this:

Where shall I begin to weep for the things I have done in my life? What first fruit shall I offer you, Lord, for this sorrow of mine?

Leo writes it down. He waits.

Andrew reads on. And the next thing you notice, if you listen, is who he is talking to. He is not talking to a crowd. He is not talking to you or to me. Most of the time, he is talking to his own soul.

Come, my wretched soul, he says. Come with your body, and confess to the maker of everything. And from now on, leave your old foolishness, and bring tears of repentance to God.

And then, verse by verse, he begins to walk through the Bible.

He starts at the very beginning, with Adam and Eve, in the garden. And he says to his soul, you are like Adam. You reached out for the one thing you were told not to take. And you lost the garden.

Leo writes. The lamp flickers. Outside, the wind goes through the olive trees.

Then Cain and Abel. Andrew says to his soul, you have been more like Cain than like Abel. Abel brought God his best. You have offered God the leftovers.

Then Noah, building his boat while everyone laughed. Then Lot's wife, who looked back at the burning city and stood there turned to salt. Andrew says, my soul, do not look back. Do not stand still, looking at what you have left behind. Run for the mountains.

"Father," says Leo, at about this point. "Are you going to go through the whole of the Old Testament?"

"Yes," says Andrew.

"All of it?"

"Most of it."

Leo looks at the pile of fresh parchment. Then he looks at the old man. "What about Leviticus?" he asks.

Andrew laughs out loud. "I will go lightly on Leviticus," he says. "For your sake."

* * *

And so they go on, night after night.

Jacob comes, with his ladder going up to heaven. Andrew says to his soul, you have a ladder too. It goes up, step by step, by doing good and by prayer. And you have hardly put a foot on the first rung.

Joseph comes, sold by his brothers. Moses comes, and the bush that burned and was not burned up. Andrew says, my soul, be like Moses. Take off your sandals. Go near and look.

Then the judges, and the kings. David comes, the shepherd boy who became king, who did a very wicked thing, and who then, when the prophet Nathan told him a story and he understood it was about himself, said, I have sinned against the Lord. And Andrew says to his soul, you have copied David in his sin. Why have you not copied him in his sorrow?

And Elijah comes, carried up to heaven in the chariot of fire. And Elisha with his double share of the spirit. Many of the people we have met in this show over the past few months turn up in the Great Canon, one after another, like old friends coming in at the door.

And after every single verse, there is a short refrain that the people sing back. It goes:

Have mercy on me, O God. Have mercy on me.

Over and over again, two hundred and fifty times or so. Have mercy on me, O God. Have mercy on me.

"Doesn't it get tiring," Leo asks one night, "saying the same thing so many times?"

Andrew thinks about this for a while.

"When you were small," he says, "and you were frightened in the night, and you came to find Anna in the kitchen. Did you say, Anna, I am frightened, just once, and then go away?"

Leo smiles. "No," he says. "I said it about forty times."

"And did she get tired of hearing it?"

"No," says Leo. "She gave me honey cake."

"Well, then," says Andrew. "God is at least as patient as Anna."

Leo writes that down too, in the margin, very small. Then, because he is a careful scribe, he crosses it out, because it is not part of the hymn. But he remembers it.

* * *

And then, at last, they get to the New Testament.

And here something happens to the hymn. If you listen to the whole thing, you can hear it. In the Old Testament part, Andrew keeps saying to his soul, you are like the people who fell. You are like Adam, like Cain, like those who would not listen. It is all rather heavy, I admit.

But in the New Testament part, he starts to say, look. Look who came home.

Zacchaeus comes, the little tax collector, up his tree, who climbed down and gave half his goods to the poor. And Andrew says, be like him. Climb down. Christ is coming to your house.

The woman comes who washed the Lord's feet with her tears and dried them with her hair. And Andrew says, my soul, bring your tears like her.

The son in the parable comes, the one who took his share and wasted it in a far country, and came to himself among the pigs, and walked home. And Andrew says, I have wasted everything, just like him. And then he says to God, but take me back, as the father took his son. Run to me, as he ran.

And the thief comes, the one who hung on a cross next to the Lord, and said, Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom. And heard the answer, today you will be with me in paradise. Andrew says, the thief got in at the last moment. Do not leave me out.

There is one short verse in the canon, a single little hymn that sits in the middle of it, that is sung in a lot of Orthodox churches through the whole of Lent. In our own plain words it goes like this:

My soul, my soul, get up. Why are you sleeping? The end is coming near, and you will be troubled. Wake up, then, and be watchful, so that Christ our God may spare you, who is everywhere and fills all things.

Now, I know. It is a slightly odd thing to tell you at bedtime. Wake up, my soul. You are trying to go to sleep, and here I am saying wake up.

But it does not mean the body. Go to sleep. Your body needs it. It means the other kind of waking. The kind where you notice that God is everywhere, and fills all things, even this room, even your pillow, even the dark. And that he is waiting, like the father at the end of the road, for anybody who turns round.

* * *

That is the Great Canon. And in our story, on the last night, Leo writes the last verse, and puts down his pen, and shakes out his aching hand.

"Two hundred and fifty," he says. "Or thereabouts. I lost count somewhere in Kings."

"Thank you," says Andrew. "I could not have done it without you."

"You could," says Leo. "It would just have had a lot more blots."

The old man laughs. And then the two of them sit for a little while without talking, and listen to the wind in the olive trees, and the dog, still barking at nothing, far away.

[pause]

Andrew lived on for many years as archbishop of Gortyna. The old Life tells one more story of those years, and I will tell it as the Life's report. It says that raiders came to the island by sea, and besieged one of the Cretan towns, and that the people were afraid. And Andrew prayed with them, and the raiders went away, and the town was spared. It does not tell us much more than that, and we cannot check it. But it fits a man who spent his life looking after people who could not look after themselves.

When he was an old man, about eighty, he had to make a journey to Constantinople on church business. We do not know exactly what the business was. And on the way back, sailing home, he fell ill. The ship put in at the island of Lesbos, at a little town called Eresos. And there, the old sources say, he died, in about the year seven hundred and forty. Some sources put it a few years earlier. He was buried there, on Lesbos.

In his last years, a new storm was beginning in the Church, a long quarrel about the holy icons. It would trouble the Church for more than a hundred years. But that is a story for another night, and Andrew was nearly at the end of his road by then.

What happened to the Great Canon?

It became part of the life of the Church. Not just on Crete. Everywhere. And especially in Lent, the forty days before Easter.

Today, in the Orthodox Church, and in the Eastern Catholic Churches that pray in the same way, the Great Canon is sung twice a year, but always in Lent. In the first four evenings of Lent, from Monday to Thursday of the very first week, it is sung in four parts, one part each evening, in a quiet service at night. The churches are dim. The priest comes out in dark robes. The people stand, and every time the refrain comes, have mercy on me, O God, have mercy on me, many of them bow down, or make the sign of the cross, or kneel and touch their foreheads to the floor.

And then, in the fifth week of Lent, on the Thursday, the whole canon is sung all at once, from beginning to end. All two hundred and fifty or so verses. With all the bowing. It takes hours. I am told that on the next morning, there are a great many people walking around with sore knees, and that the coffee is particularly welcome.

And here is the thing I asked you to hold on to.

On that same night, in the middle of the Great Canon, the Church reads aloud the whole Life of Saint Mary of Egypt. The woman from Alexandria who came to Jerusalem, and could not get in at the church door, and prayed to the Mother of God, and went out into the desert across the Jordan to spend the rest of her life in repentance. Later on, other writers added verses to the canon in her honour, and verses in honour of Andrew himself, so that the two of them are sung about together. The young secretary from Jerusalem, who must have grown up hearing her story in the very city where she was converted, ends up sharing a night with her, every single year, in churches all over the world.

[pause]

Saint Andrew of Crete is honoured as a saint in both the Orthodox and the Catholic Church. His feast is on the fourth of July. He is remembered on that day in the Orthodox Church, and in the Roman Martyrology, which is the Catholic Church's great list of saints.

And I think there is something you can take from him to bed tonight.

He was not a perfect man. He got something important wrong, and he got it wrong in public, and everyone knew. And he did not hide from that, and he did not give up. He turned back. He came home. And then he wrote a song that has helped people come home for thirteen hundred years.

So if you have had a day when you got something wrong. If you said a thing you wish you had not said, or did a thing you wish you had not done. You do not have to sort it all out tonight. You could just say the old refrain, once, or forty times, as many as you like. Have mercy on me, O God. Have mercy on me.

He is at least as patient as Anna. And he does not run out of honey cake.

And you could think of Leo, who never existed, but who stands tonight for all the scribes and copyists and orphans and ordinary people whose hands carried these old songs down to us, page by page and blot by blot. Nobody wrote their names down. But the hymn got here because of them.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

What we know about Andrew comes from a few places. There is an old Greek Life of him, written after his death by a man called Niketas, a high official in Constantinople. There is the short entry for him in the old church calendar of Constantinople, on the fourth of July. There are the records of the councils and synods of his time. And there are his own works: his homilies, especially on the feasts of the Mother of God, which you can read in English in the collections by Brian Daley and Mary Cunningham, and above all the Great Canon, which you can read in the Lenten Triodion translated by Mother Mary and Archimandrite Kallistos Ware.

What is history. That he was born in Damascus in the middle of the six hundreds. That he served the church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem and worked for Theodore, who kept the patriarch's throne there. That he went to Constantinople with Jerusalem's agreement to the Sixth Council and stayed, as a deacon of Hagia Sophia in charge of an orphanage and a home for the old. That he became archbishop of Gortyna on Crete. That he went along with Philippikos's synod in seven hundred and twelve and returned to the council's faith after it. That he wrote the Great Canon, and preached on Mary's feasts. That he died on Lesbos in about seven hundred and forty.

What is the old Life's report. That he could not speak until he was seven and spoke after receiving Communion. That a raid on a Cretan town was turned back at his prayers. We have told these as the Life tells them, and we cannot check them.

What we do not know. When and where he wrote the canon. We set it on Crete, late in his life, but that is our choice, not a fact. And we do not know why he went along with Philippikos.

What we invented. Leo, both Annas, the honey cake, Leviticus, and almost all the words anybody says. The lines of the canon are given in our own plain paraphrase, not in anybody's translation.

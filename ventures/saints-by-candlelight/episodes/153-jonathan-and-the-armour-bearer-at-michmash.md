There is one more thing I want to tell you, and it is not from the Bible. It is from a book by a British officer named Vivian Gilbert, who served in the First World War, in the army that marched through this same hill country in the winter of nineteen eighteen.

He tells it like this. The army was facing an enemy force dug in at a village on a hill. And the village was called Mukhmas, which is Michmash. And a brigade major, the night before the attack, kept trying to remember where he had heard that name. And he got out his Bible, and read, by candlelight, this very chapter. The pass. The two crags. The garrison at the top. The half a furrow's length.

And he woke his brigadier, and they sent scouts out in the dark, and the scouts came back and said, the path is there. The two crags are there. And in the morning, Gilbert says, a small company climbed the cliff where Jonathan had climbed it, and the soldiers holding the village woke up, and thought they were surrounded, and left.

Now, I want to be honest with you about that story. It is one man's memoir, written a few years after the war, and it has been retold many times since, and it has probably grown a little in the telling, as stories about armies do. Nobody now can check every detail. So take it as a story a soldier told, not as a fact I can prove.

But I like that somebody read this chapter by candlelight, the way you and I are hearing it now, and found that the rock was still there. That Bozez and Seneh were still standing in the gorge, nearly three thousand years on, waiting for someone to look up.

The blacksmiths and the pim are from chapter thirteen. Saul's offering at Gilgal and Samuel's rebuke are from the same chapter, and we told them only in outline. Jonathan's love for David and the gift of his sword are from chapter eighteen, and the Song of the Bow is from the Second Book of Samuel, chapter one. The law about the blood is from Leviticus, chapter seventeen, and Deuteronomy, chapter twelve.

Several things in the chapter are uncertain, and we tried not to pretend otherwise. The meanings of the names Bozez and Seneh are guesses. The measure of the first skirmish, half a furrow in a plot of land, is hard Hebrew, and we gave only its sense. Where Saul says bring the ark of God, the old Greek says the ephod, and scholars disagree about which is original. The pim weights are real finds, and their weight, about two-thirds of a shekel, is what made sense of the word.

The armour-bearer is unnamed in the book, and so he is unnamed here. His thoughts, his mother, the little plant on the cliff, the breakfast smoke, the bees, and the small talk on the way to the pass are our own invention.

The story of the British officer in nineteen eighteen is from Vivian Gilbert's memoir, The Romance of the Last Crusade, published in nineteen twenty-three, and is told here as his account, not as checked history.

It is morning at a well in the desert, and the water in the stone troughs is shining.

Seven girls have been working since the sky turned grey. * * *

To understand how Moses came to be sitting on a stone by a well in the desert, we have to go back a little, to Egypt.

You may remember how Moses' life began. His people, the Hebrews, were living in Egypt, and the king of Egypt, the Pharaoh, had grown afraid of how many of them there were. So he made them slaves, and set taskmasters over them, and made them build store cities out of brick. And when that did not make them fewer, he gave a much worse order, which we will not dwell on at bedtime, about the baby boys.

And Moses' mother, when he was born, hid him for three months. And when she could hide him no longer, she made a little basket out of papyrus reeds, and covered it with tar and pitch so that it would float, and put the baby in it, and set it among the reeds at the edge of the river Nile. And his big sister stood a little way off, to see what would happen.

What happened was that the daughter of Pharaoh came down to the river to bathe. And she saw the basket among the reeds, and sent her maid to fetch it, and opened it, and there was the baby, crying. And the book says, very simply, that she took pity on him.

And the big sister, who was clearly a quick thinker, popped out of the reeds and said, would you like me to go and find a Hebrew woman to nurse the baby for you? And the princess said yes. And the girl ran off and fetched, of all the people in Egypt, the baby's own mother. Which I have always thought is one of the most satisfying moments in the whole of the Old Testament.

So Moses was nursed by his own mother, and then, when he was older, he was brought to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. And she gave him the name Moses.

Saint Stephen, many centuries later, telling this story in the Acts of the Apostles, adds that Moses was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, and that he was mighty in his words and his deeds. So picture a young man raised at court. He has been taught to read and write, which in Egypt was a long and serious business. He has been taught how to speak, and how to behave in front of important people, and probably how to drive a chariot. He has almost certainly never, in his whole life, had to fetch his own water.

Keep that last point in mind. It will matter later.

* * *

And then, the book says, one day when Moses had grown up, he went out to his own people, the Hebrews, and he looked at their burdens.

Saint Stephen says he was forty years old by then, and that it came into his heart to visit his brothers. Whatever had been going on inside him, all those years in the palace, it came to the surface that day. He went out to the brickfields, and he saw the heavy work, and he saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his own people.

And Moses looked this way and that way, and saw that there was no one about. And he struck the Egyptian down, and the Egyptian died, and Moses hid him in the sand.

The book tells this in one verse, and it does not stop to say whether it was right or wrong. It simply tells us what he did, and then what happened next, which is usually the Bible's way of letting us think about it for ourselves.

What happened next was this. The following day, Moses went out again, and this time he saw two Hebrew men fighting with each other. And he said to the one who was in the wrong, "Why are you striking your neighbour?"

And the man turned round and said, "Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Are you going to kill me, the way you killed the Egyptian?"

And Moses was afraid. Because he thought, then the thing is known.

It was known. Pharaoh heard about it, and Pharaoh tried to have Moses killed. And Moses, the young man raised in the palace, mighty in his words and his deeds, ran away.

The book says he fled from Pharaoh, and he stayed in the land of Midian. And he sat down by a well.

That is one sentence in the book. But I think it is worth slowing down over, because it covers a great deal of ground. Quite literally.

* * *

We do not know exactly which way Moses went, or exactly where Midian was. The Bible does not draw us a map. Most scholars today place the land of Midian on the far side of the Gulf of Aqaba, in what is now the north-west of Saudi Arabia, though there are other suggestions, and the Midianites, who were wanderers with their flocks, may well have roamed over a good deal of the Sinai desert too. Either way, from the green strip along the Nile to the land of Midian is a very long way, through very dry country, for a man on foot.

So let us imagine the journey, because the book leaves it to us.

Imagine a man who has always slept in a bed, sleeping on the ground. Imagine a man who has always had his meals brought to him, trying to work out how long a bag of bread will last when there is nowhere to buy any more. Imagine the rocks, and the heat in the middle of the day, and the cold at night, which always surprises people who have never been in a desert. Imagine the sandals, which were made for walking across a courtyard, slowly coming apart.

The old sources do not tell us what he took with him. I suspect, from the speed of the whole business, that it was not much.

And imagine what he was thinking about, all those long days. He had been a prince, more or less. He had been a man of words and deeds. And he had tried to do something for his people, and it had gone wrong in every direction at once. The Egyptians wanted him dead. And his own people, the ones he had gone out to help, had looked at him and said, who made you a judge over us?

I think, by the time he came over the last rise and saw the well, he was not feeling much like a prince. I think he was feeling like nobody in particular. A tired man, a long way from home, with nowhere to go.

And when you are in the desert and you do not know what to do next, there is one sensible thing to do, and that is to find water and sit down beside it.

So he did.

* * *

Now, a well in the desert is not like a well in a fairy tale, with a little roof and a handle that you wind. In that country, a well was often a deep shaft cut down through the rock to where the water was, sometimes with a heavy stone over the mouth of it to keep the sand and the animals out. Beside it there were troughs, long hollowed stones or channels, and the shepherds would let a bucket down on a rope, and haul it up full, and pour the water into the troughs, so that the flock could drink.

It was hard work. A thirsty flock drinks a great deal of water, and a leather bucket on a rope does not hold very much. Pulling it up, over and over, under the sun, is the kind of job that makes your shoulders ache by the end of the morning, and your hands rough.

And a well was the centre of everything. It was where every flock in the district came to drink. It was where you heard the news. It was where you met people. And so it is no surprise, really, that some of the most important meetings in the whole of the Book of Genesis happen at wells.

You might remember two of them from earlier on this show.

There was the servant of Abraham, who went all the way back to the old country to find a wife for Isaac, and sat down by a well with his ten camels, and prayed that the right girl would offer water not only to him but to his camels too. And along came Rebekah, who did exactly that, and watered ten thirsty camels, which, if you have ever seen how much a camel drinks, is something close to heroic.

And there was Jacob, Rebekah's son, who ran away from home, a little like Moses, and came to a well in the east where the shepherds were waiting around, and saw Rachel coming with her father's sheep. And Jacob went up and rolled the great stone off the mouth of the well all by himself, and watered her flock.

So when we hear that Moses, too, has run away from home, and has sat down beside a well, anyone who knows the old stories is already leaning forward a little. Because we know that, in this part of the Bible, when a lonely young man sits down by a well, something is about to happen.

And something did.

The book now introduces us to a new family. Now the priest of Midian, it says, had seven daughters.

The priest of Midian is called Reuel in this chapter. Later on, the book calls him Jethro, and there is another name in the Book of Numbers too, and people have been puzzling over how the names fit together for a very long time. We will not try to settle it tonight. For this story, we will call him what the chapter calls him, Reuel.

He was a priest, and he was also, like most people in Midian, a man with flocks. He seems not to have had any sons, or at least none old enough to do the work, because it was his daughters who took the flock out to water. That was not unusual. Rachel, remember, kept her father's sheep. A girl with a stick and a flock was a perfectly ordinary sight.

Seven daughters. The book gives us the name of only one of them. She was called Zipporah, which means a little bird. The other six, it does not name at all, which I think is a great oversight, and which leaves me, I'm afraid, calling them things like the eldest one and the second one and the one in the middle.

So let us imagine them coming down to the well that morning. Seven sisters, of different ages and sizes, with the flock trotting along in front of them, and a few goats wandering off in the wrong direction, as goats always do. The eldest is in charge, and makes sure everyone knows it. The middle ones are arguing about whose turn it is with the bucket. The youngest is mostly in charge of the goats who wander off. And Zipporah, wherever she comes in the line, is somewhere in the middle of all of it.

They came, the book says, and they drew water, and they filled the troughs to water their father's flock.

They knew the work. They had done it many times. One of them let the bucket down, and pulled it up, and passed it along, and it was poured out, and the bucket went down again. Up and down. Up and down. The troughs filling slowly, the water rippling in the stone. The sheep crowding in.

And the young man on the stone, a little way off, sat and watched them, because he had nothing else to do, and nowhere else to be.

And then the shepherds came.

The book says it in very few words. The shepherds came, and drove them away.

Now, the book does not tell us who these shepherds were, or how many there were, or why they did it. But I think we can make a good guess. They had their own flocks. Their flocks were thirsty too. And here were seven girls, who had done all the hard work of hauling up the water and filling the troughs. And so the shepherds came down, and pushed the girls aside, and shouted, and waved their sticks, and drove the girls' flock off, and let their own animals drink the water that the girls had drawn.

It saved them a great deal of hauling. It was also very unfair. And there is a hint in the story, which we will come to, that this was not the first time it had happened.

So picture it. The girls being shouted at, and pushed back. The father's sheep scattering. The other flocks jostling in and drinking from the troughs. The youngest sister, perhaps, saying, oh no, not again. And the eldest standing with her arms folded and her face very red, because there was nothing she could do about it, and she knew it.

And then Moses stood up.

That is what the book says. But Moses stood up, and helped them, and watered their flock.

The Hebrew word that is translated helped is a strong one. It is the word for saving, for delivering, for rescuing someone. It is the same family of words as the name Joshua, and as the name Jesus. Moses did not just help in the sense of lending a hand. He came to their rescue.

We are not told how. We are not told what he said, or whether he had to do more than stand up and walk over. He was a grown man, and by Saint Stephen's reckoning about forty years old, and he had been raised in the court of Pharaoh, where people expected to be obeyed. Perhaps he simply stood in front of the troughs, and looked at the shepherds in the way he had seen great men look at people in Egypt, and said, in his best palace voice, I don't think so.

And perhaps the shepherds looked at this stranger, in his strange good clothes, with his strange way of talking, and decided that today, just for once, they would wait their turn.

However it happened, the shepherds were driven back. And then Moses did something that I find quite wonderful.

He did not just stand there with his arms folded, having made his point. He took the bucket, and let it down into the well, and hauled it up, and poured it into the troughs. And then he did it again. And again. And he watered the whole flock.

Remember the man who had probably never fetched his own water in his life? Here he is, in the desert, with his palace hands on a rough wet rope, hauling up bucket after bucket for seven girls he has never met and their father's sheep.

I like to imagine the sisters standing round watching him. And I like to imagine that, the first few times, he did it badly. That the bucket banged against the sides of the shaft, and came up half empty, and he spilled most of it on his feet. And that the eldest sister said, no, no, not like that, let it sink first, and showed him. And that by the end he had got the hang of it.

But the book does not say that. That is just me.

What the book says is that he watered their flock. And then, as far as we can tell, he went back and sat down on his stone again. Because the story does not say that the sisters invited him home. In fact, it rather pointedly says that they did not.

* * *

The seven sisters went home to their father, and the flock trotted happily in front of them, full of water.

And their father Reuel looked up, and said, "How is it that you have come home so soon today?"

Now, think about that question. Think about what it tells us. It tells us that the sisters usually came home late. Much later. It tells us that, on an ordinary day, they would draw the water and fill the troughs, and then the shepherds would come and drive them off and drink it, and the girls would have to wait until the other flocks had finished, and then start all over again from the beginning. And so, on an ordinary day, it took them most of the morning, and they came home tired and cross and late.

Readers have noticed this for a long time. The father's little question, how is it that you have come home so soon today, tells us that what happened at the well was not a rare bad day. It was the way things always were. The sisters had simply got used to it. And their father had got used to them coming home late.

And today, they were early.

And the sisters said, "An Egyptian rescued us from the hands of the shepherds. And he even drew water for us, and watered the flock."

An Egyptian. That is what they called him. He was a Hebrew, of course, a son of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob. But nobody in Midian knew that. He came from Egypt. He dressed like an Egyptian. He probably talked like one, and carried himself like one. And so, to seven girls at a well, he was simply the Egyptian. I think that would have stung a little, if he had heard it. The Egyptians had wanted to kill him, and his own people had not wanted him, and here in the desert, he was the Egyptian.

And notice the little word even. He even drew water for us. The sisters seem to have been more surprised by that than by anything else. Standing up to the shepherds was one thing. But a man who looked like he came from a palace, hauling up the bucket himself? That was the part they could not get over.

And then their father asked the best question in the whole chapter.

He said, "And where is he? Why did you leave the man there? Call him, so that he may eat bread with us."

I can just picture it. Seven sisters, looking at each other. And then at their feet. And then at each other again.

Because in that part of the world, then and now, there was nothing more important than hospitality. If a stranger did you a kindness, you invited him in. You fed him. You gave him a place to sleep. You did not leave him sitting by a well in the middle of the desert, with nowhere to go and nothing to eat, while you went home early and pleased with yourselves.

Why did you leave the man? It is the sort of question every parent has asked at some point. You went to a birthday party, and someone was kind to you, and you didn't say thank you? Why did you leave the man?

And I imagine the sisters all talking at once, the way sisters do. Well, we didn't know if we should. Well, he was a stranger. Well, she was supposed to ask him. No, I wasn't, you're the eldest. And Reuel holding up his hand, and saying, quietly, go and get him.

So one of them went back. The book does not say which one. Perhaps it was Zipporah. Perhaps they all went. I would like to think the youngest one ran on ahead.

* * *

Let us go back to the well for a moment.

It is the middle of the day now. The other shepherds have watered their flocks and gone. The troughs are almost empty again, just a little water in the bottom of the stone, glinting. And Moses is sitting on his stone, with his rope-burned hands in his lap, and the sun high overhead.

What do you do, when you have done the one good deed that was in front of you, and you have nothing left to do, and nowhere to go?

I think perhaps he sat there thinking about Egypt. About the man in the brickfields, and the man who said, who made you a judge over us? He had tried to rescue someone once before, in Egypt, and it had all gone horribly wrong. And today he had tried again, here, with seven girls and a flock of sheep, in the most ordinary way. No one had died. No one had been hidden in the sand. He had just stood up, and said no, and then carried water. And it had gone right.

He did not know it yet, but it was a sort of rehearsal. One day he would stand up in front of a much greater bully than a few shepherds at a well, and say, let my people go. And one day he would bring water for a great many more thirsty people than seven sisters, in a much drier place than this. But all of that was a long way off.

For now, there was a small figure running over the rise towards him, waving.

"Our father says," she called out, when she had got her breath back, "that you're to come and eat bread with us. And he says, why did we leave you here. And we don't know. Sorry."

And Moses got up off his stone, and went.

* * *

The tents of Reuel would not have been grand. A priest of Midian was a man of some standing, but he lived like everyone else in that country, in tents of woven goat hair, black and brown, that could be rolled up and carried when the flock had to move on to new grazing. Inside, there would have been rugs on the ground, and cushions, and the smell of woodsmoke and cooking.

And there, in the doorway of the biggest tent, stood Reuel, waiting.

We do not know what he looked like. In my mind he is a broad, calm man with a beard just starting to go grey, the kind of man who notices things. The kind of man who asks, how is it you have come home so soon today, because he has been paying attention every day, even on the days when nobody thought he was.

And what did they eat? The book only says bread, which in the Bible's way of speaking means a meal, a proper meal. So let us imagine it. Flat bread, baked on a hot stone by the fire. Perhaps lentils, cooked slowly with onions. Milk from the goats, or the soft cheese made from it. Dates, if they were lucky. Nothing fancy. But to a man who had been walking through the desert for days, eating whatever was left in the bottom of his bag, I think it would have been the best meal of his life.

And seven sisters, sitting round the edge of the tent, trying not to stare at him, and staring at him anyway.

And I like to imagine Reuel asking him, over the bread, where he had come from, and Moses not quite knowing how much to say. And Reuel, being the kind of man who notices things, perhaps noticing that the young man did not want to talk about it, and changing the subject to sheep. Because the book tells us nothing at all about what Moses told him that night, and I suspect that, at first, it was not very much.

But here is what the book does tell us. It says, Moses was content to dwell with the man.

Content. It is a lovely word to find there. After the palace, and the brickfields, and the long, frightening flight across the desert, Moses found a place where he was content. Not in a palace. Not among his own people. In a tent of goat hair, in a foreign land, with a kindly priest and his seven daughters and a great many sheep.

He had been looking for somewhere to belong. And it turned out that the place was at the end of a very simple question. Why did you leave the man? Call him in.

* * *

And so Moses stayed.

He stayed, and he learned to be a shepherd. The book tells us, at the beginning of the next chapter, that Moses was keeping the flock of his father-in-law, the priest of Midian. So he must have learned it all, from the beginning. How to find grazing in country where there seems to be none. How to tell when a ewe is about to lamb. How to count a flock in the evening without counting every single animal, which is a skill shepherds have and the rest of us do not. How to carry a lamb across your shoulders. How to find your way home in the dark by the stars.

I imagine that he learned a great deal of it from the sisters. They had been doing it all their lives. I like to think that the eldest one, especially, enjoyed telling the man from the palace that he was doing it wrong. And I like to think he took it well.

And I also like to think that, after that day, the shepherds at the well were a good deal more polite. Though the book does not say so, and I suppose they might simply have started coming earlier.

And Reuel gave Moses his daughter Zipporah, to be his wife.

The book does not tell us anything about their wedding, or about what Zipporah thought of it, or whether she was the sister who came running back to the well. It tells us that she became his wife. And later on, much later, it tells us that she went with him on the road back to Egypt, with their sons on a donkey. And that she came back across the desert with her father, years after that, to meet him at the mountain of God. That was the story of the other night, with the judges and the small cases.

And Zipporah gave birth to a son, and Moses named him Gershom.

The book tells us why. It says that Moses named him Gershom, for he said, I have been a sojourner in a foreign land.

A sojourner is someone who is living somewhere that is not their own home. A stranger who has come to stay. And the name Gershom sounds, in Hebrew, like the word for a sojourner, ger, and the word for there, sham. A stranger there. So every time Moses called his little boy in for supper, he was saying, in a way, I am a long way from home.

That might sound like a sad name for a baby. But I don't think it was only sad. I think it was honest. Moses was a stranger in Midian. He had not been born there, and he did not belong to its people, and he had not forgotten Egypt, or his own people in the brickfields. But he had been taken in. He had been given bread, and a home, and a wife, and now a son. A sojourner is a stranger. But a sojourner is a stranger who has been allowed to stay.

The Bible has a great deal to say, all the way through, about sojourners. Again and again in the Law that Moses would one day bring down from the mountain, the people of Israel are told, you shall not wrong the stranger who lives among you, you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers once, in the land of Egypt. And I have always wondered whether, when Moses gave the people those words, he thought about a tent of goat hair in Midian, and a priest who said, why did you leave the man? Call him in.

[pause]

So the years went by in Midian. The book says it was a long time. Saint Stephen, in Acts, says it was forty years before anything else happened. Forty years of sheep and goats and wells and tents. Long enough for Moses to forget most of what he had learned in the palace. Long enough for his hands to grow rough, and his skin brown, and his good Egyptian clothes to wear out and be replaced by a shepherd's cloak. Long enough, I suspect, for nobody to call him the Egyptian any more.

And the book, very quietly, before it moves on, tells us what was happening back in Egypt all that time.

It says that in the course of those many days, the king of Egypt died. And the people of Israel groaned under their slavery, and cried out. And their cry for help rose up to God.

And God heard their groaning. And God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. And God saw the people of Israel. And God knew.

Heard, remembered, saw, knew. Four little words, one after another, at the end of the chapter. All those years, while Moses was out on the hillsides with the sheep, thinking perhaps that his life had come to a quiet end in a foreign land, God had not forgotten anybody. Not the people in the brickfields. Not the shepherd in Midian.

And one day, not tonight, but one day, Moses would lead the flock out to the far side of the wilderness, to a mountain called Horeb, and he would see a bush that was burning and was not burnt up. But we have told that story on another night, and it can wait.

For tonight, let us leave him where the chapter leaves him. A shepherd, with a wife and a little son. A sojourner, who has been taken in.

And let us go back, just once more, to a tent of goat hair in the evening.

The flock is in for the night. The fire is low. Zipporah is singing to the baby, very softly. The sisters are talking in the corner about tomorrow, and whose turn it is with the bucket. And old Reuel is sitting in the doorway of the tent, looking out at the stars over the desert, the way he does every night.

And Moses comes and sits down beside him. And for a while neither of them says anything, because they don't need to.

And then Moses says, "Can I ask you something? That first day. Why did you send them back for me? You didn't know anything about me."

And Reuel thinks about it, for a long while, the way he does. And then he says, "You watered my sheep. And it was getting dark. And you had nowhere to go. What else would I do?"

That is our own invention, of course. The book does not tell us that conversation, or whether it ever happened. But I think it is the sort of thing Reuel might have said. Because he was the sort of man who noticed when his daughters came home early. And the sort of man who asked, why did you leave the man? And that, it turned out, was enough to give a runaway a home.

And the fire burns down. And the sheep settle. And the baby falls asleep. And somewhere out there in the dark, the well is quiet, and the water in the bottom of it is still and cold and full of stars.

And everyone sleeps.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Exodus, chapter two, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. The basket in the reeds is from the first part of that chapter, and the brickfields, the Egyptian, the two quarrelling men, Moses' flight, the well, the seven daughters, the supper and the naming of Gershom are from verses eleven to twenty-two. The king's death and God hearing, remembering, seeing and knowing are verses twenty-three to twenty-five. Moses keeping Reuel's flock is from chapter three, the road back to Egypt from chapter four, and Zipporah's return with her father from chapter eighteen.

Saint Stephen's retelling, with Moses instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, forty years old when he went out to his people, and forty years in Midian, is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter seven. Exodus itself does not give those ages, and we have told them as Stephen gives them. The well scenes with Rebekah and Rachel are from Genesis, chapters twenty-four and twenty-nine.

The chapter calls Moses' father-in-law Reuel; later chapters call him Jethro, and Numbers calls Hobab the son of Reuel. We have not tried to settle how the names fit together. Where exactly Midian was is not certain; the north-west of Arabia is the most common view.

The meaning of the name Gershom is the book's own explanation, and the meaning of Zipporah's name is the usual one.

Moses' journey, the stone he sat on, the six unnamed sisters and everything they said, the shepherds' daily habit, beyond what the father's question implies, the bucket lessons, the supper, the lentils, and the last conversation by the tent are our own invention.

Reuel and his daughters have no feast day of their own, but Moses, the prophet and lawgiver, is remembered on the fourth of September, by the Orthodox Church and in the Roman Martyrology.

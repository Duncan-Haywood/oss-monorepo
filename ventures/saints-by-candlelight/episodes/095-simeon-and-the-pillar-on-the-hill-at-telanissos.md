Imagine a hilltop in the north of Syria, at the end of a long, hot day.

The sun is going down behind the mountains. The stones of the hill are still warm, and they give off a smell of dust and thyme. Down below, in the village, someone is lighting a lamp. And in the middle of the hilltop, inside a low ring of stone, there is a pillar. A tall, plain, round pillar, taller than the tallest house you have ever seen. At the top of it there is a small platform. And on the platform there is a man.

He is standing with his arms raised. Then he bends, all the way down, until his forehead almost touches his feet. Then he stands up again, arms raised. Then he bends again. Up, and down. Up, and down. Slowly, steadily, like a man pulling water from a very deep well.

At the foot of the pillar, a visitor from the city has decided to count. He is a practical sort of person. He likes numbers. He folds his arms and he counts the bows, under his breath. Ten. Fifty. A hundred. Five hundred. The light goes orange and then red and then grey. A thousand. One thousand two hundred. One thousand two hundred and forty-four.

And then the visitor stops counting, and unfolds his arms, and sits down on a stone, and says, mostly to himself, "No. I give up."

And the man on the pillar, who has not noticed any of this, goes on bowing.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Let me start by saying, very plainly, that this is the strangest story we have told on this show. And we have told the one about the lion who was very bad at guarding a donkey.

A man lived on top of a pillar. For more than thirty-five years. In the rain, in the snow, in the summer sun of Syria, which is not a gentle sun. People came from as far away as Spain and Britain to look up at him and ask for his prayers. Emperors wrote him letters. And I want to say at the very beginning, before anybody at home gets ideas, that the Church has never asked anyone to do this. Simeon did not ask anyone to do it either. It was his own odd, particular road to God, and the people who knew him best thought it was odd too. So, please, nobody climb anything tonight. Stay in bed. That is the whole point of the show.

But I also want to say that this story is not mainly about a pillar. It is about a man so full of love for God that people walked for months just to stand near him. And when they got there, he talked to them, very kindly, about ordinary things. And that is the part that I think is worth a bedtime story.

So where are we?

We are in the north of Syria, in the hills between the great city of Antioch and the city of Aleppo, about fifteen hundred years ago, give or take. The Roman Empire still rules here. But this is not the Rome of marble statues and senators in togas. This is the late empire, and it is Christian now, and it speaks Greek in the cities and Syriac in the villages. Syriac is a language close to the Aramaic that the Lord Himself spoke, which is a lovely thing to think about.

The country is limestone hills, bare and pale, with olive trees and vines on the slopes and little stone villages tucked in among them. The villages were rich, for villages. They grew olives and pressed them into oil and sent the oil down to Antioch and out across the sea. The houses were built of beautiful square-cut stone. And the remarkable thing is that many of them are still there. Whole villages of empty stone houses, with their doorways and their windows and their olive presses, standing on the hills in the sun, as if the people had just stepped out. People today call them the Dead Cities, which is a bit gloomy. I prefer to think of them as the villages that are waiting.

And in those hills, at the time of our story, there was a very particular kind of Christian. The monks of Syria. Now, monks in Egypt were famous for going off into the desert. The monks of Syria were famous for being, how shall I put it, extremely enthusiastic. Some of them lived in the open air with no roof at all. Some of them lived in tiny huts so low they could not stand up. Some of them ate only grass. One of them, the old writers tell us, lived in a cage. The bishop who wrote down most of tonight's story collected the lives of these people in a book, and reading it is a little like reading a list of very holy, very determined, slightly alarming great-uncles.

And the most famous of all of them was Simeon.

* * *

Simeon was born in a village called Sisan, up near the border between Syria and Cilicia, which is now the south of Turkey. His family were ordinary country people. Christians. They kept sheep. And when he was a boy, Simeon's job was to look after his father's flock.

Now, the bishop who wrote his story, whose name was Theodoret, tells us how it all began, and it began with the weather.

One winter it snowed. It does snow in those hills, more than you would think. And when it snows, you cannot take the sheep out. They stay in the fold, and they are cross about it, and there is nothing for a shepherd boy to do. So Simeon, with nothing to do, went with his parents to church.

And in church that day, the reading was from the Gospel. The Beatitudes. Blessed are the poor in spirit. Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted. Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

And the boy, who had probably heard those words before without really hearing them, heard them. And he wanted to know what they meant. So after the service he went up to an old man in the church, and he asked him. What does it mean, to be pure in heart? How do you do that? How do you get to see God?

And the old man, Theodoret says, told him. He told him that there was a way of life for people who wanted, more than anything, to belong entirely to God. A life of prayer, and fasting, and giving things up, and living very simply. The life of the monks. He said it was hard. He said it was a narrow road. He said it was the best road there is.

I like this old man very much. We do not know his name. He was just someone at church who was kind enough to answer a child's question properly, instead of saying, "Oh, you'll understand when you're older." If you are ever that person, you never know what you may be starting.

Simeon went away and thought about it. Theodoret says he went to a nearby shrine of the martyrs and lay down there and prayed, and fell asleep. And he had a dream.

In the dream, he was digging. Digging a foundation, as if for a building. And a voice told him to dig deeper. So he dug deeper. And the voice told him, deeper still. And he dug deeper still. Four times, the voice told him to keep going. And then at last it said: that is enough. Now you can build. And the work will be easy, because you have dug down far enough.

That is Theodoret's report of the dream, and Theodoret says Simeon told it to him himself, years later. I think it is a very good dream. Most of us would like to skip straight to the building. Simeon's whole life, in a way, was spent on the digging.

* * *

So Simeon, as a teenager, went off to be a monk. He spent a couple of years with some holy men nearby. And then he went to a bigger monastery, at a place called Teleda, which was led in those days, Theodoret says, by two abbots, called Eusebonas and Abibion. It was a big community, dozens of monks. Simeon stayed for about ten years.

And here is where we first meet the problem with Simeon, which is that he did not know when to stop.

The other monks fasted. Simeon fasted more. The other monks ate every other day. Simeon ate once a week. The other monks slept on hard beds. Simeon hardly slept. And then, Theodoret tells us, he took a rough rope made of palm fibre, the kind of rope you would use for a well bucket, and tied it round his waist, under his clothes, so tightly that it hurt him badly. He did not tell anyone. It was the other monks who found out, because they could see he was not well.

And the abbot was not impressed.

Now, I want you to imagine this conversation, because I think it is one of the most sensible conversations in the whole history of monasteries. The words are our own, but the sense is Theodoret's. The abbot sat Simeon down, and he said something like:

"Simeon. My son. We are glad you love God. We all love God. That is why we are here. But this is a community. There are a great many of us. And the young ones look at you, and they think that this is what holiness looks like. And some of them will try to copy you, and they will make themselves ill. And I am responsible for them. So I am afraid, my son, that you will have to go."

And Simeon went.

I find this very reassuring, actually. Even a great saint can be too much for a monastery. The abbot was not wrong, and Simeon, as far as we can tell, did not argue. He did not stomp off. He went quietly. And he did not, in the end, ask anyone else to live the way he lived. I think that came partly from this conversation. He had learned that his road was for him.

* * *

Simeon wandered for a while, and then he came to a village called Telanissos. It sat at the foot of a hill, in a valley among the olive groves. And there, near the village, he found a small hut, and he decided to stay.

And Lent came round. And Simeon decided that for the whole of Lent, all forty days, he would not eat at all.

Now, at home, please do not do this either. People can die doing this. And the priest of the area, a sensible man called Bassus, who was in charge of the country churches round about, thought so too. So Bassus came to the hut before Lent began, and he brought ten loaves of bread and a jug of water, and he put them inside. And Simeon asked him to seal up the door of the hut with mud, and come back at the end of Lent.

So Bassus did. He sealed the door with mud. And forty days later he came back and broke the seal and went in.

And the ten loaves were still there. Not one had been touched. And the water jug was still full. And Simeon was lying on the floor, very weak, unable to speak or move. And Bassus, Theodoret says, took a sponge and wet his lips, and gave him Holy Communion. And Simeon got up. And he slowly ate a little lettuce and a few other green leaves, and got his strength back.

That is Theodoret's report, and he says that Simeon kept this Lenten fast every year after that, for twenty-eight years, by the time Theodoret was writing. Though in later years, Theodoret says, he did it standing up for as long as he could, then sitting, then lying down, as his strength gave out towards the end of each Lent. And Theodoret, I should say, is a sober sort of writer. He was a bishop and a scholar and a historian. He was not a man who believed everything he was told. But he believed this.

Whatever you make of it, the people of the villages around Telanissos started to notice that there was a holy man living near them. And they started to come.

* * *

Now, at this point we need a boy.

The old books do not give us one. But there must have been boys in Telanissos, and some of them must have been curious, and some of them must have been given jobs to do for the holy man on the hill. So let me introduce one. He is our own invention. His name is Habib, which means beloved, and it is still a common name in that part of the world. He is about nine years old. His father grows olives. His mother makes the best flatbread in the village, or so Habib says. He has two older sisters who boss him about, and a goat he is supposed to be watching and generally is not.

And one morning, Habib's father says to him, "The holy man has moved up the hill."

Because Simeon had. He had left the hut and climbed up to the top of the hill above the village. And there he had built himself a ring of rough stones, a low wall in a circle, not even a roof, just a wall. And inside it, he stood and prayed. Day and night, in the open air.

And to make sure he did not wander out of his circle, he had taken an iron chain, about twenty cubits long, which is about as long as a big room, and fixed one end to a heavy stone and the other end to his right foot. So he could walk round inside his circle, but no further.

Habib goes up the hill with a jug of water, because his mother has told him to, and he looks over the wall, and there is Simeon, a thin man in a rough leather tunic, with a chain on his foot. And Simeon looks at him and smiles. And Habib, who was expecting someone frightening, finds that he is not frightened at all.

"Is that heavy?" says Habib, pointing at the chain.

"Not very," says Simeon. "It reminds me to stay where I am."

"My goat could do with one of those," says Habib.

And Simeon laughs. And that is the beginning of a friendship. A one-sided sort of friendship, because Simeon spends most of his time praying and Habib spends most of his time losing the goat. But a friendship.

* * *

Now here comes the bishop. Not Theodoret yet. A different one.

His name was Meletius, and he was what they called a country bishop, a bishop who looked after the villages in the hills on behalf of the great bishop of Antioch. And he heard about the holy man with the chain on his foot, and he came up to see for himself.

And Meletius, Theodoret tells us, said something very wise. He said: a man does not need iron to keep him still. If you want to stay in one place for God's sake, your own will is enough. Your love for God is a better chain than this one.

And Simeon listened to him. He had a smith come up and take the chain off. That tells you something about Simeon. He was extreme, yes. But he was not stubborn. When a sensible person in authority told him to stop something, he stopped.

And now comes my favourite detail in the whole story, which is also Theodoret's report.

Under the iron ring round his ankle, Simeon had wrapped a piece of leather, to stop the iron from rubbing him raw. And when the smith took off the chain, they had to cut away the leather too. And when they did, Theodoret says, out came more than twenty large bugs, who had been living under there quite happily for some time.

And Simeon had known they were there. And he had not minded. Or rather, he had decided that this was one more small thing he could put up with for God's sake.

Habib, if he had been there, would have been absolutely delighted. I have no doubt at all that the story of the bugs went round the whole village by suppertime, and that every child in Telanissos did impressions of the bishop's face.

* * *

And now things started to get out of hand.

Because word got round. Not just round the villages. Round everywhere. The holy man on the hill at Telanissos. The man who prays all day and all night. The man who does not eat in Lent. The man who will listen to you.

And people came. At first from nearby, the farmers and their families. Then from Antioch, the big city, a couple of days' walk. Then from the rest of Syria. Then from further. Theodoret gives us a list, and I love this list, because you can hear the bishop getting more and more amazed as he writes it. People came, he says, from the desert tribes. From Persia. From Armenia. From Iberia, which means Georgia, in the Caucasus. From the far west, too: from Spain, from Britain, from Gaul, which is France, and from Italy.

And in Rome, Theodoret says, Simeon was so famous that people set up little pictures of him at the doorways of their workshops, for protection and blessing. A man on a hill in Syria, and in the shops of Rome, the cobblers and the bakers had his picture by the door. Like a postcard. I find that very touching.

And of course the hilltop filled up. Theodoret says it was like a sea, with rivers running into it from every direction. Pilgrims camping. Sick people being carried up on stretchers. Mothers holding up babies. Men with lawsuits. Men with problems. Everybody wanting to touch the holy man. And this was the trouble. Because they wanted to touch him. Literally. They wanted to touch his leather tunic, and they wanted, if possible, to take home a little bit of it, as a blessing. So they pulled at it. They tore off little bits. Hundreds of people, every day, pulling and grabbing.

And Habib, who is now a bit older, eleven or twelve perhaps, stands at the wall of the circle with the other village boys, who have been given the job of keeping the crowds in order, and he watches the holy man being pulled about like a coat on a peg. And he thinks this is not right.

"They're going to pull you to pieces," he says, one evening, when the crowds have gone down the hill.

"They mean well," says Simeon.

"They mean well and they're going to pull you to pieces," says Habib.

And Simeon is quiet for a while. And then he says, "Yes. I think you may be right."

* * *

So Simeon had a pillar built.

It was not very tall to begin with. Theodoret says the first one was six cubits. A cubit is the length from your elbow to the tip of your middle finger, so about half a metre, a bit less. Six cubits is about the height of a ceiling and a bit. Tall enough that nobody could grab at him. Not so tall that he could not talk to people.

And at the top of it, a small flat platform. We do not know exactly how big. The old books do not say, and modern guesses vary. Not very big. Probably with a railing round it, so that he could lean, and so that he would not fall off in his sleep, if he ever slept. And people brought him what he needed, which was not much, by a ladder.

I like to think that the first person up the ladder was Habib. With a jug of water and a bit of his mother's flatbread. And that he stuck his head over the edge of the platform and looked at Simeon and said:

"Well? How is it?"

And Simeon looked out over the hills, the olive groves and the stone villages and the long line of the mountains in the west, all turning gold in the evening, and said: "Closer."

* * *

Now. Over the years the pillar got taller. Theodoret tells us the sequence. Six cubits. Then twelve. Then twenty-two. And by the time Theodoret was writing, thirty-six cubits. That is something like sixteen or seventeen metres, as near as we can work it out. As tall as a house of five or six storeys. Later writers say it ended up taller still, about forty cubits. People have argued over the exact figures for a long time, and I am not going to settle it tonight.

And some people, then as now, thought this was very odd. People wrote to Theodoret and said, what is this? What is a man doing on a pillar? Is this not just showing off?

And Theodoret answered them. And his answer is rather good. He said: look at the prophets. God told Isaiah to walk about without his cloak and his sandals, as a sign. God told Jeremiah to wear a wooden yoke on his neck, like an ox. God told Ezekiel to lie on one side for a very long time, and then on the other side. God told Hosea to marry a wife who would break his heart, as a picture of God's love for an unfaithful people. These were all very strange things to do. And God asked for them because people who will not listen to words will sometimes stop and look at something strange. And then, once they have stopped, they can hear.

Theodoret said it was like a king who puts a new picture on his coins, so that people will look at the coins again. The pillar, he said, was a new picture. People came to stare at the odd sight. And then they stayed to listen.

And what did they hear? This is the bit I like best.

Simeon, standing up on his pillar, taught the crowds twice a day. And Theodoret says he spoke to them about very ordinary things. Not about pillars. Not about fasting forty days. About how to live a good life down on the ground. To be honest. To be fair. Not to swear by God's name. To pray. To look after the poor. To trust God. He told them, in other words, the same things that any good parish priest would tell them. The very same things that old man in the church at Sisan had told a shepherd boy on a snowy day.

And in the afternoons, Theodoret says, he listened. People called up their troubles. Arguments about land. Quarrels in families. A dispute over a debt. And Simeon, from the top of the pillar, gave his judgment. And people accepted it, because who would argue with the man on the pillar? He was, in a way, the most trusted judge in Syria. He had no money and no property and nothing to gain. He could not be bribed. You cannot bribe a man who eats once a week and owns a leather tunic.

And sick people came, and he prayed for them. And Theodoret says that many of them were healed. And he is careful to say how. Simeon prayed. God acted. Simeon was not the one doing it. He was just the one asking, very persistently, from very high up.

* * *

Now, let us bring in Theodoret himself.

Theodoret was the bishop of a city called Cyrrhus, a couple of days to the north. He was a clever man, a scholar who wrote books about the Bible and about history. He knew Simeon well and visited him several times. And on one of those visits, he had the most alarming experience of his life, which he tells us about with a lovely honesty.

A great crowd of Bedouin had come to see Simeon. These were the tribes of the desert to the east, whom Theodoret calls Ishmaelites, the children of Ishmael. They had lived in the deserts for centuries and many of them still worshipped the old gods. And they came in their hundreds, even thousands, to see the holy man. And many of them, Theodoret says, listened to him, and gave up their old gods, and were baptised. They broke up their idols, there at the foot of the pillar, and asked to be taught the faith.

And on this particular day, Theodoret was standing at the foot of the pillar. And Simeon called out to the Bedouin and told them to go and get a blessing from the bishop. Which was kind of him. Except that the Bedouin were very enthusiastic. And they all rushed at Theodoret at once. They pressed in from every side. Some pulled at his beard. Some pulled at his robes. Some grabbed his hands. All of them wanted a blessing, now, this minute, and they were not going to queue.

And Theodoret, a bishop and a scholar and a man of dignity, was very nearly trampled flat.

And then from above came a great shout. Simeon, up on the pillar, shouting at them to stop. And they stopped. And they backed away. And Theodoret got up, a little crumpled, and gave out his blessings one at a time, in a much more orderly fashion.

I love that he wrote this down. Bishops do not usually write down the day they were nearly flattened by an affectionate crowd. Theodoret did. I think it is one of the reasons we can trust him.

* * *

And now we come back to the counting. The cold open, if you remember. The man who counted the bows.

That is in Theodoret too. Simeon prayed, Theodoret says, not just with words but with his whole body. He would stand with his arms out. Then he would bow down, deeply, his forehead almost touching his feet. Then he would stand. Again and again. For hours. And one of the men in Theodoret's party decided to count them one evening. And he got to one thousand two hundred and forty-four. And then he gave up.

Theodoret does not tell us the name of the man who counted. I like him. He is all of us, really. We start out wanting to measure the saints. How much did he pray? How long did he fast? How tall was the pillar? And sooner or later we lose count, and sit down on a stone, and just watch.

Habib, I think, never counted. He just brought the water. Every morning, for years. Up the ladder, down the ladder. He grew up. He got married, to a girl from the next village, who thought it was very funny that her husband took breakfast to a man on a pillar. He had children. And when his own son was about nine, Habib took him up the hill one morning, and handed him the jug, and said, "Go on. Up you go. Don't drop it."

And the boy climbed up, and stuck his head over the edge of the platform, and looked at the thin old man there, and said, "Is it heavy? Being up here?"

And Simeon smiled. "Not very," he said. "It reminds me to stay where I am."

* * *

There is one more story I want to tell you before we start to wind down. It is not from Theodoret. It comes from a later historian called Evagrius, who wrote about a hundred and thirty years after Simeon died. So it is not as close to the events, and you should hear it as the story that was told about Simeon later on.

Evagrius says that when the old monks of the desert heard about the man on the pillar, they were worried. A pillar? Nobody had ever done such a thing. Was this pride? Was this a man showing off? Was this some kind of trick of the devil, to make a monk think himself very special?

So they decided to test him. They sent a messenger to the foot of the pillar, with a message. The fathers of the desert say: come down. Stop this new and strange way of life. Come down and live like the rest of us.

And they told the messenger: if he refuses, if he argues, pull him down. Because then we will know it is pride. But if he obeys, if he is willing to come down, then leave him there. Because then we will know it is from God.

So the messenger shouted up the message. And Simeon, without a word of argument, put his foot on the ladder, and began to climb down.

And the messenger called out, quickly, "No, no. Stay. Stay where you are. It is from God."

I love that story. Whether it happened exactly that way, I cannot tell you. But it gets something true about Simeon, the same thing as the abbot at Teleda, and Meletius and the chain. He was strange. He was extreme. But he was not proud. When the Church told him to stop, he stopped. When the Church told him to stay, he stayed.

[pause]

So let us slow down now. Let us start to wind down, like the end of a long summer day on a hilltop in Syria.

Simeon stayed on his pillar for a long, long time. Year after year. Through summers so hot that the stones burned. Through winters with snow on the hills. Through rain. Through wind. He got older. Theodoret mentions that he had a bad sore on his foot, from all the standing. The pilgrims kept coming, more and more of them, until the hilltop was like a town, with inns and stables and markets at the bottom of the hill. Emperors wrote him letters, asking for his prayers and his advice. And still he taught twice a day, and listened in the afternoons, and prayed all night, and bowed and stood and bowed and stood.

And then, at last, in the year four hundred and fifty-nine, in the late summer, he died. He was about seventy years old. He had been up there, on one pillar or another, for more than thirty-five years. His disciples, the old account says, did not realise at first that he had died, because he was bowed in prayer, as he so often was. They waited. And he did not stand up. And then they knew that he had gone home to God.

His body was carried down from the pillar. And a great procession of soldiers and bishops and people took him to Antioch, the big city, where he was buried with enormous honour. The emperor in Constantinople wanted the body brought to the capital, but the people of Antioch asked to keep it, and they kept it. He was their saint.

And on the hill at Telanissos, some years later, the emperors built an enormous church round the pillar. Not one church, but four great halls, arranged in a cross, with the pillar in the very middle, under the open sky. It was one of the largest churches in the whole world at the time. And pilgrims kept coming, for centuries. And they chipped little bits off the pillar to take home, the way their great-great-grandparents had pulled little bits off his leather tunic. Chip, chip, chip, for hundreds of years, until the tall pillar was worn down to a stump. A boulder, really. About the height of a man.

And the church is still there. In ruins now, but beautiful ruins, pale stone arches and carved doorways standing on the hilltop in the sun. People call it Qalat Seman, which means the fortress of Simeon. It was damaged during the recent war in Syria, which is a sad thing to say. But it is still there. And the little stump of the pillar is still there, right in the middle, where the four halls meet, where a man once stood and prayed for thirty-five years.

* * *

Simeon was the first of the pillar saints, but he was not the last. Other men copied him. His own disciple Daniel went to Constantinople and stood on a pillar there, outside the capital. A century later there was another Simeon, Simeon the Younger, who lived on a pillar on a mountain near Antioch, and who is a different saint altogether, so do not mix them up. And for hundreds of years afterwards there were stylites, which is what the pillar saints are called, from the Greek word for a pillar, all over the Christian East.

And I want to say again, now that we are near the end, that none of us is meant to copy Simeon. That is not what the story is for. Simeon himself, as we heard, told the crowds to live good, honest, prayerful lives down on the ground. He did not tell them to build pillars.

But I think you can take something from him to bed tonight. And it is this.

He was a boy who asked an old man a question at church on a snowy day. Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God. What does that mean? How do you do that? And he spent the rest of his life trying to answer it. He dug down deep, deeper, deeper still, the way the dream told him. He got it wrong sometimes, and when sensible people told him so, he listened. And when the crowds came and pulled at him, he did not shout at them or send them away. He just went up a little higher, so that he could keep loving them without being pulled to pieces. And he talked to them, every day, about being kind and honest and fair.

So if you have had a day where everyone wanted a piece of you. If you have been pulled about like a coat on a peg. If you have lost count of all the things you had to do. You could think of Simeon. Standing quietly on his pillar, high above the noise, as the sun goes down over the hills of Syria. Bowing, and standing, and bowing, and standing. Not to show off. Just to stay close.

And you could think of Habib, who never existed, but who stands tonight for all the real, unnamed people of Telanissos who carried the water up the ladder, every morning, for thirty-five years. Nobody wrote their names down. But they did the work. And I like to think that God, who counts everything, did not lose count.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Most of tonight's story comes from Theodoret, the bishop of Cyrrhus, who wrote a book of the lives of the holy monks of Syria. His chapter on Simeon was written while Simeon was still alive and still on the pillar, and Theodoret had visited him himself. Robert Price translated it into English. There are two other early Lives of Simeon: one in Greek, by a man called Antonius, who says he was Simeon's disciple, and a long one in Syriac. Robert Doran has translated all three together. The historian Evagrius, writing about a hundred and thirty years later, tells the story of the desert fathers who ordered him to come down.

What is history. Simeon's birth at Sisan and his boyhood with the sheep, his years at the monastery of Teleda and his being asked to leave, his time at Telanissos, the enclosure on the hilltop and the chain taken off at Meletius's word, the pillars getting taller, the crowds from many nations, the pictures in Rome, his teaching and his judging of disputes, the Bedouin, Theodoret's near trampling, the counting of the bows, his death in four hundred and fifty-nine, the burial at Antioch and the great church built round the pillar, whose ruins still stand.

What is told as the old writers' report. The dream of digging, the untouched loaves of the first Lent and the bugs under the leather are Theodoret's report, and he says he had some of it from Simeon himself. The test of obedience is Evagrius's report, from later.

What we left out. The other early Lives tell some harder stories, including a sad one about Simeon's mother, which did not seem right for bedtime and are left for another night.

What we invented. Habib, his family, his goat, his ladder, his breakfasts, and everything he says, and the words of the abbot at Teleda. Theodoret's words are in our own plain paraphrase.

That's the story of Saint Simeon the Stylite for tonight. His feast is on the first of September in the Orthodox Church, and on the fifth of January in the Roman Martyrology.

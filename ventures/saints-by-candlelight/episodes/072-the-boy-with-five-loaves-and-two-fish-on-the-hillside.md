Picture a hillside in the late afternoon, in the spring, with the grass so green that it looks wet.

Thousands of people are sitting on it, in little patches, like a field after a very large picnic that has not started yet. There is a low murmur, the sound of a great many people trying not to say how hungry they are. And in the middle of all those people, there is a boy, holding a small cloth bundle in both hands, with his heart going fast.

Because a man he has never met has just looked at him, and the man is smiling, and the man's friend has said the words, Is that your lunch?

And the boy has to decide what to say.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Gospel of John, chapter six, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, with the other three Gospels beside it, and we will tell it as it is told there. The boy's morning, and the small things he thinks and says, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

We begin, as the Gospels do, with a lot of people.

You may know this story already. It is one of the best known in the whole New Testament, and it has a peculiar distinction. Nearly everything Jesus did is told by one or two of the four Gospel writers, and some by three. But this one, the feeding of the five thousand, is told by all four. Matthew tells it, and Mark, and Luke, and John. When four people who each had a different reason for writing a book all stop and tell the same story, I think we may fairly say it was the sort of thing nobody could leave out.

And yet, if you read all four, you find that only one of them tells you about the boy.

Matthew, Mark and Luke say that the disciples had five loaves and two fish. They do not say where they got them. It is only John, the last of the four, who tells us that they came from a boy. A single sentence. A boy who has five barley loaves and two fish. Then the boy is gone from the page, and we never hear his name, or where he lived, or whether he ever told his mother. Which seems a little unfair to him, given how much depended on his lunch.

So tonight, we are going to follow the boy. We shall not make up what Jesus did, because that is the heart of the story, and we shall tell that exactly the way the Gospels tell it. But the boy's morning, and what he thought on the way up the hill, the Gospels leave blank, and I shall fill in a very small amount of it, and tell you when I do.

First, where are we?

We are by the Sea of Galilee. It is not really a sea. It is a big freshwater lake in the north of the land of Israel, about thirteen miles long, and shaped a little like a harp, which is why one old name for it is the Sea of Chinnereth, from the Hebrew word for a harp. Round the edge of it are small towns and fishing villages, and in those days many of the people in them were fishermen, and many others were farmers, and the rest, I imagine, were people who sold things to the fishermen and the farmers.

Jesus has been teaching and healing in that country for some time. And the Gospels tell us that a large crowd has been following him, because they had seen the signs that he did on the sick. People do follow, when they have seen something like that. Some come out of love, and some out of curiosity, and some because their neighbour is going and they do not want to be left out. John also mentions, in passing, that the Passover is near. That is the great spring feast, when the whole country thinks about bread, and about being brought out of slavery. Keep that in the back of your mind. John clearly wants us to.

Now Jesus goes across the lake, to a quiet place on the far shore, and goes up on the hillside, and sits down there with his disciples. Mark tells us why. The disciples have just come back from a journey, full of news, and there are so many people coming and going that they cannot even stop to eat. So Jesus says, Come away by yourselves to a deserted place, and rest a while.

I want you to notice that. It is a very kind sentence. The first thing he says, in this story, is not a miracle. It is, Come and rest.

But the people see them go. And the crowd goes round the shore on foot, hurrying, and arrives before them.

[pause]

* * *

Let us leave the hillside for a moment and go to the boy.

I shall make him about ten. John's word for him is a small word, the sort that can mean a young boy, or a young servant, and nobody can say for certain which he was. I like to think that he is a boy, and I shall say so, but you may think otherwise. He lives, let us say, in a village not far from the lake, in a house with a flat roof and a courtyard and a fig tree, and he has a mother, and a little sister, and a donkey that does not like him.

This morning, he is up before the sun. Word has come round the village the night before that the teacher everyone has been talking about is on the other side of the water, and that half the village is going out to see him. The boy has begged and pleaded, and his mother has said, in the voice that mothers have always had, We shall see.

So he is up early, and dressed, and sitting in the courtyard being very good, and he has not said a word about it, in case he spoils it.

His mother comes out with a face that is half cross and half smiling, and she says, You will not go anywhere without eating.

And he says, I will not be hungry.

And she says, You are a boy. You will be hungry before you have gone a mile.

She goes in, and comes out again, with a cloth.

Now I should like you to see what is in the cloth, because this is the whole story. In it are five loaves. They are small, flat, round loaves, rather like the ones you might get in a shop that sells flatbread, about the size of a large hand. And they are barley.

That matters. In that country, the best bread was made from wheat. Barley was what poor people ate. It is coarser, and darker, and heavier, and it has a taste that I would call honest. It is the sort of bread that you eat because it is cheap, and filling, and because you have a family. Somewhere in the Old Testament, the prophet Elisha feeds a hundred men on twenty barley loaves, so it was food people shared, but it was never grand. If you wanted to say a family was not rich, you would say they ate barley. And this family, I think we may say, is not rich.

And with the loaves, the boy's mother puts in two fish. John's word for these is not the ordinary word for a fish. It is a word that means something eaten with bread, a relish, a little extra. Many readers think they would have been small fish, perhaps dried or pickled, the sort you could keep in a cloth in your hand all day and not be sorry for it. It is how you made a plain loaf into a proper meal. That is all it is. Five small loaves, and two small fish.

She ties the corners of the cloth together, and she gives it to him, and she says, That is for you. Not for your friends. Not to give away to the first person who says they are hungry. You are not to be silly with it.

And the boy, who is ten, and who has meant to be silly with it since the day he was born, says, Yes, mother.

She watches him go down the lane. And then, because she is a mother, she calls after him, Wear your cloak!

[pause]

* * *

I do not know how the boy got across the lake, or round it. The Gospels do not say, and there are two ways it could have been done. Mark tells us that the people ran on foot along the shore and got there ahead of the boat, so I think that is the better guess. It is a long walk, and a boy would have found it a very long one.

So let us say that he goes with his neighbours. There is his friend, who is a year older than him and knows everything, and a fisherman with a bad knee, and a woman who is carrying a child who has been ill for a very long time. And I imagine that they do not talk very much, because they are walking, and because it is a serious sort of walk. People do not go that far on foot for a small thing.

When they get there, in the early afternoon, the boy sees a green hill and a great many people, and a small group in the middle of them. And he has never seen so many people in one place in his life.

I should like to say something here about what the Gospels tell us of the man in the middle, because it is the reason the crowd is there.

When Jesus lifts his eyes and sees them, he does not sigh. He does not say, Oh, not now. Mark says that he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. And Luke says that he welcomed them, and spoke to them about the kingdom of God, and cured those who had need of healing.

So that is how the afternoon goes. It goes with teaching, hour after hour. The boy sits on the grass, not very close, with his friend, and the cloth on his knee, and listens as hard as a ten-year-old can listen, which is not quite as hard as he would like, because the sun is warm, and the grass is soft, and there is a beetle on his sandal.

And he is fine, for a while. He has his lunch. He does not touch it, because his mother told him not to be silly with it, and he has decided that being silly with it includes eating it too soon.

But the afternoon goes on. And then it begins to go the way afternoons go.

[pause]

* * *

Now we come to the disciples.

John says that when Jesus looked up and saw the great crowd coming, he said to Philip, Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat?

And then John adds a sentence that I find rather lovely. He says, He said this to test him, for he himself knew what he would do.

So Jesus is not worried. That is worth saying. The one person on that hillside who knows what is about to happen is the one who is asking the question. He is not asking for information. He is giving Philip a chance.

Philip is a practical man, and he does what a practical man does. He does the sum. He says, Two hundred denarii would not buy enough bread for each of them to get a little.

Now a denarius was a Roman silver coin, and it was the usual wage for a day's work. Jesus himself says so, in one of his parables, when a landowner hires workers for a denarius a day. So two hundred denarii is two hundred days of a labourer's pay. That is most of a working year, for a man with a family. And Philip says, quite rightly, that even all of that would not do it.

I want to say a word for Philip. He gets a hard time, sometimes, as the disciple who did the arithmetic and missed the point. But I think he is doing something honest. He looks at the crowd, and he looks at the money, and he says plainly what is true. It is not enough. It is not even close. And he says it out loud, to the one person who can do something about it. I would call that a kind of prayer.

The other three Gospels give us a slightly different version of the same moment, and they are worth putting beside John's. In Mark and Matthew, it is the disciples who come to Jesus, late in the day, and say, This is a desert place, and the hour is now late. Send them away, so that they may go into the villages and buy themselves something to eat. And Jesus says, You give them something to eat.

Now think about how that sounds. They have been with him a long time, and they know he can do remarkable things, and he says, You give them something to eat. It is the sort of thing a teacher says to a pupil who thinks the answer is to give up. And they answer, quite sensibly, that they would have to go and spend two hundred denarii on bread. And he says, How many loaves have you? Go and see.

Which brings us, at last, to Andrew.

[pause]

* * *

Andrew is one of the fishermen. He is the brother of Simon Peter, and he was one of the first to follow Jesus, and he has a habit that the Gospels mention more than once. He brings people. In the first chapter of John, he brings his brother Peter. Later, in chapter twelve, he brings some Greek visitors who want to meet Jesus. And here, in chapter six, he brings a boy.

I think that is wonderful. In all of this crowd, with its thousands, Andrew has gone looking, and he has found someone who has something. He does not know what it is worth. He is not at all sure that it is any use. But he brings it.

So I imagine him, an ordinary man in a fisherman's tunic, going along the edge of the crowd, asking people quietly, Does anyone have any food? And people saying no, or looking away, or saying, We brought enough for ourselves.

And then he comes to a boy with a cloth on his knee.

Now here is the part I like best, and here is where the boy has to decide.

Andrew crouches down. And he says, in a friendly way, Hello. What have you got there?

And the boy, who has been warned that he must not be silly, looks at the cloth. He looks at his friend, who says nothing, which is unusual. He looks at the man in front of him, who is a stranger, but a kind one. And he looks, past the man, at the person sitting in the middle of the hill, who is looking at him.

And he knows that it is not very much. It is five barley loaves and two small fish. It is not even a good lunch. It is what a poor family sends with a boy who has said he will not be hungry.

But he does not hide it. He does not say, It is mine. He does not say, I need it. He holds out the cloth.

And that is all that the Gospel tells us. Andrew says to Jesus, There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish. And then, in the same breath, because he is an honest man, he says the thing that all of us would think. But what are they among so many?

I should like you to hear how honest that is. Andrew does not pretend. He does not say, This will be enough, Lord. He says what he sees, and he says it as a question, and he leaves it in front of Jesus. As if to say, I have found what there is. It is not much. I have no idea what you will do with it. But here it is.

And that, I think, is as good a model of prayer as there is.

[pause]

* * *

Jesus says, Make the people sit down.

Now there is a good deal of detail here, and the Gospels give us all of it, so we shall too.

John says that there was much grass in the place. Mark says, in a phrase that I think nobody would invent, that it was green grass. It is spring, and the hills are green, which is the one season of the year when they are. And Mark also tells us that Jesus has them sit down in groups, by hundreds and by fifties. I like to think of how that must have looked from the top of the hill. A great green slope, divided into rows and squares of people in coloured cloaks, like a patchwork. Like a field of flowers, one old reader called it.

I have often thought it must have been very orderly, and quite surprisingly calm. Nobody pushes. Nobody rushes for the front. A great crowd, who have walked a long way, and who are hungry, sit down quietly in rows on the grass and wait. I do not know quite what made that possible. The Gospels do not tell us. But I notice that the thing Jesus does first is to make them sit down, and be at ease, and be counted, as if they were guests at a feast and not a mob at a fair. Which, as it turns out, is what they are.

John tells us the number. The men sat down, he says, about five thousand in number. Matthew adds a small phrase, which I think we ought not to skip. Five thousand men, besides women and children. So the whole crowd was larger than five thousand, though nobody counted the others, which tells you something about how people counted in those days, and which I think the women and children probably noticed.

The boy sits down in the middle of a row of fifty, with his friend on one side and a stranger on the other, and he is still holding out his hands. Because Andrew has gone. And the cloth has gone with him.

He sees it passed up the hill, from hand to hand, until it comes to the man in the middle. And he cannot take his eyes off it.

[pause]

* * *

Now we come to the moment, and I shall tell it as plainly as I can, because the Gospels tell it plainly.

Jesus takes the loaves.

He does not do anything dramatic. He does not raise his arms. He takes the bread in his hands, as a father does at the beginning of a meal. John says, He gave thanks. The other three say that he looked up to heaven, and blessed, and broke the loaves. And then he gave them to the disciples, and the disciples gave them to the people. And the same with the fish.

That is what it says. He took, he gave thanks, he broke, and he gave.

I should like you to notice how ordinary those four things are. They are what any head of a household does at a meal. You take the bread. You say the thanks. You break it, because that is how you share a loaf. And you hand it round. There is nothing in it that a father in a village could not do on any evening of the week.

And yet, a long time afterwards, the church heard those four words again, at another table, on another night, in an upper room. And that is why the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and many others, have always heard a second meaning in this story. We shall come back to it in the notes. But it is nothing I have invented, and you will hear it said in both traditions, and it is quite right to say that John, who was a careful writer, put the word for giving thanks here, and wants us to catch the echo.

Now, what happens next, the Gospels do not explain. And I shall not either.

I shall tell you what they say. They say that the disciples went on giving out the bread, and the fish, and that the people ate. John says, as much as they wanted. Not a crumb each. Not a little to be going on with. As much as they wanted.

And that is all. There is no sentence that says how it happened. Nobody in the four Gospels says, And then the bread grew. They only say that Jesus took what there was, and gave thanks, and gave it, and that everybody ate, and that when everybody had had enough, there was bread over.

I think the Gospels leave it there on purpose. It is not a trick to be explained. It is a gift to be received, and we are meant to look at the one who gives, and not at the loaves. So I shall do the same.

But I can tell you what the boy sees, because that is the part that is mine.

He sees the stranger beside him get a piece of bread, and break it, and hand half of it on. He sees his friend, who knows everything, take a piece of fish and say nothing at all, for the second time that day. He sees the woman with the child take a piece of loaf and put a crumb of it in the child's mouth. And then he gets his own piece. It is a piece of barley bread. It is the bread his mother baked.

He knows it is hers. He can tell by the little burnt place on the crust.

And he sits there, with the bread in his hands, and he does not eat it for a minute. He looks at it.

Because it is the same loaf. And it is feeding all of these people.

[pause]

* * *

Now let us take a breath, and let the story slow down.

John tells us what happened when they had all had enough. He says that Jesus said to his disciples, Gather up the fragments left over, so that nothing may be lost.

I love that sentence. After all of that, after the largest picnic in the history of the world, with more than five thousand fed from five loaves, the very next thing he says is about the crumbs. Not about the crowd. Not about the wonder of it. About the leftover bread. Do not let it go to waste. As if every piece of it mattered.

Perhaps you are the sort of person who has been told, as a child, not to waste your food. I was never a child, so I can only imagine it. But I think this is where that idea comes from, in one place. That what has been given is not to be thrown away. That a crumb is not nothing.

So the disciples go among the groups, and gather up the broken pieces. And all four Gospels tell us how much. Twelve baskets full.

Twelve baskets. One for each of the twelve disciples, as many readers have noticed. The disciples who said they had nothing, and who said, What are they among so many, go home each with a basket heavier than anything they started with.

And I should like you to see that this is where the story turns around. It began with Philip saying that two hundred denarii would not be enough. It ends with the leftovers filling twelve baskets. Which is more than the boy had, in the beginning, by a very long way. A boy gave away a lunch. And the crumbs were more than the lunch.

I have no idea what became of the boy's cloth. I should like to think that somebody handed it back to him, empty and folded, with a smile. I do not think I am saying anything that the Gospels would mind.

[pause]

* * *

The evening comes on.

You can feel it coming over the hill, as it does in that country in the spring. The light turns gold, and then a pale rose, and the green of the grass goes grey. The sea, down below, goes the colour of a pearl. The great crowd, who have eaten as much as they wanted, are not hurrying anywhere. There is the sound of a lot of people who are comfortable, and full, and a little amazed.

And John tells us what they say. He says that when the people saw the sign that he had done, they said, This is indeed the prophet who is to come into the world.

They are right, as far as it goes. They are thinking, I imagine, of what Moses said, that God would raise up a prophet like him. And they are thinking of the manna, the bread that fell from heaven in the wilderness, because that is the other great story about a hungry crowd in a deserted place. John is going to come back to that in the very next part of his chapter, and he will have Jesus say something far greater than any of them expect. But that is for another night.

Then comes a sentence in John that is rather sad. He says, Perceiving then that they were about to come and take him by force to make him king, Jesus withdrew again to the mountain by himself.

They want to make him a king. They have just been given a good dinner, and they would like to be given one every day. And I do not blame them. I would like it too. But he knows what sort of king he is to be, and it is not the sort they want. So he goes up the mountain alone, to pray, and leaves them to their cloaks and their baskets, and sends his disciples across the lake in a boat.

Which, if you have been listening to this series for a while, is the beginning of another story, about a storm, and a man who walked out on the water, and another man who tried. We told that one some time ago. It is the very next thing that happens.

And the boy?

[pause]

* * *

The boy has his piece of bread in his hand, and the sky is turning to stars.

Everybody around him is beginning to stand up and brush the grass off their cloaks. There is a good deal of talking, and a baby crying, and a man somewhere on the slope who is telling everybody who will listen that he knew it, he knew it all along. And the boy's friend, who knows everything, says nothing. He has not said anything for a very long time, which the boy thinks is the greatest miracle of the day.

And then the boy gets up too.

And he goes home. He walks back around the shore, in the dark, with all the neighbours, with the fisherman with the bad knee, and the woman with the child, who is asleep now, with her head on her mother's shoulder. And they walk quietly, because it is a serious sort of walk. People do not talk much, after a thing like that.

And his mother is standing at the door, with a lamp.

She has been standing there a long time. She heard, before he did, that something had happened by the lake, because news goes faster than a boy. And she sees him coming up the lane, in the dark, and she does not say anything cross.

She says, You are very late.

And he says, I know.

And she says, Did you eat?

And the boy, who is ten, and who has meant for years to be silly with everything he was ever given, looks up at his mother, and says, Mother, I gave them the lunch.

And she looks at him.

And I should like to tell you what she said, but the Gospel does not tell us, and I do not know. So I shall say only that I like to think that the lamp, which was tilted a little, did not wobble. And that she put her hand on his hair. And that he went in, and the door was shut, and somewhere in the house there was a small piece of barley bread on a shelf, saved.

[pause]

* * *

Now, before the night gets any later, let us stop and be quiet for a moment.

I have told you a story about a boy with a small lunch. And I want to say one thing about it, and only one, so that you can take it to bed with you.

There is a great deal in the world that is too big for us. There are crowds, and needs, and sums that do not come out. And most of us, most days, are Philip. We look at what is needed, and we look at what we have, and we say, honestly, that it is not enough. And that is not wrong. It is true.

But there is also a boy in this story, and he does something that is very simple. He holds out what he has. It is not much. It is cheap barley bread and a couple of small fish. And it is not he who feeds the crowd. But the Gospels tell us that when Jesus fed the crowd, he began with what was in the boy's hands.

So if there is something that you have, tonight, and it is small, and you are not sure that it is any use, I hope you will not think that it does not matter. The Gospels do not say that it does not matter. They say that Andrew found it, and that Jesus took it, and that he gave thanks.

And if there is something that you need, that is too big, and that you cannot do, I hope you will hold out your hands for that too.

[pause]

The lamp has gone out in the boy's house. The hill is empty, with a few crumbs on it that nobody found, and the birds will be glad of those in the morning. The lake is very still, and a long way out on it, there is one small boat, with a lantern. And above the whole country, which has a great deal on its mind, the stars have come out, very large and very close, as they do in the spring.

Nobody is hungry now.

Rest well.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is told from the Gospel of John, chapter six, verses one to fifteen, with Matthew chapter fourteen, Mark chapter six and Luke chapter nine beside it. It is Scripture for both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches, and we told it straight.

What is Scripture: the great crowd following Jesus because of the signs he did on the sick; the crossing to the far side of the sea and the hillside; the Passover near; Jesus' question to Philip, and John's remark that it was to test him; Philip's answer about two hundred denarii; Andrew, the brother of Simon Peter, and the boy with five barley loaves and two fish, and Andrew's question, what are they among so many; the command to sit down; the much grass; the groups of hundreds and fifties in Mark; the men numbered about five thousand, and Matthew's note, besides women and children; Jesus taking the loaves, giving thanks, breaking and giving; the people eating as much as they wanted; the command to gather the fragments so that nothing be lost; twelve baskets; the crowd's words about the prophet; and Jesus withdrawing alone to the mountain when they would make him king. Mark's compassion, and his words, you give them something to eat, are Scripture too.

What we flagged as not in the text: John's word for the boy can mean a boy or a young servant, and we chose a boy, and said so. We do not know how the boy came to be there, or where he was from, and the Gospels say nothing of his family. The word for the fish means something eaten with bread, and the picture of small dried or pickled fish is a common reading, not a statement of the text. The shape of the lake and its old name are not in tonight's passage. How the bread was multiplied the Gospels do not explain, and neither did we.

What we invented: the boy's morning, his mother and sister and the donkey, the packing of the cloth, the walk, the friend who knew everything, the little burnt place on the crust, the evening at the door with the lamp, and the small things that he and his mother say. None of it changes what happens or what it means. We were also careful to say that his mother's reply at the end is not known.

What is tradition, or a reading: the hearing, in both churches, of the four actions, took, gave thanks, broke and gave, as an echo of the Eucharist, and of the twelve baskets as pointing to the twelve; and the link John makes, in the rest of his chapter, to the manna in the wilderness. We named these as readings, and did not argue them. Some later writers have given the boy a name, or told what became of him, and none of that is in the Gospels, so we left it out.

For further reading, the Gospel of John, chapter six, is the best place, and tonight's verses are short enough to read aloud in a few minutes. The rest of that chapter is well worth the time.

[pause]

That's the story of the boy with the five loaves for tonight. He is not remembered with a feast day, as he is not named, so we shall name none, and the feeding is read in the churches at different times, so you may wish to ask at your own parish. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Give us this day our daily bread, and forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us. And may the one who takes what is small and gives thanks over it, keep you tonight, and be near you in the morning. Goodnight, and God bless.

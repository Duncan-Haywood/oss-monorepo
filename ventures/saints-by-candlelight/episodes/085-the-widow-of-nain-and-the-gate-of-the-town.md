Imagine the quietest procession you have ever seen.

No band. No speeches. A good many people, walking slowly, most of them looking at the ground. In front, four men carrying a board with a long shape on it, wrapped in cloth. And behind the board, alone, even though she is surrounded by people, a woman who walks as though someone else were moving her legs.

Nothing is going to hurry her. Nothing could. She is going to the place where the road ends, outside the gate, and she knows exactly what she will do when she gets there. She has done it once before.

And then, from the other direction, there is a crowd, coming up the hill. Much louder. Much happier. Walking the other way.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the widow of Nain, from the seventh chapter of Luke's Gospel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Luke tells it in only seven verses, and gives the woman no name and no spoken words, so a few small things are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original: her morning, the talk on the road, and the long walk back. What the Lord says, what he does, and what the crowd cries out follow Luke in plain words. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

First, the place.

Nain is a small town in the hills of lower Galilee. If you stood in Nazareth and looked south and a little east, you would be looking in the right direction, and if you walked, it would take you most of a morning. It sits on the north slope of a ridge that rises quietly out of a great flat plain, and the plain is the Valley of Jezreel, which is the best farmland in the country. In the spring, I am told, you can see for miles in every direction, green and gold, and the air smells of wheat and warm earth. It is a nice place to live. It is a nice place to be buried, too, if you want to put it that way, and the people of Nain did, because the hillside behind the town is soft stone, and for a very long time they have cut their tombs into it. You can still see the little doorways in the rock today.

Now, what was it like to live there? Quite ordinary, which is the point. Houses of mud brick and stone with flat roofs. A well, or a spring, which was the news exchange. Olive trees. A few fig trees. Goats, which are the one animal I would say every ancient town had in numbers far beyond what was sensible. A road going out of the gate, and one coming in. And a lot of people who knew a great deal about each other.

That last part matters for tonight. A town like that is a very kind place and a very close place, both at once. If you have a good day, forty people know by noon. And if you have the worst day of your life, forty people know that too, and every one of them will come to your door.

Luke tells us only that it was a town called Nain, and that the Lord went to it. He had come from Capernaum, the town on the lake where he lived for a time, which is a good long day's walk away, perhaps twenty miles, by the road through the hills. I say perhaps, because the exact road is our guess. With him went his disciples and what Luke calls a large crowd. People were beginning to follow him in numbers by then. He had just healed the servant of a Roman officer, and word had gone ahead of him, as word does, faster than a donkey.

So that is one half of our meeting. A tired, dusty company, coming up a hill at the end of a day's walk, in good spirits, talking. And I would like you to keep them in your mind for a minute, and put them down just outside the town, while we go in through the gate the other way, and see who is coming out.

[pause]

* * *

Let us start in the morning, in the house.

I will tell you plainly that this part is ours, and a guess. I do not know what the house was like, or what the woman was called, and Luke does not either. I am going to call her the widow, because that is what Luke calls her, and because I think it is what her neighbours called her too, and it has been, you will notice, a rather heavy word to carry. It tells you who she lost. It does not tell you who she is.

She is up before the light. She has not really slept, she has been sitting. In the next room is her son, and he has been dead since yesterday.

I want to say that gently, because there is nothing in it for us to be afraid of. This is a bedtime story, and it is also a true one, and sooner or later all the true stories come to the same door. In the hot countries of the old world, the dead were buried quickly, usually on the day of the death or the day after, because there was no other way of keeping them. So there was no long vigil. There was a night, and there was a morning, and there was a walk.

He was a young man. Luke uses a word that means a young man in his strength, not a boy, and not a middle-aged one. Perhaps twenty. Perhaps a little older. Strong enough to have worked in the fields, or learned a trade. We are not told how he died, and I think that is a mercy, for the story is not about that. It is only said, in Luke's short way, that he was the only son of his mother, and she was a widow.

Now, those two facts together would have meant more in that world than they do in ours. A woman who had lost her husband was in a hard place already. Without a husband, and without a grown son, a woman in an old village might have no one to own land on her behalf, no one to speak for her at the gate, no one to bring home the harvest. The Law of Moses is full of tender and stern instructions about the widow, to leave the gleanings of the field for her, not to take her cloak as a pledge, not to deny her justice. The psalm says that God is the father of orphans and the protector of widows. The fact that the Law had to say it so often should tell us how often it was needed. A widow was someone the whole community was meant to look after, and a community is a sum of ordinary people, who are not always as good as the Law asks.

And she had one son. He was her husband's name, and her bread, and her old age, and her company, all in one tall young man who went out in the morning and came home at night and left his boots in the doorway. And this morning, the boots are still in the doorway, and nobody has moved them.

[pause]

Her neighbours have been in since before dawn. This is the thing about a town like Nain. Within an hour of the news, the house is full. Someone has brought bread. Someone has brought a jar of water. Someone has swept the floor without being asked, which, if you have ever lived in a house where something terrible has happened, you will know is one of the most loving things a person can do. A friend of hers, whom I will call the baker, because it is useful to have someone to talk to, sits down beside her and does not say anything at all.

I think the baker is the wisest person in this part of the story. She does not say, he is in a better place. She does not say, it was the will of God, though she believes it. She does not say, you must be strong. She sits on the floor with the widow, and holds her hand, and lets the silence be as big as it is.

Because, you see, there is not one thing in the world that anyone can say that will help. And everyone in that room knows it. And all the same, they have come.

I think that is a kind of holiness, to turn up when you cannot fix anything. We do not make enough of it. When we remember the saints, we remember the ones who did great things, who preached to emperors and built churches and tamed wolves. But for every saint with a wolf there are a thousand sitting on the floor of a kitchen, saying nothing, holding a hand. Their names are not recorded. Heaven has the list.

[pause]

* * *

Now the walk.

When the time comes, the men of the town do what men of the town have always done. They lift him, and they lay him on the bier. That is the word Luke uses, a Greek word that means a bier, a plain open board, or a basket, or a frame, on which a body is carried, wrapped in linen. It is not a coffin with a lid. It is open to the sky, and everyone can see that there is a person on it. And they carry him on their shoulders, four men, and the town falls in behind.

You should imagine this is not a tidy little procession. Luke says, a considerable crowd from the town was with her. That is a lot of people, for a small place. In a village where everyone knew everyone, a funeral was an obligation, and it was also a way of loving someone. The whole town goes, because the whole town has watched the boy grow up. There was the day he learned to walk. There was the day he fell out of the olive tree. There was the day he first carried a load alone. These are the things that a village remembers, and tonight they are all being walked out of the gate.

I imagine that a few of them cry out, as mourners did, and that most of them do not. I imagine the older women, a little ahead, with their heads covered, saying the old words. I imagine that the men are not saying much, and that the youngest ones are the most awkward, because they were his friends, and nobody has ever told them what you do with your hands at a time like this.

And I imagine the widow, in the middle, just behind the bier, close enough to touch it. She does not cry out. Perhaps she did, in the night, and now there is nothing left. She walks, and each step is a step she does not want to take, and it is also a step she has to take, because the one who is on the board in front of her is hers, and no one else in the world is going to walk him there.

They come to the gate.

Every old town had a gate, and the gate was the centre of the town in a way that is hard for us to picture. It was where business was done. It was where the elders sat in the shade and judged cases. It was where news came in and where news went out. And it was the border between the living and the dead, because the tombs were always outside. To go out through the gate with a body was to walk across a line. On one side, the world you know. On the other side, the field, the hillside, the little dark door in the rock.

So here is the widow, at the gate of her own town, about to step across that line. And on the other side of it, on the road up from the south, in the evening light, is a crowd.

[pause]

* * *

Two companies are about to meet. Let me tell you what each can see.

The crowd on the road sees a funeral. They can see the board, and the shape on it, and the people dressed plainly and walking slowly. They know at once what they are looking at. And I think they do what polite people do, which is to step off the road, and go quiet, and let the sorrowful company pass. That is what you do. You do not push in front of a funeral.

The people of Nain see a crowd. A big one, dusty from the road, with a man in the middle of it, though they do not know which, and they probably do not care. Some of them have heard of him. Most of them have had a very bad day, and they would very much like to be left alone. It is one more thing that is happening, in a day full of things that should not be.

And in the middle of all of this, the Lord sees her.

That is what Luke says. It is just a single word. When the Lord saw her. Not when he saw the board, not when he saw the crowd. Her. Of all the people at the gate, in all that noise and dust, his eyes went to a woman who was not asking him for a thing.

I want to dwell on that, because it is one of the loveliest details in the Gospel. Almost everyone else who comes to Jesus in the Gospels comes asking. The Roman officer sends friends to ask. Jairus falls at his feet and asks. The blind man by the road shouts. The woman in the crowd, with her long illness, reaches out. They know who he is, or at least they have heard, and they come to him with their need in their hands.

This woman does not ask. She does not know who he is. She has not heard of him, or she has but it is not on her mind. She is not looking for a miracle. She has stopped looking for anything. She is only walking out of the gate behind her son. And it is the Lord who looks at her first.

Luke says, he had compassion on her.

The Greek word is a very strong one. It is a word that comes from the inward parts, the gut, the place where you feel a thing before you think it. Luke uses it only a few times, and every time it is a moment where the Lord's whole heart goes out, and the Gospel wants us to see it. The good Samaritan has it, on the road to Jericho. The father in the story of the prodigal son has it, when he sees his boy a long way off. And here it is, in the first scene, the first thing the Lord feels. Before he does anything, he is moved.

I think that is worth keeping for the night. Before any miracle in this story, there is a look, and a feeling. It is not that the Lord does a thing because it is a good time for a sign. It is that he sees someone in pain, and it matters to him, all of it, right down to the root. It is the very beginning of the story, and it will be the very end of it too.

[pause]

* * *

He says to her, do not weep.

Stop for a second on that, because it is an odd thing to say, if you hear it the wrong way. If a stranger came up to you at the funeral of your only child and said, do not weep, you would be entitled to be angry. We say it all the time, in a kindly way, to children who have dropped an ice cream. We do not say it, or we should not, to a woman who is walking behind her son's body.

But this is not the stranger who says it. He says it as one who knows what he is about to do. It is not, stop crying because it is no use. It is, stop crying, because I am here. It is the first sign of the thing that comes next. A very old promise, from the prophet Isaiah, says that God will wipe the tears from every face. Here, at the gate of Nain, at the end of an ordinary day, someone is beginning to do it.

And then he does something that makes the whole crowd draw in its breath.

He goes up to the bier, and he touches it.

You have to understand what this meant. In the Law of Moses, and in the careful customs of the day, to touch a dead body, or the thing that carried it, was to become unclean for seven days. Not wicked, not guilty. Only set apart for a time from the worship of the Temple, until the days of cleansing had passed. It was a rule meant to teach, in a physical way, that death is not natural to the people of God, that it is an enemy and not a friend. And most people took very great care not to touch.

The Lord puts out his hand and lays it on the board.

The bearers stop. Luke says that, and it is a small and wonderful touch. They stop, because they do not know what else to do. Four grown men who have been carrying a heavy load all morning, in step, with a purpose, stand still in the road with their shoulders under the board, and look at one another. The mourners behind them stop too. The line folds up like a concertina. And in the silence that follows, nobody says a word, and it seems to me that even the birds go quiet.

There is nothing in all of this that Luke tells us about faith. He does not say that the widow believed. He does not say that anyone did. He does not say that a word of prayer was spoken. It is, entirely, a thing the Lord does, and it is given, as a gift, to someone who did not ask. That is one of the things I most like about the story. It is not a reward. It is a mercy. And mercy, in the Gospel, does not wait for you to deserve it.

[pause]

* * *

Then he speaks.

He says, young man, I say to you, arise.

Seven words in English. Only three in the Greek. Think how quiet they are. He does not shout. He does not call down a thunder. He does not go through a long ritual, or wave his hands, or kneel on the ground and wrestle with God. He speaks to the dead young man the way you would speak to someone asleep who was going to be late for breakfast.

That is a thing worth setting beside the old stories of the prophets, because there are two of them that Luke seems to have been thinking of. Long before, in the land of Zarephath, a widow lost her only son, and the prophet Elijah took the boy up to the room where he was staying, and stretched himself over the child three times, and cried to the Lord, and the child lived. And later, in the town of Shunem, which is just on the other side of this very ridge, a few miles from Nain, as the hills go, the prophet Elisha did much the same for a woman who had lost her son. We told that one in an earlier episode, if you want to listen again. In both of those stories, the prophet prays. In both of those, there is effort. There is a struggle. The prophet is asking God for something, and he is clearly not sure how it will come out.

And in this story, the Lord does not ask. He says, I say to you. It is a command, and it is addressed to the dead man, and it is spoken as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Christians have always seen in that a quiet sign of who it is that is walking up the road. A prophet asks God for life. Here is someone who gives it.

I will say again that this is a reading, a way the Church has understood it, and Luke does not spell it out. What Luke does say, at the end of the story, is what the crowd said. We will come to it.

And the dead man sat up.

That is how Luke says it. He sat up, and he began to speak. Not a word is recorded. Not a single sentence of what he said. I find myself wishing I knew, and also, I find that I am glad I do not. It is so ordinary. A young man sits up on a board, with a cloth around him, and opens his mouth, and says something, and what he says is between him and his mother and God.

Perhaps it was only her name.

[pause]

* * *

Luke finishes with one of the loveliest lines in the whole Gospel. He says, and he gave him to his mother.

Those are almost exactly the words used about Elijah, long ago, when the widow's son in Zarephath was alive again. The prophet took the child, carried him downstairs, and gave him to his mother. In the Greek Old Testament that the first Christians read, the wording is almost the same, and I do not think it is an accident. Luke wants us to hear the old story under the new one, like a bass note under a tune. Something that happened once, at the edge of Israel, in a time of famine, is happening again, at a town gate, and it is bigger.

But set that aside, for a minute, and think about what is happening to the woman.

For all of this, she has done nothing. She has not asked, or bargained, or argued. She has simply stood in the road, and watched. First a stranger spoke to her. Then he touched her son's bier. Then the bearers stopped, and the whole procession stopped, and she must have thought, I do not know what she thought. And then her son sat up and spoke.

And the next thing that happens is that the stranger takes the young man by the hand, I imagine, or by the shoulder, though Luke does not say how, and brings him over to her. And he gives him back.

She does not have to carry him to the grave. She does not have to go home to a house with boots in the doorway and an empty room. She has her son. He is standing in front of her, on his feet, in the evening light, with a cloth coming off his shoulders and a bewildered look on his face. He is breathing. And she is going to be able to put her arms around him.

I do not know what she said. Luke does not either. But if you want to know what the Gospel means by the word compassion, I think you could do worse than to look at that moment, a woman and her son, in the road, with a crowd all around, and a stranger who steps back a pace to let them hold on to each other.

[pause]

* * *

Now what did the crowd do?

Luke says that fear seized all of them. Not terror, as you would feel at a sudden noise, but the deep, silent awe that people feel when they find themselves standing very close to something far greater than they are. It is the same fear that comes upon people in the Old Testament when they meet an angel or hear a voice. It is the sort of fear that is half joy. They did not run away. They stayed. And they began to glorify God.

And they said two things.

The first was, a great prophet has risen among us. That is what they could say. They had a word for a man who spoke with God's authority and did what God did. They did not have a word, yet, for what this man was. They were reaching for the largest word they had. And the Church has always said, that is a good start, and there is more to come. A prophet is a great deal. He is not everything.

The second was, God has visited his people.

I love that one. It is a very old phrase in Israel. It is what people said when God came near, to rescue, to forgive, to put things right. The same words are in the song that Zechariah sang when his son John was born. We told that story as well. God has visited his people. The crowd on the road did not know all that the words meant. But they had said them, and they were true.

And then Luke tells us that the news went out. This report about him spread through all Judea and the surrounding country. Of course it did. It had been a town of perhaps a few hundred, at a gate, in the evening, with at least two crowds as witnesses. There was no keeping that quiet. By the next morning people in the next village knew. By the end of the week the story had gone down the road to Jerusalem.

But it began here. With a woman, who had stopped asking for anything. With a man who looked at her. And with an ordinary evening, in a town at the edge of a great green plain.

[pause]

Now I want to slow down.

The light is going, and it is getting cool. The crowd has got quieter, not because anything is wrong, but because there is nothing more to say. A few people have started to laugh, softly, the way people do when they are very relieved. A child has been lifted up on someone's shoulders to see. The bearers have put the board down on the ground. It is only a board. Somebody has taken off his own cloak and put it round the young man's shoulders, because it is cold, and he is shivering, and what in the world do you say to someone like that.

The widow takes his face in her hands.

It is warm.

And the whole company, the people of Nain and the travellers from Capernaum, no longer two crowds but one, turn around together, and walk back in at the gate. This time nobody is carrying anything.

Luke does not tell us what they did after. He does not tell us what supper there was, or whether anyone slept, or who sat up late and listened to a young man tell what he could remember, which may have been very little. I like to think the baker was the first one through the door, with the bread she had brought that morning for the mourners. It is a good thing to have bread to hand, at the end of the day, at either kind of party.

I like to think of the boots in the doorway. I like to think that someone, at last, moved them aside to let him in.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

The whole of tonight's story is in the Gospel according to Luke, chapter seven, verses eleven to seventeen. It is only seven verses. I would recommend reading it, slowly, tonight or tomorrow, and noticing how much is not there.

What we have kept from Luke: the Lord going to a town called Nain, with his disciples and a large crowd; the company coming near to the gate; the dead man being carried out, the only son of his mother, and she a widow; a considerable crowd from the town with her; the Lord seeing her and having compassion on her; his words, do not weep; his touching the bier; the bearers standing still; the command, young man, I say to you, arise; the dead man sitting up and beginning to speak; the Lord giving him to his mother; fear on all of them and the glorifying of God; the cry, a great prophet has risen among us, and God has visited his people; and the report spreading through Judea and all the surrounding country.

What we flagged as a reading, or as a guess. That the first Christians heard the stories of Elijah at Zarephath and Elisha at Shunem behind this one is a reading, and a very old and very natural one, helped by the nearness of the wording; Luke does not say so. That the Lord's words, I say to you, show a difference between his way and the prophets' way is a reading that Christian writers have long drawn out and that the Gospel leaves for us to notice. That the touching of the bier would have made a man unclean under the rules of Numbers is background, and a reasonable one, and Luke does not remark on it. The distance from Capernaum, about twenty miles, is our estimate from the map, and the roads may have been longer. The reading of the Greek word for the young man as someone in his twenties is an approximation. Some old manuscripts say, soon afterwards, where others say, on the next day, for the time of the visit, and we have not chosen between them.

On the place. Nain is a real village in lower Galilee. The modern village of Nein stands on the same hillside, and rock-cut tombs of the old kind are found there, which fits Luke's picture of a gate and a burial place outside it. That is as far as the evidence goes. Nobody can point to the very gate, and we have not claimed to.

On the people. Later tradition has given the woman and her son names, and a good many later adventures. None of it is in Luke, and we have not relied on any of it. The woman is a widow, the young man is her son, and that is how the Gospel leaves them.

What we invented: the morning in the house; the neighbours sweeping the floor; the baker and her silent company; the boots by the door; the town's thoughts as it carried the bier; the thoughts of the crowd on the road; the cloak round the young man's shoulders; and the walk back in at the gate. None of it is in Luke, and none of it changes what happens or what it means. Luke gives the widow and her son no words at all, and we have given them none, apart from the guess, in a single place, that the young man might have said her name, which is offered as a guess and nothing more.

One honesty note about the world of the story. We said that the Law of Moses commands care for the widow; that is true, and the clearest places are in Deuteronomy and in the psalms, and it is well known. We said that burial in that part of the world was usually quick, on the day of the death or the next, and that is the common view of scholars, drawn from many sources, and Luke himself assumes it. We said that touching a body brought a week of uncleanness, which is from Numbers, chapter nineteen.

For further reading, the story just before this one in Luke is the healing of the Roman officer's servant, in the first ten verses of the same chapter, and the story that follows is the question that John the Baptist sends from prison. Two of our earlier episodes echo tonight's. The Woman of Shunem and the Child on the Prophet's Bed tells the story of Elisha and the mother who lost her son, a few miles from Nain. And Jairus and the Little Girl in the House by the Lake tells of another child, raised by the same voice. Listen to them in whatever order you like. The Gospels do not mind.

[pause]

That's the story of the widow of Nain for tonight. Luke does not tell us when it happened, and the Churches keep no feast of it; the Catholic Church reads it at Mass on the tenth Sunday in Ordinary Time, in the third year of its cycle, and the Orthodox Church reads it on a Sunday of the year. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. May the Lord who saw the widow at the gate see each one of us, in whatever road we are walking, and give us back what we thought we had lost, or give us himself, which is better. Goodnight, and God bless.

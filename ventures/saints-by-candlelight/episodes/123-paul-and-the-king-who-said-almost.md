It is morning, in a great hall by the sea.

The hall is full. There are officers in polished armour along one wall, and the leading men of the city in their best clothes along another. There is a governor in a white toga with a purple stripe. Last time, if you were with us, Saint Paul was in Caesarea, a city by the sea, in a room in the palace that King Herod the Great had built on a rocky point above the waves. He was a prisoner. He had not done anything wrong. But he had been arrested in Jerusalem, after a riot in the temple, and sent down to the coast at night in the middle of four hundred and seventy soldiers, to keep him away from forty men who had sworn to kill him.

And at Caesarea the governor, whose name was Felix, had heard the case, and had not decided it. He put it off. He sent for Paul often, in the evenings, to talk about faith in Christ. And when Paul talked about justice, and self-control, and the judgement to come, Felix grew afraid, and said, go away for now, I will send for you when it is convenient.

And it was never convenient. For two whole years.

And then Felix was called back to Rome, and a new governor came. And that is where we begin tonight.

Now, I should tell you about this new governor, and I am sorry to say that I cannot tell you very much. His name was Porcius Festus. He came to Judea in about the year fifty-nine or sixty, historians argue about which. The historian Josephus, who tells us a great deal about the governors before him and after him, tells us only a little about Festus, and most of it is that he was busy. The countryside was full of bandits. There were men with daggers hidden in their cloaks in the crowds at Jerusalem. There was a man out in the desert promising people freedom if they would only follow him into the wilderness. And Festus spent his time sending soldiers out after all of them.

Which is not a bad thing to be remembered for, as governors go. The one before him was remembered for his cruelty, and for hoping for bribes. The ones after him were remembered for much worse. Festus seems to have been an ordinary, sensible, hard-working Roman official, who arrived in a very difficult province and tried to keep it in order. If he had been a modern man, I think he would have had a very full diary, and a very small amount of patience with meetings.

And on his very first week in the job, he found a file on his desk.

Not a file exactly. A scroll, or a little bundle of wax tablets. A case left over by the last man. A prisoner, held for two years, with no verdict. A letter from a commander in Jerusalem saying he could not find anything wrong with him. And a note, perhaps, from the clerks, saying that the chief priests in Jerusalem were still very, very interested in this case.

If you have ever started a new job and found, in the drawer of your new desk, something your predecessor never quite got round to dealing with, you will know exactly how Festus felt.

Luke tells us that Festus arrived in his province, and after three days, he went up to Jerusalem.

Three days. That is a man who does not waste time. He had barely unpacked. But Jerusalem was the heart of the province, and the temple was the heart of Jerusalem, and a new governor needed to go and meet the people who ran it. Rather like a new head teacher going round to meet the parents.

And the chief priests and the leading men of Jerusalem came to see him. And of all the things they could have talked to the new governor about, the bandits, the taxes, the roads, the weather, the very first thing they brought up was Paul.

Two years had passed. And they had not forgotten him.

They laid out their case against him. And then they asked the new governor for a favour. Would he be so kind as to have the prisoner sent up to Jerusalem? To be tried here, in the holy city, where the trouble had happened?

It sounded very reasonable. But Luke tells us what was really behind it. They were planning an ambush, he says, to kill him on the way.

Which tells you that some of the men from two years ago, the ones who had sworn not to eat or drink until Paul was dead, were still about. I do hope, for their sake, that they had given up the fasting part of the oath fairly early on.

Now, Festus did not know about the ambush. He had been in the country less than a week. But he was a Roman, and he had the Roman habit of doing things in the proper order. And he said, no. Paul is being kept at Caesarea. I am going back there myself, shortly. So let the ones among you who have the authority come down with me, and if there is anything wrong about the man, let them accuse him there.

Which is to say: if you want to bring a case, you can bring it to my court. In my city. In front of me.

I do not think Festus was protecting Paul, exactly. I think he simply did not like being hurried. But God can use a man's dislike of being hurried, just as well as anything else, to keep his servant safe.

And Festus stayed in Jerusalem, Luke says, not more than eight or ten days. And then he went back down to the sea.

The very next day, Luke says, Festus took his seat on the judgement seat, and ordered Paul to be brought in.

Again, notice. The very next day. Felix had put this case off for two years. Festus got back to Caesarea and dealt with it the following morning. Whatever else you say about him, he was not a man who said, not tonight.

And Paul was brought in from his room. I imagine him blinking a little in the light of the courtyard. He was older now. Two years older than the last time he stood in this court. A little greyer, perhaps. A little stiffer in the knees. But the same man.

And the men who had come down from Jerusalem stood round him, Luke says, and brought many serious charges against him. Which they could not prove.

Luke does not tell us all the charges this time. He does not need to. They were the same ones as two years before. That Paul was a troublemaker, that he stirred up riots, that he had tried to profane the temple. And Paul's answer was short. Luke gives us one sentence of it, and I think it may be the whole of what he said.

I have done nothing wrong, said Paul. Not against the law of the Jews. Not against the temple. And not against Caesar.

Three things. The law, the temple, the emperor. The religion, the holy place and the government. I have not offended against any of them.

And then Festus did something that tells us a great deal about Festus. He was new. He had to live with these people. He had to work with the chief priests for years to come, if he could. And Luke says that Festus, wishing to do the Jewish leaders a favour, turned to Paul and asked him a question.

Are you willing, he said, to go up to Jerusalem, and be tried there, on these charges, before me?

It was a very polite question. And it was a very dangerous one. Because the road from Caesarea to Jerusalem is long, and goes up into the hills. And there were men waiting on it.

And Paul knew. He did not need anybody to tell him. He had been through this before. And he said, and I am giving you his words in plain modern English, with nothing added:

I am standing before Caesar's court, which is where I ought to be tried. I have done the Jews no wrong, as you yourself know very well. If I am in the wrong, and I have done something that deserves death, I am not trying to get out of dying. But if there is nothing in what these men accuse me of, nobody can hand me over to them.

And then four words. I appeal to Caesar.

Now I should explain what that meant. Paul was a Roman citizen. He had been born one, in the city of Tarsus. And a Roman citizen, in a case like this, had the right to ask that his case be heard, not by a governor in a province, but by the emperor himself, in Rome. Historians argue about the exact legal details, and about how often it was done, and I am not going to argue with them. But the heart of it was simple. Paul was saying, I will not be tried in Jerusalem. Send me to the top.

And the emperor, at that moment, was Nero. Which, if you know anything about Nero, may make you raise your eyebrows a little. But this was early in Nero's reign, when he was still young, and his advisers were still running things fairly sensibly, and nobody yet knew what he would become.

And there was one other thing, which Paul knew, and Festus did not. Two years before, in the barracks at Jerusalem, on the night after Paul had stood before the council, the Lord himself had stood beside him, and said: take courage. As you have borne witness about me in Jerusalem, so you must bear witness also in Rome.

You must bear witness in Rome. And for two years, Paul had sat by a window watching the ships go out of Herod's harbour, and none of them was for him. And now, here was a way.

Festus, Luke tells us, talked it over with his council, his advisers. I imagine a short, low conversation round the judgement seat. Can he do that? Yes, sir, he is a citizen, he can do that. Are you sure? Yes, sir. Well, that is a relief, in a way.

And Festus turned back to Paul. And he said, in what I imagine was a very formal voice:

You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go.

Now Festus had a problem. And this is where our invented friend comes in.

When a governor sent a prisoner to the emperor, he had to send a letter with him. A letter explaining what the prisoner was accused of, and why. You could not just put a man on a ship to Rome with a note saying, here is somebody, please deal with him. The emperor would not be pleased. And when the emperor was not pleased, governors tended to stop being governors.

So Festus called his secretary. The old sources do not tell us anything about his secretary, so I am going to give him a name, and the name is Decimus. Decimus was a neat, careful man with ink on his fingers and a stack of wax tablets, and he had written a great many letters to Rome, and they had all been about things he understood.

And Festus said, Decimus, write to the emperor about this man Paul.

And Decimus sat down, and smoothed the wax, and said, very good, sir. What is he charged with?

And Festus opened his mouth. And shut it again.

Because what was he charged with? Not with murder. Not with stealing. Not with rebellion against Rome; nobody had said anything about leading an army or refusing to pay taxes. The charges were all about the Jewish law, and the temple, and things that Festus, newly arrived from Italy, did not understand at all.

Shall I put, said Decimus, carefully, disagreements about religion?

The emperor, said Festus, is not going to be very interested in disagreements about religion.

No, sir, said Decimus. And he put down his pen, and waited. And the letter stayed blank.

And that is the situation, more or less, in which King Agrippa arrived.

A few days later, Luke says, King Agrippa and Bernice came to Caesarea, to pay their respects to the new governor.

And now I need to tell you who these two were, because they are interesting people, and because, if you were with us last time, you have met their family before.

Do you remember the king who died at Caesarea? The one who made a great speech in the theatre, in robes of silver, and the crowd shouted, it is the voice of a god and not of a man, and he did not give the glory to God, and he was struck down and died. That was King Herod Agrippa the First. And he had four children. A son, who was seventeen when his father died. And three daughters. One of them, the youngest, was Drusilla, who married Felix, and who sat beside him on the couch in our last story, listening to Paul.

And the son was this king. King Agrippa the Second. And the eldest daughter, who was a year younger than her brother, was Bernice.

So, you see, the new governor of Judea was about to receive a visit from the brother and sister of the last governor's wife. It was all very much a family business, the Herods and the Romans. A little like a very small village, where everybody's cousin has married everybody else's cousin, except that the village stretched from Rome to the Euphrates.

Agrippa had grown up in Rome, at the court of the emperor Claudius. When his father died, he was thought too young to rule his father's whole kingdom. So instead, over the years, the emperors gave him pieces. A little kingdom here, up in the mountains of Lebanon. Then a better piece there, in the hills and the high plains north-east of the Sea of Galilee, round the springs of the Jordan. Then some towns in Galilee itself, and across the river. A king, with a crown and a capital and coins with his face on, but a king who knew very well who had given him all of it, and who could take it away again. He even renamed his capital city after the emperor Nero, which is the kind of thing you do when you know exactly where your bread is buttered.

And he had one other very important job. Agrippa had the right, given him by the emperors, to choose the high priest in Jerusalem. He could appoint one, and he could take one away, and he had a say in the temple's affairs. So if anybody in that part of the world understood the Jewish law, and the temple, and the arguments of the priests, it was Agrippa. He had grown up a Roman prince, but he was a Jewish king, and the temple was, in a way, his responsibility.

I will tell you one story about Agrippa from Josephus, and it happened at almost exactly this time, while Festus was governor. Agrippa had a palace in Jerusalem, up on the high ground to the west of the temple. And he built himself a fine new dining room on the top of it. And from that dining room, if you lay on your couch at dinner, you could look straight down into the temple courts, and watch everything that was happening there.

The priests were not pleased about this. They did not think the temple was something to be watched over dinner, like the entertainment at a party. So they built a wall. A good high wall, at the western edge of the temple, just tall enough to block the king's view.

And Agrippa was very cross. And so was Festus, because the wall also blocked the view of the Roman soldiers who kept watch on the temple at festivals. And Festus ordered the wall to be pulled down. And the priests sent messengers all the way to Rome to complain to Nero. And Nero's wife, Poppaea, who Josephus says was friendly to the Jewish religion, took their side. And the wall stayed up.

So there you are. A king who wanted to watch the temple from his dinner table, and a governor who wanted to help him, and a wall that won. I tell you this partly because it makes me smile, and partly because it tells you what kind of man Agrippa was. He knew all about the temple. He cared about it, in his way. He just liked to have a good seat.

And Bernice. Bernice is harder to describe. She had been widowed twice already by this time, though she was only about thirty, and she was living at her brother's court. Josephus repeats some unkind gossip about her, which I am going to leave where he left it. Later in her life she did one very brave thing, which I will tell you about at the end, and she was loved by an emperor, which I will also tell you about at the end. But on this day, she was simply a queen, travelling with her brother the king, coming to call on the new governor. And I expect she had very good jewellery.

They stayed many days, Luke says. And one evening, perhaps at dinner, perhaps on a terrace with the sea going pink and gold below them, Festus brought up the case.

He was, I think, very glad to see a man who might understand it.

There is a man here, he said to Agrippa, who was left a prisoner by Felix. And when I was in Jerusalem, the chief priests and the elders told me about him, and asked me to pass sentence on him.

And I told them, said Festus, and I imagine him being rather proud of this, that it is not the Roman way to hand any man over before he has met his accusers face to face, and had the chance to defend himself against the charge.

And here we see Festus at his best. It is not the Roman way. That was a good thing about Rome, and there were not as many good things about Rome as the Romans thought, but that was one of them. You do not hand a man over without a hearing.

So, said Festus, when they came here, I did not waste time. The very next day I took my seat on the judgement seat and had him brought in. And when his accusers stood up, they did not bring any of the charges I was expecting. Not crimes at all. Only some questions about their own religion.

And here Luke gives us the exact Greek word Festus used for religion, and it is a funny little word. It can mean religion. It can also mean superstition. It is more or less the same word Paul had used, years before, in Athens, when he stood up in front of the philosophers and said, men of Athens, I see that you are very religious. It is a word that could be a compliment or an insult, depending on how you said it. And Festus, talking to a Jewish king, at the king's own dinner, very probably said it in the politest possible way.

Some questions about their own religion, said Festus. And about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul kept saying was alive.

I love that sentence. A certain Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul said was alive. It is the whole of the Christian faith, said by a puzzled Roman official at a dinner party, as if he were describing a slightly odd item in the news. He has no idea what he has just said. He has said it the way you might say, there is a man in the market who says the moon is made of cheese.

But it is all there. A man called Jesus. He was dead. He is alive.

And I was at a loss, said Festus, quite honestly, how to look into questions like that. So I asked the man whether he would go to Jerusalem and be tried there about them. But he appealed to be kept in custody for the decision of the emperor. So I have ordered him to be held until I can send him to Caesar.

And Agrippa, who had been listening with great attention, said, I should like to hear the man myself.

And Festus said, with what I imagine was enormous relief, tomorrow. Tomorrow you shall hear him.

And somewhere in the palace, I like to think, Decimus heard the news, and sharpened his pen.

So the next day, Luke says, Agrippa and Bernice came with great pomp.

The Greek word Luke uses is a word for show, or display, or pageantry. The same root gives us our word fantasy. It is the sort of word you would use for a royal wedding or a coronation, and you can tell Luke was not entirely impressed by it. You can almost hear him saying, and they came in with a great deal of fuss.

They came into the audience hall. And this is the only time in the New Testament that this word for an audience hall appears, a place for hearing things, a room where people went to listen to speeches and cases and readings. And it was full. The tribunes were there, the commanders of the soldiers at Caesarea, whose title in Greek meant commander of a thousand. The leading men of the city were there. It was the most important crowd in Caesarea, all in one room, all in their very best clothes.

And then, at Festus's order, Paul was brought in.

I want you to picture it. All that gold and purple, all those polished breastplates, and in through the door comes one short, tired man, in his ordinary clothes, with a chain on his wrist. He had come by ship and by road and on foot, all over the world, and he had stood up in market places and synagogues and theatres. He had been beaten, and stoned, and shipwrecked. And he had preached to slaves and to sailors and to philosophers. And now here he was, in front of a king. And I do not think he was nervous at all. I think he was rather pleased.

Festus stood up, and made a little speech. He was a good organised man, and he liked to explain things.

King Agrippa, he said, and everybody here with us. You see this man. The whole Jewish people have petitioned me about him, both at Jerusalem and here, shouting that he ought not to live any longer.

But I found, said Festus, that he had done nothing that deserves death. And since he himself has appealed to the emperor, I have decided to send him.

But I have nothing definite to write about him to my lord.

And there, I like to think, somewhere at the back of the hall, Decimus nodded very hard.

So I have brought him here before you all, said Festus, and especially before you, King Agrippa, so that after we have looked into it, I may have something to write. Because it seems to me unreasonable, to send a prisoner, and not to say what he is charged with.

Which is, I think, the most honest thing a governor has ever said in public. I have no idea what this man has done, and I need help writing the letter.

And Agrippa said to Paul, you have permission to speak for yourself.

And Paul stretched out his hand.

Luke loves that detail. He tells us about Paul's hand more than once. It was what Paul did before he spoke, a little gesture, like a teacher at the front of a class, or a man calming a crowd. And I imagine the chain on his wrist shifting as he did it, with a small clink, in the quiet of that great room.

And he began. And again, I am giving you his words in plain English, with nothing added that he did not say, though I have left out a little here and there to keep it short for bedtime.

I count myself fortunate, King Agrippa, said Paul, that it is before you that I am making my defence today, against all the things I am accused of by the Jewish leaders. Because you know all the customs of the Jews, and all the questions they argue about. So I beg you, listen to me patiently.

Which was not flattery, by the way. Tertullus, the hired speaker two years before, had flattered Felix with a great deal of nonsense about peace and reforms. But what Paul said to Agrippa was simply true. Agrippa did know all about these things. He chose the high priests. He was exactly the right man to understand this case. And Paul was glad, really glad, I think, to be speaking at last to somebody who would know what he was talking about.

All the Jews know how I have lived, said Paul, from my youth, from the beginning, among my own people and in Jerusalem. They have known me for a long time, if they were willing to say so. They know I lived as a Pharisee, the strictest party of our religion.

And now, said Paul, I am standing here on trial, because of my hope in the promise God made to our fathers. The promise that our twelve tribes hope to reach, as they worship God earnestly, night and day. It is for that hope, O king, that I am accused.

And then he turned, I think, and looked round the whole room, at the governor and the tribunes and the leading men of the city, Romans and Greeks and Jews all together, and he asked them a question.

Why should any of you think it incredible, that God raises the dead?

[pause]

I want to stop there for a moment, because that is the centre of the whole story.

Notice what Paul is saying. He is not saying, I have left my people, or I have given up the faith of my fathers. He is saying the opposite. He is saying, the hope I am on trial for is the hope of my own people. The hope of the prophets. The hope that one day God will raise the dead. Daniel had written it down, centuries before, that many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall wake. Paul believed it as a Pharisee, and he believed it still. The only difference now was that he believed God had begun to do it. That it had already started. With one man.

And he asks, why is that so hard to believe? If God made everything in the first place, why should it be strange for him to give life back?

Then Paul did something brave. He told them about the worst thing he had ever done.

I myself, he said, was convinced I ought to do everything I could against the name of Jesus of Nazareth. And I did it, in Jerusalem. I locked up many of the saints in prison, with authority from the chief priests. And when they were put to death, I voted against them. I went round all the synagogues, punishing them, again and again, and trying to make them speak against God. I was so furious with them that I chased them even to cities abroad.

He did not hide it. He did not make it sound better than it was. In front of a king and a governor and the most important people in the city, he said, I was a persecutor. I hunted down the people I now belong to.

And then he told them about the road.

In the course of this, said Paul, I was travelling to Damascus, with the authority and the orders of the chief priests.

And at midday, O king, on the road, I saw a light from heaven. Brighter than the sun. Shining round me, and round the men who were travelling with me.

Midday. Think of that. Not a light in the darkness, where any lamp would look bright. A light at noon, in the hot, hard sunshine of the Syrian road, and brighter than the sun.

And we all fell to the ground, said Paul. And I heard a voice, speaking to me in my own language, the language of home. And it said:

Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you, kicking against the goads.

Now, a goad, if you have never met one, is a long wooden stick with a sharp point on the end, which a farmer used for driving oxen. If the ox was stubborn, and kicked back at the goad, the only thing that happened was that the ox hurt its own leg. And the Greeks had a saying about it. It turns up in plays by Aeschylus and Euripides, the great playwrights of Athens, the way an old proverb turns up in Shakespeare. Do not kick against the goads. Meaning, do not fight against something that is stronger than you, because you will only hurt yourself.

Paul was telling this story to a room full of educated people, and he knew they would recognise the saying. And I think there is something very gentle in it. The voice on the road did not say, how dare you. It said, it is hard for you. As if the Lord, on the road, was sorry for the man who was fighting him. As if he was saying, Saul, Saul, you are only hurting yourself.

And I said, said Paul, who are you, Lord?

And the Lord said, I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.

But get up. Stand on your feet. I have appeared to you for this reason: to make you my servant, and a witness of what you have seen, and of what I will show you. I will rescue you from your own people, and from the nations, to whom I am sending you, to open their eyes. So that they may turn from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan to God. So that they may receive forgiveness of sins, and a place among those who are made holy by faith in me.

[pause]

To open their eyes. So that they may turn from darkness to light.

Paul had heard words like those before. They were the words of the prophet Isaiah, who wrote about a servant of God who would be a light to the nations, to open eyes that are blind, to bring out of the prison those who sit in darkness. And here was the voice on the road, giving that work to Paul.

And here is a thing I like. When Paul tells this story, he is standing in front of a king and a queen who, you might say, were sitting in a sort of darkness of their own. And a governor who could not make head or tail of any of it. And a room full of officers and officials who had come along for the pomp. And Paul is, quite simply, doing the job he was given on the road. He is trying to open their eyes.

So, King Agrippa, said Paul, I was not disobedient to the vision from heaven. I went and told people, first in Damascus, then in Jerusalem and all over Judea, and then to the nations, that they should turn round, and turn to God, and live lives that show they have turned.

That is why, said Paul, some of my own people seized me in the temple and tried to kill me. But I have had help from God, to this very day. And so I stand here, telling it to small and great alike. And I am saying nothing except what the prophets and Moses said would happen. That the Christ must suffer. And that, by being the first to rise from the dead, he would bring light to our own people and to the nations.

* * *

And at that moment, Festus jumped up.

I do not think Festus had been following very closely. A great deal of it was about prophets and Moses and things he had never heard of. But he had heard the last part very clearly. A man risen from the dead. A light to the nations. And to a sensible Roman official with a full diary, it was too much.

Paul, he shouted, in a loud voice, you are out of your mind! All your learning is driving you mad!

And I think that is rather a compliment, in its way. Festus had looked at this short, tired prisoner in a chain, and seen a man of great learning. The Greek says, more or less, your many letters are turning you mad. Your books. Your scrolls. Too much reading. It is what people have said, ever since, to anybody who takes ideas very seriously. You have been reading too much. Go and get some fresh air.

And Paul did not get angry. He did not even raise his voice. He said:

I am not out of my mind, most excellent Festus. I am speaking words that are true, and sober, and sensible.

And then he turned back to the king.

For the king knows about these things, said Paul, and so I can speak freely to him. I am sure that none of this has escaped his notice. Because this was not done in a corner.

This was not done in a corner. That is a wonderful line. Jesus was not crucified in some hidden place. He died outside the walls of Jerusalem, at Passover, with the whole city watching. The empty tomb was talked about in every street. The apostles had been preaching about it in the temple courts for nearly thirty years. A Jewish king who chose the high priests could not possibly have avoided hearing about it. It was not a secret. It was not a private vision. It happened in public, in daylight, in the middle of the world.

And then Paul asked the king a question. Straight out. In front of everybody.

King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets?

And before Agrippa could answer, Paul answered for him, very kindly.

I know that you believe.

[pause]

Now think about the position Agrippa was in.

He was sitting on a raised seat, in front of all the most important people in Caesarea, with the Roman governor on one side of him and his sister on the other. And a prisoner had just asked him whether he believed the prophets.

If he said no, he would be saying, in front of everybody, that the Jewish king did not believe the Jewish Scriptures. He could not possibly say that.

But if he said yes, then everybody in the room knew what Paul would say next. Then you believe what they say about the Christ. And the Christ is Jesus. And he rose from the dead.

He could not say no. And he would not say yes. So he said something else. And this is the line that people have argued about for two thousand years.

In the Greek, it is only a few short words. And the oldest English Bibles put it like this, and if you have heard it before, you have probably heard it this way:

Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian.

Almost. Almost you persuade me. Which makes it sound as though Agrippa was right on the edge. As though he was nearly there. As though one more sentence, one more push, and he would have stood up and said, yes, I believe.

And preachers loved that. For hundreds of years, there were sermons about being almost persuaded. There is even an old hymn about it. Almost persuaded, now to believe.

But I have to be honest with you, because that is the rule in this show. Most people who study the Greek today do not think Agrippa said almost. The words he used are more like, in a little, or in a short time, or with so little. And so modern translations mostly have him saying something like: In such a short time, you think you can make me a Christian? Or even: Are you trying to make me a Christian, so quickly as this?

The old Latin Bible has it, in modico, which means in a little. And the old Catholic English Bible, which was translated from the Latin, puts it, in a little thou persuadest me to become a Christian.

So what did he mean? Was he nearly persuaded? Was he joking? Was he being sarcastic, a king gently mocking a prisoner who had got above himself? Or was he just doing what clever people often do, when a question comes too close, and turning it into a little joke so that everybody laughs and nobody has to answer?

I do not know. Nobody knows. It is one of those places where the Bible leaves us a man's words, and does not tell us what was in his heart. I like to think there was a little of all of it. A little joke. A little sharpness. And underneath, just a little, the sound of a man who knew that he had been asked a real question, and had not answered it.

And notice one more thing. The word Christian. It had only been invented a few years before, at Antioch, where people started calling the followers of Jesus by that name, perhaps as a nickname. It appears only three times in the whole of the New Testament. Once at Antioch, where it starts. Once in a letter of Saint Peter, who says, if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed. And once here, in the mouth of a king, who said it, I think, with a raised eyebrow. As if to say, you want me to become one of those?

* * *

And Paul's answer is, I think, one of the loveliest things he ever said.

He did not argue. He did not get cross. He did not say, how dare you laugh. He said:

Whether in a little or in a lot, whether it takes a short time or a long time, I would pray to God that not only you, but everybody listening to me today, might become what I am.

And then he lifted his wrist, perhaps, just a little, so that the chain clinked. And he said:

Except for these chains.

[pause]

I love him so much for that.

Here is a man who has been in prison for two years for nothing. Who has been beaten and stoned and shipwrecked. Who has every reason in the world to be bitter. And he looks at a king and a queen and a governor and a room full of officers, the people who could set him free and have not, and he says, I wish you were all like me. I wish you all had what I have. The joy, the hope, the friendship of Jesus, the knowledge that the dead will rise. I wish you had all of it.

But not the chains. I would not wish the chains on any of you.

It is the kindest thing you could say to people who are keeping you in prison. And I think, if you listen very carefully, there is even a little smile in it. A little joke of his own, to answer the king's. You laughed at me, King Agrippa. Here is my answer, and it is a blessing.

* * *

And then the king stood up.

Luke says the king rose, and the governor, and Bernice, and all the people who had been sitting with them. Which tells you that when the king stood up, the meeting was over. Nobody else could stay sitting.

And they went out of the hall. And outside, in some side room or corridor, away from the crowd, they talked together. And Luke tells us what they said. Which makes me think that somebody in that corridor, perhaps a servant, perhaps an officer, perhaps a member of the household who later became a Christian, told Luke what was said. Or perhaps it was simply obvious from what happened next.

This man, they said to one another, is doing nothing that deserves death, or even prison.

And Agrippa turned to Festus. And he said:

This man could have been set free, if he had not appealed to Caesar.

[pause]

Now that is the last line of chapter twenty-six. And it sounds very sad. If only Paul had waited one more day. If only he had not said those four words. He would have been free.

But I do not think it is sad at all. Paul did not appeal to Caesar because he was frightened, or because he had made a mistake. He appealed to Caesar because the Lord had told him, take courage, you must bear witness in Rome. And now he was going to Rome. Not as a free man, but as a prisoner, on a government ship, with his fare paid by the empire, and a Roman centurion to look after him.

God, it seems, has a sense of humour about travel arrangements.

And somewhere, I like to think, Decimus sat down at last with his wax tablets. And smoothed them. And wrote a letter to the emperor which said, more or less: this man is accused by the leaders of the Jews of matters concerning their own religion. King Agrippa has heard him, and agrees with me that he has done nothing that deserves death or chains. He has appealed to you. So I am sending him.

And then, perhaps, Decimus sat and looked at the letter for a while. And wondered, as I think a great many people in that hall must have wondered that night, lying awake in the dark, about a certain Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul said was alive.

[pause]

And what happened to them all?

Paul sailed for Rome, not long afterwards. Luke went with him. You can tell, because Luke's story starts saying we again, the moment the voyage is decided. A friend called Aristarchus, from Thessalonica, went with them too. And the voyage turned into one of the most famous shipwrecks in history, fourteen nights in a storm, and a landing on the island of Malta. If you have been with us for a while, you have already heard that story on another night. And at the end of it all, Paul reached Rome. And he did bear witness there, just as the Lord had said. And the church remembers that he gave his life there, for Christ, some years later, and went home to God.

Festus did not stay governor long. He died in office, after only about two years, still busy, I expect, still chasing bandits. Josephus tells us very little else about him. But I think kindly of him. He kept Paul out of the ambush. He would not hand a man over without a hearing. He listened to a story he could not understand, and he said honestly that he did not understand it. That is more than many people with his power have done.

Bernice, a few years later, did the brave thing I promised to tell you about. When the governor after Festus's successor, a cruel man called Florus, let his soldiers loose on the people of Jerusalem, Bernice was there, in the city, keeping a religious vow. And she went to Florus, barefoot, as people under such a vow did, and stood in front of his judgement seat, and begged him to stop. He would not listen. But she went. And later still she went to Rome, and the man who would one day be the emperor Titus loved her. And when he became emperor, the Roman historians say, he sent her away, against his wishes and against hers, because the Romans would not have an eastern queen in the palace. And after that, she disappears from history.

And Agrippa. When the great war began between the Jews and Rome, Agrippa went up to Jerusalem, and stood on the roof of a palace, with Bernice beside him, and made a long speech to the crowds, begging them not to go to war, because they could not win. Josephus says he wept as he finished it. And the crowd would not listen, and in the end some of them threw stones at him, and he went home. He stayed loyal to Rome all through the war, and lived for many years after it, the last king of Herod's family. He died somewhere near the end of the century; historians argue about exactly when. And so far as we know, he never did become what Paul was.

But I do not know that. Nobody does. Paul's prayer for him is still there, in the Bible, where anybody can read it. Whether in a little or in a lot. And God is very patient. And it is never too late, until it is.

And the hall at Caesarea is gone. But Caesarea is still there, by the sea, golden stone among the sand and the wild flowers. You can walk on the rocky point where the palace stood, and see the pool in the middle, with the waves coming in over the rocks. And if you stand there in the evening, with the sun going down into the water, and listen very hard, I think you can almost hear it. A chain clinking, just a little, as a short man lifts his hand. And a voice saying, very kindly, I wish you were all like me. Except for these chains.

[pause]

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapters twenty-five and twenty-six. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept. The words of Festus, of Paul and of Agrippa are in our own plain rendering, adding nothing to what Luke reports, though Paul's speech is shortened in a few places.

Agrippa's famous answer is translated differently. The old English Bibles have almost thou persuadest me; most modern translations read it as in a short time, or with so little. We have given both, and we have not decided what Agrippa meant, because Luke does not say.

Festus's busy time as governor and his death in office, Agrippa's age, his kingdom, his choosing of high priests, and the story of the dining room and the wall are from Josephus. Bernice before Florus, and Agrippa's speech against the war, are from Josephus's history of the war. Bernice and Titus are in Tacitus and Suetonius. The saying about the goads is in Greek plays by Aeschylus and Euripides.

The secretary Decimus, his blank letter, the weather, the rooms, and what everybody was thinking are our own invention. So is the guess about who told Luke what was said in the corridor.

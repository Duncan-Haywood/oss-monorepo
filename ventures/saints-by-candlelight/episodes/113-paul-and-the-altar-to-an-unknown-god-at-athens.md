It is a warm afternoon on a rocky hill in the middle of a famous city, and a man is standing up to speak.

Below him, on one side, is a market full of people, and beyond it the roofs of the town running down towards the sea. Above him, on the other side, close enough that it seems to lean over his shoulder, is another, higher rock, and on top of it a great temple of white and honey-coloured marble, with a goddess inside it made of gold and ivory and taller than a house.

Round him, on stone benches and on the bare rock, sit perhaps forty or fifty people. Some are old men in good cloaks, very dignified. Some are philosophers, with the beards philosophers wore so that you would know they were philosophers. Some are just curious. And one, at the very back, is a boy of about twelve, holding a bundle of scrolls that are not his, who should really be somewhere else.

The man in the middle is not tall. He is dusty from the road. He looks round at all of them, and he takes a breath, and he says, "People of Athens."

And the boy at the back leans forward, because he has a feeling something is about to happen.

Last time on Saints by Candlelight, we were on a high plain in the middle of what is now Turkey, with Paul and Barnabas, and a town that tried to worship them with oxen and garlands. Tonight we have moved on a few years, and a long way west. Barnabas is not with us. He and Paul have gone their separate ways, Barnabas to Cyprus and Paul on a new journey with a new companion called Silas, and later a young man called Timothy, the one we wondered about at Lystra.

This new journey had brought Paul across the sea into Europe for the first time, into the country called Macedonia, in the north of Greece. And it had been, if we are honest, a bumpy trip. At Philippi he and Silas were beaten and put in prison, and then let out with apologies. At Thessalonica there was a riot. At Beroea, the next town, things went well for a while, and then people came from Thessalonica to start another riot. So the believers at Beroea did a sensible thing. They put Paul on the road to the coast, and some of them went with him, all the way south, by sea or by land, Acts does not say which, to Athens. Silas and Timothy stayed behind for the moment. And Paul sent a message back with his escort: come and join me as soon as you can.

So for a little while, Paul was in Athens on his own.

Now, Athens. You have probably heard of Athens. Everyone has heard of Athens. Even in Paul's day, everyone had heard of Athens, which was rather the trouble with it.

Five hundred years before our story, Athens had been the most remarkable city in the world. That was the Athens of the great playwrights, and the first democracy, and the philosopher Socrates walking about the market asking people annoying questions. That was when they built the Parthenon, the great temple on the high rock called the Acropolis, for Athena, the goddess the city was named after. By Paul's time all of that was a long time ago. Athens was no longer powerful. It belonged, like everywhere else round the Mediterranean, to Rome. But the Romans loved Athens, a little the way people love a grand old university town. Rich young Romans went there to study. Emperors gave it new buildings. And the Athenians, who were very proud of their past, went on talking about philosophy in the market, as their great-great-great-grandparents had done.

They also went on putting up statues. A great many statues. Gods, goddesses, heroes, emperors, generals, and benefactors who had paid for a fountain and wanted everyone to remember it. Luke, who wrote the Acts of the Apostles, uses a word for the city that you could translate as smothered in idols, or, less politely, idol-infested, the way you would say a house was mouse-infested.

And it is into that city, on his own, waiting for his friends, that we follow Paul.

* * *

Let's meet our other traveller, the one I made up.

His name is Lykos, which means wolf, though he is not at all wolf-like. He is a skinny boy of about twelve, with hair that will not lie flat, and he works for a philosopher. His job is to carry the philosopher's scrolls, fetch his lunch, hold his cloak while he talks, and keep quiet. He is very good at the first three.

The philosopher's name is Theron, and he is a Stoic. I will tell you what that means in a moment. He is a kind man, in a distracted way, and he has a long grey beard and a voice like a cello, and he has been talking about the nature of the universe in the Painted Porch every morning for thirty years. The Painted Porch, by the way, is a real place. It was a long covered colonnade on the side of the market, with great paintings of old battles along the back wall, and the Stoics were named after it, because stoa is the Greek for porch. So a Stoic is, more or less, a porch person. I find this very pleasing.

On the morning our story begins, Lykos was sitting on the steps of the porch, with the scrolls in his lap, watching the market.

The market of Athens, the agora, was a big open square, with temples and offices and long colonnades round the edges, and in the middle, under the plane trees, people selling things. Figs and olives. Honey from the mountain. Cheap little clay lamps. Cheap little clay gods, too, if you wanted one for your shelf at home. There were lawyers hurrying to the law courts, and tourists staring at the statues, and dogs asleep in the shade, and, everywhere, people talking. Athenians loved to talk. Luke says so, a little drily. He says that all the Athenians, and the foreigners living there, spent their time on nothing else but telling or hearing something new. If you have ever met somebody who always has to know the latest news before anyone else, you have met an ancient Athenian.

And that morning, there was a new man in the market.

Lykos noticed him because he was not looking at the statues the way tourists did. Tourists looked at the statues with their heads on one side, saying, isn't it beautiful. This man looked at them the way a doctor looks at a rash. He walked slowly along the row of them, one after another, and his face grew more and more troubled.

Then he stopped in front of a small stone altar, at the end of the row, near the steps where Lykos sat. It was an old altar, chipped at the corners, with a few dry flowers on it. The man bent down and read the words carved on the front. He read them twice. And then, to Lykos's surprise, he smiled.

"Excuse me," said the man, looking up. "Do you know who this altar belongs to?"

Lykos stood up. "It says," he said.

"I know what it says. It says, to an unknown god. But who put it up? And why?"

Lykos had walked past the altar every day of his life, and had never thought about it. He thought about it now. "There's lots of them," he said. "My master says it's because, in the olden days, there was a plague, and nobody knew which god was angry, so they put up altars to whichever one it was, just to be safe."

"Just to be safe," said the man, nodding slowly. "To a god they did not know."

"Yes," said Lykos. "So they wouldn't miss anyone out."

The man looked at the altar for a long moment more. Then he said, "Thank you," very warmly, as if Lykos had given him a present, and went away across the market, and Lykos sat down again and wondered what that had been about.

I should say, here, a word about the story Lykos told. The story about the plague is really an old Athenian tale. A Greek writer called Diogenes Laertius, who lived about two hundred years after Paul, tells it. He says that long, long ago, when Athens had a plague, the Athenians sent for a wise man from Crete called Epimenides, and Epimenides let a flock of sheep loose on the Hill of Ares, and wherever a sheep lay down, he had an altar built and a sacrifice made to the god of that place, whoever it might be, and the plague stopped, and some of those altars still stood, with no names on them. That is Diogenes's story, and it may be no more than a story. But another writer, Pausanias, who wrote a sort of guidebook to Greece about a hundred years after Paul, says plainly that there were altars at Athens to gods called unknown. So Paul's altar is the kind of thing you really would have found there. Which is reassuring. Nobody likes a guidebook that makes things up.

* * *

Over the next few days, Lykos saw the new man a great deal.

He went to the synagogue on the Sabbath, because Athens had a Jewish community, as most big Greek cities did, and there he talked with the Jews and with the Greeks who came to pray with them, about the Scriptures, and about the Messiah. That was always where Paul started, in every town. But he did not stop there. Acts says that every day he talked in the market with whoever happened to be there. And whoever happened to be there, in the market of Athens, often turned out to be philosophers.

So Paul talked. He stood under the plane trees, or sat on the steps of one of the porches, and he told people about Jesus, and about Jesus rising from the dead. And people stopped to listen, because something new was being said in the market, and Athenians could never resist that.

Now I promised to tell you about Stoics, and Luke mentions another sort of philosopher too, the Epicureans. Here is the very short version, and if any philosophers are listening, I apologise in advance.

The Epicureans were the followers of a man called Epicurus, who had taught in Athens about three hundred years before, in a garden. He said that the best life was a quiet life, free from pain and fear, with good friends and simple food. As for the gods, he said they probably existed, but they lived far away, perfectly happy, and took no interest at all in us, so there was no need to be afraid of them. Everything, he said, was made of tiny particles called atoms, bumping about, and when you died, your atoms simply drifted apart, and that was that.

The Stoics, the porch people, thought nearly the opposite. They said the whole universe was filled and held together by a divine reason, a sort of fire of intelligence running through everything, from the stars to the stones to you. They said that a wise person should live in harmony with that reason, calmly, doing their duty and not getting upset about things they could not change. That is why, even today, we call somebody stoical when they put up with trouble without complaining.

So you can imagine the conversations in the market. Paul says something about God, and an Epicurean says, the gods don't care about us, and a Stoic says, of course God cares, God is in everything, and Paul says, no, wait, listen, and they all start again.

Lykos, with his master's scrolls, listened from the edges. He listened because his master listened. Theron the Stoic had found Paul on the second day and had been arguing with him ever since, very politely, with great enjoyment, the way some people enjoy a good game of chess.

Not everybody was so polite. On the third or fourth day, an Epicurean with a sharp nose said, loudly enough for Paul to hear, "What is this seed-picker trying to say?"

That is the word Luke reports, and it is a wonderful word. In Greek it is spermologos, a seed-picker. It was what Athenians called the little birds that hop about the market picking up seeds and crumbs that have fallen from the stalls. And then it came to mean a person who picks up scraps of ideas here and there, a bit of this and a bit of that, and repeats them without really understanding them. A chatterer. A magpie. A babbler.

Lykos looked at Paul, to see if he minded being called a sparrow. Paul did not seem to mind in the least. He had been called much worse in Thessalonica.

Other people in the crowd said, "No, he seems to be preaching some foreign gods." Because, Luke explains, he was telling them about Jesus, and about the resurrection.

Now here is a little puzzle, and I'll give it to you as a puzzle. Why would anyone think Paul was preaching foreign gods, plural, if he was talking about Jesus? Saint John Chrysostom, the great preacher of Constantinople, who has an episode of his own on this programme, had a guess. In Greek, resurrection is anastasis. And Anastasis also sounds very like a woman's name. So Chrysostom thought some of the Athenians heard Paul talking about Jesus and Anastasis, and decided he was introducing a new god and a new goddess, a sort of divine couple. Many readers since have liked that guess. It is only a guess. But if it is right, it is a lovely example of people hearing every word of something and missing the whole point of it, which, to be fair, happens to all of us.

* * *

One evening, as the light went gold over the market, Theron the Stoic stood up from the step where he had been arguing with Paul, and stretched, and said, "My friend. I have enjoyed this very much. But I think the council ought to hear you."

"The council?" said Paul.

"The Areopagus," said Theron. "They look after this sort of thing. New teachings. Religious matters. Who may lecture in the city and who may not. Don't look so worried. They will not throw stones at you. We are Athenians. We will only ask you questions until you wish we had thrown stones."

Lykos laughed out loud, and then looked at his feet.

Here I must explain the Areopagus, which is a little confusing, because it is two things with one name.

The first thing is a hill. Areopagus means the Hill of Ares, Ares being the Greek god of war. It is a big bare lump of grey rock, just below the Acropolis, between the Acropolis and the market. You can still climb it today. There are steps cut in the rock, very worn and very slippery, and from the top you can see the whole of the old market spread out below you.

The second thing is a council, which was called the Areopagus because, in the very old days, it met on that hill. It was the oldest council in Athens, made of very respectable people, and by Paul's day, under the Romans, it looked after a good many things, including, it seems, which teachers and new religions were allowed in the city.

So when Acts says that they took Paul and brought him to the Areopagus, does it mean they took him up the hill, or brought him before the council? It might be the hill. It might be the council, which in Paul's time may often have met down in the market, in one of the porches. Scholars have argued about it for a long time, and they are still arguing, and I am not going to settle it at bedtime. Tonight, because it is a story, and because it is a beautiful place, we are going to climb the hill. But you should know that the hill is a guess.

They went up the next afternoon. Theron went, and the sharp-nosed Epicurean, and a number of the council, solemn old men in fine cloaks, and a good many people from the market who did not want to miss anything new. And Lykos went too, because his master told him to bring the scrolls, and then forgot he was there.

At the top, they sat on the rock and on a few stone benches, and Paul stood in the middle. And one of the councillors, a man with a gentle face and a very good cloak, said, kindly enough, "May we know what this new teaching is that you are presenting? You are bringing some strange things to our ears. So we would like to know what they mean."

And Paul stood up in the middle of them, on the Hill of Ares, with the temple of Athena gleaming above him, and the market below, and the evening coming on.

* * *

I am going to tell you what Paul said. Not in his exact words, because I am telling it in plain modern English, but nothing added and nothing changed. It is not a long speech. Luke gives it to us in about ten verses. You could say it out loud in two minutes. But people have been thinking about it for two thousand years.

"People of Athens," Paul said. "I can see that in every way you are very religious."

Now that word, very religious, is a clever word, and I think Paul chose it on purpose. In Greek it can mean very devout, very respectful of the gods, which is a compliment. Or it can mean very superstitious, which is not. Paul leaves it hanging there, and every man on that hill can take it how he likes. The councillors probably smiled. The Epicurean probably raised an eyebrow.

"Because as I was walking round your city," Paul said, "and looking at the things you worship, I found an altar with this written on it. To an unknown god."

And at the back, Lykos sat up very straight, because he knew that altar. He walked past it every day.

"So then," said Paul. "What you worship without knowing it, that is what I am telling you about."

You can see what he has done. He has not marched up the hill and told them they are all wrong. He has started with something of theirs. A little chipped altar at the edge of the market, with a few dry flowers on it, put up by people who knew there might be a god they had missed. And he says, in effect, you were right. There is one you have missed. Let me tell you about him.

"The God who made the world and everything in it," Paul said, "who is Lord of heaven and earth, does not live in temples made by human hands."

I think, at that moment, a few people may have glanced up at the Parthenon.

"And he is not served by human hands," Paul went on, "as if he needed anything. Because he is the one who gives everyone life, and breath, and everything else."

That would have made the Epicureans nod. They agreed that God needs nothing. They just thought it meant he did not care. Paul is about to say the opposite.

"From one man," Paul said, "he made every nation of human beings, to live on the whole face of the earth. And he set the times for them, and the boundaries of the places where they would live. He did this so that they would look for God, and perhaps reach out for him, the way you feel your way in the dark, and find him."

Lykos thought about that. Feeling your way in the dark. He thought of getting up in the night to fetch water, and putting out his hand for the wall, and finding it.

"And yet," said Paul, "he is not far from any one of us. Because, as someone has said, in him we live, and move, and have our being."

That line, by the way, is one of the most famous lines in the whole Bible, and we are not quite sure who first wrote it. A Syriac Christian writer, many centuries later, said it came from Epimenides of Crete, the same wise man with the sheep from the story of the altars. That may be so. Nobody now can check. But whoever first wrote it, Paul makes it his own, and it has been ours ever since.

"And as some of your own poets have said," Paul went on, "we are his children too."

And this line we can check. It comes from a poem called the Phaenomena, about the stars and the weather, by a Greek poet called Aratus. Near the very beginning of the poem, Aratus says that everything is full of God, the streets and the markets and the sea and the harbours, and that we are his offspring. And here is the lovely thing. Aratus came from Cilicia. So did Paul. Aratus grew up perhaps a day's walk or two from Tarsus, three hundred years before. So Paul, standing on the Hill of Ares, quotes a poet from his own home country to the cleverest people in Greece. It is a little like a visitor to a famous old university quoting a poet from his own home town, and finding that everybody in the room knows the line. I imagine Theron the Stoic was delighted. Aratus was much loved by the Stoics.

"So then," said Paul, "since we are God's children, we ought not to think that God is like gold, or silver, or stone, a statue shaped by human skill and imagination."

And again, perhaps, a few eyes went up to the temple on the rock, where Athena stood in gold and ivory, taller than a house.

"In the past," Paul said, "God overlooked those times when people did not know. But now he is calling on all people everywhere to turn back to him. Because he has set a day when he is going to judge the world with justice, by a man he has chosen. And he has given proof of this to everyone, by raising that man from the dead."

And there, Paul stopped. Or perhaps he was stopped. Luke does not say. But the next thing that happens is the crowd.

* * *

When they heard the words raising from the dead, Luke says, some of them laughed.

You must understand that for most educated Greeks, this was the silliest part. The Epicureans thought that when you died, your atoms drifted away, and that was the end. Many of the Stoics thought your soul might go on, for a while, but the body was finished with, and good riddance. And even the poets agreed. In one of the old Athenian plays, a god says, when the dust has soaked up a man's blood, once he is dead, there is no rising again. Rising from the dead, with a body, was not something you heard about in polite company. So some of them laughed, and the sharp-nosed Epicurean laughed loudest of all, and some of the old councillors shook their heads and began to gather up their cloaks.

But not all of them. Some of them said, "We would like to hear you about this again."

They may have been brushing him off politely, the way you say, let's have lunch some time, to somebody you hope never to see again. Or they may have meant it. Probably some meant it and some did not. That is usually how it goes.

And Paul, Luke says, went out from among them.

That is all. There was no riot. Nobody threw stones. Nobody put him in prison. He simply went down the worn steps of the hill, in the evening, and the crowd broke up and went home to supper, and Athens went on being Athens.

Lykos stood at the top of the hill holding the scrolls. His master was talking to the councillor with the gentle face and the good cloak, and the two of them were walking slowly down together, deep in conversation. Lykos looked round for Paul and saw him, already at the bottom, walking off into the market by himself.

And then Lykos saw something else. A few people were going after him. Not many. Three or four, and then a few more. A woman whose name he did not know. And the councillor with the gentle face, who said goodbye to Theron at the foot of the steps, and bowed, and then turned and followed Paul.

Luke tells us the names of two of them. Some people, he says, joined Paul and believed. Among them was Dionysius, a member of the Areopagus, and a woman named Damaris, and others with them.

That is all we are told about Damaris. Just her name. We do not know who she was, or how old, or why she was there, on the hill or in the council room, among all those men, which was not a very usual place for a woman to be. Some people have guessed she was a foreigner, or a woman of some learning. Nobody knows. But Luke thought her name worth writing down, out of all the people who were there that day, and so, two thousand years later, we know it, and we can say it. Damaris. I think that is rather wonderful.

* * *

That night, Lykos could not sleep.

He lay on his mat in the little room behind his master's house, and listened to the dogs barking at the end of the street, and thought about the altar. To an unknown god. He had walked past it his whole life and never wondered about it. And then a stranger had come along, and read it twice, and smiled.

In the morning he did something he had never done before. Before his master woke, he went down to the market, in the cool blue early light, before the stalls were up, and he stood in front of the little altar.

There was an old man there, sweeping. He was the man who swept that end of the market. Lykos had seen him a thousand times and never spoken to him.

"Who looks after this one?" Lykos asked.

"Nobody," said the old man, leaning on his broom. "Nobody's ever known whose it is. That's the point of it, isn't it? I put a flower on it now and then. Seems a shame, otherwise. Somebody ought to remember him, whoever he is."

"There was a man here," said Lykos, "who said he knew."

The old man looked at him for a while. "Did he now," he said. "And did you believe him?"

Lykos thought about it.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I think I want to hear him again."

"Well," said the old man, and went back to his sweeping. "That's how it starts."

I should say very clearly, again, that Lykos and the old man are my own invention. Luke does not tell us about them. But I think there must have been people like them in Athens that week. People at the edges of the crowd, who did not laugh, and did not quite believe, and wanted to hear more. Luke says some people joined Paul and believed, and others with them. I like to think of those others with them. They are the ones who do not get their names written down.

[pause]

That is nearly the end of the story.

Paul did not stay long in Athens. Timothy came down to join him, and Paul, worried about the young church he had had to leave so suddenly at Thessalonica, sent him straight back north to see how they were. We know this because Paul says so himself, in a letter he wrote to the Thessalonians a little later. He says, we thought it best to be left behind at Athens alone, and we sent Timothy. You can hear, in those few words, how much he missed them.

And then Paul left Athens and went on to Corinth, a big, rough, busy port a couple of days away, and there he stayed for a year and a half, and made tents, and founded a church that would give him a great deal of trouble and a great deal of joy, and to which he would write some of his most famous letters.

Some people have said that Athens was a failure, because Paul did not found a big church there, and because some people laughed at him. I do not think Luke thought so. Luke gives this speech more care than almost any other in his book. And there were people who believed. A councillor, and a woman, and others with them. That is how most churches start. Not with crowds, but with a few people who stay behind to hear more.

As for Dionysius, the councillor. A little over a hundred years later, a bishop called Dionysius of Corinth wrote that Dionysius the Areopagite had become the first bishop of Athens. We know this because the historian Eusebius quotes him. Later still, other stories grew up round his name. Some time around the year five hundred, somebody wrote a set of beautiful and very deep books about God and the angels and the liturgy, and put Dionysius the Areopagite's name on them. People read them for a thousand years as if he had written them, and they are still read and loved, in East and West, but scholars now agree that they come from much later. And in the Middle Ages, in France, people came to believe that Dionysius was the same man as Saint Denis, the first bishop of Paris, who lived two hundred years later. He was not. Poor Dionysius. He said very little in Acts, and afterwards everybody kept putting words in his mouth. I hope he does not mind.

The Orthodox Church remembers Saint Dionysius the Areopagite on the third of October, and the Roman Martyrology names him on that day too. Damaris, too, has been honoured among the saints, though we know nothing more about her than her name.

The altars to unknown gods are gone now. Nobody has found the one Paul read, and probably nobody ever will. The statues are mostly gone too, or in museums. The gold and ivory Athena has gone altogether. The Parthenon is still there, on its rock, roofless and beautiful, and every day thousands of people climb up to look at it.

And below it, the Hill of Ares is still there too. It is just a bare grey rock, polished smooth and slippery by all the feet that have climbed it. And at the bottom of the steps, set into the rock, there is a big bronze plaque. On it, in Greek, are the words of Paul's speech, the one we heard tonight. People stop and read it, in the evening, in a great many languages. People of Athens. I can see that in every way you are very religious. Some of them smile.

And sometimes, in the evening, when the tourists have gone down to supper, it is very quiet up there. The wind comes up from the sea. The lights come on in the city below, one after another. And you can sit on the warm rock and feel your way, in the dark, towards the one who is not far from any of us.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter seventeen, verses fifteen to thirty-four, with the first verse of chapter eighteen. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Paul says on the hill is from those verses, in our own plain rendering, with nothing added. Paul's being left alone at Athens and sending Timothy back is from his First Letter to the Thessalonians, chapter three.

The line about being God's children is from the Phaenomena of Aratus, a poet from Cilicia. The line about living and moving and having our being is attributed to Epimenides of Crete only by a much later Syriac commentator, and we offer that as a guess. The old tale of Epimenides, the plague and the sheep is told by Diogenes Laertius, two hundred years after Paul, and is told as his story. Altars to unknown gods at Athens are mentioned by Pausanias. Saint John Chrysostom's idea that the Athenians took Anastasis for a goddess is from his thirty-eighth homily on Acts, and is a reading. Whether Paul spoke on the hill or before the council meeting somewhere else is not settled; we chose the hill. The line from an old Athenian play is from Aeschylus, in the Eumenides, which happens to be set on the Areopagus itself.

Dionysius as first bishop of Athens comes from Dionysius of Corinth, quoted by Eusebius. The writings under Dionysius's name are from about five hundred years later, and his identification with Saint Denis of Paris is a medieval confusion. No feast day is claimed here for Damaris.

Lykos, his master Theron, the old sweeper, the councillor's gentle face, the weather and the streets are our own invention, and so are Lykos's thoughts. The bronze plaque at the foot of the hill is real.

Saint Paul is remembered with Saint Peter on the twenty-ninth of June, and Saint Dionysius the Areopagite on the third of October. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

And here is a blessing, from the words Paul spoke on the hill. May the God who made the world and everything in it, who gives you life and breath and everything else, be near you tonight, as he is near every one of us. May you feel your way towards him in the dark, and find him. And may you rest in him, in whom we live, and move, and have our being.

Goodnight, and God bless.

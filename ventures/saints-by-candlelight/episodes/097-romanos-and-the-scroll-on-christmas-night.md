Imagine a church at night, a long time ago.

It is not a big church. It has a dome, and a few columns, and an old wooden floor near the door that creaks if you step on the wrong board. It is very late on the night before Christmas. Most of the lamps have burned down. There is a smell of oil and smoke and cold stone.

Near the front, on the floor, sits a man in a deacon's robe. He is not old and not young. He has his back against a column and his chin on his chest, and he is asleep. Not the polite sort of asleep where you are just resting your eyes. The real kind, with his mouth a little open.

A small boy with a long taper is creeping along the wall, relighting the lamps one by one. He stops beside the sleeping man and looks at him. He thinks about waking him up. Then he decides not to, because the man looks tired, and because it is Christmas.

So he goes on lighting the lamps.

And, if the old story is right, by the next morning the man asleep against the column will be singing a hymn that people have been singing every Christmas for nearly fifteen hundred years.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is about Saint Romanos the Melodist, the great hymn-writer of Constantinople, from what his own hymns tell us and from the short life in the old church calendar of that city, which was written down long after he died and which we will tell as its report, and the boy who lights the lamps and the woman who sells the bread are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

We are going back to Constantinople again. If you have been listening to the last few episodes, you will know the place by now. The great city on the water, where Europe and Asia nearly touch. Hills and domes and harbours, and ships coming in from everywhere. We have been here with Saint John Chrysostom, preaching so long that people forgot to clap. We have been here with Saint Olympias, who gave away a fortune and ran a house full of praying women beside the great church. And last time we were here with Saint Daniel, standing on his pillar above the Bosporus while the boats went up and down below him.

Tonight we are a little later. Daniel died in the year four hundred and ninety-three. A few years after that, a new man arrives in the city. He comes, the old calendar says, in the time of the emperor Anastasius, who reigned from four hundred and ninety-one until five hundred and eighteen.

His name is Romanos.

He is from Syria. The old calendar says he was born at Emesa, which is the city we now call Homs. It was a busy, old, sunny city on a river, in a land where people spoke Syriac and Greek, and where the churches had been singing long, beautiful poems for a very long time. Some scholars have guessed, from small hints in his hymns, that his family may have been Jewish before they were Christian. That is only a guess. The old calendar does not say.

What the old calendar does say is that before he came to Constantinople, Romanos was a deacon at Berytus, which is Beirut, on the coast. A deacon, if you have not met one before, is a man ordained to serve. In those days, deacons read the Gospel aloud in church, and called out the prayers, and helped at the altar, and looked after the poor, and did a great many practical things that bishops were too busy for.

And then, for reasons nobody wrote down, he packed up and came to the capital.

Now, I want you to picture what that would have been like. Coming from Beirut to Constantinople at the beginning of the five hundreds would be a bit like coming from a pleasant seaside town to the biggest city in the world. There were perhaps several hundred thousand people. There were races at the Hippodrome, and the races had teams, and the teams had fans, and the fans had opinions. There were markets selling silk and pepper and fish and icons and cheap sandals. There were churches on every corner. And there were a very large number of clergy, all of whom, I suspect, were quite sure they were the best singer in the building.

Romanos, according to the old story, was not the best singer in the building.

He was attached to a church of the Mother of God in a part of the city called Kyrou. It was an old church, and a well-loved one, but not one of the giant ones. He served there. He prayed there. And, the story says, he was a very devout man, quiet and kind and faithful, who spent his nights in prayer.

But he could not sing very well. And in a church where nearly everything is sung, that is a little like being a cook who cannot smell.

* * *

Let me introduce you to two people who are not in the old story at all.

The first is a boy called Stephanos. He is about ten. His job, if you can call it a job, is to help with the lamps at the church in Kyrou. Every evening he goes round with a long taper and a jar of oil, filling the little glass lamps that hang in front of the icons, trimming the wicks, and lighting them. He is paid in bread and the occasional coin and being allowed to sleep in a warm corner by the door. He is very proud of the lamps. He knows exactly which ones smoke and which ones gutter and which ones you have to light twice.

The second is a woman called Eudokia. She sells bread from a basket at the bottom of the steps outside the church. She has a very loud voice and a very soft heart, and she knows everybody's business, because everybody buys bread.

Neither of them existed. We made them up. But there were certainly boys who lit lamps and women who sold bread on the steps of every church in Constantinople, and nobody wrote their names down, so tonight we are borrowing two of them.

Now, on the evening Romanos first arrives at the church, Stephanos is up a ladder.

"Mind the third step," he calls down. "It wobbles."

The new deacon looks up. He has a travelling bag over one shoulder and dust on his boots, and he is smiling.

"Thank you," he says. "Do you do all of these yourself?"

"All forty-one," says Stephanos. "Except the big one over the altar. The priest does that one. He says I'll drop it. I won't drop it."

"I'm sure you won't," says Romanos. "I'm Romanos. I've come from Beirut."

"Is that far?"

"Quite far. You go down the coast and then along a lot more coast and then up some more coast, and then there's a great deal of sea."

Stephanos thinks about this. "Did you come to sing?"

And Romanos laughs, a little sadly.

"I came to serve," he says. "I'm not sure anybody is going to want me to sing."

* * *

The old story is short. It is only a few lines in a book called the Synaxarion, which was the church calendar of Constantinople, a sort of great list of saints for every day of the year, with a little paragraph about each one. These paragraphs were written down a long time after the people in them had died, sometimes centuries afterwards, and they collect what people remembered and what people told. So we are going to take it gently, as a story that was told about him, and not as a report from somebody who was there.

But the story it tells is lovely.

It says that Romanos was given a place among the clergy, and that he served faithfully. It says he did not have a fine voice, and that he was not learned in the way some of the other clergy were. And it suggests, as stories like this often do, that some of the others did not think very much of him. I can imagine how it went. There is always somebody in a choir who rolls their eyes.

We do not know their names. I am not going to invent any villains for tonight. I will just say that there were people in that church who were better at singing than Romanos, and who knew it, and who were not always as kind about it as they might have been.

Here is how I imagine it looked from the bottom of the steps.

"How's the new deacon?" says Eudokia, handing Stephanos a roll.

"He's nice," says Stephanos. "He helped me with the lamps. He's very bad at ladders."

"And the singing?"

Stephanos makes a face. "He tries very hard."

"Oh dear," says Eudokia.

"Some of the others laugh. Not out loud. They do it with their eyebrows."

"Well," says Eudokia, "God doesn't listen with his eyebrows. You tell him that from me."

* * *

Before we go on, I want to tell you what kind of singing we are talking about, because it is not quite like anything in most churches today.

In the churches of the East at that time, there was a special kind of long sung poem, sung at the night vigils before great feasts. Later, people called it a kontakion. Nobody is quite sure why. One explanation, and it is only one, is that it comes from the word for the little wooden rod that a long scroll was wound round. So a kontakion would be, roughly, a thing on a scroll.

A kontakion worked like this. First there was a short opening verse, a sort of prologue. Then came a long string of stanzas, eighteen or twenty or twenty-four of them, all in exactly the same shape and all sung to the same tune. The stanzas were called houses, which I think is a nice word for them. You go from one house to the next, like walking down a street. And every house ended with the same line, a refrain, which the people in the church could sing back. So the singer, standing up high in the pulpit, sang the story verse by verse, and at the end of every verse the whole congregation sang the refrain together.

If you want a modern comparison, it is a little like a very long folk song with a chorus everybody knows. Except that it was also a sermon. The kontakion told a story from the Bible, and acted out the people in it, and gave them speeches and conversations, and explained what it meant, all in verse, all sung, in the middle of the night, by candlelight.

And there was one more clever thing. If you took the first letter of every house and read them down the page, they spelled something out. That is called an acrostic. It might spell the name of the feast. Or it might spell the name of the poet.

Hold on to that. It matters later.

* * *

A few weeks before Christmas, in our story, Stephanos finds Romanos sitting on the church steps in the dark, after everyone else has gone in to supper.

It is cold. The wind is coming off the sea, the way it does in that city in December, and it smells of salt and woodsmoke. Eudokia has packed up her basket and gone home. Down the hill, the lamps are coming on in the windows, one by one, like somebody lighting a very large church.

Stephanos sits down next to him.

"Are you sad?" he says. Ten-year-olds do not believe in going round the side of a question.

Romanos thinks about it. "A little," he says. "I sang the wrong note at vespers. Twice. In the same place."

"I heard," says Stephanos. "Everybody heard."

"Thank you, Stephanos."

They sit for a while.

"Why don't you stop?" says Stephanos. "Singing, I mean. You could just do the readings. You read very well. Or you could help me with the lamps. You're bad at ladders, but you'd get better."

Romanos laughs. Then he is quiet for a bit.

"When I was a boy," he says, "about your age, in Emesa, I used to stand at the back of the church on feast nights and listen to the hymns. Long ones. Stories from the Bible, all sung, verse after verse, and everyone answering. And I thought it was the most beautiful thing in the world. I thought, if I could give people one night like that, just one, I wouldn't mind about anything else."

"But you can't sing."

"No," says Romanos cheerfully. "I can't. That's the difficulty."

Stephanos frowns at the lamps down the hill.

"You could pray about it," he says at last. "My mother says if you can't do something, you ask, and then you do the bit you can, and then you wait."

Romanos looks at him for quite a long time.

"Your mother," he says, "should be a bishop."

"She sells fish."

"Well," says Romanos, "so did some of the apostles."

And he gets up, and dusts off his robe, and goes in to light the lamps with Stephanos, because that is the bit he can do.

* * *

Now we come to the night itself.

It is the vigil of Christmas. In those days, the great feasts began with long services through the night, and the people came and stood, and prayed, and sang, and the lamps were all lit, and the incense went up. The old story says that Romanos had kept that vigil, and that afterwards he was worn out, and he fell asleep in the church.

One version of the story says it was the great church of the Mother of God at Blachernae, at the far end of the city by the walls, where the emperor sometimes came for the feasts. The story does not much mind where. What it cares about is what happened next.

Here is how I imagine it. The service is over. The people have gone home. The other clergy have gone off to bed. And Romanos sits down against a column near the front, because his legs are tired and his heart is a bit heavy, and before he knows it he is asleep.

And little Stephanos, who has followed the clergy over for the vigil to help with the lamps, comes creeping round with his taper. And he sees the deacon from Beirut asleep on the floor. And he thinks about waking him up. And he does not.

That is the moment I gave you at the beginning.

What happened inside Romanos's sleep, nobody but Romanos could ever know. But this is what the old story says.

It says that the Mother of God came to him in a dream. That she held out to him a scroll, a roll of paper. And that she told him to eat it.

Now, I know that sounds odd. If somebody gave you a roll of paper in a dream and told you to eat it, I think you would at least ask what kind of paper it was. But if you know your Bible, you might recognise something. In the book of the prophet Ezekiel, God gives Ezekiel a scroll and tells him to eat it, and Ezekiel says it was as sweet as honey in his mouth. And in the book of Revelation, Saint John is given a little scroll and told to eat it too. So the people who first told this story were not being silly. They were saying something quite serious. They were saying that what Romanos was about to sing had been given to him, the way words were given to the prophets. That it was a gift, and not something he had made up out of his own cleverness.

That is the story's way of saying it. We cannot check a dream. But we can say what the story means, and that is what it means.

And, the story says, in the dream, Romanos ate the scroll.

* * *

Then he woke up.

Imagine it. The church is cold and grey. The lamps have burned low again. The first light of Christmas morning is coming in through the high windows. Stephanos is curled up asleep by the door with the empty oil jar next to him, because he is ten and it has been a very long night.

And Romanos gets up.

The old story says that when the time came in the morning service, he went up into the ambo. The ambo was the raised pulpit in the middle of the church, a sort of stone platform with steps up to it, where the readings were read and the hymns were sung. He climbed the steps. The church was full again by now. And he began to sing.

And what he sang was a hymn for Christmas that began, in our own plain words, something like this.

Today the Virgin gives birth to the one who is above all things. And the earth offers a cave to the one nobody can come near. Angels give glory with the shepherds. Wise men travel with a star. Because for our sake a little child has been born, who is God before all the ages.

That last line was the refrain. A little child, God before the ages. Every house of the hymn came back round to it.

* * *

Now, I am going to stop the story for a moment, because this is the point where I want to tell you which part is legend and which part is true.

The dream and the scroll and the voice that suddenly worked, those are the old story. We do not know if they happened the way it says.

But the hymn is real. It survives. Scholars have studied it carefully, and they are confident that Romanos wrote it. The opening verse, the one about the Virgin and the cave and the little child, is still sung in Orthodox churches all over the world every Christmas. It is in the service books. If you went to the Christmas Liturgy in a Greek or Russian or Romanian or Serbian or Antiochian church, you would very likely hear it, in some form. And people did not only sing it in church. For a long time afterwards, it was sung at Christmas at the emperor's own table in Constantinople.

So whatever happened that night, something happened. A deacon who was not much of a singer gave the Church a Christmas hymn that has been sung, in one form or another, for nearly fifteen hundred years.

* * *

Let's go back to Eudokia, at the bottom of the steps, on Christmas morning. The church doors are open and the singing is coming out.

Stephanos comes running down the steps, with his hair sticking up because he has only just woken up.

"Eudokia! Eudokia! It's him! It's the deacon from Beirut!"

"What's him?"

"Singing! That's him singing! Listen!"

And Eudokia stops with a loaf in each hand and listens. Out of the doors comes the voice, high and clear, and then the whole church answering it. A little child, God before the ages.

"That's him?" she says.

"That's him."

Eudokia puts down the bread. She does not say anything for a while. And then she says, "Well. I told you. God doesn't listen with his eyebrows."

And she goes up the steps to listen properly, and leaves her basket at the bottom, and for once in her life she does not worry about whether anybody will take a roll.

* * *

From then on, the old story says, Romanos wrote hymns. A great many hymns. The Synaxarion says about a thousand. That is almost certainly a generous way of counting, the way people say somebody has told you a thousand times to tidy your room. What we actually have today is something like eighty-five hymns that have his name on them, and of those, scholars accept about sixty as truly his.

And this is where the acrostic comes in. Remember how the first letters of every house spelled something out? In many of Romanos's hymns, they spell out his name. And not just his name. They spell out, in Greek, something like, this is the hymn of the humble Romanos.

Of the humble Romanos.

I find that very touching. Here is a man whose hymns were being sung at the emperor's feasts, and when he signs them, he signs them, the humble one. He hides his name inside the song, where you have to look for it, and even there he calls himself humble.

Some people think that was just a fashion of the time. Perhaps it was. But I like to think he meant it. I like to think he remembered the eyebrows. And the floor of the church. And being too tired to stay awake on Christmas night.

* * *

Let me tell you about a few of the hymns, because they are the real story of his life. We know almost nothing else about what he did. We know what he sang.

He wrote a hymn about Noah, and about Joseph and his brothers, and about Elijah. He wrote about the woman at the well, and the man who was born blind, and Lazarus coming out of his tomb, which we have told on this show too. He wrote about the ten bridesmaids with their lamps. He wrote about the son who left home and came back, and the father who ran down the road, which we have also told. He wrote about Peter, weeping after he denied the Lord. He wrote about the Resurrection, many times.

And he did something with these stories that was quite bold for his time. He let the people in them talk. Not just say one line from the Bible. Really talk, back and forth, for whole stanzas, so that you could hear them thinking and arguing and being afraid and being glad. He wrote them almost like little plays, sung by one man in a pulpit in the middle of the night.

One of the most beautiful is about Mary at the Cross.

It goes like this, more or less. Mary is following her Son as he is led away to be crucified. She is grieving, as any mother would. And she speaks to him. Where are you going, my child? Why are you going so fast? And he answers her, gently. He tells her not to be afraid. He tells her why he is doing this. He tells her that he is going to bring back Adam and Eve, and everyone who has been lost. And she listens. And at the end of every stanza, the refrain comes round, and the refrain is just Mary's words: my Son and my God.

My Son and my God.

The whole sorrow of the Cross, and the whole hope of it, in five words. I think that is a very great thing to have written. And it was written, if the old story is right, by a man who once could not sing a note.

* * *

Romanos lived a long time. He saw the old emperor Anastasius die. He saw another emperor, Justin, and then Justin's nephew, the great Justinian, the one who wrote the law codes and built the churches.

And in the year five hundred and thirty-two, he saw the city burn.

There were riots that year, the biggest the city had ever seen. They began at the races, with the fans of the two great teams, who usually hated each other, joining together against the emperor. For several days, the city was out of control. Fires were set. A great deal of the centre of Constantinople burned down. And among the buildings that burned was the great church of the city, Hagia Sophia, the church of the Holy Wisdom, standing on the same spot where, about a hundred and thirty years before, Olympias had her house beside the church of her day.

I am not going to tell you about the riots tonight. They were frightening, and a great many people died, and this is a bedtime story. I will only tell you what came after.

Justinian decided to rebuild Hagia Sophia, bigger and more wonderful than anything that had ever been built before. And Romanos wrote a hymn about the earthquakes and the fires. It is one of the few hymns of his that we can date, because it talks about the burning and about the new church being built. In it, he says that the troubles were a call to the city to turn back to God and to pray. And then he describes the new church going up, and the people's joy. Read today, it is like a voice from inside the city in those years, looking at the burnt-out streets and the scaffolding and the new dome beginning to rise, and saying, God has not left us.

The new Hagia Sophia was finished in five hundred and thirty-seven. It is still standing. You can visit it today, in the city now called Istanbul. It is one of the most famous buildings in the world. And for a few years, at least, Romanos was alive to see it.

* * *

Let's look in one last time on Stephanos.

He is not ten any more. In our story, the years have gone by, and Stephanos has grown up. He is a grown man with a beard, and he is still at the church in Kyrou, because some people find the place they are supposed to be and stay there. He does not light the lamps any more. Other boys do that now. He is a reader in the church, and he sings in the choir, and, I am sorry to say, he is a very good singer, and he knows it.

One evening, a young boy is up the ladder, filling the lamps, and the ladder wobbles on the third step, and the boy nearly drops the oil.

And from below, an old voice says, "Mind the third step. It wobbles."

It is Romanos. He is old now. He walks with a stick. His hair is white. He is still a deacon, after all these years. He was never made a bishop or anything grand. He is just the humble Romanos, who lights lamps and writes songs.

And Stephanos, who is standing nearby, watches the old man help the boy down the ladder. And he hears the boy say, "Are you the one who writes the hymns?"

And the old man says, "I am one of the people who sing them."

That is not in any old book. It is only how I imagine him. But I think it is true to the man who wrote "of the humble Romanos" at the beginning of all his songs.

[pause]

We do not know exactly when Romanos died. We know he lived on into the reign of Justinian, and some of his hymns seem to come from quite late in that reign. Scholars usually say he died some time after the middle of the five hundreds. The old calendar says he was buried in the church of the Mother of God at Kyrou, the same church where he had served, and lit the lamps, and been the deacon who could not sing.

His hymns went on being sung for centuries. Later on, the Church's music changed, as music always does. The long kontakia with their twenty houses were too long for the newer services, and bit by bit, most of the houses were dropped. Usually all that was kept was the opening verse and the first house. So the Christmas hymn you would hear in church today is only the doorway of the whole great building Romanos made. The rest of it waited in old manuscripts, in libraries, until scholars in the last century and a half brought it back out, and edited it, and translated it, so that people can read the whole thing again.

There is one more hymn you may have heard of, a very famous one, called the Akathist, a great hymn to the Mother of God, sung standing. For a long time, people said Romanos wrote it too. Most scholars today are not sure he did, and many think he did not. So we will leave that one out tonight, and say only that people loved him so much they gave him the credit for some of the most beautiful things they knew.

He is a saint in both the Orthodox and the Catholic Church. His feast is on the first of October. In the Orthodox Church, that is also the feast of the Protection of the Mother of God, which I think he would have been very happy about. In the Roman Martyrology, which is the Catholic Church's great list of saints, he is remembered on the same day, as Romanos, deacon, called the Melodist. And not long ago, a pope gave a whole talk about him at one of his weekly audiences in Rome, which is not bad for a deacon who could not sing.

[pause]

And I think there is something you can take from Romanos to bed tonight.

He was not the best at the thing he most wanted to do. Other people were better. Other people, I am sure, told him so, with their eyebrows if not with their words. And he did not storm off, and he did not give up. He stayed. He served. He lit the lamps and kept the vigils and prayed through the long nights. And when the gift came, he did not keep it for himself. He gave it to the whole Church, and he signed it, the humble one.

So if you have had a day when you were not very good at something. If somebody laughed. If you felt like the person in the choir who could not quite find the note. You could think of Romanos, asleep on the cold floor of the church on Christmas night, tired out from trying. And of the little boy who saw him there, and did not wake him, and went on quietly lighting the lamps.

And you could think of Stephanos and Eudokia, who never existed, but who stand tonight for all the lamp-boys and bread-sellers and ordinary people who stood at the back of those churches in the night, and sang the refrain back when the time came. A little child, God before the ages. My Son and my God. Nobody wrote their names down. But they were the ones the songs were for.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

What we know about Romanos's life is very little, and it comes from two places. The first is the short entry for him in the Synaxarion of Constantinople, the old church calendar, on the first of October. Hippolyte Delehaye edited it more than a century ago. The second is his own hymns. The great modern edition of the hymns accepted as genuine is by Paul Maas and Constantine Trypanis. José Grosdidier de Matons wrote the classic French study of his life and work, and edited the hymns in French. In English, Archimandrite Ephrem Lash translated many of the hymns on the life of Christ, and Derek Krueger has written well about why Romanos signed himself the humble one.

What is history. That he came from Emesa in Syria. That he was a deacon at Berytus, which is Beirut. That he came to Constantinople under the emperor Anastasius and served at the church of the Mother of God in Kyrou. That he wrote the long sung sermons we now call kontakia, about sixty of which survive and are accepted as his, many with acrostics naming him as the humble Romanos. That the Christmas hymn is his, and its opening is still sung. That he wrote about Mary at the Cross, and about the earthquakes and fires after the riots of five hundred and thirty-two. That he lived into the reign of Justinian and was buried at Kyrou.

What is the old story's report. That he had a poor voice and was looked down on. The dream of the Mother of God, and the scroll, and the singing from the ambo on Christmas morning. The number of a thousand hymns. We have told these as the Synaxarion tells them, and we cannot check them. Which church the vigil was in varies in the telling.

What we left out. The old idea that he wrote the Akathist hymn, which most scholars now doubt. The riots of five hundred and thirty-two, apart from a line or two. And all but a few of his hymns.

What we invented. Stephanos the lamp-boy, Eudokia the bread-seller, the wobbly third step, the eyebrows, and almost all the words anybody says. Romanos's hymns are given in our own short, plain paraphrase, not in anybody's translation.

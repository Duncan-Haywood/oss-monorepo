It is early in the morning, in a city in the north of Mesopotamia, and a small crowd of people is standing in front of a stone gateway.

There is an old bishop, with a white beard and a plain dark robe. There are a few clergy, and a boy holding a lamp that nobody needs any more, because the sun is up. And there is a woman in a dusty travelling cloak, who has come an extremely long way to stand in exactly this spot, and who is trying very hard not to look as excited as she feels.

The bishop prays. Then he unrolls a sheet of writing, and he begins to read aloud, slowly, the way you read something you love.

"Blessed are you," he reads, "who believed in me without having seen me."

The woman closes her eyes. She has read these words before, at home, in a copy of a copy. But she has never heard them read here, at the gate they came through.

If you have been with us these last few nights, you know Egeria already.

If you haven't, here is all you need. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, very probably from the north-west corner of Spain, though we cannot be quite sure, because the beginning of her letter is lost. Some time around the year three hundred and eighty, she travelled east to see the places in the Bible with her own eyes. She lived in Jerusalem for about three years, and from there she went out on journey after journey: to Egypt, to Mount Sinai, to Mount Nebo, to the far side of the Jordan to look for the tomb of Job. And she wrote all of it down, in a long, chatty, wonderfully polite letter to a group of women back home, whom she calls her sisters, and her ladies, and sometimes, when she is feeling very fond of them, her souls.

Only part of the letter survives. It turned up in eighteen eighty-four, in a single old copy, in a library in the Italian town of Arezzo, with pages missing at the beginning and the end. But what is left is a treasure.

And tonight, Egeria has decided to go home.

This is how she puts it. "Some time later," she writes, "when three full years had passed since I came to Jerusalem, and I had seen all the holy places I had come to pray at, I decided, in God's name, to return home."

That sounds very final. But if you know Egeria at all, you will not be surprised by the next sentence. Because she goes on: but first, she wanted to go to Mesopotamia.

Mesopotamia, if you have not been there lately, means the land between the rivers, the two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates. In Egeria's time the western part of it belonged to the Roman Empire, and the eastern part belonged to the Persians, and the line between them moved about, depending on who had won the most recent war. It was not, in other words, on the way home to Spain. It was in roughly the opposite direction. It was rather as if you lived in Lisbon, and you were on holiday in Rome, and you decided to go home by way of Moscow.

Why did she want to go?

Two reasons, she tells us. First, there were monks there. Holy monks, she says, very many of them, and of such a good life that it could hardly be described. Egeria collected holy monks the way some people collect stamps. She could not hear of a good one without wanting to go and meet him.

And second, there was the city of Edessa. And in Edessa, she writes, there was the martyrium of Saint Thomas the apostle, which is to say the shrine built over the place where he was buried, "where," she says, "his whole body is laid."

And then she adds something that I think is very like her. She says that there is not a single Christian who has reached the holy places in Jerusalem who does not also go on to Edessa to pray there.

Not one. Every single one of them.

I would gently suggest that this may have been a slight exaggeration. Edessa is a very long way from Jerusalem. It is about as far as from London to the very top of Scotland, on a mule. I suspect there were quite a lot of Christians who reached Jerusalem, looked at the map, and said, that's lovely, but I think I'll go home now. But Egeria was not one of them. And to Egeria, I think, it simply did not seem possible that anyone could come so far, and stop.

* * *

So she went north.

First to Antioch, the great city of Syria, the city where, the Acts of the Apostles tells us, the followers of Jesus were first called Christians. Then on, through the province, to a city called Hierapolis, which means holy city. Egeria calls it very beautiful and very rich, and full of everything a person could want. And just beyond it, she came to the river.

"I came," she writes, "to the river Euphrates, which the Bible very rightly calls the great river Euphrates. It is very big, and really rather frightening. It flows down with a current as strong as the river Rhone, except that the Euphrates is bigger still."

The Rhone. I love that she says the Rhone. It is one of the little clues that makes people wonder about where she came from. The Rhone is the great fast river of the south of France, and if you have ever stood on a bridge over it, in Avignon or Lyon, and looked down at the water going by, green and muscular and in a very great hurry, you will know what she meant. Whether she had seen it on her way east, or whether she had grown up near it, we cannot tell. But she knew it. And when she saw the Euphrates, the Rhone was what came into her mind. And then she had to admit, honestly, that the Euphrates was bigger.

There was no bridge. You crossed the Euphrates by boat, and not by any old boat. "It has to be crossed in ships," she says, "and big ships only." And so she waited at the river bank for, she tells us, more than half a day, while the ships went to and fro, and the animals and the people and the baggage were all got across.

She does not tell us what she did with that half day. So let us imagine it.

It is a hot morning, and the river is very wide and very brown, and it is making a noise like a crowd a long way off. On the bank there is a great muddle of people waiting: merchants, with bales of cloth; a family with three goats, two of whom do not want to get on a boat; some soldiers; and one pilgrim woman from the far west, sitting on a stone, writing.

And a ferryman, who has finished loading one ship, and is waiting for it to come back, comes and sits down on the next stone, because it is the only shady one.

"You are writing," he says. He says it the way you might say, you are juggling. It is not a thing he sees very often.

"I am writing to my sisters," says Egeria. "At home."

"Where is home?"

She tells him. He has never heard of it. She tells him it is at the end of the world, beside the great ocean, where the sun goes down into the sea, and he nods, politely, the way you would if somebody told you they came from the moon.

"And what do you tell them," he says, "about my river?"

"I tell them it is like the Rhone," says Egeria. "But bigger."

"What's the Rhone?"

"A river," she says. "In Gaul. It's very fast. People are very proud of it."

The ferryman considers this. He looks out at the Euphrates, which is going past at the speed of a trotting horse, carrying a whole tree with it, roots and all.

"Put that it's a lot bigger," he says.

And she laughs, and says she will. And I like to think that she did, because, as we have seen, she wrote "bigger".

The ship came back. The goats were persuaded. And Egeria crossed the great river Euphrates, which once marked the edge of the land God promised to Abraham, and came, she says, into the land of Mesopotamia of Syria.

* * *

Edessa was, and still is, a city on a low plain, with a ridge of hills rising behind it to the south and west. Today it is called Urfa, or Sanliurfa, and it is in the south-east of Turkey, not far from the border with Syria. In Egeria's time it was a Roman city, and an important one, close to the frontier with Persia. Its people spoke Syriac, a cousin of the Aramaic that Jesus spoke, and they were very proud indeed of their city, for a reason we will come to.

Egeria arrived, and the first thing she did was what she always did.

"As soon as we arrived," she writes, "we went straight to the church and the martyrium of Saint Thomas. And there, as is our custom, we made our prayers, and did all the other things we are used to doing in the holy places. And we also read some passages about Saint Thomas himself."

You may remember her habit, from the other nights. Wherever she went, she prayed, and then she read the part of the Scriptures that belonged to the place, and then there was a psalm, and then she prayed again. At the burning bush, the story of the burning bush. On Mount Nebo, the death of Moses. And here, at the tomb of the apostle Thomas, she read about Thomas.

She does not tell us which passages. But there are not very many places in the Gospels where Thomas speaks. In fact there are three, and all of them are in the Gospel of John. So let us read them with her.

The first is on the road. Jesus has heard that his friend Lazarus is ill, in the village of Bethany, near Jerusalem. And the disciples are worried, because the last time Jesus was near Jerusalem, people tried to stone him. Going back there seems a very bad idea. But Jesus says he is going. And it is Thomas who speaks up. "Let us also go," he says to the others, "that we may die with him."

Which is a brave thing to say. Possibly a slightly gloomy thing to say, as well. Thomas, I think, was the kind of man who always expected the worst, and then went along anyway. Those are very good people to have with you on a journey.

The second time is at the Last Supper. Jesus has been telling his friends that he is going away, to prepare a place for them in his Father's house. "And you know the way," he says, "to where I am going."

And Thomas says, "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?"

Every group of friends has somebody who says what everybody else is thinking. Thomas was that person. And because he asked, we have the answer, one of the most famous sentences in the whole of the Gospels. Jesus said to him, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life."

And the third time is the one everybody remembers.

It is after the Resurrection. Jesus has come to the disciples on the evening of Easter day, through the locked doors, and stood among them, and said, peace be with you. But Thomas was not there. And when the others told him, we have seen the Lord, he said, quite flatly, that he would not believe it. Not unless he saw the marks of the nails in his hands, and put his finger in them, and put his hand into his side.

A week later, the disciples were in the house again, and this time Thomas was with them. And the doors were locked again. And Jesus came again, and stood among them, and said, peace be with you. And then he turned to Thomas, and he did not tell him off. He simply held out his hands. "Put your finger here," he said, "and see my hands. Put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not be unbelieving, but believe."

And Thomas said, "My Lord and my God."

Nobody else in the Gospels says it quite like that. The man who doubted most is the one who says it most plainly. And Jesus said to him, "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen, and yet have believed."

That last sentence is about us, really. About everyone who came after. About a woman from the end of the world, kneeling at a tomb in Edessa, who had never seen him either.

And tuck that sentence away somewhere safe, because we are going to hear it again before the night is over. In a slightly different form, and from a slightly surprising place.

* * *

The church of Saint Thomas, Egeria says, was very big and very beautiful, and newly built, and truly worthy, she says, to be the house of God. And since there were a great many things in Edessa that she wanted to see, she says, she had to stay there for three days.

Had to. I like that. As if it were a terrible inconvenience.

There were many other martyria in the city, she tells us, the shrines of other martyrs, and many holy monks, some living right beside the shrines, and others in cells further out, in quiet places away from the town. And there was the bishop.

"The holy bishop of that city," she writes, "a truly religious man, a monk and a confessor, welcomed me very kindly."

A confessor, in those days, meant somebody who had suffered for the faith, and lived. Who had been threatened, or imprisoned, or sent into exile, for refusing to give up what he believed. Historians think this bishop was probably a man named Eulogius, who had been sent away into exile in Egypt some years before, and had come home again when times changed. Egeria does not tell us his name. She just tells us he was kind. And then she tells us what he said to her, which is one of my favourite moments in the whole letter.

"Daughter," he said, "I see that you have taken on a very great labour, for the sake of your faith, coming from the very ends of the earth to these places. So, if you like, we will show you all the places here that Christians love to see."

And Egeria, she says, thanked God first, and then the bishop, and said yes, please.

Of course she did.

Now, the bishop could not personally show her round every corner of the city. He was a bishop. He had things to do. So, as bishops do, he found somebody young to do the walking. And since Egeria does not tell us who that was, we shall invent him.

His name was Abda, which in Syriac means servant. He was about sixteen. He was a reader in the church, which meant that he read the Scriptures aloud at the services, and he was very good at it, and very proud of it, and he spoke Syriac and Greek, and was learning Latin from a book, and so far had got as far as the word for table.

"Mensa," he said to Egeria, as soon as he was introduced. "Table."

"Very good," said Egeria.

"It is the only one I know," said Abda. "We will have to speak Greek."

And so they spoke Greek.

* * *

The bishop came with them for the first part, and the first place they went was the palace of the king.

Edessa, you see, had had kings. Not Roman governors, at first, but kings of its own, a line of them, ruling a little kingdom on the edge of the great empires. And the most famous of them all, the one the people of Edessa told stories about, was a king called Abgar.

In the palace, the bishop showed Egeria a great marble statue of King Abgar. "Very like him, they said," she writes, "with a sheen to it, as if it were made of pearl." And from the face, she says, he seemed to have been a very wise and honourable man. Next to it was a statue of his son, Magnus, which also, she says, had something gracious about the face.

And then, standing in front of the statue, the bishop told her the story. The story of why Edessa was so very proud of itself.

Now, this is the moment for me to put on my honest voice. What follows is the tradition of Edessa, the story the city told about itself. Egeria believed it. The bishop believed it. A great many Christians, for hundreds of years, believed it. But it is not in the Bible, and most historians today think it is a legend, a beautiful one, that grew up in Edessa long after the time of Jesus. We will tell it as the bishop told it. And at the end, I will tell you what we can and cannot know.

Here is the story.

Once upon a time, in the days when Jesus was going about Galilee, healing the sick, King Abgar of Edessa was ill. He was very ill, with a disease that his doctors could not cure, and he was in a great deal of pain. And news reached him, from travellers and merchants coming up the roads from the south, of a man in Palestine who was healing people. Not with medicines, or herbs, but with a word. The blind could see, the lame could walk, and even, the travellers said, the dead were raised.

And King Abgar did what kings do. He wrote a letter.

The letter, as Edessa remembered it, went something like this. "Abgar, ruler of Edessa, to Jesus, the good healer who has appeared in the country of Jerusalem, greetings. I have heard about you, and about your healings, that you do them without medicines or herbs. And when I heard all this, I decided that one of two things must be true. Either you are God, come down from heaven, or you are the Son of God. So I am writing to ask you to take the trouble to come to me, and heal the illness that I have. I have also heard that people are grumbling against you, and want to do you harm. My city is very small, but it is beautiful, and it is big enough for the two of us."

I have always loved that last line. It is so kind, and so practical, and so very much the kind of thing a small king might say. Come and live with me. They don't appreciate you down there.

The king gave the letter to a messenger named Ananias, and Ananias rode south, all the way to Jerusalem, and found Jesus, and gave it to him.

And Jesus, the story says, wrote back. Or, in some versions, dictated his answer to Ananias. And here is the answer, as Edessa remembered it.

"Blessed are you, who believed in me without having seen me. For it is written of me that those who have seen me will not believe in me, and that those who have not seen me will believe, and live. But about what you wrote to me, asking me to come to you: I must first fulfil everything here for which I was sent, and after I have fulfilled it, I must be taken up again to the one who sent me. And when I have been taken up, I will send you one of my disciples, to heal your suffering, and to give life to you and to those who are with you."

Blessed are you who believed without seeing. You see. I told you we would hear it again.

And then, the story goes on, after the Resurrection and the Ascension, the apostle Thomas, the very one who had needed to see, sent a disciple named Addai to Edessa. In Greek and Latin he is often called Thaddaeus. And Addai came, and healed the king, and preached in the city, and Edessa became Christian.

That is the story. And whatever you make of it, you can see why the people of Edessa loved it. Other cities had apostles, or martyrs, or bishops who had been at the great councils. Edessa had a letter. In the handwriting, or very nearly, of the Lord himself.

Egeria stood in front of the marble king for some time. Abda, who had heard the story every day of his life, stood next to her and tried not to fidget.

"Do you think he looked like that?" she asked him, quietly, in Greek, nodding at the statue.

Abda considered. "I think," he said, "he probably looked like a man who was ill, and who wanted very much to get better. But a statue cannot show that. So they made him look wise instead."

And Egeria thought that was a very wise thing for a boy of sixteen to say, and she said so. And Abda went pink, and said "mensa" again, for no reason at all.

* * *

And then the bishop took them further into the palace, and showed Egeria the water.

There were pools there, Egeria says, inside the palace grounds. Big ones. And they were full of fish. Fish such as she had never seen in her life, she says, so very large, and so very bright and shining. The water was clear, and the fish moved through it slowly, like a crowd of silver coins that had decided to go for a swim.

Egeria watched them for a long time. You will by now have gathered that Egeria watched most things for a long time.

And the city, the bishop told her, had no other water at all. Only this. The water that came out of the ground here, inside the palace, ran out from the pools and through the whole city, and it flowed, Egeria says, like a great river of silver.

"And now," said the bishop, "I will tell you how this water came to be here."

This is the second half of the tradition, and it is a story the bishop told Egeria himself, and that she wrote down as he told it.

Some time after King Abgar had received the Lord's letter, he said, the Persians came, and laid siege to Edessa. A great army of them, all around the walls. And King Abgar took the letter. He carried it to the city gate, with his whole army behind him, and there, out loud, in front of everyone, he prayed.

"Lord Jesus," he said, "you promised us that no enemy would enter this city. And look, the Persians are attacking us."

And he held up the letter in his hands, in the open gate.

And at once, the bishop said, a great darkness came down. But only outside the city, over the Persians. Inside, it was still daylight. And the Persians, in the dark, could not find their way to the gate, and wandered about, lost, for miles around.

When it was light again, the Persians gave up on the gate, and settled down to a long siege instead. They camped around the city, for months. And they did the thing that besiegers always did. They found the stream that brought water into Edessa, and they dug it away, and turned it off somewhere else, so that the city would die of thirst.

And at that very moment, the bishop said, by the will of God, here inside the palace, on higher ground where there had been no spring before, these springs burst out of the earth. All at once. Clear water, enough for the whole city.

And the water the Persians had diverted, out in their camp, dried up. So that it was the besiegers who had nothing to drink, and not the besieged. And the Persians went home.

"And since that day," said the bishop, "whenever an enemy has come against this city, we bring out the letter, and read it at the gate. And the enemy is driven back."

Egeria wrote it all down. She believed it, and she was moved by it, and she said so. And I want to be fair to her, and to the bishop, and also to you.

So here is what can be said. We do not know which siege the bishop meant, or when it happened. The springs at Edessa are real, and old, and plentiful. They may well have always been there, or there may have been a time when the city's water supply was changed. Whether God protected the city in a time of danger, in answer to the prayers of its people, is not something a historian can say yes or no to. Christians have always prayed for their cities, and given thanks when they were spared. But the letter itself, and its promise that no enemy would ever take the city, are part of the legend, and should be held as a legend.

And Edessa, I am sorry to say, was not always spared. Like every city on a frontier, it was besieged and taken more than once, in the centuries that followed. The promise in the story was not a promise God made. It was a hope the city held.

That does not make the prayers foolish. It does make the story a story.

* * *

And then they went to the gate.

This, for Egeria, was the high point. The gate, the bishop said, through which the messenger Ananias had come back into the city, carrying the Lord's letter.

And there, at the gate, the bishop stopped, and prayed. And then he took out the letters, the letter of Abgar to the Lord, and the letter of the Lord to Abgar, and he read them aloud, standing in the gateway, to Egeria and her companions. And then he blessed them, and prayed again.

This is the moment we started with, at the beginning of the episode.

"Blessed are you," he read, "who believed in me without having seen me."

And standing in the gateway, Egeria, who had walked up Sinai, and stood on Nebo, and stood by the spring in the garden of Saint John, who had seen more holy places with her own eyes than almost anyone alive, heard the words about those who believe without seeing.

I think she would have smiled at that. Because in the end, that was every holy place she ever visited. She saw the mountain, but not the fire on it. She saw the bush, but it was not burning. She saw the valley where Moses was buried, but no one knows where. She saw the gate, but not the messenger. She went all that way to see, and in every single place, the most important thing was the thing she could not see, and believed anyway.

Then the bishop told her something else about the gate. From the day that Ananias came in through it with the letter, he said, the people of Edessa had kept a rule. No one who was in mourning passed through that gate, and no funeral, no dead body, was ever carried out through it. It was kept for the living, and for joy.

I find that very touching. A city that kept one door, out of all its doors, just for good news.

Abda, standing beside her, said quietly, in Greek, "I come this way every morning. To the church."

"Every morning?"

"It is the long way," he said. "But I like to."

* * *

The bishop showed her the tomb of Abgar too, and of his whole family, very beautiful, she says, but made in the old style. And he took them up to another part of the palace, higher up, where King Abgar had lived at first, and showed them that, and other places besides.

But the thing that pleased her most of all, she says, she saved for the end of her account of Edessa.

"Something that pleased me very much," she writes, "was that I was given the letters, the one Abgar wrote to the Lord and the one the Lord wrote to Abgar, which the holy bishop had read to us. And although I have copies of them at home, I was very glad to receive them from him there, in case what had reached us at home was less complete. And indeed, what I received here is fuller."

And then she turns to her sisters, and says, "So if Jesus our God wills, and I come home, you will read them, ladies, my souls."

I love this, for two reasons.

The first is how very like a traveller it is. She has been away for years. She has seen wonders. And here she is, writing home, and you can practically hear her patting the bag. I've got something for you. You'll love it. You'll have to wait.

And the second reason is a scholar's reason, so I will be quick. Egeria says her new copy was fuller than the one she had at home. And as it happens, we have the letters in more than one form. Eusebius, the great historian of the early Church, who wrote about sixty years before Egeria, had found them in the archives of Edessa, he says, and translated them from Syriac into Greek, and put them in his history. And in Eusebius's version, the Lord's letter does not have the promise about the city. It promises a disciple, and healing, and life. But it does not say that no enemy will ever take Edessa. That promise appears in the later Syriac telling, called the Doctrine of Addai.

So when Egeria says that her copy from the bishop was fuller, scholars have wondered whether the extra part was exactly that. A promise to the city that the city had come to believe, and to read aloud at its gate.

A century and a half after Egeria, the historian Procopius told the story of Abgar and the letter too, and he said, rather drily, that the promise about the city had been added later by the people of Edessa themselves. And a list of books from the sixth century, the one called the Gelasian Decree, put the letter of Christ to Abgar firmly among the apocrypha, the writings the Church does not accept as genuine.

None of which Egeria knew, or could have known. She carried her copies home, and was glad.

* * *

On her last evening in Edessa, let us imagine, Abda walked her back from the shrine of Thomas, through the streets of the city, as the sun went down behind the hills.

"May I tell you a story?" he said. "About the apostle."

"Please," said Egeria, who could never in her life say no to a story.

"In the book about him that we read sometimes," said Abda, "the book of his acts, which is not in the Scriptures, but is very old, it says that when the apostles divided up the world, to go and preach, Thomas was given India."

"India," said Egeria, impressed. She had heard of India. It was even further than Edessa. It was even further than the end of the world, only in the other direction.

"And he did not want to go," said Abda. "He said, I am a Hebrew. How can I preach to the Indians? But he went. And in India the king, Gundaphorus, wanted a palace. And he heard that Thomas was a builder, and gave him a great deal of silver, and said, build me a palace."

"And did he?"

"He gave all the money to the poor," said Abda, with great satisfaction. "All of it. To the widows, and the sick, and the hungry, all over the country. And when the king came and said, where is my palace, Thomas said, you will not see it until you leave this life. It is in heaven."

Egeria laughed. "The king can't have been pleased."

"He was not pleased," said Abda. "He was going to kill him. But his brother died, and saw the palace in heaven, the most beautiful palace, all made of light, and the angels told him, this is your brother's. And he came back to life, and told the king. And the king believed."

They walked a little further.

"It is not in the Scriptures," said Abda again, because he was a careful reader, and wanted her to know that he knew.

"No," said Egeria. "But I think it is a very good story to tell about a man who asked, how can we know the way."

And that, I should say, is a legend, from a book called the Acts of Thomas, written probably in Syriac, in this part of the world, about two hundred years after the apostles. Nobody now thinks it is history. Though, curiously, there really was an Indian king called Gondophares, whose coins have been found, so somebody had heard of him. And it is an old tradition, too, that Thomas really did go east, to India, and Christians in the south of India have called themselves the Christians of Saint Thomas for many hundreds of years.

But a palace in heaven, built out of silver given to the poor. You do not need to know whether it happened, to know that it is true.

[pause]

Egeria left Edessa after her three days. She did not go straight home, of course. She never did anything straight. She went on, further still, to the city of Harran, where Abraham had lived before God called him on to Canaan, and to the well where, the people said, Jacob had watered Rachel's sheep. And that is a story for another night.

And after that, at last, she turned west, and went by the shrine of Saint Thecla, where she found an old friend waiting, and on to Constantinople. And that is a story we have told already.

We do not know what happened to her after that. The letter breaks off. We do not know whether she ever reached home, and handed her sisters the copies of the letters, and said, there, you see, they're fuller. I hope she did.

And Edessa?

Edessa went on. It became one of the great cities of Syriac Christianity, a city of scholars, and poets, and hymns. Saint Ephrem, the great hymn-writer of the Syriac Church, spent the last years of his life there, not very long before Egeria came, and some of the old people she met may have heard him sing.

The relics of Saint Thomas did not stay for ever. In the troubled centuries that followed, they were moved, and moved again, and some of them came at last, in the thirteenth century, to the little town of Ortona, on the east coast of Italy, where they are honoured to this day. And in Chennai, in the south of India, Christians honour a tomb of Saint Thomas too. Between them, I think, the apostle has been to more places since his death than even Egeria managed in her life.

And the gate, where no funeral ever passed? Nobody now knows which gate it was. The walls of Edessa have been built, and broken, and rebuilt, many times over.

But the water is still there.

If you go to Urfa today, in the middle of the old city, under the hill, you will find pools. Long, clear pools of spring water, with old stone buildings round them, and trees. And they are full of fish. Great fat carp, hundreds of them, moving slowly through the clear water. Nobody catches them. Nobody would dream of it. Children buy little bags of food to throw to them, and the fish come up, and open their mouths, and go down again.

The people who live there now tell a different story about the pools, a story about Abraham, and they love their fish very much. Whether these are the very same pools that Egeria saw, nobody can say for certain. But they are in the right place. And the fish are very big, and very bright and shining. And the water still comes up out of the ground, cold and clear, and runs away through the city, like a river of silver.

I like to think of Egeria, on her last morning, standing by the pools, watching the fish, for a long time, before she climbed back up on to her mule.

And of a boy called Abda, who never existed, taking the long way to church through the gate kept for good news.

And of a man called Thomas, who would not believe until he saw, and then said, my Lord and my God.

And of everyone after him who never saw at all. And believed anyway. And was blessed.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from chapters seventeen to nineteen of Egeria's Travels, the long Latin letter written by a pilgrim woman to her sisters at home, probably in the early three hundred and eighties, and found in a single incomplete copy in Arezzo in eighteen eighty-four. Her decision to go home after three full years, the detour to see the monks of Mesopotamia and the shrine of Thomas, her claim that every Christian who reaches Jerusalem goes on to Edessa, Hierapolis, the great river Euphrates and the Rhone, the big ships and the half day's wait, the large new church, the reading about Thomas, the three days, the bishop who was a monk and a confessor and called her daughter, the marble statue of Abgar with its sheen like pearl and the statue of his son Magnus, the pools of great shining fish, the water like a silver river, the bishop's story of the siege, the darkness and the springs, the reading of the letters at the gate of Ananias, the rule that no mourner and no corpse went through it, the tombs of Abgar's family, and the fuller copies she carried home, are all in her letter. Her words, and the bishop's, are given in our plain rendering, shortened.

That the bishop was Eulogius is a historians' identification, not something she says.

The correspondence of Abgar and Jesus is the tradition of Edessa. Eusebius gives it in book one of his Church History; the promise about the city appears in the later Doctrine of Addai; the Gelasian Decree calls the letter apocryphal; and Procopius thought the promise was added later. Most historians today regard the letters as legend. The story of Thomas and the palace in heaven comes from the Acts of Thomas, which is not Scripture. What Thomas says and what Jesus says to him come from the Gospel of John, chapters eleven, fourteen and twenty, told straight.

The ferryman, the reader Abda and every word anyone says apart from what Egeria herself reports are our own invention.

That's the story of Egeria, and the letter at the gate of Edessa, for tonight. Egeria has never been made a saint and has no feast day, but Saint Thomas the apostle, whose shrine she went so far to pray at, is remembered on the third of July in the Roman calendar, and on the sixth of October in the Orthodox Church, which also remembers him on the Sunday after Easter, called Thomas Sunday. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day.

And here is a prayer, the shortest one in the Gospels, and one of the oldest. It is what Thomas said, when he saw. My Lord and my God. And here is the answer, which is for everyone who never has. Blessed are those who have not seen, and yet have believed.

Nobody ever records Joseph saying anything.

That is the first odd thing about him. We have two whole chapters of Matthew about this man, and Luke gives him a few more lines, and in all of it, in every Gospel, there is not one word that Joseph speaks. Not a sentence, not a question, not a sigh. He listens. He wakes up. He does what he is told. And then, every time, the Gospel says it quite plainly: he rose, and he took the child and his mother, and he went.

So tonight's story is about a man who does not talk. Which makes it a very good story for bedtime.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is Joseph, the husband of Mary, and the two dreams in Matthew's Gospel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. Matthew gives us very little detail, so a few small things are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original: the evening in the workshop, the packing, a neighbour at the gate, the dark road, a well, and a household in Egypt. Matthew does not give Joseph a single spoken word, so any words we give him are ours, and we will tell you again in the notes at the end. Everything the angel says follows Matthew, in plain words. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

First, who is Joseph?

Matthew introduces him as the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, and Matthew's very first page is a family tree that comes down through King David to Joseph. When the angel finally speaks to him, he is addressed as son of David. It is a title with weight. It says that Joseph belongs to the old royal line, which had fallen a very long way. The house of David had once held a palace. Now, as far as Matthew tells it, the son of David is a working man in a hill village in Galilee.

What was his work? Matthew, in a later chapter, has the villagers asking, is this not the carpenter's son? The Greek word is tekton, and it means a builder in the widest sense, someone who works in wood and also in stone, who makes a door and a roof beam and a yoke for an ox and a plough. Not a master of a great guild. A man whose hands knew the grain of things. In a village the size of Nazareth he would be the person you went to when anything broke.

Keep that in mind. A man who mends what is broken is going to be asked, tonight, to mend something much bigger, and he will do it the way he mends everything, quietly, with what he has in his hands.

[pause]

* * *

We begin in Matthew's first chapter, and with a problem.

Matthew says it very plainly. When his mother Mary had been engaged to Joseph, before they came together, she was found to be with child from the Holy Spirit. That is the sentence. The Church has always read it as Matthew meant it, and we will say no more than he says.

Now think what that meant, for Joseph, at the time. Engagement in those days was a serious thing, much more binding than ours. The couple were already pledged, and the bond could only be ended by a formal act, the way a marriage can. And the fact that Mary was expecting a child, and that Joseph knew it was not his, was the kind of fact that could ruin a woman in a small village. The old Law was severe about some such cases. And the village would not be kind, whatever the Law said.

So here is Joseph, and what does he do? Matthew gives us one sentence that is the key to the whole man. Joseph her husband, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly.

Read that again. Being a just man, and unwilling to put her to shame. Those two things, being just and being gentle, are not always found together. A just man might say, the Law is the Law. A gentle man might say, I cannot bear to hurt her. Joseph is both. He will not pretend that nothing is wrong, and he will not make Mary pay for it in front of the whole village. He does not know the truth, and he reaches for the kindest thing he can find that is also honest, which is to let her go without a word of public blame.

The old writers loved this. Some of them thought that his holiness was already showing in this moment, before any angel spoke, in how he treated a person he thought had wronged him. We cannot say what he thought, for Matthew does not tell us. But I find it a lovely thing to notice, that the first thing we are told about Joseph is that he was kind in a hard place.

[pause]

Now let us imagine the evening. I will tell you plainly that this part is ours.

Imagine him in the workshop at the end of a day, with the light going orange along the wall, and shavings on the floor, and the smell of cut wood, which is one of the best smells there is. The tools hang on their pegs. He is not working. He is sitting on an upturned crate with a piece of wood in his hand, a short straight piece of olive, and turning it over, and over, and he is not seeing it at all.

He has made up his mind. That is the dreadful thing about it. It has taken him days, but he has settled on the kind thing, and the kind thing is going to hurt both of them very much. And he sits in the workshop and thinks that tomorrow he will go and find two witnesses, which is what the custom required, and do it quietly, and be done.

And then, because a day of carrying that is a heavy day, he lies down on his bed earlier than usual, with the window open, and the sound of the village settling, a dog, a door, someone's pot being scraped, and he sleeps.

[pause]

And in the night, Matthew says, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream.

I always notice that this is how God speaks to him. Not at noon, in the street. Not in front of witnesses. In a dream, in the one place a man is not busy deciding things. The earlier Joseph, the one in Genesis, the one with the coat that had long sleeves, was a dreamer too, and a dreamer who ended up in Egypt. Matthew's readers, who knew their Scripture well, would have felt the echo. Two men called Joseph, both of whom listen to dreams, and both of whom end up in Egypt. I am not going to make anything of it beyond that, but I think it is a little like a rhyme in a poem, and Matthew had a good ear.

The angel says, Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife.

Do not be afraid. That sentence is in the Bible a great many times, and it always lands in the same kind of place, at the edge of something that is too big to hold. Do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child who is conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit. She will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.

Notice what the angel gives him, and what he does not give him. He does not give him an explanation. He does not give him a long lecture. He gives him four things: a name for Mary's child, a reason for the name, a job, and a word of courage. The name is Jesus, which means, the Lord saves. The job is to name the child, and in the custom of the time, to name a child was to claim him, to receive him as your own. And the courage is in the first sentence, do not be afraid.

Matthew then adds, for his readers, that all this took place to fulfil what the Lord had spoken by the prophet, that a virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Emmanuel, which means, God with us. That is Isaiah, and we leave it with Matthew, who knew his prophets better than we do.

[pause]

And now the most important sentence of the first chapter, in my opinion. When Joseph woke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took his wife.

When Joseph woke. Not, when Joseph had thought about it for a month. Not, when he had consulted his relations. He woke, and he did it. He took Mary into his house, and he did not know her, as the old translations say, until she had borne a son. And he called his name Jesus.

I have always thought that this is the heart of Joseph. It is not that he never doubted. The Gospel says he did. It is that when the word came, he did not need to be told twice.

[pause]

* * *

Now there is a gap. Matthew does not fill it, and neither will we, except to say what he says. Jesus was born at Bethlehem of Judea in the days of King Herod, and wise men came from the east asking where the child was who had been born king of the Jews. We told that in other episodes, the shepherds and the night watch, and the old man and the old woman in the Temple. Tonight we pick up after the wise men have gone home.

Matthew says they had been warned in a dream not to go back to Herod, and so they went home another way. That small detail matters, because it means that Herod has been tricked, in the way a bad king would see it, and he is a man who does not take that well.

Now a word about Herod, because we cannot tell this story without him, and I do not want to frighten anyone, so I will be brief and plain. King Herod, the one history calls the Great, ruled the land for a long time, about thirty years. He built enormous things, a harbour, fortresses, a rebuilt Temple. And he was also a man so afraid of losing his throne that he had members of his own family killed. That is not a legend. The historian Josephus, who wrote about a century later, tells it at length, and he is not at all kind to the old king. So when Matthew says that Herod was troubled by the news of a newborn king, and all Jerusalem with him, any listener at the time would have said, yes, that sounds like Herod, and that is going to go badly.

[pause]

So here is the second dream.

Matthew says, now when they had gone, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream and said, rise, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt, and stay there till I tell you, for Herod is about to search for the child, to destroy him.

Let me say what I notice here.

The first thing is that the angel says the child and his mother, not, your wife and your son. Matthew says it again and again, the child and his mother. It is a way of showing where the centre is. Joseph is the guardian, and the child is the one who is guarded. And Joseph, who has a perfectly good claim to be the head of the household, does not mind. He is perfectly happy to be the one who walks beside.

The second thing is that the angel gives him no comfort here at all. No, do not be afraid, this time. Just the order, and the reason. Rise, take, flee, stay. Four short words, a little like the orders a shepherd gives. And a promise that he will be told when it is time to come back.

And the third thing is the one I find most moving of all. The angel does not say, I will take care of everything. He says, you take them. God, who could do anything, asks a carpenter to carry his child through a wilderness. That is how God usually does it, you know. He does not do for us what He has asked us to do for each other.

[pause]

And what does Joseph do? Matthew says, and I like to read this slowly, and he rose and took the child and his mother by night, and departed to Egypt.

By night. Matthew is quite definite. Not in the morning when the neighbours were about, not after a decent interval for packing. By night, with whatever there was, at once.

Let me tell you how I imagine it. This part is ours, and we say so.

He wakes, and the room is dark, and for a moment he does not know why his heart is going so fast. And then he knows. He lies there, just a breath or two, with the words still ringing, rise, take, flee. And then he gets up.

Mary is already half awake, because she heard him move. And in a few short words, which the Gospel does not record and which we will not make up, he tells her. There is no long argument. There is a look. She has been through a good deal herself, and she has learned what an angel's word sounds like, and she says only, what shall we take?

And that is the whole difficulty, is it not? What do you take, when you must leave your home in the dark and you do not know for how long? You cannot take a house. You cannot take the loom or the bench. You can take what you can carry on your own back and on the back of one animal, if you have one, and we should say Matthew mentions no donkey, and the donkey is something the old paintings added, so we will leave it out of the story, and have Joseph carry what he can.

[pause]

So this is what I imagine they put in the bundles.

A blanket for each of them, and one small one for the child. A water skin, filled at the jar by the door. Some flat bread, still a little soft from the evening, and a handful of dried figs, and a lump of cheese in a cloth. A lamp, but not lit. A little oil in a flask. And Joseph, who cannot bear to leave without them, a few of his smallest tools wrapped in a scrap of leather, a chisel, a little adze, a plane no longer than your hand. Because he is a man who works with his hands, and where he is going, he will need to earn bread, and he does not intend to arrive as a beggar.

And I will add one other thing, with a little more caution, for it is a reading and not a fact. Matthew tells us that the wise men had given the child gifts of gold, frankincense and myrrh. Many readers, over many centuries, have thought it a lovely providence that the visitors from the east had left, without knowing it, exactly the sort of thing a family on the run could sell. It is a nice thought, and it may well be true, but Matthew does not say it, and neither can we. We will say only that it is the sort of kindness that God tends to arrange, and leave it there.

They close the door. Not locked. Who locks the door of a house they might never see again? They close it as one closes a door on a sleeping person, softly, and they step out into the lane.

[pause]

* * *

Now it is the middle of the night, in Judea, and we should say a word about the road, because the road is the real character of the next part.

Bethlehem is a small town in the hills, a few miles south of Jerusalem. To go to Egypt from there you go south and west. You go down out of the hills, toward the coast, and across the dry country at the edge of the desert, and out along the coast road, to the border of Egypt, where the land turns to sand and then to the green of the great river. It is a long way. A person walking at an ordinary pace, with a small child and a mother and the bundles, would take, we think, ten days or two weeks to reach the border, and rather longer to get anywhere in Egypt. That is our own rounding, and the old tradition gives no figures at all. But you can see, it is not a stroll.

Matthew says nothing about the road. Not one stop, not one night. The writers of later centuries could not bear that, and filled it with stories, which we will say a word about in the notes. For tonight, we shall keep to what any road at that time must have been, and make no claim.

[pause]

Picture them going out through the town in the dark.

The street is empty. A cat goes by. Somewhere a lamp is still lit, in the window of someone who cannot sleep, and Joseph keeps to the other side of the lane so that its light does not fall on them. The houses are shuttered. At the last turning of the lane, there is a gate, and someone is leaning on it, an old man, a neighbour, who has the habit of walking about at night because he never sleeps well. And he sees them.

He says, in a low voice, that is a strange hour for a journey.

And Joseph says, we have people to visit.

I am sorry to say that is the closest I can come to giving Joseph a line, and it is a very small untruth, or is it? It is true enough, I suppose, in a sense. But I will say that I do not much like it, and I am only putting it there to show you how easy it is for a good man in a hurry to say something less than the whole. The old man looks at the bundles, and the sleeping child, and he says nothing more, but he lifts his hand, in a kind of blessing, and lets them pass. And I think that was a kind thing, and it is not in Matthew, and it is ours.

[pause]

They walk on. The stars are very bright, because there is no moon, and no town lamps to spoil them. Mary carries the child, wrapped against her. Joseph walks a little ahead, and now and then he stops, and waits for her, and they go on. They do not speak much. When you walk at night your voice sounds too loud.

And I think this is a good moment to stop and ask what they were thinking, and the honest answer is, we do not know. Matthew does not say. But I think we can say that they were afraid, which is a perfectly human thing to be, and that they were also calm, which is the other half of the story. They were not alone. The angel had said, stay until I tell you. That meant someone would tell them. They were not simply running away. They were being sent, and that makes all the difference. One kind of fleeing is a panic. The other is an obedience. And the second kind, however frightening, has a floor under it.

[pause]

By the first grey of morning they are in the foothills, and they stop. Under a rock shelf, where the track bends. They sit. Mary gives the child some milk, and Joseph breaks a flat loaf and gives half to her, and takes the other half himself, and eats it slowly.

And I want to tell you about the water. There is a well, a little farther on, an old one with a stone rim worn smooth, and a woman is there already, drawing water with a rope, with two small boys beside her, who stare at the strangers with the open interest of children. She lets down the bucket, and draws it up, and hands the first cup to Joseph, without a word, because that is simply what you do. And he drinks it, and thanks her, and fills the skin.

Then she looks at the child in Mary's arms, and her face changes, the way a mother's face does, and she says something in the dialect of the hills, and Mary smiles. And the woman puts into her hand a small round cake of barley bread, still warm from her own baking, and turns back to her boys.

That is an ordinary kind of kindness. It is the kindness of the road, the kind that belongs to every place where there are wells and travellers. And God, in my experience, is very fond of it. When He sends someone on a long journey, He tends to put a woman with a cake of bread at the well.

[pause]

* * *

I said I would tell the hard part briefly, and I will.

Behind them, in Bethlehem, Herod does what Herod does. Matthew says that when Herod saw that he had been tricked by the wise men, he was in a furious rage, and he sent and killed all the male children in Bethlehem and in all that region who were two years old or under, according to the time he had ascertained from the wise men.

That is in the Gospel, and it is a grief, and the Church has never pretended otherwise. Both the Catholic and the Orthodox Churches remember those children as martyrs, the Holy Innocents, in the last days of the year. The Catholic Church keeps them on the twenty-eighth of December, and the Orthodox Church on the twenty-ninth, by its own calendar. And Matthew, who has been quoting prophets all the way through, here quotes Jeremiah. A voice was heard in Ramah, weeping and loud lamentation; Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they were no more.

I will not say more than that. It is a sorrow, and it is not ours to smooth over. We can say that Matthew does not give a number, and that Bethlehem was a small town, so that many scholars think the number of children was small, though a small number is not a small grief. And we can say, because the Church says it, that these little ones are held in God's hand. It is a bedtime story, and we shall not go into the details. If anyone listening is a little one who is not yet asleep, they can be told, quite truly, that Joseph and Mary and the child were already far away, and safe, because a man did what an angel told him.

[pause]

* * *

Let us go on, then, with Joseph and the road.

Matthew has nothing at all to say about their time in Egypt, apart from a single line, and he was there until the death of Herod. That is all. No town, no house, no trade, no neighbours. Not even how long. So we must invent, if we invent anything, very carefully.

Here is what we do know, from outside the Gospel. Egypt, in those days, was home to a very large Jewish community, especially in the great city of Alexandria, but also in smaller towns along the river. There were synagogues, and Jewish neighbourhoods, and old family connections. A refugee from Judea, who spoke Aramaic and kept the Sabbath, would not have been the only one of his kind. It does not prove where the Holy Family went. Matthew does not say. But it does remind us that Egypt was not a blank. A man like Joseph could have found work, and a roof, and people who prayed the same psalms.

And this is how I like to picture it. It is ours.

A small town on the edge of the delta. A narrow house with a flat roof, rented from a landlord who asks few questions. A courtyard with a fig tree, or some other tree, I do not know what grows there and I will not pretend. Joseph, on the first morning, takes his little leather roll of tools, and walks into the market, and stands near the carpenters' stalls, not asking for work, only looking at the wood. It is different wood. It is heavier, darker, from a different country. A carpenter at the next stall, an older man, with a bald head and big forearms, says something in a language Joseph does not know. Joseph says, in his own, I am a builder. And the old man, with a face that says he has heard worse, hands him a plane and points at a plank.

That is how it goes, I think. Work comes to a man who is willing to begin. And it is a hard season, and a lonely one, and a long one, by any measure. Years, we suppose, though Matthew gives no number. A child who was carried on his mother's back when they left learns to walk, and to talk, and to help sweep the courtyard. Mary teaches him the psalms, in the evenings. Joseph shows him how to hold a chisel, the right way, with the other hand behind the blade. And outside the walls, the great river goes by, and the barges, and the white birds that stand in the shallows.

[pause]

And it is here, in my mind, that I think a little about Hosea.

Matthew says that this was to fulfil what the Lord had spoken by the prophet, out of Egypt I called my son. In Hosea, those words are about Israel, the whole people, who were brought out of Egypt in the Exodus long before. And Matthew sees something in them for Jesus. Now, we should be careful here, for it is a reading, a way of seeing, not a plain prediction, and the Fathers of the Church spent a great deal of time on how the story of Israel is gathered up in the story of Christ. All we need say for bedtime is that Matthew is quietly telling his readers, this child is walking the same road his people walked. Down into Egypt, and out again. It is not an accident. It is the shape of the story.

[pause]

* * *

Years pass, and then, as the angel had promised, another dream.

Matthew says, but when Herod died, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared in a dream to Joseph in Egypt, saying, rise, take the child and his mother and go to the land of Israel, for those who sought the child's life are dead.

Herod died, we think, in or near the year four before the common era. The calendar we use was worked out centuries later, and it was a few years out, so that Jesus was born, by our count, a little before the year one. That is a lovely little oddity, and it is the kind of thing that the historian Josephus lets us check. He describes Herod's last illness, and his death, and how his kingdom was divided among three of his sons.

So the angel says, rise, take, go. The same three words, near enough, as before, and I think that is deliberate. It is a pattern. Joseph rose and took the child and his mother and went to the land of Israel.

And now I will tell you the thing that I think shows the character of this man best.

He goes. He is on the road, coming back, no doubt with the same bundles, though they are smaller and the child is bigger and walks part of the way himself. And somewhere, on the way, he hears that Archelaus is reigning over Judea in place of his father Herod. Archelaus, we should say, was no gentle man. Josephus tells how, right at the start of his rule, he put down a protest in Jerusalem with great violence, and within about ten years the Romans removed him for his cruelty.

And Joseph was afraid to go there. Matthew says so, plainly. He was afraid. The man who rose at once, in the night, when an angel told him to, is also a man who gets frightened when he hears the name of a bad ruler. I find that comforting. It means that being obedient is not the same as having no nerves. And what does he do with his fear? Does he sit down at the roadside and stop? No. Matthew says that being warned in a dream, he withdrew to the district of Galilee.

Warned in a dream. Again, the same way. We are not told what the dream said, but it was enough to point him north, and away from the dangerous throne, to the same country that Herod's other son, Antipas, ruled, a quieter place, and a place where Joseph already had a house.

And Matthew finishes: and he went and lived in a city called Nazareth. Back to the town of the well, and the small hills, and the old neighbour at the gate.

[pause]

Let me end the story there, with the door.

It is a morning, and the road has been very long, and they come up the lane to the house. It is smaller than Joseph remembered, and the roof needs mending, and a plant has grown through the doorstep. The old neighbour is at the gate. He has grown stooped. He looks at the man, and the woman, and the boy of a certain age walking between them, and he says nothing for a moment. And then he says, you were visiting people.

And Joseph, who has nothing at all to say, and never did, says, we were.

And the old man laughs. And that, I am sorry to say, is all the conversation there is. It is ours, and a very small thing. But I like to think that the first thing Joseph did, when he got home, was to look at the roof beam and say, I can fix that. And then he went and found his tools.

[pause]

* * *

Now let us wind down, and say a few things about what came after, and how the Church has remembered this man.

Matthew has one more line for us, a puzzling one, and I will be honest about it. He says that Joseph settled in Nazareth so that what was spoken by the prophets might be fulfilled, he shall be called a Nazarene. The trouble is that no one has ever found those exact words in any one prophet. The Church's teachers have offered several readings, a play on a Hebrew word for a branch, or a word that meant someone from a despised little place, which would match the way Nathanael talked about Nazareth. We shall not choose between them tonight. We say only that it is a line which readers have wondered about for a very long time, and that we are happy to wonder with them.

After this, Joseph appears in the Gospels only once more, in Luke, when Jesus is twelve years old and is found in the Temple. And then he fades from the page. By the time Jesus begins his public work, he is not mentioned among the family, and most Christians have always understood that he had died before that, though the Gospels do not say. The Church calls him the patron of a happy death, because tradition says he died with Jesus and Mary beside him. That is a tradition, and a tender one, and not in Scripture.

And as for the feasts. The Catholic Church keeps the Solemnity of Saint Joseph on the nineteenth of March, and keeps the feast of the Holy Family on the Sunday within the days after Christmas, and on the first of May the memory of Joseph the Worker, who reminds us that honest labour is a holy thing. The Orthodox Church keeps the memory of Joseph the Betrothed on the Sunday after the Nativity, together with King David and James the brother of the Lord, and keeps the Flight into Egypt as part of the same days. Two traditions, and the same quiet man.

I think the reason so many people love him is that he is, in the end, the saint of getting up. A man is woken, and he gets up. He is told to go, and he goes. He is told to come home, and he comes home. And he is, throughout, as the Gospel says, a just man. A man who does what is right without making a noise about it.

So if you are lying in the dark tonight, a little worried about tomorrow, here is Joseph's whole advice, in the only way he ever gave it. He gave it with his hands, and not with words. When you are afraid, do the next thing you know to be right. Then do the one after that. And trust that the One who sent you on the road has put a well somewhere along it.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

The whole of tonight's story is in the Gospel according to Matthew, chapter one, verses eighteen to twenty-five, and chapter two, verses thirteen to twenty-three. It takes a few minutes to read, and I would recommend reading it slowly, once, tonight or tomorrow. Notice how little it says, and how many times it says, he rose, and he took, and he went.

What we have kept from Matthew: Mary found to be with child from the Holy Spirit before they came together; Joseph, a just man, unwilling to put her to shame, resolving to divorce her quietly; the angel in the dream, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife; the child from the Holy Spirit, the name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins; Joseph waking and doing as he was commanded, and taking his wife; the wise men warned in a dream; the angel's second word, rise, take the child and his mother, flee to Egypt, stay until I tell you, for Herod will search for the child to destroy him; Joseph rising and taking them by night; Herod's rage and the killing of the boys of two years and under; the quotations from Hosea and Jeremiah; the third dream after Herod's death, take the child and his mother and go to the land of Israel; the fear of Archelaus; the warning and the withdrawing to Galilee; and the settling in a city called Nazareth.

What we flagged as a reading or as later tradition. That the gifts of the wise men paid for the journey is a reading, loved by many but not in Matthew. The meaning of Hosea's words as applied to Jesus is a reading that the Fathers of the Church developed, and Matthew himself gives it. The line, he shall be called a Nazarene, has several readings, and we have named some and chosen none. The death of Joseph with Jesus and Mary beside him is a tradition and not Scripture. The figure of Herod and the date of his death come from Josephus and not from the Gospel, and the date is given as in or near the year four before the common era, for scholars differ by a year or so. The number of the children killed in Bethlehem is not given by Matthew, and the guess that the number was small is a guess. The length of the road, ten days to two weeks, is our own rounding.

What the later writings say, and we left out. Writings that are not Scripture, such as the Infancy Gospel that goes by the name of Matthew, which was put together some centuries later, tell of a palm tree that bent down to give fruit to Mary, of idols that fell down in an Egyptian temple as the child entered, of robbers who turned back, and of a great many named stops along the way. Churches in Egypt, especially the Coptic Church, keep a rich tradition of the places the Holy Family stayed and honour them, and we respect that tradition, and say plainly that it is a tradition and not something Matthew tells. We have also left out the donkey, which Matthew does not mention, though it appears in almost every painting.

What we invented: the evening in the workshop and the piece of olive wood; the lying down early; the packing, and the small tools wrapped in leather; the neighbour at the gate, and what he said, and what Joseph said to him; the walk in the dark; the well and the woman with her two boys and the cake of barley bread; the courtyard, the market and the older carpenter in Egypt; the boy learning the psalms and the chisel; the return up the lane and the roof beam. None of it is in Matthew, and none of it changes what happens or what it means. We have given Joseph a handful of words for the sake of the telling, and Matthew gives him none. The words of the angel are in our own plain wording and stay close to the Gospel.

One honesty note about our own telling. We let Joseph say that they were visiting people. That is our invention, and we flagged it as a small half truth, because we did not want to make our Joseph perfect where Matthew is silent. Nothing in the Gospel suggests it.

For further reading, the story of the wise men is the scene just before this one, and the finding in the Temple, in Luke, is the scene just after. And if you want to meet the earlier Joseph, the dreamer of Genesis, we have an episode for him, Joseph and the Coat with Long Sleeves.

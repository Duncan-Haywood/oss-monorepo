Imagine a very small room, lit by one lamp, with a smell so thick you could almost lean on it. Sweet smoke, rising in a thin grey line. A gold altar, no bigger than a table. And standing beside it, one old man in white, with his hands raised and his mouth open, and no sound coming out at all.

He is not praying. He is not singing. He has just been told the best news of his whole life, and he has said the one thing you should not say to an angel. And now, quite politely, he has been struck silent.

It will be a long nine months. And at the end of it, a pen.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Gospel according to Luke, chapter one, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the weather, the smell of the incense, the neighbours' chatter, and the quiet moments between the verses, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let us start with the country.

It is the days of Herod, king of Judea. Luke gives us that much, and it is a good clue, because Herod was the sort of king who built enormous things and frightened everyone who lived near them. He rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem, for instance, and made it so splendid that people from across the sea came just to look at it. But he did not do it out of love for the God of Israel. He did it, as far as anyone can tell, because he liked a good building and wanted to be remembered. So the Temple stands there, gleaming, in the middle of a country that is occupied, and a little bit afraid, and rather tired of waiting.

Waiting is the word to hold on to tonight. Israel has been waiting for a very long time. Nearly four hundred years have passed since the last of the prophets spoke, and many people were beginning to wonder whether anyone was going to speak again. And in a small house in the hill country south of Jerusalem, there is a married couple who have been waiting for something of their own, for even longer than they can bear to count.

Luke introduces them in one careful sentence. There was a priest named Zechariah, he says, of the division of Abijah, and his wife was of the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth.

Let us take that slowly.

Zechariah is a priest. In those days, the priests of Israel were all descended from Aaron, the brother of Moses, and there were a great many of them, thousands, far too many to serve in the Temple all at once. So long ago, in the time of King David, they had been sorted into twenty-four families, called divisions, and the divisions took turns. Each one came up to Jerusalem for a single week, twice a year, and the rest of the time its priests went home and did whatever priests do when they are not on duty. They farmed, mostly. They kept sheep. They taught the neighbours' children their letters. They mended walls.

The division of Abijah, Zechariah's family, was the eighth on the list. It is named in the First Book of Chronicles, and we can hear a faint, comfortable sort of pride in Luke's mention of it. This is a proper family, on the proper list, with its proper turn.

And Elizabeth is not only the wife of a priest. She is herself of the daughters of Aaron, a priest's daughter as well. In our own day we might say that these two had been born into the family business, and married into it twice.

Their names are worth knowing, too. Zechariah means, more or less, the Lord remembers. We will come back to that, because the whole story hangs on it. And Elizabeth's name is a very old one, and we will leave it alone, because we would rather not guess.

[pause]

Now Luke says something about the pair of them that is one of the nicest things said about anyone in the Gospels. He says that they were both righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord.

Both. Not just the priest, whose job it was. Both of them. Quietly, and every day, and for decades, they did what they were supposed to do, and they did it well.

And then Luke adds a sentence that sounds like a door closing. But they had no child, because Elizabeth was barren, and both were advanced in years.

We should say something plainly about that, because it can be hard to hear, and because it was harder in their world. Today, a couple who cannot have children is sorrowful, and rightly so, but they are not usually treated as a problem for the whole village. In Zechariah's day, many people took childlessness to be a sign of something gone wrong, a kind of disgrace, though of course it was no such thing. Elizabeth will say as much herself, a little later, in one of the loveliest lines in the story. She had lived for years with the looks, and the kind questions, and the longer silences when the other women talked about their grandchildren.

And remember, they were righteous. That is what makes it hard. They had done everything right. They had kept all the rules, and prayed all the prayers, and given to the poor, and gone up to Jerusalem when it was their turn. And still the cradle in the corner of the house stayed empty, or never got built at all.

If you have ever done your best and had no answer, you will understand them. Luke does not say they were angry. He does not say they gave up. He simply says that they were old now, and that is where the sentence ends.

The Scriptures, as it happens, are full of couples like this. Abraham and Sarah, in the tent at Mamre, when three visitors came to dinner. Hannah, at the door of the shrine at Shiloh, praying so hard that the priest thought she was drunk. We have told both of those on other nights. Every time, it is the same pattern: a long wait, a wonder, and a child who turns out to matter far more than anyone guessed. Luke is writing, quite deliberately, in that old tradition. He wants us to remember Sarah and Hannah, and to feel the family resemblance.

[pause]

Now we come to the day.

Zechariah's division is on duty. It is their week in Jerusalem. The old priest has packed a bag, and kissed his wife goodbye, and walked up into the city, with the other priests of the eighth division, past the market stalls and the money changers and the long, shiny wall of the Temple.

Let us say that it is a cool afternoon, because we like cool afternoons. We do not know what the weather was.

Here is how the work was shared out. Every morning and every evening, a priest burned incense on the golden altar inside the Sanctuary, the holy house itself, the very centre of the Temple. This was a great honour. It was the nearest thing to God's presence that an ordinary priest could ever go. And there were so many priests that, according to the later Jewish tradition written down in the Mishnah, a man might serve his whole life and never be picked. So how did they choose? They cast lots. They drew, much as you might draw straws, and whoever got the short one went in.

And Luke tells us, in half a sentence, what happened. According to the custom of the priesthood, it fell to Zechariah by lot to enter the Sanctuary of the Lord and burn incense.

We should pause here, to enjoy that.

There he is, an old man from a small village, who has not had much good luck in life. A man who has watched other men's sons grow up. And on one particular day, out of all the days in the year, the little stick or stone or marked piece of wood lands in his hand. We can imagine the other priests clapping him on the back. We can imagine him not quite believing it. He might have said, as we would, are you sure?

Luke does not say. But he does say that, outside, the whole multitude of the people were praying at the hour of the incense. Because the smoke was a kind of prayer, rising, and the people stood in the courts and prayed along with it. So the old priest is going in alone, carrying the prayers of a whole crowd. He is the only one in the Sanctuary. And everyone he cannot see is, in a manner of speaking, holding him up.

[pause]

Picture the Sanctuary. It is the first great hall of the Temple, hung with curtains, and lit by the seven-branched lamp, the menorah, on one side, and the table of the bread on the other. It is quiet and warm and golden, and ahead of him, in front of the last great curtain, stands the little altar of incense. The Book of Exodus says exactly what is burnt on it. A sweet blend of spices, ground fine, and offered every morning and every evening, for all time.

Zechariah steps forward. He lays the incense on the coals. The smoke rises. And then, on the right side of the altar, there is somebody standing there.

Luke says it very simply. An angel of the Lord appeared to him, standing on the right side of the altar of incense.

Not appeared in a blaze of light, as in some stories. Not with a great shout. Just, there. Standing. In a room that was supposed to be empty.

And Zechariah is troubled, says Luke, and fear falls upon him. We think that is entirely reasonable. We have all been startled by a person who was not there a moment ago, in a kitchen, say, or a hallway. Now put it in the holiest room in the world, in the middle of your one great day, with your heart already beating hard.

The angel says what angels almost always say first.

Do not be afraid, Zechariah.

And then he says something that must have stopped the old man's breath. He says: your prayer has been heard.

Think about that. Which prayer? There had been so many. For years and years, Zechariah and Elizabeth had been praying for a child. Perhaps they had stopped saying it out loud. Perhaps by now it was only the shape of a sigh, in the evening, in the dark. But the angel says: it has been heard. Not forgotten. Not set aside. Heard.

[pause]

The angel goes on.

Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall call his name John.

John means, the Lord is gracious. And then, in a flood, the angel tells him what the child will be.

You will have joy and gladness, says the angel, and many will rejoice at his birth. For he will be great before the Lord. He shall drink no wine or strong drink. He will be filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother's womb. And he will turn many of the children of Israel to the Lord their God.

And now the angel gives a line that comes right out of the last page of the last prophet. He will go before him, he says, in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of fathers to their children, and the disobedient to the wisdom of the just, to make ready for the Lord a people prepared.

That is from Malachi, the very last of the prophets, the voice that went quiet four hundred years before. And here is Gabriel, picking up the thread where it was dropped. After all the silence, the story is moving again.

We will say, as we always do, that the angel does not do anything flashy. He is not a hero in a movie, with a sword. He is a messenger. He stands, and he speaks, and he gives the old man the most astonishing news. A son. A great one. A prophet. The one who will prepare the way.

Now, if you had been Zechariah, what would you have said?

We think most of us would have said something like this. Yes. Thank you. Could you say that again.

Zechariah says something rather different.

[pause]

He says to the angel: how shall I know this? For I am an old man, and my wife is advanced in years.

Now, let us be fair to him, because we are quite fond of Zechariah. It is not a wicked question. He is not mocking. He has simply done the sums, and the sums are very discouraging, and he has had a great many years of disappointment to get used to. Elizabeth is old. He is old. Everything he knows about the world says, no. And he is standing in a room, being told, yes, and some part of him cannot get from the one to the other.

It is, if you like, the most human thing in the story. Hope, after a long enough winter, can feel almost dangerous. You are afraid to pick it up in case it breaks.

But there is a difference, and Luke makes it clear, between his question and a similar one that comes only a few verses later. When Mary asks the same angel, how can this be, since I do not know a man, she is asking how it will happen. Zechariah asks for a sign that it will. He wants proof. And the angel, who is not unkind, but who is not a man to be bargained with, answers him with some dignity.

I am Gabriel, he says. I stand in the presence of God. And I was sent to speak to you and to bring you this good news.

That is a splendid thing to say. I stand in the presence of God. It is as if he were saying, my friend, you have asked me for a sign, and I am the sign. Look at who is speaking.

And then he gives him one anyway, which is very much like God, when you think of it. Not a punishment so much as an answer. Behold, you will be silent and unable to speak, until the day that these things come to pass, because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled in their time.

Silent. Not for ever. Not as a curse on him. Only until the day. We might say that Zechariah asked how he could know, and was given nine months in which to find out, with nothing at all to say about it.

[pause]

Outside the Sanctuary, meanwhile, the people are waiting.

This is a sweet detail, and Luke gives it plainly. The people were waiting for Zechariah, and they wondered at his delay in the Sanctuary.

Incense does not take that long. You lay it on, you wait for the smoke, you come out, you bless the people. It is rather like a doctor who has said he will be five minutes. After ten, everyone starts to shift about. After twenty, someone says, should we send someone in? And they are standing in the courtyard, a crowd of ordinary people, and no one is willing to be the one who knocks on the door of the holy place.

At last he comes out.

And here the people are waiting for the blessing. It was the custom that the priest would come out and say the old words over them, the ones from the Book of Numbers. The Lord bless you and keep you. The Lord make his face to shine upon you. We know how that goes. And Zechariah stands on the step, looking at them, and opens his mouth, and nothing comes.

He tries again. Nothing.

He lifts his hands and shapes the words, and no sound.

Luke says: he could not speak to them, and they perceived that he had seen a vision in the Sanctuary. And he kept making signs to them and remained mute.

So they understood. Not by being told, but by seeing. They looked at the old man's face, and the way his hands shook, and the shine in his eyes, and they knew that something had happened to him in there, something that no one was going to be able to explain.

We find that rather moving. They did not laugh at him. They did not decide he was ill. They watched him, and they believed him, without a word.

[pause]

Zechariah finishes his week.

That is the next line, and we love it for its ordinariness. When his days of service were ended, he went to his home.

Of course he did. Even after an angel, you still have to go home, and walk the road, and carry your bag, and take your turn at being tired. One of the surprising things about the Scriptures is how often the most astonishing events are followed by a long, plain walk home.

So let us walk with him.

It is a few hours, perhaps a day, down out of Jerusalem and into the hill country. A road with olive trees on both sides, and low stone walls, and goats. We don't know the name of the village, and neither does Luke, though later tradition has pointed to one, near Jerusalem, called Ein Karem. We name that as tradition, and no more.

Imagine the old man, walking. He cannot talk to himself, even, as people do on long walks. He cannot sing. He cannot call out to a shepherd, or say good evening to a passing cart. All he can do is think. And what does he think about? We are inventing now, and we say so. We think he thinks about her. About Elizabeth. About how on earth he is going to tell her.

He cannot say it. He will have to write it, or act it out. And she has never seen him do either. We think that, at some point on that road, he might have laughed. A very quiet laugh, because there is nobody there to hear it. A man who has been given the best news in the world, and who cannot say a single word of it out loud.

[pause]

Now we come to Elizabeth, and to the line we promised you.

Luke says: after these days, his wife Elizabeth conceived, and for five months she hid herself.

We will be honest, we do not know exactly how the news came to her. Luke does not tell us. Perhaps Zechariah wrote it in the dust. Perhaps she saw it in his face the moment he came through the door. It would be like her. A woman who has watched a man for forty years knows when he has seen something.

And then she conceives. And then she hides herself. Five months at home, behind the door, not telling a soul, and why? Luke does not say why, and we won't guess too hard. Perhaps she wanted to be sure. Perhaps she was afraid of speaking too soon, after so many years. Perhaps she simply wanted to keep it close, a secret between herself and God, for as long as she could.

And what she says, in that hidden time, is one of the most beautiful sentences in the Gospel.

Thus the Lord has done for me, in the days when he looked on me, to take away my reproach among people.

Look at that. In the days when he looked on me. For all those years, she had felt overlooked. She had felt that she was somewhere on the edge of God's attention. And now she says: he looked. He turned his face and he saw me, and the shame is gone.

There is something in that for anyone who has ever felt unseen. She does not say that she was never loved, in all those years. She says that now she knows it.

And remember the name of her husband. The Lord remembers. That is what it means. Zechariah. The Lord remembers. And here is a house in the hill country in which a man who cannot speak, and a woman who is hiding for joy, are living proof of it.

[pause]

Luke now moves on, quickly, past some very important things. After this scene, the story moves to Mary, and the angel Gabriel is sent again, to Nazareth, and then Mary hurries off to the hill country to visit her relative Elizabeth. We told that on another night. It is the story of the two mothers, and the leap in the womb, and Mary's great song, and it is worth hearing again. In that story, we hear nothing at all from Zechariah. Luke says Mary stayed about three months, and then returned home. In all that time, the old man was in the house, and Luke gives him no line. We find ourselves rather fond of that. Two women at the table, singing and laughing and comparing notes, and in the corner a very happy old priest, passing the bread.

And then Luke says: now the time came for Elizabeth to give birth, and she bore a son.

Just that. No fuss. The long wait is over.

And what happens next is the part of the story that we love best, because it is so very like a village.

[pause]

Her neighbours and relatives heard that the Lord had shown great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.

Imagine it. News going down the lane like a bird. Women in doorways, shading their eyes. Someone running to tell somebody's aunt. All those people who had said nothing for years, or the wrong things, or the kind things that did not help, now come crowding into the house with bread and cheese and babies of their own. And Elizabeth is in the middle, holding her son, and she is no longer hiding.

We think, a little wickedly, that there might have been someone in that room who had to put down her cup, very carefully, and sit down.

On the eighth day, they come to circumcise the child. This was the law of Israel, from the time of Abraham: on the eighth day, a baby boy was brought into the covenant, and it was also the day he was given his name. A little ceremony, and a great deal of family.

The relatives gather. There are a lot of them. The house is full. And someone, as someone always does, takes charge. They were going to call him Zechariah, says Luke, after his father.

Of course they were. It was the custom, or at least a very common one. The first son was often named for the grandfather or the father, so that the name went on down the family like a lamp passed from hand to hand. And there is no reason to think they meant any harm. They were being kind. They were honouring the old man. After all he had been through, he deserved a son with his own name.

But Elizabeth says: no.

Luke gives her words exactly. Not so. He shall be called John.

Now, think about this. How does she know? Zechariah has not said a word. He cannot. Either he has written it down for her, or else the Spirit of God has told her, and Luke does not say which. We prefer to leave it open, as Luke does. What matters is that she is quite certain. Her voice is calm. She does not argue. She does not explain. She simply says, that is his name.

And the relatives, being relatives, do what relatives do. They object.

None of your family is called by that name, they say.

It is a lovely line, because it is entirely true, and entirely beside the point. Of course nobody in the family is called John. The name has just been sent from heaven. But they do not know that, and so they argue from tradition, in a perfectly decent way. This is not how we do things. There is nobody called John on any of the family lists.

And then, because they cannot quite believe that the mother is the one to decide such a thing, they turn to the father. They made signs to him, says Luke, to find out what he wanted him to be called.

Made signs. We find that very interesting. It seems that they were not just speaking more loudly at him. They were making signs, as if he could not hear either. Luke's word for his silence can mean both mute and deaf, and some readers have thought that the old man lost his hearing for these nine months as well as his voice. We do not know. It is a small point, and we only mention it because it makes the next moment all the sweeter. The whole company are waving their hands at this old man, in the middle of a crowded room, and he sits there, and smiles, and holds up one finger.

[pause]

He asked for a writing tablet.

We do not know what it looked like. In those days, a tablet was often a small wooden board with a thin layer of wax on it, and you wrote on the wax with a pointed stick, called a stylus. When you had done, you smoothed the wax over with the flat end and began again. It was the notebook of the ancient world, and every schoolboy had one.

So somebody finds him a tablet. It is probably a rather scruffy one, borrowed from a child. He takes it onto his knee. He picks up the stylus. The whole room goes quiet, the way a room does when a person is about to do something that will matter.

And Zechariah writes. Just four words, in the Greek of Luke's telling. His name is John.

Not, I think it should be John. Not, let us consider it. His name is John. It is already his name. The old man is not choosing. He is only telling them what is true.

And here Luke says something that is better than any commentary. They all marvelled.

Of course they did. Because this is the second time in a few minutes that two people, who could not have talked to each other, have given the same answer. The mother said it. The father wrote it. And neither had been told by the other, as far as anyone could tell. The relatives look at one another. Something is going on in this house that none of them understand.

And at that very moment, says Luke, immediately, his mouth was opened and his tongue loosed, and he spoke, blessing God.

Immediately. As the last stroke of the stylus is lifted. Not a moment before. Because he had obeyed, in the end. He had named the child as the angel had said, and the silence, which had never been a punishment, but a sign, had done its work.

We can only wonder what his first word was. Luke does not give it. He tells us only that Zechariah spoke, and that what he spoke was a blessing. Perhaps it was something very small. Perhaps it was just the name. John. Perhaps it was Elizabeth's. We would like to think that, after nine months of silence, the first thing he did was say her name.

[pause]

Now there is a great deal of noise.

Fear came on all their neighbours, says Luke, and all these things were talked about throughout all the hill country of Judea. And all who heard them laid them up in their hearts, saying: what then will this child be? For the hand of the Lord was with him.

What then will this child be? That is the question the whole village is asking, and it is a good one. It is the question asked about every baby that ever was. But here it feels different, because everybody can tell that the answer is not going to be ordinary. A priest struck silent. A barren woman with a son. A name nobody in the family has ever had. A mouth opened with a pen. They don't know what it means. They only know that it is something.

And then Luke gives us the song.

Zechariah, filled with the Holy Spirit, prophesied. And the thing he said has been sung in churches ever since. It is called the Benedictus, from its first word in the old Latin, blessed. In many places it is said at dawn, every morning, which seems right, because it ends with the sun coming up.

We will not give the whole of it tonight, because it is long, and you ought to be getting sleepy. But we can give you a plain version of its shape, in our own words.

Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, he sings, for he has visited his people and set them free. He has raised up a horn of salvation for us, in the house of his servant David, as he promised long ago through his holy prophets. He has remembered his covenant. He has kept the oath he swore to our father Abraham.

Notice that word. Remembered. Zechariah, the Lord remembers, is singing about God remembering. He is singing his own name.

And then the song turns, very tenderly, to the baby. He is looking at the child in his arms. And you, child, he says, will be called the prophet of the Most High, for you will go before the Lord to prepare his ways, to give his people knowledge of salvation in the forgiveness of their sins.

And it ends with the best lines of all. Because of the tender mercy of our God, the dawn from on high will break upon us, to give light to those who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death, and to guide our feet into the way of peace.

The dawn from on high. We have all sat in the dark and wished for morning. This is a man who has sat in the dark for nine months, with his mouth shut, and who now sings of the sun.

[pause]

Luke finishes the story with one last sentence, and it is the quietest in the whole account.

And the child grew and became strong in spirit, and he was in the wilderness until the day of his public appearance to Israel.

That is all. The child grew up. He went out to the desert. And for thirty years or so we hear nothing more of him. We will meet him again, as a grown man, by a river, wearing a coat of camel hair, telling people to get ready. But that is another night.

We may only say, as we draw the curtain, that it all began with a lamp, and a lot, and a very old man who found it hard to believe a very good thing. And that the Lord remembered him anyway.

[pause]

Let us slow down now.

The village is quiet. The crowd has gone. The relatives have gone back to their own houses, still arguing quietly about names. In the small house on the hillside there is one lamp lit. The baby is asleep in the corner, in a cradle that is no longer empty. Elizabeth is sitting beside it, with her hands in her lap, not doing anything, only looking.

And Zechariah is there too, in the doorway, with the evening air on his face.

He has a voice again. He could say anything he likes. He could shout. He could sing the song again, as loud as he liked. But instead, tonight, he says nothing. He listens to the sound of his wife breathing, and the baby breathing, and the wind in the olives. It is a different kind of silence from the one he had in the Temple. It is not a silence of fear, nor of waiting. It is the silence of a man who has been given what he asked for, and has nothing more to ask.

If you have been waiting for something for a very long time, we hope that you will hear this story as a gentle word. Not a promise that everything will arrive on the day you want, because that is not what Luke says. He says that the Lord remembers. He says that you were heard before you knew it. He says that even the doubt of an old priest, standing at an altar, can be met with patience, and turned, in the end, into a song.

And if you are tired tonight, you might do what Zechariah did. You might stop trying to say the right thing. You might sit in the doorway in the evening, and be quiet, and let the quiet be enough.

[pause]

Here are the notes for the curious.

The story of Zechariah and Elizabeth is told in the Gospel according to Luke, chapter one, verses five to twenty-five, and fifty-seven to eighty, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. That is our only full source for them, and everything that Zechariah, Elizabeth, Gabriel and the neighbours do and say in the story is kept as Luke gives it. The priestly division of Abijah is named in the First Book of Chronicles, chapter twenty-four. The incense altar is described in the Book of Exodus, chapter thirty. The saying that a priest might never be chosen twice by lot comes from a later Jewish writing, the Mishnah, which was put together long after the Temple fell, and we have given it only as a later report.

We invented the weather, the smell of the incense, the look of the Sanctuary, the old priest's thoughts on the road home, the village chatter, and the look of the writing tablet. The meaning of the names, the Lord remembers and the Lord is gracious, is the usual reading, and we have offered it as a reading.

There is a palm tree on a hillside, standing by itself, which is a strange thing to see in that country, because palm trees like the warm low valleys and this is high up in the hills, where the wind is cool and the winters are wet.

Under the palm tree there is a woman sitting on a flat stone. She is not young and she is not old. She has a cup of water beside her and a stick across her knees, and in front of her, in the dust, two men are arguing about a wall.

Behind them, waiting their turn, there is a line of people. A farmer with a basket. Two sisters who are not speaking to each other. And a small boy holding a rope, and on the end of the rope there is a goat, and the goat is eating the hem of the farmer's cloak.

The woman under the palm tree looks at the goat. She looks at the boy. And then, very slowly, without saying a word, she starts to smile.

And then, the book says, after Ehud died, the people of Israel again did what was evil in the sight of the Lord. And the Lord sold them into the hand of Jabin, king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor.

Let me tell you about Hazor, because it matters.

If you go to the far north of the land, up past the Sea of Galilee, into the green country under the mountains, there is a great flat-topped mound rising out of the plain. That mound is Hazor, or what is left of it. It is enormous. It is one of the largest ancient city mounds in that whole part of the world, and archaeologists have been digging there, on and off, for most of the last hundred years. In its day it had a high city on top, with palaces and temples, and a huge lower city spread out below, and a great ditch and rampart all the way round. The Book of Joshua calls it the head of all those kingdoms. It was the big city. The capital. The place where the roads met.

And the king of Hazor, in tonight's story, had a general. His name was Sisera. And Sisera had chariots.

Nine hundred chariots, the book says. Chariots of iron.

Now, we should be a little careful here. Scholars think these were probably not chariots made entirely out of iron, which would have been far too heavy for a horse to pull. They were most likely wooden chariots, light and fast, with iron fittings, or iron on the wheels, in an age when iron was still new and rare and frightening. But that hardly matters. What matters is this. A chariot, in those days, was the most terrifying machine anyone had ever seen. Two horses at a gallop, a driver, a man with a bow or a spear, coming at you across the flat ground faster than any man can run. If you were a farmer with a hoe, and you saw a line of chariots coming, there was nothing at all you could do except get out of the way.

Except for one thing. Chariots need flat ground. Flat, firm, dry ground. They are very good in a wide open valley in the summer. They are no good at all on a steep stony hillside, among the terraces and the olive trees. And so, for twenty years, the people of Israel lived up in the hills, where the chariots could not follow them, and the Canaanites held the valleys and the roads.

Twenty years. The book says Sisera oppressed the people of Israel cruelly for twenty years. And in the song that comes later, there is a little picture of what that was like, which I find very moving. It says that in those days the highways were abandoned, and the people who had to travel went by the crooked back paths instead. It says the villages stopped. Nobody went down to the wells in the open. Nobody took the main road to market. You stayed close to home, and kept your head down, and hoped nobody noticed you. It is the picture of a country holding its breath.

And the people of Israel cried out to the Lord.

* * *

Which brings us, at last, to the palm tree.

The book says that at that time Israel was being judged by a woman called Deborah, a prophetess. And she used to sit under a palm tree, between Ramah and Bethel, in the hill country of Ephraim, and the people of Israel came up to her there for judgment.

That is right in the middle of the country, high up in the hills, a morning's walk north of where Jerusalem is now. Ramah and Bethel are two old towns, a few miles apart along the ridge. And somewhere between them there was a palm tree, which people called the palm of Deborah, after her.

I love that this is how we are introduced to her. Not on a throne. Not in a palace. Not at the head of an army. Under a tree. Sitting outside in the open air, where anybody at all could walk up the path and find her.

And there is a little echo in that place, which the people who first heard this story would have caught. Because a long, long time before, in the Book of Genesis, there was another Deborah, who was buried in exactly that same part of the country. She was the old nurse of Rebekah, who had looked after Rebekah when she was a baby, and gone with her when she married Isaac, and then, it seems, stayed with the family for the rest of her very long life, looking after the next generation too. And when she died, Jacob buried her under an oak tree below Bethel, and they called that tree the Oak of Weeping, because they missed her so much.

So in the hills near Bethel there was an oak for one Deborah, who looked after a family. And a palm for another Deborah, who looked after a whole people. I do not know if anybody planned it that way. But I like to think the second Deborah knew about the first one, and walked past her oak sometimes, and said a prayer.

The name Deborah means a bee. A honeybee. Which I think suits her. Busy, and sweet, and not to be trifled with.

And the book says she was the wife of Lappidoth. That is all it says about her husband. We are never told anything else about him at all. But the word Lappidoth means torches, or flames, and so some people, a long time ago, wondered whether it was really a name at all, or whether it was a description of Deborah herself, and meant something like, a woman of fire. There is also an old Jewish tradition, written down in the Talmud, that Deborah used to make the wicks for the lamps that burned in the sanctuary, the holy tent of the Lord, and that is why she was called the woman of the torches. That is a tradition, not something the Bible tells us. But on a podcast called Saints by Candlelight, I was hardly going to leave it out.

What did it mean, to judge Israel, in those days? It did not mean quite what we mean by a judge. There were no courtrooms, no wigs, no little wooden hammers. A judge was someone the Lord had raised up to lead the people, and sometimes that meant leading them into battle, and sometimes it meant settling their quarrels. With Deborah, the first thing the book tells us is that she settled quarrels. People came up to her with their troubles, and she listened, and she gave judgment, and because she was a prophetess, and the Lord spoke through her, people trusted that her judgment was fair.

And you can imagine what kind of troubles they were, in a country holding its breath. Who owns this field, now that its owner has fled. Whose sheep got into whose barley. Who should look after the orphan whose father went down to the valley road and never came back. Two sisters who have stopped speaking to each other.

And a goat.

* * *

The boy with the goat was called Ozni. He is not in the Bible, I should say straight away. He is our own invention for tonight. But there must have been a great many boys like him.

He lived in a village on the hill below Ramah, with his grandmother, and he looked after her sheep and goats, and he was about ten years old, and on the morning of our story he had walked for two hours up the ridge road because of a goat called Little Thorn.

Little Thorn was not his goat. That was the whole problem. Little Thorn belonged to his grandmother's neighbour, a large loud man called Shillem, and Little Thorn had a habit of squeezing through the gap in Shillem's wall and coming over to eat Ozni's grandmother's vegetables. And the day before, when Ozni had tried to chase her home, Little Thorn had bolted, and jumped onto a terrace wall, and knocked down a whole length of it, stones and all, into the neighbour's olive grove below.

And now Shillem said the wall was Ozni's grandmother's wall, and she must mend it. And Ozni's grandmother said it was Shillem's goat, and he must mend it. And they had shouted at each other over the hedge until the whole village came out to listen. And in the end, Ozni's grandmother, who was a small fierce old woman, folded her arms and said, very well, we will ask Deborah. And Shillem, who was not quite as sure of himself as he sounded, said, very well, we will. And then both of them, for different reasons, sent Ozni.

So here he was, under the palm tree, with the goat on a rope, and the goat eating a farmer's cloak.

When it was his turn, Ozni stepped forward and opened his mouth, and found that he had completely forgotten everything he was going to say.

Deborah waited. She had a calm face, a face that did not seem to be in any hurry, and her eyes were dark and quick.

"Take your time," she said. "Is the goat the one who did something, or is the goat the one something was done to?"

"The goat did something," said Ozni.

"I thought so," said Deborah. "She has that look."

And then it all came out of him at once, the gap in the wall, and the vegetables, and the terrace, and the shouting over the hedge, and his grandmother folding her arms, and Shillem going red. Deborah listened to all of it. She did not interrupt once. Little Thorn finished the farmer's hem and started on Ozni's sleeve.

When he had finished, Deborah was quiet for a moment.

"Your grandmother is a widow?" she said.

"Yes."

"And Shillem has sons?"

"Three. Big ones."

"Then here is my judgment," said Deborah. "The goat is Shillem's, and a man answers for his own animals. Shillem's three big sons will put back every stone of the terrace by the next new moon. And your grandmother will give them bread and a cup of something when they have finished, because they are her neighbours, and she will need them again before the winter is out. And you will mend the gap in Shillem's wall, the one the goat gets through. Because you are the one who knows exactly where it is."

Ozni thought about this. It seemed to him that everybody had got something, and everybody had lost something, and nobody was going to be happy about it, and that somehow this meant it was right.

"Will they listen?" he said.

"Tell them Deborah said so," she said, "and tell them I asked after them. And tell your grandmother that I know her. I don't, but I would like to, and I expect she will take it as a compliment."

And then something happened that Ozni remembered for the rest of his life.

A man came running up the path from the north. Not walking. Running, with dust on his legs up to the knees, the way a man runs who has been running for a long time. And he went straight past the line of people, and knelt down by the stone, and said something to Deborah in a low voice. Ozni could not hear it. But he saw her face change.

She sat very still for a while. She looked up at the palm leaves, moving in the wind. The people in the line went quiet. Even the goat stopped eating.

And then she said, to the running man, "Go north. Go to Kedesh, in Naphtali. Find Barak, the son of Abinoam. And tell him to come to me."

* * *

Kedesh in Naphtali was a long way off. It was right up in the far north, in the hills near Hazor itself, the city of the king with the chariots. Eighty miles or so as the crow flies, and a great deal more than that on foot, by the back paths, the crooked ones, because nobody went by the main road in those days. Five days there, perhaps, and five days back, for a man in a hurry.

So the people under the palm tree had time to wonder who Barak was.

Nobody knew very much about him. A soldier, people said. A man of the north, who had kept the fighting men of his tribe together in the hills, and knew the country, and was not afraid of much. His name, Barak, means lightning. Which is a very good name for a soldier, and also, if you remember that Deborah may have been called the woman of the torches, a very good name for the man she was about to send for. Torches and lightning. It is almost as if somebody wanted us to notice.

Ozni went home and told his grandmother what Deborah had said, and told Shillem. And it was exactly as he had thought. Shillem grumbled, and his grandmother grumbled, and the three big sons came over and put back every stone of the terrace by the new moon, and his grandmother gave them bread and a jug of something, and stood and watched them eat it with her arms folded, and was secretly very pleased. And Ozni mended the gap in Shillem's wall with stones and thorn branches. And Little Thorn stood on the other side of it and looked at him with her yellow eyes, as if to say, you have not seen the last of me.

But all the while, Ozni was thinking about the running man, and about Kedesh, and about Barak.

And so, when he heard in the village that a man from the north had come up the ridge road with a few companions and gone to the palm tree, Ozni put down his crook and ran all the way there, and squeezed in at the edge of the crowd.

Barak was a big, quiet man, with a scar through one eyebrow, and the kind of face that looks as if it has been out in all weathers for a long time. He stood in front of Deborah, and she spoke to him, and this time Ozni could hear every word. And this part is in the Bible, so I will tell it to you just as the book does.

She said to him, has not the Lord, the God of Israel, commanded you? Go, gather your men at Mount Tabor. Take ten thousand men from the tribes of Naphtali and Zebulun. And I will draw out Sisera, the general of Jabin's army, to meet you by the river Kishon, with his chariots and his troops. And I will give him into your hand.

Notice how she says that. Has not the Lord commanded you? As if Barak had already heard it, somewhere in his heart, and only needed someone to say it out loud.

And Barak was quiet for a while. And then he said something that people have been arguing about ever since.

He said, if you will go with me, I will go. But if you will not go with me, I will not go.

* * *

Now, some people hear that and think Barak was a coward. And I understand why. Here is a soldier, a big man with a scar, and the Lord has promised him the victory, and he says, I will only go if she comes too. It sounds a little like a boy who will only go into the dark room if his big sister holds his hand.

But I do not think that is fair to him. And neither does the New Testament. Because in the Letter to the Hebrews, in the eleventh chapter, there is a great long list of the heroes of faith, the men and women who trusted God when it was hard, Abraham and Sarah and Moses and the rest. And towards the end of that list, the writer says, and what more shall I say? For time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David and Samuel. Barak is there. In the list of the faithful. The New Testament remembers him not as a coward, but as a man who through faith conquered kingdoms.

So what was he saying? Well, think about it from where Barak stood. Deborah was the prophetess. The word of the Lord came through her. If he was going to lead ten thousand farmers down off a mountain against nine hundred chariots, he wanted the word of the Lord standing right beside him. Not five days' walk away under a palm tree. Right there, on the mountain, on the day, to say, now. Go now.

There is an old Greek translation of the Old Testament, called the Septuagint, made a couple of centuries before Christ, and in that translation Barak's answer has an extra line in it. It says something like, because I do not know the day on which the Lord will send his angel to give me success. That line is not in the Hebrew. But I think the old translators understood something about Barak. He was not afraid of the battle. He was afraid of getting the timing wrong. And he knew that he did not know. And he wanted someone with him who would.

And Deborah said yes.

She said, I will surely go with you. Then she said, but the road you are taking will not lead to your glory. Because the Lord will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman.

And Barak did not argue. He did not say, oh, well, in that case. He simply nodded, and turned round, and they went north together.

Ozni, who had heard all of this standing at the edge of the crowd, assumed that she meant herself. Everyone did. A woman would get the glory, and it would be Deborah, the prophetess under the palm tree. That seemed obvious.

It was not, in the end, quite what she meant. But we will come to that.

* * *

I should tell you how Ozni came to go with them, since I have made him up and he is my responsibility.

His grandmother said no. Of course she did. He was ten years old and a battle was no place for a boy. But Deborah was going north with a few people of her household, to carry water and food, and the road would be long, and she needed someone who was good with animals, to look after the donkeys. And Ozni's grandmother, who had never had a compliment from a prophetess before, thought about it for a long time, and then said he could go as far as the mountain, and not one step further, and if he went one step further she would know, and he did not doubt it for a moment.

So Ozni walked north, beside the donkeys, behind Deborah and Barak.

They went by the back paths, through the hills, keeping off the open roads, five days of it. They went to Kedesh first, Barak's own town, up in the north, and from there Barak sent out the call, to the men of Naphtali and the men of Zebulun. And the book says that ten thousand men went up at his heels, and Deborah went up with him.

And while they were in the north, there is a little detail in the story, which I want to mention now, because it matters later. There was a man called Heber, who was a Kenite. The Kenites were a clan of travelling people, metalworkers, perhaps, who had come up from the south long before, and were related by marriage to Moses himself. And this Heber had moved away from the rest of his clan, and pitched his tents far up in the north, by a great oak tree, near Kedesh. And he had made peace with Jabin, the king of Hazor. He was, you might say, keeping his head down, and staying on good terms with whoever was strongest. Which in those days was a very sensible thing to do.

Heber had a wife. Her name was Jael. That is all the book says about her, at this point.

And then they came to the mountain.

* * *

Mount Tabor is one of the most beautiful mountains in that whole country. If you have ever seen a picture of it, you will know what I mean. It rises up out of the flat green plain all by itself, round and steep, like an upturned bowl, nearly two thousand feet high, with trees on its sides and a rounded top. You can see it from miles away in every direction. And from the top of it, you can see the whole of the great valley spread out below you, the wide flat plain of Jezreel, running away to the southwest towards the sea.

Many centuries later, Christians came to believe that Mount Tabor was the high mountain where our Lord was transfigured, and shone like the sun, with Moses and Elijah beside him. There are churches on the top of it now. But in Deborah's day it was a wild steep hill, with a flat place on the summit, and that is where Barak gathered his ten thousand men.

It was a very good place for ten thousand men with no chariots. The sides were too steep for horses. You could see anyone coming for miles. And down below, in the flat of the valley, was exactly the kind of ground where chariots were at their best.

Ozni stood at the edge of the camp, at the top of the mountain, where Deborah had told him to stay with the donkeys, and looked down. And he saw them coming.

Somebody had told Sisera that Barak had gone up to Mount Tabor. And so Sisera called out all his chariots, all nine hundred, and all the men with him, from a place called Harosheth-hagoyim, which nobody now can find on a map, to the river Kishon.

The Kishon is not a big river. For most of the year it is a stream, a modest little stream wandering across the valley floor, which a man could jump over in places. It runs northwest along the valley and out to the sea near Mount Carmel. And all along the valley beside it is wide flat ground, perfect for chariots.

Ozni had never seen a chariot before. Now he saw nine hundred of them, spread out along the valley floor in the distance, glittering where the sun caught the metal, like a line of beetles. And behind them, the soldiers on foot. And he heard, very faintly, carried up the mountain on the wind, the sound of the horses.

He looked round at the ten thousand men of Naphtali and Zebulun on the mountaintop. Most of them were farmers. Some had spears. A great many had nothing much at all. There is a line in the song afterwards that says, was there a shield or a spear to be seen among forty thousand in Israel? And Ozni looked at them, and looked down at the chariots, and felt his stomach turn over.

And then Deborah stood up.

She said to Barak, up! For this is the day on which the Lord has given Sisera into your hand. Does not the Lord go out before you?

This was the day. This was the thing Barak had wanted her there for. Not her sword. She did not have a sword. Her word. Now. Go now.

And Barak went down from Mount Tabor, with ten thousand men following him.

* * *

Here is where I am going to tell you what the book says, and what the song says, and then tell you very honestly what we do not know.

The book says simply this. The Lord routed Sisera, and all his chariots, and all his army, before Barak. And Sisera got down from his chariot, and fled away on foot.

That is a very strange thing for a general to do. A general with the best chariot in the land got down from it, and ran away on his own two feet. Something had gone badly wrong with the chariots.

And the song, which Deborah and Barak sang afterwards, gives us a hint of what it was. It says this. From heaven the stars fought. From their courses they fought against Sisera. The torrent Kishon swept them away, the ancient torrent, the torrent Kishon. And earlier on, the song says, Lord, when you went out, the earth trembled, and the heavens poured, and the clouds poured down water.

The heavens poured. The little stream that a man could jump over became a torrent, and swept them away.

So many people who have read this story, for a very long time, have pictured it like this. A sudden storm, out of season, rolling in from the sea. The rain coming down in sheets. The little Kishon rising and rising, and bursting its banks, and spreading out across the valley floor. And the wide flat ground, the perfect ground for chariots, turning in an hour into deep, sticky, clinging mud. The wheels sinking in up to the axles. The horses floundering. Nine hundred of the most frightening machines in the world, stuck fast in a field, as useless as wheelbarrows. And ten thousand farmers coming down the mountain on foot, who did not need wheels at all.

The Jewish historian Josephus, writing about a thousand years later, in the time of the apostles, tells it that way too. In his version, a great storm of rain and hail came down just as the battle began, and the wind drove it straight into the faces of the Canaanites, so that they could not see, and their bows and slings were no use, and their hands were too cold to hold their swords. That is Josephus, telling the story his own way, a very long time afterwards. It is not in the Bible. But you can see how he got there.

Now, I should be honest. The Bible does not say there was a storm, in so many words. It says the stars fought from heaven, and the heavens poured, and the river swept them away. That is poetry, and poetry is not a weather report. Some people read it quite literally, as a real downpour that the Lord sent at exactly the right moment. Some read the stars fighting as a way of saying that the whole of creation was on Israel's side that day. Some read it as both. The book does not settle it, and so neither will I.

But I will tell you what I think is the heart of it. The thing that the people of Israel had been most afraid of, for twenty years, was those chariots. The chariots were why they had stayed in the hills. The chariots were why they took the crooked paths and kept their heads down. And on the day of the battle, the chariots were the thing that went wrong. The great strength of the enemy turned into his great weakness. And the people who had nothing, no shields, no spears to speak of, came down off the mountain and found that the Lord had gone out before them, just as Deborah said.

The book says Barak chased the chariots all the way back to Harosheth-hagoyim, and that the whole of Sisera's army fell. I will not tell you more about that part at bedtime. It was a battle, and battles are not gentle things.

Ozni saw it only from very far away, from the top of the mountain, through the rain. He had stayed with the donkeys, as he was told. He did not take one step further. He was quite sure his grandmother would know.

* * *

And Sisera, the great general, on foot now, ran.

He ran north, away from the battle, through the rain, on his own. And he came, at last, to a cluster of tents by a great oak tree. The tents of Heber the Kenite. The man who had made peace with Jabin, king of Hazor. A friend. A safe place.

And Heber's wife Jael came out of her tent to meet him.

She said, turn aside, my lord. Turn aside to me. Do not be afraid. And he turned aside into her tent, and she covered him with a rug. And he said, please give me a little water to drink, because I am thirsty. And she opened a skin of milk and gave him a drink, and covered him again. And he said, stand at the door of the tent, and if anyone comes and asks you, is anyone here, say no.

And he was so tired that he fell asleep.

And while he slept, Jael ended the war. The book tells how she did it, plainly and in one verse, and it is not a thing for bedtime, so I will leave it in the book where it belongs. But when Barak came by, chasing Sisera, Jael went out to meet him, and said, come, and I will show you the man you are looking for. And Sisera, the general with the nine hundred chariots, who had frightened a whole people for twenty years, would never frighten anybody again.

And so Deborah's word came true, and not in the way that anyone had expected. The Lord had sold Sisera into the hand of a woman. Not Deborah, the prophetess under the palm tree, with the whole country coming to her for judgment. Jael. A woman in a tent at the edge of the world, whose husband had made his peace with the enemy, and who, when the moment came, chose which side she was on.

The book says, so on that day God subdued Jabin, the king of Canaan, before the people of Israel. And the hand of the people of Israel pressed harder and harder against Jabin, king of Canaan, until they had destroyed him.

* * *

And then they sang.

The whole of the fifth chapter of the Book of Judges is a song. It is called the Song of Deborah, and the book says that Deborah and Barak sang it together, on that day. Many scholars think it is one of the very oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry that we have anywhere, older than most of the rest of the Bible around it, its words strange and rough and ancient, the way the oldest songs are. Some of its lines are so old that nobody is quite sure any more what they mean.

It is not a polite song. It is loud, and fierce, and full of names. But it is wonderful, and I want to give you a few pieces of it, because it tells us more about Deborah than anything else does.

It begins like this. That the leaders took the lead in Israel, that the people offered themselves willingly, bless the Lord! Hear, O kings. Give ear, O princes. To the Lord I will sing. I will make melody to the Lord, the God of Israel.

Notice who she praises first. Not herself. Not Barak. The people who offered themselves willingly. The farmers who came up the mountain with no shields.

And then there is that picture I told you about, of the country holding its breath, the empty roads and the crooked paths and the villages gone quiet. And then this. The villages ceased in Israel. They ceased, until I arose. I, Deborah, arose, as a mother in Israel.

A mother in Israel. Not a queen. Not a general. A mother. That is how she thought of herself. Someone who looks after people. Who settles quarrels between brothers, and between sisters, and between neighbours with a goat. Who knows when to say, take your time, and when to say, up, this is the day.

And then the song calls on everyone to tell the story. You who ride on white donkeys, it says, and you who sit on rich carpets, and you who walk by the road, tell of it. And then there is a lovely line. At the watering places, among the people drawing water, there they tell of the righteous acts of the Lord. The roads are open again. The women can go down to the wells in the open, in the daytime, and stand around and talk, which is what people have always done at wells. And what they talk about, now, is what the Lord has done.

And then there is a line that I love more than almost any other in the Old Testament. Awake, awake, Deborah! Awake, awake, and sing a song! It is as if the song is calling to its own singer. Wake up. You have something to sing about.

Then the song goes through the tribes of Israel one by one, saying who came and who did not, like a teacher calling a register. Ephraim came. Benjamin came. Zebulun and Naphtali risked their lives on the heights of the field. And then the ones who stayed at home. The tribe of Reuben, the song says, sat among their sheepfolds, listening to the shepherds whistling for the flocks, and had great searchings of heart. I think that is a very kind way of putting it. They thought about it a great deal. They discussed it at length. And then they stayed at home. Some of us have been on committees like that. And Dan, the song says, stayed by the ships, and Asher sat still by the seashore. A little gentle teasing, from three thousand years ago, for the people who did not turn up.

And then a line that is pure sound, the galloping of the horses. In the Hebrew, the words themselves sound like hooves, thudding and thudding, galloping, galloping, away.

And then Jael, blessed among women in the tents.

* * *

And then there is one more scene in the song. And it is the one I want to end on, because it is the strangest and the most surprising, and I think it says something about Deborah's heart.

At the very end of the song, it suddenly goes somewhere else entirely. Far away, to a house in the city of the Canaanites. To a window with a lattice in it. And at the window there is an old woman, looking out. It is Sisera's mother.

She is watching the road for her son. And the song says, she peered through the window, and cried out through the lattice, why is his chariot so long in coming? Why are the hoofbeats of his chariots so slow?

And her ladies in waiting, her wisest ladies, answer her. And she answers herself too. They must be dividing up the spoils, they say. That is why he is so late. There is so much treasure to share out. Coloured cloth for Sisera. Embroidered cloth for the necks of the victors. He will be home soon.

But he will not be home soon. We know that, and the song knows it, and so, perhaps, somewhere deep down, does she.

I find that astonishing. Here is a victory song, sung by the winners, about their enemy. And right at the end of it, the singer stops, and goes to the window of the enemy's mother, and stands beside her, and watches the road with her. A mother in Israel, looking at a mother in Canaan. It does not make the war any less a war. The song does not pretend that Sisera was a good man, and it does not wish the victory undone. But it sees her. It does not look away from her.

And then the song ends with a prayer. So may all your enemies perish, O Lord. But may those who love him be like the sun, as he rises in his might.

And the book adds one last line. And the land had rest for forty years.

[pause]

That is nearly all the Bible tells us about Deborah. She does not appear again. We are not told how long she went on sitting under her palm tree, or when she died, or where she was buried. But I like to think she went home, after the battle, all the way back down the crooked paths that were not crooked any more, because now people could use the main road. And I like to think she sat down again on her flat stone under the palm tree between Ramah and Bethel, and someone came up the path with a quarrel about a wall, and she said, take your time.

Ozni, I am glad to tell you, went home to his grandmother, all in one piece, and told her everything, twice. She listened with her arms folded, the way she did, and at the end she said, hmph, and gave him a very large piece of bread with honey on it, which in that house was how you said I love you. Honey, of course, being what bees make. And she went on telling the story of the battle of the Kishon to anyone who would listen for the rest of her life, and in her version Ozni had been standing right next to Deborah the whole time, which was nearly true. And Little Thorn, the goat, got through the wall again by the following spring, as everybody had known she would.

That last part is not in the Bible either. But I would be very surprised if it did not happen somewhere.

There are not many women in the Old Testament who are called prophetesses. There is Miriam, the sister of Moses, who took up her tambourine by the Red Sea, and led the women in a song about horses and riders thrown into the sea. There is Deborah, under her palm tree, who sang another song about horses, and a flooded river. And many years later there was Huldah, in Jerusalem, whom the king's own men went to ask what the newly found book of the law meant. And all of them, it is worth noticing, are remembered at least partly for their words. Two of them for their songs.

And a very long time after Deborah, there was a young girl in Nazareth, who went up into the hill country to visit her cousin, and when she got there, she sang too. My soul magnifies the Lord. He has put down the mighty from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly. If you listen to the Song of Deborah, and then to the Magnificat, you can hear that they are cousins. The mighty with their chariots brought down. The people with nothing lifted up. A woman singing about what the Lord has done. The Church has always heard those old songs of Miriam and Deborah and Hannah as getting ready, in a way, for that one.

In the Orthodox Church, Deborah and Barak do not have a day of their own that we know of. But they are remembered, along with all the faithful men and women of the Old Testament, on the Sunday of the Holy Forefathers, the second Sunday before Christmas. Abraham and Sarah. Moses and Miriam. Caleb and Achsah and Othniel, whom we met a few weeks ago. And somewhere in that long line, a soldier called Lightning, who would not go without the word of the Lord beside him. And a woman of torches, sitting under a palm tree in the hills, listening to a boy explain about a goat.

And it is a good thing to think of, I think, just before you go to sleep. That the things you were most afraid of today, the iron chariots of your own life, may turn out to be stuck fast in the mud by morning. That you do not have to have a shield, or a spear, or very much of anything. Only to go where you are sent, and let the Lord go out before you.

And if you cannot sleep, because the quarrels of the day are still going round and round in your head, the walls and the goats and the things people said over the hedge, then perhaps you can imagine bringing them up the hill path, one by one, to someone who will listen to every word, and will say, take your time. And then lay them down.

The roads are open again. The women are talking at the wells. The land has rest.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Judges, chapter four, which tells it as a story, and chapter five, which tells it as a song. Both are told straight, in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation, and the song is given only in pieces. Shamgar and his ox goad are in Judges, chapter three, verse thirty-one. Rebekah's nurse Deborah and the Oak of Weeping are in Genesis, chapter thirty-five, verse eight. Hazor as the head of all those kingdoms is in Joshua, chapter eleven, and Kedesh in Naphtali is in Joshua, chapter nineteen. Barak among the heroes of faith is in the Letter to the Hebrews, chapter eleven, verse thirty-two. The extra line in Barak's answer is in the Septuagint, the old Greek translation, at Judges four, verse eight, and is not in the Hebrew. The storm of rain and hail is Josephus's retelling, in his Jewish Antiquities, book five; the Bible itself says the stars fought from heaven and the Kishon swept them away, and how to picture that is not settled. The tradition that Deborah made wicks for the lamps of the sanctuary is in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Megillah, folio fourteen, and is a tradition, not Scripture. The meanings of the names Deborah, Barak and Lappidoth are how most scholars read them. That the chariots had iron fittings rather than iron bodies, and that the song is among the oldest Hebrew poetry, are what many scholars think, not certainties. Where Harosheth-hagoyim stood is not known. That Mount Tabor is the mountain of the Transfiguration is an old and much loved Christian tradition; the Gospels say only a high mountain.

Ozni, his grandmother, Shillem and his sons, and Little Thorn the goat are our own invention. So are the cases under the palm tree, the running messenger's words, Barak's scar, the walk north and the view from the mountain. Nothing has been added to what Deborah, Barak, Jael, Sisera or the Lord say or do in the book.

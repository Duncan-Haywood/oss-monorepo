An old man is standing at the edge of a river.

It is not a wide river, here. You could throw a stone across it, if your arm was younger than his. The water is brown and quick, and there are willows and tamarisks along the bank, and a smell of mud and reeds.

Behind him, a great crowd of people is getting ready to cross. There are soldiers and women and children and donkeys and somebody's goats. There is a ferry boat going back and forth, low in the water. There is a lot of shouting, most of it about the goats.

And in front of him, with his feet already wet, is the king.

The king has just made the old man an offer. It is the kind of offer most people wait their whole lives for, and never get. Come with me, the king has said. Come and live with me in Jerusalem. I will look after you. You will never want for anything again.

The old man is eighty years old. He leans on his stick. He looks across the river towards the west, where Jerusalem is. And then he looks back over his shoulder, towards the east, towards the hills, towards home.

And he smiles.

Let me set the scene.

We are east of the river Jordan, roughly a thousand years before Christ. Historians argue about the exact dates, as historians always do, so let us just say: a long time ago.

If you stood on the Mount of Olives, outside Jerusalem, on a clear morning, and looked east, you would see the land drop away below you, down and down, through dry brown hills, to the deep valley where the Jordan runs. And beyond the valley you would see the land climb up again, on the other side, into a long blue line of hills. Those hills are Gilead.

Gilead is good country. It gets more rain than the hills around Jerusalem. It has oak woods, and pasture, and springs. In the old days it was famous for two things. One was its sheep and cattle, because there was grass. The other was its balm, a sweet-smelling ointment made from the resin of some tree or shrub that nobody is now completely sure about. The prophet Jeremiah, centuries later, would ask, is there no balm in Gilead? Which tells you that everybody knew there was. It was the place you went for balm, the way you might go to a particular town for its cheese.

The people of Gilead were, by the standards of the time, a little bit out of the way. Over the river. Over the hills. Countryfolk. The kind of people who are slightly amused by the city, and slightly suspicious of it, and who would rather have a good flock and a dry roof than a seat at anybody's court.

And in Gilead, in a place called Rogelim, lived a man called Barzillai.

Let me tell you what we know about him. It will not take long, because the book gives us only a few lines. But they are very good lines.

His name, Barzillai, most likely comes from the Hebrew word for iron. So you might call him something like Iron Man, or Mister Ironside, though I suspect he did not look the part. We do not know where Rogelim was. Nobody has found it for certain. The name may mean something like the place of the fullers, the men who washed and thickened new woollen cloth by treading it with their feet in tubs of water. Which would make sense, in sheep country. So picture, if you like, a village on a stream in the hills, with sheep on the slopes, and cloth spread out to dry on the stones.

The book says that Barzillai was a very great man. That does not mean he was tall. It means he was rich. He had land and flocks and herds and servants and storerooms. And the book tells us one more thing, which matters very much to our story. He was old. He was eighty years old.

Now, there is a psalm, the ninetieth psalm, which the old title gives to Moses, and it says this. The days of our years are threescore years and ten, and if by reason of strength they be fourscore, yet is their strength labour and sorrow, for it is soon cut off, and we fly away.

Threescore and ten is seventy. Fourscore is eighty. So Barzillai, at eighty, was at the very far end of what the psalm expected anyone to get. He was the oldest man for miles. He was the kind of man people point out to visitors. That is Barzillai, they would say. He remembers when Saul was made king.

He would have remembered it, too. When Barzillai was a young man, there was no king in Israel at all. Then there was Saul. And then, after Saul died on the mountains of Gilboa, there was David.

* * *

You may know the beginning of David's story. The shepherd boy. The giant. The harp. The years as an outlaw in the wilderness, hunted by Saul. Then, at last, the crown.

What you may not know so well is the sadder part, the part that comes later, when David is no longer young. Because David had a son called Absalom.

Absalom was, the book tells us, the most handsome man in all Israel. From the sole of his foot to the crown of his head there was no blemish in him. He had a magnificent head of hair, which he cut once a year, and when he cut it, he weighed it, which tells you something about Absalom. He was charming. He was clever. He used to stand by the road at the city gate, early in the morning, and whenever somebody came to the king with a lawsuit, Absalom would take him by the hand and say, oh, your case is good and right. If only I were judge in the land, I would see you got justice. And so, the book says, Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.

And then one day he went down to Hebron, the old royal town in the south, and had himself proclaimed king.

When the news came to Jerusalem, David did not stay to fight. He said, let us flee, or none of us will escape from Absalom. Perhaps he did not want a battle in the streets of his own city. Perhaps he could not bear to fight his own son. So he went out of Jerusalem with his household and his loyal men, and he crossed the brook Kidron, and he went up the Mount of Olives. And the book says, in one of the saddest sentences in the whole Bible, that he went up weeping as he went, with his head covered, and barefoot. And all the people who were with him covered their heads, and they too went up, weeping as they went.

There is a psalm, the third psalm, whose old title says it was made by David when he fled from Absalom his son. It begins, O Lord, how many are my foes. Many are rising against me. And then, a little later, it says something that I want you to keep in your pocket for tonight. I lay down and slept. I woke again, for the Lord sustained me.

So David went down to the Jordan, and in the night his people crossed the river, every last one of them, so that by morning, the book says, not one was left who had not gone over. And they went up into the hills of Gilead, to a town called Mahanaim.

Mahanaim is an old name. It means two camps. The book of Genesis says that when Jacob, long before, was coming home with his wives and his children and his flocks, the angels of God met him on the way, and when he saw them he said, this is God's camp, and he called that place Mahanaim. So the town had been named, people said, after a night when angels came. Which, as we shall see, is a nice thing for a town to be named after, on the night before somebody very ordinary turns up and behaves like one.

* * *

Now let us go back to Rogelim, to Barzillai's house, on the evening the news came.

I am going to imagine this part, so you know. The book does not tell us how Barzillai heard, or what he said, or who was in the house. But somebody must have come up the road with news, because news always comes up the road, and I picture it like this.

It is the end of the day. The sheep are in. There is a fire in the courtyard, and a pot on it, and the smell of lentils and onions. Barzillai is sitting on a bench with his back against the warm wall of the house, which is where he likes to sit at the end of the day, because the stones hold the heat of the sun. There are grandchildren about, and great-grandchildren probably, because at eighty you have rather a lot of those, and a dog who is mostly asleep.

And a young man comes running in at the gate. One of the shepherds, perhaps, who has been down to the valley.

The king, he says, out of breath. The king is at Mahanaim.

Barzillai does not move. Which king, he says, because he is eighty and has seen a few.

King David. He has run from Jerusalem. His son Absalom has made himself king, and the whole country has gone over to him, or most of it, and David has crossed the Jordan with his people, and they are at Mahanaim.

How many people, says Barzillai.

Hundreds, says the young man. Thousands, maybe. Soldiers, and women, and children. They left in a hurry. They say the king walked up the Mount of Olives barefoot.

Barzillai is quiet for a while.

Then he says, have they eaten?

The young man looks at him. I don't know, he says. I don't suppose so. Not properly. They came across the wilderness. There is nothing to eat between the river and the hills.

And Barzillai says, well, then.

And he gets up off the bench. Which takes a little while.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part of the story that is really in the book, and I want to give it to you exactly.

The book says that when David came to Mahanaim, three men came out to meet him. One was Shobi, the son of Nahash, from Rabbah of the Ammonites. One was Machir, the son of Ammiel, from Lo-debar. And one was Barzillai the Gileadite, from Rogelim.

And here is what they brought. I am going to read you the list, more or less as the book gives it, because the list is the whole point.

Beds. Basins. Earthenware pots. Wheat. Barley. Flour. Roasted grain. Beans. Lentils. Honey. Curds. Sheep. And cheese from the herd.

And then the book gives their reason, in their own words. They said: the people are hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness.

That is it. That is all they said. They did not say, the king will remember this. They did not say, this will be good for us when the war is over. They did not even say, we are loyal to David and not to Absalom, though they were risking a great deal by turning up at all, because nobody yet knew who was going to win. They said, these people are hungry.

I love that list. I love it because it is so practical. Somebody thought of beds. Think about that. Somebody looked at a crowd of tired families who had walked all the way from Jerusalem, sleeping on the ground, and thought, they will need something to lie on. And basins, to wash in. And pots, because you can bring all the lentils in the world, but if there is nothing to cook them in, you are just a man with a sack of lentils.

That is the kind of thing a grandmother thinks of. Or a very sensible old farmer. It is not the kind of thing an army thinks of.

So I picture the next morning at Rogelim. The whole household turned out. Carts in the yard, or more likely donkeys with panniers, because the roads in those hills were not much more than tracks. Sacks of wheat and sacks of barley being carried out of the storerooms. Somebody arguing about whether the good beds should go or only the second-best ones. The grandchildren running about with baskets of roasted grain, eating at least a quarter of it. Jars of honey wrapped in straw. Rounds of cheese stacked like cartwheels. And a small flock of sheep, very surprised to be going on a journey, and making their feelings known.

And Barzillai standing in the middle of it all, leaning on his stick, saying, more flour. They will want more flour than that. Have you seen how much soldiers eat?

And then the whole procession sets off down the road to Mahanaim, with the old man riding on a donkey near the front, because he is not going to stay at home and miss it.

* * *

Let me tell you about the other two men for a moment, because they are interesting, and because this story is about the kind of people who turn up.

Machir son of Ammiel, from Lo-debar. If you heard our episode about Mephibosheth, you have met him. Lo-debar was the little out-of-the-way place where Machir had taken in Mephibosheth, the lame grandson of King Saul, and hidden him and looked after him for years, until David sent for him and gave him a place at the king's table. So Machir was a man who had already been kind to one helpless person nobody else wanted. And now here he was again, with his beans and his lentils.

And Shobi, the son of Nahash, from Rabbah of the Ammonites. Now this one is a surprise. Rabbah was the capital of the Ammonites, the people who lived to the east of Gilead, and who had been at war with Israel more than once. Rabbah is where modern Amman, in Jordan, stands today. A king called Nahash had once ruled there, and when he died his son Hanun had insulted David's messengers very badly, by shaving off half their beards and cutting their robes off at the hips, and there had been a war about it. So if this Shobi was a son of that Nahash, as many readers think, then he was the brother of a king who had been David's enemy. And here he was, coming out to feed David's people.

So picture the three of them arriving at Mahanaim. A man from a backwater. A man from an enemy country. And a man so old that he really ought to have been at home in front of the fire. None of them important. None of them in the army. None of them, as far as we know, anybody David had ever met.

I like to think the three of them met on the road, or at the gate, and looked at one another's carts, and nodded. You too, then. Me too. The way people nod at each other when they both turn up at a neighbour's house with a casserole on the day of a funeral.

* * *

What was the camp at Mahanaim like? I have to imagine this, but I do not think I am imagining too far.

It was crowded. Every house in the town was full, and there were tents and shelters all round the walls. There were soldiers sharpening things. There were mothers trying to get children to sleep in strange places, which, as anybody who has done it will tell you, is a battle in itself. There were people who had left behind everything they owned in Jerusalem, and did not know whether they would ever see it again.

And into all this came Barzillai's donkeys, and Machir's, and Shobi's, with beds and pots and flour and honey and cheese.

I think it must have been like the arrival of the first lorry of supplies after a flood. People do not cheer, exactly. They are too tired to cheer. They just come quietly towards it, and somebody starts handing things out, and somebody else gets a fire going, and soon there is a smell of bread, and the children stop crying, and a sort of ordinary evening begins to happen, right there in the middle of the disaster.

The book says Barzillai provided the king with food all the time he stayed at Mahanaim. Not once. All the time. So this was not a single grand gesture. Every few days, I imagine, another string of donkeys would come down the hill road from Rogelim, with more flour, and more cheese, and somebody's apologetic note about the honey running low.

Did Barzillai stay in the camp himself? The book does not say, and I rather doubt it, at his age. But he must have come at least sometimes, because by the end of the story he and the king know each other well. So I picture him coming in now and then, riding his donkey slowly through the camp, nodding to people, checking that the lentils were being cooked properly and not burnt, and sitting for a while with the king in the evenings.

And what would they have talked about, the old farmer and the king in exile?

I think they talked about sheep, mostly. David had been a shepherd, after all, before he was anything else. I think the old man asked the king about the wolves and lions and bears he had fought off as a boy, and the king asked the old man about the grazing in Gilead, and the winters, and how many lambs he had had that spring. I think it was a relief to David to talk about something that was not the war. And I think Barzillai knew that, and did it on purpose.

And perhaps, one evening, Barzillai said something like this. When I was a young man, my lord, a lot younger than you are now, I had a son who quarrelled with me. Oh, not like this. Nothing like this. But he left the house, and I did not see him for a year. And I used to sit on the bench by the wall every evening and look at the road.

And the king said, what happened?

And Barzillai said, he came back. Not every son does. But mine did.

I made that up, of course. I do not know if Barzillai ever had a quarrel with a son. But I do know that old men in every country of the world have sat beside younger men in trouble, and said very little, and that the little has helped.

Now the war. I will tell this part quietly, because it is sad, and because it is not Barzillai's part of the story.

Absalom gathered all Israel and crossed the Jordan after his father. David divided his men into three companies, under three commanders, and sent them out to fight. He wanted to go with them, but his men would not let him. You are worth ten thousand of us, they said. Stay in the city, and send us help from there. So the king stood beside the gate of Mahanaim while all the army marched out past him by hundreds and by thousands.

And as they went, he called out to the three commanders, in front of everybody. Deal gently, for my sake, with the young man Absalom.

Every soldier heard it.

The battle was fought in a forest, the forest of Ephraim, and David's men won. And Absalom, riding his mule under the thick branches of a great oak, was caught by his head in the tree, and the mule went on from under him, and he was left hanging there. And Joab, the hard old commander of David's army, who had heard the king's words as well as anybody, killed him anyway.

That is all I will say about it.

Two runners brought the news to the king, who was sitting between the two gates of the town, waiting. And when he understood that his son was dead, the king went up to the room over the gate, and wept. And as he went, he said, O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom. Would that I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son.

I think it is one of the most human things in the whole of Scripture. Absalom had tried to take his father's crown, and very likely his life. And his father wept for him as if he had been a little boy.

The book says that the victory that day was turned into mourning for all the people, and that the soldiers crept back into the town as quietly as soldiers do who are ashamed because they have run away from a battle, though they had not run away. They had won. They just did not know what to do with their faces.

In the end it was Joab, of all people, who went up to the king and told him, rather bluntly, that he had better come down and sit in the gate and thank his men, or by nightfall he would not have a single one of them left. And the king, who was a king as well as a father, got up, and came down, and sat in the gate. And all the people came before him.

I do not know where Barzillai was that day. Perhaps at home. Perhaps in the camp, sitting quietly at the back. But I like to think that some time in the days that followed, the old man came and sat with the king, and did not try to say anything clever, and did not say that it was for the best, or that it would all look different in a year. Just sat. And perhaps brought some cheese.

Now we come to the river.

After Absalom's death, the tribes of Israel began to argue among themselves about whether to bring the king back, which is what tribes do. But in the end the men of Judah sent word to David, come back, you and all your servants. And so the king set out from Mahanaim to go home.

They came down out of the hills of Gilead to the Jordan, the whole great crowd of them, to cross back over at the place where they had crossed in the dark, some weeks or months before. And this time there were people waiting for them on the other side, and people coming down to meet them. There was a ferry boat taking the king's household across. There were men of Judah come to bring the king over. There were people who needed to apologise, and people who wanted to be seen, and people who wanted favours, and the book tells us about several of them.

And there was Barzillai.

The book says that Barzillai the Gileadite came down from Rogelim, and went on with the king to the Jordan, to see him over the river. He did not need to. The war was over. The danger was over. The king was going home in triumph, with plenty of people to look after him now. But the old man came down from his hills anyway, to walk the last part of the road beside him. The way you walk a guest to the end of your lane, and then to the corner, and then a little further, because you are not quite ready to say goodbye.

And at the river, the king turned to him.

Come over with me, said the king, and I will look after you with me in Jerusalem.

I want you to hear how much that meant. The king's table, in Jerusalem. The best food in the land. Musicians. Servants. A fine room in the king's own house. Every honour. The king's friendship, in front of everyone, for the rest of his life. It was exactly what David had done for Mephibosheth, for Jonathan's sake. And now he was offering it to Barzillai, for Barzillai's own sake, because Barzillai had fed him when he had nothing.

And here is what Barzillai said. This is in the book. I am only putting it into plain words.

How many years have I still to live, he said, that I should go up with the king to Jerusalem? I am eighty years old now. Can I tell good from bad any more? Can your servant taste what he eats or what he drinks? Can I still hear the voices of singing men and singing women? Why should your servant be one more burden on my lord the king?

And then, more gently. Your servant will go a little way over the Jordan with the king. But why should the king repay me with such a reward? Please, let your servant go back, so that I may die in my own town, near the grave of my father and my mother.

* * *

Isn't that lovely?

I think it is one of the loveliest things anybody says in the Bible. And I want to tell you why, because it is easy to hear it as sad, and I do not think it is sad at all.

First of all, he is funny. He is being funny on purpose, I am sure of it. My lord, he is saying, you are offering me the finest cooking in Israel, and I cannot taste my dinner any more. You are offering me the court musicians, and I cannot hear them. You are offering me a fine bed in a palace, and I shall only fall asleep in it and snore, and somebody will have to help me up and down the stairs. It would be a terrible waste of a good cook. There is a twinkle in it. You can almost see him lean on his stick and raise one eyebrow.

There is a passage in the Book of Ecclesiastes, written later, about old age, that sounds a lot like Barzillai. It speaks of the days when the grinders cease because they are few, which means the teeth, and when the daughters of song are brought low, which means the hearing, and when a man is afraid of heights, and of things in the road, and the almond tree blossoms, which means his hair has gone white. Barzillai knew all about it. He was not complaining. He was just being honest.

The second thing is this. He did not do it for a reward. When he loaded his donkeys at Rogelim, he was not thinking about Jerusalem. He was thinking about hungry people. And now that the reward was offered, he did not quite know what to do with it, because it was not what he had been after. Why should the king repay me with such a reward? he says. I only did what anybody would do. Well, no, Barzillai, not anybody. But you thought it was, and that is rather the point.

And the third thing, the best thing, is that he knew what he wanted. He wanted to go home. He did not want to spend the last of his life somewhere grand and strange. He wanted his own bench, against his own warm wall, in the evening, with his own hills in front of him. And when the time came, he wanted to be laid to rest beside his mother and his father.

That is not a small thing to want. It is not a failure of ambition. It is a man who has had a long life, and knows it has been a good one, and is not afraid of its ending, and wants to finish it where it began. Some of the wisest people you will ever meet are like that. They have stopped wanting more. They have started wanting enough.

* * *

But Barzillai did not just say no and walk off. He said one more thing.

Here is your servant Chimham, he said. Let him go over with my lord the king. And do for him whatever seems good to you.

The book does not say who Chimham was. Most readers have taken him to be Barzillai's son, and later on David speaks of the sons of Barzillai, which makes that likely. So I am going to picture him as a young man, the old man's youngest son perhaps, standing just behind his father's shoulder at the riverbank, trying very hard to look as if he had not been hoping for this all along.

And you can see what Barzillai has done. He has turned down the gift for himself, and passed it on to somebody young enough to enjoy it. Somebody who can still taste the food, and hear the singers, and climb the stairs two at a time. It is exactly what a good father does. You go, he is saying. You go to Jerusalem. You see the king's house. You have the adventure. I have had mine.

And the king said, Chimham shall go over with me, and I will do for him whatever seems good to you. And whatever you ask of me, I will do for you.

And so they came to the end of it.

All the people went over the Jordan. The boat went back and forth, and the soldiers waded across at the shallow places with their spears held high, and somebody's goats made a terrible fuss about the whole thing, I am quite sure, and somebody's child fell in and was fished out again laughing.

And the king went over. And Barzillai went with him, a little way, just as he had said he would, across the river and a few steps up the far bank, his old feet wet, leaning on his son's arm.

And then the book says this. The king kissed Barzillai, and blessed him. And he returned to his own place.

That is all. The king kissed him, and blessed him, and he went home. Back across the river, and up the long road into the hills of Gilead, to Rogelim, with the servants and the empty donkeys, and the dog, I expect, who had come along and got very muddy.

And the king went on to Gilgal, and Chimham went on with him.

I picture the old man stopping, halfway up the hill on the eastern side, to get his breath, and turning round on his donkey to look back. And far below, across the river, the long line of the king's people, winding away westward into the hills of Judah, with dust going up from it in the afternoon light. And somewhere in that line, a young man from Rogelim, walking beside the king, looking at everything.

And then Barzillai turned round again, and went on up the hill. Home.

What happened afterwards?

We do not know when Barzillai died. The book does not tell us. It is a nice thing to hope that he got his wish, and sat on his bench in the evenings for a few more years, and told his great-grandchildren the story of the time the king came to Mahanaim, more and more grandly each time. And that one day, when he was ready, they laid him to rest in his own town, near the grave of his father and his mother, just as he had asked.

But the king did not forget him.

Years later, when David was very old himself, and lying on his bed, and knew that he was dying, he called his son Solomon to him to tell him the things a king needs to know. And in among the instructions about the kingdom, there is one small sentence that I think is one of the most beautiful things David ever said.

Deal kindly with the sons of Barzillai the Gileadite, he told Solomon. Let them be among those who eat at your table. For with such kindness they came to meet me when I fled from Absalom your brother.

So at the very end of his life, with everything else he had to think about, David remembered the cheese.

And there is something more, very faint and far away, that I want to give you.

In the Book of Jeremiah, four hundred years and more after David, there is a mention, just in passing, of a place near Bethlehem called the lodging place of Chimham. Geruth Chimham, in Hebrew. Many readers think it was named after our Chimham, and that perhaps David gave him land near Bethlehem, David's own home town, and that he settled there, and his family after him. Nobody can be sure. But it is a pleasant thought, the young man from Gilead who went over the river with the king, and whose name was still on the map four centuries later.

And further on still, after the exile in Babylon, when the people came home and made lists of all the families who returned, the Books of Ezra and Nehemiah mention a family of priests called the sons of Barzillai. They were called that, the books say, because one of their ancestors had married one of the daughters of Barzillai the Gileadite, and had taken his name. Think of that. Some long-ago priest, marrying into the old man's family, and deciding that the name worth carrying was not his own, but his father-in-law's. Barzillai. The one who fed the king.

Five hundred years after a string of donkeys came down a hill road with beds and lentils, people were still proud to be called by his name.

* * *

Barzillai never did anything that the world calls great. He did not win a battle. He did not write a psalm. He did not see an angel, though he lived near a town named after the night when angels came. He was just an old man who heard that some people were hungry, and got up off his bench.

But I think the angels at Mahanaim would have recognised him. Because that is what angels do, in the old stories. They turn up when you are tired and far from home, and they bring bread. And then, when you try to thank them, they will not take anything, and they go away.

There is a line in the psalm I mentioned at the beginning, the ninetieth psalm, the one that says threescore years and ten, or perhaps fourscore. A little further on, it says this. So teach us to number our days, that we may get a heart of wisdom.

I think Barzillai had numbered his days. He knew exactly how many he probably had left. And it had not made him sad, or frightened, or grasping. It had made him generous with the time he had, and content with the place he was in, and very clear about what mattered. A heart of wisdom. Which is a lovely thing to have, at eighty, or at any age.

And I think, too, of David, a few months before, lying down in a strange place with everything gone wrong, and saying, I lay down and slept. I woke again, for the Lord sustained me. And part of the way the Lord sustained him, those nights at Mahanaim, was an old man from Rogelim, and his beds, and his basins, and his honey.

So the river runs on, brown and quick, between the willows. And on the eastern side the hills of Gilead go blue, and then grey, and then dark, as the evening comes on. And somewhere up there, in a village nobody can find any more, an old man is sitting on a bench against a warm stone wall, with a dog asleep at his feet, watching the road, and not waiting for anything at all.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Samuel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. David's flight from Absalom is in chapter fifteen. The crossing of the Jordan and the three men who brought food to Mahanaim, with the whole list, beds and basins and cheese, are in chapter seventeen, verses twenty-two to twenty-nine. The battle and David's grief are in chapter eighteen. Barzillai at the Jordan, his answer to the king and Chimham going over are in chapter nineteen, verses thirty-one to forty. David's words to Solomon about the sons of Barzillai are in the First Book of Kings, chapter two, verse seven. The lodging place of Chimham near Bethlehem is in Jeremiah, chapter forty-one, and the priestly sons of Barzillai are in Ezra, chapter two, and Nehemiah, chapter seven. Mahanaim's name comes from Genesis, chapter thirty-two.

Everything Barzillai, David and Chimham do and say at the river is in the book, told in a plain rendering. What I invented is everything around it: the household at Rogelim, the news arriving, the loading of the donkeys, the meeting of the three men, the evenings in the camp, Barzillai's son who once left home, and the goats. The meanings of Barzillai and Rogelim, Chimham being Barzillai's son, Shobi being the son of that King Nahash, and the link between Chimham and the place near Bethlehem are what many readers think, not certain.

Barzillai is not counted among the saints with a feast day of his own, in either tradition, and neither is Chimham. King David is.

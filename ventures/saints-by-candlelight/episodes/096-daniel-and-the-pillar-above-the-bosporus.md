Imagine a winter night on the water.

A long, narrow stretch of sea runs between two shores, with hills on either side, and the wind is coming down it from the north, hard and cold, the way it does in that place in December. Little waves slap against the side of a small wooden boat. In the boat there is a man with a pair of oars and a girl wrapped in a blanket up to her nose.

The man is not rowing. He is resting on his oars and looking up.

On the shore, on a low hill above the water, there is a pillar. A tall, dark pillar against a dark sky. And on the top of the pillar, though you can only just see it, there is a shape. A man, standing. The wind is whipping at him. Sleet is coming sideways off the sea. Every house along that shore has its shutters closed and its fire lit. And the man on the pillar is standing in the dark with his arms raised, praying.

"Is he all right?" says the girl, through the blanket.

"I don't know," says her father. "I don't think anybody knows. Nobody can get up there in this."

They watch for a while longer. Then the father sighs, and picks up the oars, and turns the boat for home. "We'll come back in the morning," he says. "First thing."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Last time on this show, we told the story of Saint Simeon the Stylite, the shepherd boy from Syria who ended up living on top of a pillar on a hill for more than thirty-five years. If you missed that one, do not worry. You do not need it. But it helps to know that Simeon was the first, and that tonight's saint is the second, and that the second one knew the first one, and climbed his ladder, and was blessed by him.

And I should say, as I said last time, right at the start: the Church does not ask anybody to live on a pillar. Please do not try this at home. Nobody is meant to copy Daniel. The old monks who loved him did not copy him either. They thought he had been given a very unusual call, for a very particular time, and they watched it with a mixture of awe and worry, which is roughly the right mixture.

Now. Let me tell you about the world Daniel was born into.

It is the early four hundreds. The Roman Empire is still there, but it has more or less split into two halves. The western half, with Rome in it, is having a very bad century. The eastern half is doing much better, and its capital is a city called Constantinople, built about eighty years earlier by the emperor Constantine on the site of an old Greek town called Byzantium. Today we call it Istanbul.

Constantinople sits on a triangle of land with water on two sides. On one side is the Golden Horn, a long, sheltered harbour. On the other is the Bosporus, the narrow strait that runs north from the Sea of Marmara up to the Black Sea, with Europe on one bank and Asia on the other. The Bosporus is about twenty miles long, and in places it is less than half a mile wide. Ships go up and down it all day. Fishermen work it all night. Ferrymen row people across it for a small coin, and argue with each other about the currents, which are famously difficult. There is a surface current running south, and, the fishermen will tell you, another one running north underneath it, which is the kind of thing that makes rowing interesting.

The banks of the Bosporus, in those days, were dotted with villages, and churches, and the summer houses of the rich, and vineyards climbing up the hills. A little way north of the city walls, on the European side, there was a place called Anaplus. Nobody is quite sure exactly where it was, though it was probably near the modern neighbourhood of Arnavutköy. Remember that name, Anaplus, because that is where the pillar will be.

But Daniel was not born anywhere near there. He was born a long way to the east, in a village called Maratha, near the town of Samosata, on the river Euphrates, in what is now the south-east of Turkey. He grew up speaking Syriac, which is a kind of Aramaic, the language family Our Lord himself spoke. Greek was the language of the big city, and Daniel would never be entirely at home in it.

His parents were called Elias and Martha. The Life tells us that for a long time they had no children, and that Martha, like Hannah in the Bible, prayed for a child and promised that if God gave her one she would give the child back to God. And God gave her a son. And they called him Daniel.

So that is our boy. A village boy from the far east of the empire, promised to God before he was born, who will end up standing in the wind above the busiest water in the world.

* * *

When Daniel was very small, his parents took him to a monastery to offer him, the way Hannah took Samuel to Shiloh. The Life says the abbot looked at the little boy and decided he was too young, which, frankly, he was. So they took him home again.

Daniel did not forget. When he was twelve, he set off by himself, without telling anybody, to a monastery not far from his village, and he asked to be let in. The abbot told him he was still too young, and that the life was hard, and that he should go home. Daniel refused to go home. He stayed by the gate. And eventually, as tends to happen with very determined twelve-year-olds, the monks gave in.

His mother, when she found out, came to see him. And the Life says she was not angry at all. She was glad. She had promised him to God, and here he was. She told him to stay.

So Daniel grew up in the monastery. He learned the psalms by heart. He learned to work, and to pray at night, and to keep quiet, which I suspect was the hardest. He was by all accounts very good at it.

And when he was a young man, something happened that would change everything. His abbot had to go on church business to the great city of Antioch, and he took Daniel with him. The road to Antioch went past a hill called Telanissos. And on the hill at Telanissos, as everybody in Syria knew, there was a pillar, and on the pillar there was a man called Simeon.

They stopped, of course. Everybody stopped. And the Life tells us something lovely. The abbot and the other monks with him were a little doubtful about the pillar. They said, among themselves, that this was a very strange way to live, and they were not sure it was right. But Daniel stood at the foot of the pillar and looked up, and he was not doubtful at all. He was amazed. And Simeon, says the Life, looked down at the young monk standing there with his mouth open, and called him up the ladder, and laid his hand on his head, and blessed him. And then he told him, so the Life says, that he would have to bear a great deal for the sake of God. And he sent him on his way.

I like to imagine that climb. A young man going up a ladder, rung by rung, higher and higher, the hill falling away below, the ladder swaying a little, and at the top an old man burned brown by the sun, with kind, tired eyes. And a hand on his head. That is the kind of thing you remember for the rest of your life.

* * *

Now I am going to step away from Syria for a little while, and introduce you to two people who never existed.

His name is Kyriakos, and he is a boatman on the Bosporus. He has a small, solid wooden boat, painted blue, though most of the paint has gone, and he spends his days rowing people across the strait and up and down the shore. Merchants, priests, soldiers on leave, a farmer with three crates of chickens, a nervous gentleman who has never been in a boat before and wants to be reassured, every single minute, that it is not going to sink. Kyriakos reassures him. Kyriakos is good at that.

He has a daughter called Theodora, who is nine when our part of the story starts. Her mother died when she was small, and so she comes out in the boat with her father, and sits in the stern, and tells the passengers things they did not ask to know. Which fish are good to eat. Which of the big houses on the shore belongs to which senator. Where the current is worst. And, after a certain point in our story, all about the holy man in the old temple.

Because one day, a few years after Daniel's visit to Simeon, a stranger came down to the shore near Anaplus.

He was a monk, you could tell that from his clothes. He was not young any more, maybe in his early forties. He was thin and dusty and he had walked a very long way. He spoke Greek slowly, with a strong accent from the east. And he was looking for somewhere to live.

He had not meant to come to Constantinople at all. Here is how the Life tells it. After Simeon blessed him, Daniel went back to his monastery. Some years later, the monks there wanted to make him their abbot, and he did not want to be an abbot. He wanted to be alone with God. So he left. He went to see Simeon a second time, and stayed near him for a couple of weeks. And then he set out for Jerusalem, to pray at the holy places, which is where any monk with a long road ahead of him wanted to go.

On the way, says the Life, he met an old man, a holy man with a long white beard, who told him not to go to Jerusalem. Go to Byzantium, the old man said. Go to the new Rome. There are plenty of holy places in Jerusalem already. Go where you are needed. And Daniel, who had been taught to listen to holy old men, turned round, and went.

That is the Life's report, and I will tell it to you as the Life tells it. The writer clearly thought the old man was sent by God, and said so. What is certain is that Daniel arrived at Constantinople at around the beginning of the four hundred and fifties, in the reign of the emperor Marcian, and that he never went to Jerusalem after all.

* * *

Now, near Anaplus there was a place that everybody avoided.

It was an old pagan temple, from the days before the empire was Christian. It had been empty for years, and it had a bad reputation. The fishermen said it was haunted. The ferrymen said that at night you could hear noises from it, crashes and howls, and that stones were thrown at boats that came too close. Nobody would go near it after dark. Some people would not go near it at all.

So, naturally, that is where Daniel decided to live.

I have to imagine how Kyriakos first heard about this. I like to think it was Theodora who brought him the news, running down to the shore one morning.

"There's a man in the temple," she said.

"Nobody is in the temple," said her father. "Nobody is ever in the temple."

"There's a man in the temple. A monk. He went in yesterday and he hasn't come out, and the priest at Saint Michael's says he's from Syria and he's going to stay there. On purpose."

Kyriakos put down the net he was mending. "On purpose," he said.

"On purpose."

"Well," said Kyriakos, after a while, "either he's very holy or he's very silly. And either way he'll want bread."

Now, the Life tells us that Daniel shut himself up in that temple and prayed, night after night. And it says that the demons who lived there did their very best to frighten him out. They made noises like an army on the march. They threw stones. They made the whole building shake. They came at him in the dark with all sorts of terrors. And Daniel just went on praying, and making the sign of the cross, and singing the psalms he had learned as a boy, until, the Life says, the demons gave up and went away and the old temple went quiet.

That is the Life's report, and I am not going to describe the demons any further, because this is a bedtime story and because, frankly, they do not deserve the attention. What the Life wants us to notice is that a thin, foreign monk with a bad accent went into the place everybody was afraid of, and sat down, and stayed, and after a while the place was not frightening any more. And the fishermen started rowing past it again. And then, one by one, they started stopping.

Because once people knew he was there, of course, they started coming to see him. Fishermen. Housewives from the villages. Sailors. Sick people. People with troubles. He sat at a little window, and they came and talked to him, and he listened, and prayed with them, and gave them advice in his slow, careful Greek. And they went away feeling better. And they came back. And they brought their friends.

And this, as it always does, caused trouble. Some of the local clergy were annoyed. Who was this Syrian monk, setting himself up in an old temple and drawing the crowds away from the churches? Was he even sound in his faith? They complained to the patriarch of Constantinople, Anatolius.

Anatolius, to his great credit, did not just take their word for it. He sent for Daniel, and questioned him, and found that he was entirely orthodox, and humble, and kind, and not setting himself up as anything at all. And the Life adds a story. Some time later, Anatolius fell seriously ill, and Daniel prayed for him, and the patriarch got better. And after that, Anatolius would hear no more complaints about the monk in the temple. That is the Life's report of the healing. The friendship between the patriarch and the monk seems to have been entirely real.

Daniel lived in the old temple for nine years.

* * *

In the year four hundred and fifty-nine, far away in Syria, Saint Simeon the Stylite died on his pillar.

And not long afterwards, a man arrived in Constantinople from Syria with a parcel. His name was Sergius, and he had been one of Simeon's disciples. And in the parcel was Simeon's leather tunic, the old, worn, sun-cracked garment that Simeon had worn on the pillar. Sergius had brought it as a gift for the emperor, who was now Leo, because emperors liked holy relics and Simeon was the most famous holy man in the world.

But Sergius could not get in to see the emperor. If you have ever tried to get an appointment with somebody very important, you will understand. There were guards, and officials, and secretaries, and officials who supervised the secretaries, and he was a dusty monk from Syria with a parcel, and nobody let him through. He waited for days.

So in the end he gave up and decided to go home. And on his way, he went up the Bosporus to visit the famous holy man near Anaplus, who, he had heard, was also from Syria, and had also met Simeon. And there he found Daniel. And the two of them talked, in Syriac, which must have been a great relief to both of them. And Sergius gave the tunic to Daniel.

Now the Life tells us that Daniel had had a vision, around this time, of Simeon standing on a high pillar and calling him up. And when the tunic arrived, Daniel took it as a sign. He would go up a pillar too. He would do, here, by the Bosporus, near the great city, what Simeon had done on the hill in Syria.

I like to think the news reached Kyriakos by the usual route.

"He's going up a pillar," said Theodora.

"Who is?"

"Daniel. The holy man. He's going up a pillar, like the one in Syria. A man is building it now. On the hill above the shore."

Kyriakos thought about this for a long time. He thought about the wind that comes down the Bosporus in winter. He thought about the rain that comes off the sea. He thought about the hill above Anaplus, which had no shelter at all.

"He has never been here in a February," he said at last. "That's the trouble."

"He's been here nine years."

"Inside," said her father. "He's been here nine years inside."

* * *

The first pillar was not very tall. A friend of Daniel's paid for it, and it was put up on a piece of land on the hill. And there was immediately a problem, which was that the land belonged to somebody else.

It belonged to a man called Gelanius, who was a high official in the emperor's household. He had a vineyard there. And he came home one day to find that, without anybody asking his permission, a pillar had been put up on his property and a Syrian monk was standing on top of it, and that a large crowd of people was now coming every day to stand in his vineyard and look at him.

He was, understandably, not pleased. The Life tells us he was very angry and wanted the pillar taken down and the monk removed. And he went to complain. And then, after a good deal of talk, and after meeting Daniel himself, he changed his mind. In the end the Life says he agreed that Daniel could stay, and he went from being the angry owner of the land to being one of the holy man's friends.

I think about Gelanius sometimes. Imagine looking out of your window one morning and finding a saint on a pole in your vineyard. Imagine being the person who has to decide what to do about it. I think he did quite well, in the end.

And then the emperor came.

Leo the First was not, in most ways, a gentle man. He was a soldier who had become emperor, and he spent most of his reign fighting wars and dealing with very dangerous generals. But he came to Daniel's pillar, and he listened to him, and he came back. He came back many times. He brought his family. He brought important visitors. The Life even says that he brought a king from the far side of the Black Sea to meet the holy man, which must have been an interesting conversation.

And Leo had a better pillar built. A taller, stronger one, which the Life describes as made of two columns bound together, with a little platform and a railing at the top. And Daniel moved onto it. And the crowds grew. Now it was not just fishermen and housewives. Now it was senators, and generals, and the wives of generals, and people from the palace, coming up the Bosporus in their fine boats with their servants, to stand at the foot of the pillar and ask the holy man to pray for them.

And here is where Kyriakos comes into his own. Because, of course, all those people had to get there somehow. And quite a lot of them came by water.

"Anaplus?" Kyriakos would say, as a gentleman in a silk cloak climbed carefully into the blue boat. "The holy man? Yes, sir. Mind your feet. Theodora, move the fish."

And as he rowed, Theodora, sitting in the stern, would tell them all about it.

"He doesn't come down," she would say. "Ever. Not even when it rains. He stands up there and prays and in the afternoons he talks to people. You have to shout a bit. He's very kind. He's from Syria. He knew the first one, the one in Syria, and he climbed up his ladder. He has a ladder too, but only the disciples go up it. They take him things. Not much. He doesn't eat much."

"Theodora," her father would say. "Let the gentleman breathe."

"I'm only telling him."

* * *

Now. Let me tell you about the winter night. Because it is the story from the Life that I think about most, and it is where we started.

One winter, says the Life, a terrible storm came down the Bosporus. The wind howled for days. Sleet and snow came off the sea. And the holy man stood on his pillar through all of it, in his leather tunic, with nothing over his head, because there was no roof on the pillar. He had refused, so far, to let anybody put one up.

His disciples could not get up the ladder in that wind. They could not even see him clearly. They spent the night below, praying, terrified that in the morning they would find he had frozen, or been blown off.

And in the morning, when the wind dropped, they climbed up. And they found him standing there, still alive, but stiff with cold, with ice on his tunic and ice in his beard, unable to speak, almost unable to move. And they brought up hot water, and very gently thawed him out, and wrapped him up, and he recovered.

That is the Life's report. And after that, I am glad to say, Daniel was persuaded, by the emperor, as it happens, to accept a small shelter at the top of the pillar. Even a holy man, it turns out, can be talked into a sensible decision by somebody who cares about him.

I like to think that Kyriakos and Theodora were there that morning. That they had watched from the boat the night before, and gone home, and lain awake, and come back as soon as it was light. That they stood at the bottom of the hill with the crowd that had gathered and waited for news. And that when one of the disciples finally came down the ladder and called out, "He's alive. He's all right. He's asking for you all to go home and get warm," Theodora burst into tears, and her father put his arm round her, and said nothing at all, because he was crying too.

"Why does he do it?" she asked, on the way home, when she had stopped. "Why doesn't he just come down?"

And Kyriakos rowed for a while before he answered.

"I asked him that once," he said. "Years ago. When he was still in the temple."

"What did he say?"

"He said that the city is very loud. And very busy. And very full of people who are pulling in different directions. And that somebody has to stand still, so that the others have something to steer by." He pulled on the oars. "Like a lighthouse, he said. Except with prayers instead of a fire."

Theodora thought about this. "That's not really what lighthouses are for," she said.

"No," said her father. "But it's not a bad idea."

Those words are our own. But I think the idea in them is close to what the people of Constantinople felt about Daniel. Peter Brown, a great historian of this period, wrote a famous essay about holy men like Simeon and Daniel. His point, roughly, was that in a world of rich and powerful people pulling in every direction, the holy man was the one person who stood outside all of it, who wanted nothing, who could not be bought, and who could therefore be trusted. People came to him to settle quarrels, to ask advice, to be told the truth. And because he stood still, they could steer by him.

* * *

Here is something else you might not expect. The holy man on the pillar was made a priest, without ever coming down.

The Life tells it this way. The patriarch of Constantinople at that time was Gennadius, who had succeeded Anatolius. And Gennadius, perhaps at the emperor's urging, decided that Daniel ought to be ordained a priest. Daniel did not want to be. He had run away from being an abbot, after all, and he did not want to be a priest either.

So, according to the Life, Gennadius came to the foot of the pillar, and said the prayers of ordination from down below, while Daniel, up above, went on protesting. And then the patriarch had the ladder put up, and climbed up it, which for an elderly patriarch in his robes must have been quite an undertaking, and at the top he gave Daniel communion, and Daniel accepted, and that was that. A priest on a pillar.

I will leave the exact details of how all that worked to the old writer, who clearly enjoyed telling it. But I do love the picture of a patriarch climbing a ladder to reach a monk who did not want to be honoured. It is not every day that the most important bishop in the eastern empire has to climb up to a hermit.

The years went on. Daniel prayed, and taught, and listened, and gave advice. The Life is full of stories of people who came to him in trouble and went away healed, or comforted, or reconciled with an enemy, and it tells them as wonders, and I am not going to tell you all of them. There are a great many. The writer believed every one of them was God's work, done through the prayers of a humble man, and that is how he tells them.

It also says that Daniel warned the city of things before they happened. That he warned of a great fire, years before Constantinople burned in the reign of Leo. That is told as the Life's report too.

And Kyriakos grew older, and his arms grew tired, and his hair went grey. And Theodora grew up, and married a fisherman, and had children of her own. And on fine days she would bring them up the Bosporus in the old blue boat, which her husband had repainted, and take them up the hill, and show them the pillar.

"I used to row people here," she would say, "when I was your age. Grandfather and I. Hundreds and hundreds of people. Senators and generals and everybody. And he was up there all the time. He's still up there. Look."

And the children would look up, the way children always did, with their mouths open, the way a young monk had once looked up at another pillar on a hill in Syria.

* * *

Daniel came down from his pillar only once.

It was the middle of the four hundred and seventies, and the city was in turmoil. Leo had died. His successor, the emperor Zeno, had been driven out, and a man called Basiliscus had taken the throne. And Basiliscus had taken sides in a fierce argument about how Christians should speak about Christ, an argument that had been settled for the churches of this show, both Catholic and Orthodox, at a great council at Chalcedon about twenty-five years before. He issued a decree against that council. And the city was in uproar.

I am not going to go into the argument tonight. It was about very deep and very important things, and it divided Christians for centuries, and some of the churches that came out of it are still with us, much loved, and I would not want to tell their story badly in a few sentences at bedtime. What matters for us is this. The patriarch of Constantinople, Acacius, and the people of the city, came to the old man on the pillar, and asked for his help.

And Daniel, who had not come down in about fifteen years, came down.

The Life tells it as something close to a procession. An old man, his feet swollen and sore from standing for so long, unable to walk easily, carried and helped along by his disciples, coming down the ladder and into the boats and across the water and into the great city, with the crowds pouring out to see him. He went to the great church. He spoke for the faith of the council. And in the end, the Life says, Basiliscus came and fell at his feet and asked for his forgiveness. And not long afterwards Zeno came back, and Basiliscus was gone.

That is the Life's account, and it is told, as you would expect from a disciple, with Daniel at the very centre of everything. The historians would add that there were a great many other reasons why Basiliscus fell. But they agree that the old man on the pillar did come down, and did go into the city, and that the people went wild to see him.

And then he went back. That is the part I like best. He went back up the ladder, onto his pillar, and he never came down again.

[pause]

Daniel lived on his pillar for thirty-three years and three months altogether. He grew very old up there. The emperors changed. The patriarchs changed. The fishermen who had first stopped at the old temple grew old and died, and their grandchildren rowed up the Bosporus to see him in their turn.

When he knew he was dying, says the Life, he gave his disciples a last teaching, a kind of testament. It was not about pillars. It was not about fasting, or standing in the cold. It was about the simplest things. Love one another. Be humble. Welcome strangers, and look after the poor. Keep to the faith you were given. Do not let anything come between you and God. The same things, really, that he had been telling the fishermen at the window of the old temple, all those years before.

He died on the eleventh of December, in the year four hundred and ninety-three, at the age of eighty-four. His body was brought down from the pillar and shown to the people, and then buried, as he had asked, close to the foot of it, in a little chapel his disciples had built there.

The pillar has long since gone. Anaplus is now just a stretch of the shore of Istanbul, with houses and cafés and fishing boats, and the ferries still go up and down, and the current still runs south on the top and, the fishermen say, north underneath. If you sit by the water there on a winter evening, the wind still comes down from the Black Sea, hard and cold, the way it did then.

And I think you can take something from Daniel to bed tonight.

He was a boy whose mother promised him to God, and who kept her promise for her, all his life. He went into the place everybody was afraid of and sat down and stayed until it was not frightening any more. He stood still in a loud and busy city so that other people had something to steer by. And when he was needed, he came down. And when the need was over, he went back up, and kept on praying.

So if you have had a day that pulled you in every direction. If you live somewhere loud and busy, where everybody is rushing. You could think of Daniel. Standing quietly on his pillar above the water, as the lights come on along the shore. Not doing very much, in one way. Just standing still, and loving God, and praying for everybody going past.

And you could think of Kyriakos and Theodora, who never existed, but who stand tonight for all the real boatmen and fishermen and ordinary people of that shore who rowed the visitors up and down, and brought the bread, and lay awake on the stormy nights. Nobody wrote their names down. But they were there. And I like to think that Daniel, up on his pillar, prayed for every one of them.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Most of tonight's story comes from the Life of Daniel the Stylite, written in Greek by one of his own disciples, not long after Daniel died. Elizabeth Dawes and Norman Baynes translated it into English in a book called Three Byzantine Saints. The great Bollandist scholar Hippolyte Delehaye edited it, along with the lives of the other pillar saints, a century ago. For Simeon, we used Theodoret, as we did last time. And the idea of the holy man as someone a whole society could steer by is Peter Brown's, from a famous essay.

What is history. Daniel's birth near Samosata, his parents Elias and Martha, his going into a monastery as a boy, his visits to Simeon, his arrival at Constantinople under the emperor Marcian, his years in the old temple, the friendship of the patriarch Anatolius, Sergius and the leather tunic, the pillar at Anaplus, the quarrel and the peace with Gelanius, the emperor Leo's visits and his new pillar, the ordination by Gennadius, the one coming down in about four hundred and seventy-six, and his death in four hundred and ninety-three after thirty-three years on the pillar.

What is told as the Life's report. The old man on the Jerusalem road, the demons of the temple, the healing of Anatolius and the other healings, the frozen night, the ladder at the ordination, the warning of the fire and the vision of Simeon calling him up. The disciple who wrote them believed them and said so. We have told them as his.

What we left out. The Life tells a little story about how Daniel got his name, and a great many other wonders, and the details of the argument about the council of Chalcedon, and we have left them all for another night.

What we invented. Kyriakos the boatman, his daughter Theodora, their blue boat, and everything they say and see, and most of the words of the other people in the story. The Life's words are in our own plain paraphrase.

That's the story of Saint Daniel the Stylite for tonight. His feast is on the eleventh of December, in the Orthodox Church and in the Roman Martyrology, which means, if you are listening to this in the middle of November, it is not very far away. Holy Daniel, pray to God for us. Goodnight, and God bless.

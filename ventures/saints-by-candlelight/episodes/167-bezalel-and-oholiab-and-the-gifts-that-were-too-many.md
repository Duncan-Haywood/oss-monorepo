It is just after sunrise, and in front of a large tent at the edge of a camp there is a pile.

It is not a tidy pile. It is the kind of pile that happens when a great many people put things down in the same place, one after another, without asking each other where they ought to go. There are bundles of wool, dyed blue, and purple, and a red so deep it is nearly black. There are rolls of white linen. There are skins, rams' skins dyed red, folded and stacked and sliding gently off each other. There are planks of a hard, orange-brown wood. There are little bags that clink when you move them, and little jars that smell of spice, and on top of it all, catching the first light, a scatter of gold. Earrings. Brooches. A ring with a seal on it. A bracelet in the shape of a snake that someone has clearly been wearing for years.

And in front of the pile, a dozen craftsmen are standing with their hands on their hips, looking at it.

One of them, a man with burn marks all up his forearms, says: "It was not this big last night."

"It was not this big an hour ago," says another.

And a woman with a basket on her head walks up behind them, says "excuse me" very politely, and puts down another basket of spun goats' hair on top of the bracelet.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Let me tell you where we are.

We are at the foot of a mountain in the desert. The book calls it Sinai, and sometimes Horeb, and sometimes simply the mountain of God. It is a hard, bare, reddish mountain, all cliffs and boulders and ravines, the kind of mountain that looks as if it has never once been asked to be pretty. And at the bottom of it, spread out across the plain, are the tents of the people of Israel.

They have not been out of Egypt very long. A few months. Not long ago they were slaves, making bricks for Pharaoh, and you may remember from some earlier nights on this show that they were not even given straw. Then there were the plagues, and the night of the Passover, and the sea, and the long walk out into the desert, with a pillar of cloud in front of them by day and a pillar of fire by night.

And when they left Egypt, the book tells us something curious. It says that on the last night the people asked their Egyptian neighbours for jewellery of silver and gold, and for clothing, and the Lord gave them favour in the eyes of the Egyptians, so that they gave them what they asked. The very people they had worked for, handing over their jewellery at the door. It is as if, after all those years of unpaid work, somebody had finally settled the bill, all at once, on the doorstep, in gold.

So here they are, at the foot of the mountain. A people who a few months ago owned nothing, and who now, rather unexpectedly, have a great deal of jewellery.

Keep that in mind. It is going to matter.

* * *

Now, I have to tell you something sad first, and I will tell it quickly, because it is not tonight's story, and because it ends in forgiveness.

Moses went up the mountain to speak with the Lord. He was gone forty days and forty nights. And down below, the people got frightened and bored, in that order, and then bored and frightened again. They said to Aaron, Moses' brother: we don't know what has become of this Moses. Make us gods to go in front of us.

And Aaron, who was a good man on most days, and not on this one, said: give me your gold earrings.

And they did. They took them out of their ears, the earrings from Egypt, and they gave them, and Aaron melted them down and made a calf of gold. And the people danced around it and said, this is the god who brought you out of Egypt.

You see the trouble. They were very generous. They gave their gold away without a second's thought. They simply gave it to the wrong thing.

Moses came down the mountain, and saw, and in his anger he broke the two tablets of stone that he was carrying. There was great sorrow in the camp. And then Moses did what Moses always did, which was to go back to God and pray for the very people who had let him down. He said, if you will not forgive them, blot me out of your book too. And the Lord heard him. Moses went up the mountain a second time, and came down a second time, with two new tablets. And when he came down, the skin of his face was shining, because he had been speaking with the Lord, and he did not even know it. He had to put a veil over his face when he talked to people, because the shining frightened them.

So that is where we are tonight. The calf is gone. The people have been forgiven. Moses has come down from the mountain with his face glowing under a veil. And the very next thing he does, the book tells us, is call everyone together and talk to them about gold again.

Which is either very brave or very wise. I think probably both.

* * *

Because there was a plan. There had been a plan all along. While Moses was up the mountain the first time, before any of the trouble, the Lord had told him about it in great detail. It takes up chapter after chapter of the book of Exodus, and if you have ever tried to read straight through Exodus, you will know exactly which chapters I mean. They are the ones with all the measurements.

The plan was a tent.

Not an ordinary tent. A tent for the Lord. The Lord said to Moses: let them make me a sanctuary, so that I may dwell in their midst.

I want you to hear how extraordinary that is. The God who made the mountain, the God of the cloud and the fire and the sea, wanted to live in the middle of the camp. Not at the top of the mountain, where it would be safe and quiet and nobody would knock over a water jar outside. In the middle. Among the tents. With the goats, and the cooking smoke, and the children shouting, and the grandmothers arguing about whose turn it was at the well.

And because it was a camp of people who were always moving, the house of God would have to move too. It would have to fold up. Every piece of it would have to be carried, on shoulders and on carts, by a people who did not yet know how long they were going to be walking.

So it was a tent. A beautiful one. With a courtyard round it made of hanging linen on posts. With an altar of bronze in front, and a great bronze basin for washing. With curtains of fine linen, embroidered with angels, cherubim, in blue and purple and scarlet. With a golden lampstand inside, and a little golden table, and a golden altar for incense. And at the very back, behind a heavy curtain, in the innermost room, the most precious thing of all, a chest of wood covered in gold, which would hold the tablets of the Law, with two golden cherubim on its lid, facing each other, their wings stretched out over the top.

And all of it, the Lord told Moses, was to be made exactly according to the pattern he had shown him on the mountain.

Which raised a very practical question. A question that anyone who has ever had a kitchen redone will recognise.

Who is going to build it?

* * *

This is where our two craftsmen come in.

The Lord said to Moses: see, I have called by name Bezalel, the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah.

Called by name. I like that. Not "find me a carpenter". Not "put up a notice". The Lord had a particular man in mind, and he knew his name, and his father's name, and his grandfather's.

The grandfather, Hur, you may remember from a few nights ago. He was the man who stood on the hill at Rephidim beside Aaron, holding up the tired arms of Moses until the going down of the sun. The book of Exodus does not quite say that it is the same Hur, though the First Book of Chronicles sets out the family, Hur and then Uri and then Bezalel, in the tribe of Judah, and many readers have thought it was. I like to think so. A grandfather who held up someone's hands, and a grandson who was given hands of his own for something holy.

And here is what the Lord said about him. I have filled him with the Spirit of God. With wisdom, and with understanding, and with knowledge, and with all kinds of craftsmanship. To devise artistic designs. To work in gold and silver and bronze. To cut stones for setting. To carve wood. To work in every craft.

Now, I want to stop on that for a moment, because it is easy to rush past.

As far as I can find, this is the first time in the whole Bible that anyone is said to be filled with the Spirit of God. The first. Before the prophets. Before the kings. Before any of the great preachers. And what is he filled with the Spirit for? Not for preaching. Not for leading an army. For metalwork. For woodcarving. For setting stones in gold so that they would not fall out.

The old book seems to think that making a beautiful thing well, with care, for God, is holy work. That the Spirit who would one day speak through the prophets started, as it were, in a workshop.

I find that very cheering. Especially if you are the sort of person who is good with their hands and not very good at speeches. And there are a great many of us.

And then the Lord said one more thing. And behold, I have appointed with him Oholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan.

So Bezalel would not be on his own. He would have a partner.

Oholiab, the book tells us later, was an engraver, and a designer, and an embroiderer in blue and purple and scarlet yarn and fine linen. So if Bezalel was the man for gold and wood and stone, Oholiab was the man for thread and pattern and cloth. Between the two of them, they could make anything.

And the Lord added: and I have put skill into the hearts of all who are skilful, so that they may make everything I have commanded you.

So there were others, too. A whole company of people with clever hands. But these two were the masters.

* * *

There is a little thing about these two men that the old Jewish teachers loved, and I want to tell it to you, as a saying of theirs, not as part of the book.

Bezalel was from the tribe of Judah. And Judah was a great tribe. Large, strong, the tribe of the lion, the tribe that kings would one day come from. Oholiab was from the tribe of Dan. And Dan was counted among the lesser tribes, descended from a son of Bilhah, Rachel's servant, rather than of one of the wives, and in the marching order of the camp the tribe of Dan would walk right at the back, as the rear guard.

And the old commentary, which the great Jewish teacher Rashi repeats in his notes on this chapter, says that this was on purpose. That God put a man from the greatest tribe side by side with a man from one of the least, so that nobody could ever say that holy work belonged to the important people. In the house of God, they said, the great and the small stand together.

I don't know whether that was what was in the mind of the Lord. The book does not say. But it is a lovely thing to think, and it is very much in the spirit of the God we meet in the rest of the Bible, who seems to have a soft spot for people who walk at the back.

* * *

Before a single stone was cut, though, there was one more thing the Lord wanted said.

He said to Moses: above all, you shall keep my sabbaths. Six days shall work be done, but the seventh day is a sabbath of solemn rest, holy to the Lord.

And when Moses gathered the people to tell them about the tent, the very first thing he said, before any of the gold and the linen and the measurements, was this. On the seventh day you shall rest. You shall kindle no fire in any of your dwellings on the sabbath day.

You see what that means for a metalworker. No fire. The forge goes cold. The bellows hang on their hook. Even the house of God, even the most important building project in the history of the people of Israel, stops on the seventh day.

I think there is something wonderful in that. God would rather wait one more day for his house than have his people work themselves to the bone building it. The rest is part of the work. The sabbath is not a gap in the building. It is, in a way, the first room of it.

And then Moses spoke to the whole congregation of Israel, and he said the words that start our story properly.

Take from among you an offering for the Lord. Whoever is of a generous heart, let him bring the Lord's offering. Gold, silver and bronze. Blue and purple and scarlet yarns, and fine twined linen. Goats' hair. Rams' skins dyed red. Fine leather. Acacia wood. Oil for the light. Spices for the anointing oil and for the sweet incense. Onyx stones, and stones for setting, for the priest's garments.

And then he said: and let every skilful person among you come and make all that the Lord has commanded.

And then all the congregation went away from the presence of Moses.

That is how the book puts it. They went away. And for a moment, I think, the camp was very quiet.

* * *

Now I would like you to meet somebody. She is our own invention, and I will tell you so again at the end, but she stands for a great many real people the book does not name.

Her name is Ziva, and she is from the tribe of Naphtali, and she is about forty years old, which in that camp made her neither young nor old but simply busy. She has a husband and three children and a mother-in-law, and a tent that always has a hole in the same corner however many times she mends it.

And she owns, among other things, a mirror.

Now, a mirror in those days was not glass. It was a disc of bronze, polished and polished until it shone, so that if you held it up you could see your face in it, a little golden and a little blurry, as if you were looking at yourself at sunset. It had a handle. Hers had a handle shaped like a little woman holding a flower above her head. It had come out of Egypt with her. It was the most valuable thing she had ever owned, and it was, I think it is fair to say, the only beautiful thing.

On the evening after Moses spoke, she sat outside her tent with the mirror in her lap, turning it over.

Her eldest daughter, who was twelve and noticed everything, said: "Are you going to give it?"

"I don't know," said Ziva.

"They gave their earrings for the calf," said the daughter. "Everyone did. You gave yours."

"I know," said Ziva. "I remember."

And she was quiet for a long time, and then she said: "That is rather the point."

Because there is a kind of sorrow, after you have done something wrong, that just sits there. And there is another kind that gets up and does something. And Ziva, sitting outside her tent with a bronze mirror and the memory of a gold calf, was deciding which kind hers was going to be.

* * *

The next morning, the book tells us what happened. And it is one of my favourite passages in the whole of the Old Testament, because it is so full of people.

They came, it says. Everyone whose heart stirred him up, and everyone whose spirit made him willing, came and brought the Lord's offering for the work of the tent of meeting, and for all its service, and for the holy garments.

They came, both men and women. All who were of a willing heart brought brooches and earrings and signet rings and armlets, all sorts of gold objects, every man and woman dedicating an offering of gold to the Lord.

There it is, you see. The gold again. The same earrings, very likely, or their brothers and sisters. The jewellery from Egypt. Only this time it was going to the right place.

And everyone who had blue or purple or scarlet yarn, or fine linen, or goats' hair, or the red rams' skins, or the fine leather, brought them. And everyone who could make an offering of silver or bronze brought it. And everyone who had acacia wood, which was the only good timber in that desert, hard and orange and heavy and very difficult to get hold of, brought it.

That fine leather, by the way, is a small mystery. The old Hebrew word is tachash, and nobody is quite sure what animal it came from. Older English Bibles say badgers. Some modern ones say goatskins, and some say the skin of sea cows or dolphins from the Red Sea, and some simply give up and say fine leather. It was the outer covering of the whole tent, the bit that kept the rain out, so whatever it was, it was tough. I like to think of a scholar somewhere, very late at night, surrounded by books, saying "dolphins? in the desert?" and then going to bed.

And then the book says a thing I love. All the women who had skill spun with their hands, and brought what they had spun, blue and purple and scarlet yarn and fine linen. And all the women whose hearts stirred them up in wisdom spun the goats' hair.

So the women of the camp sat outside their tents, with their spindles turning, and the yarn growing round and fat on the spindle, while they talked and laughed and kept one eye on the children. And the goats' hair was for the great curtains that went over the top of the tent, rough and dark and waterproof. And the book calls the women who spun it wise. Not just skilled. Wise of heart. For spinning goats' hair.

And the leaders of the tribes brought the precious stones, the onyx and the stones for the priest's breastpiece, and the spice, and the oil for the light.

And the book sums it all up like this: all the men and women of the people of Israel whose hearts moved them to bring anything for the work that the Lord had commanded, brought it as a freewill offering to the Lord.

A freewill offering. Nobody counted what anybody gave. Nobody was told how much was expected. You gave what your heart told you to give, and you put it down, and you went home.

* * *

Ziva brought her mirror on the second morning.

She had meant to bring it on the first morning, and had got as far as the edge of the camp and then gone home again, which I think we can all understand.

There was already a queue. There was already the beginning of a pile. And standing by the pile, in front of the big workshop tent, was a boy of about ten with a broom much too big for him, sweeping a path through the sand so that people would not trip over the bundles.

He is also our own invention. His name is Ammi, and he is from the tribe of Dan, and he is the son of a cousin of Oholiab's, and he has been allowed to sweep the workshop on the strict condition that he does not touch anything sharp, anything hot or anything gold. Which, in that workshop, did not leave very much.

Ziva held out the mirror.

Ammi looked at it, and then at her, and then at the pile, which had a great deal of gold on it and not very much bronze.

"Is it all right?" said Ziva. "It isn't gold."

And a voice behind the boy said: "It's exactly right."

It was a tall man, not young, with a short grey beard and burn scars all up his forearms, and the kind of eyes that look at a thing for a long time before they decide what it is. Bezalel. He took the mirror from her very carefully, with both hands, and turned it so that it caught the light.

"Somebody has polished this every day for twenty years," he said.

"Since my mother," said Ziva.

"Then it's already been looked after properly," said Bezalel. "We'll take good care of it." And he gave it back to Ammi, and said, "Not on the pile. On the bench. With the bronze." And Ammi carried it away in both hands as if it were full of water.

I should say that the book does not tell us any of this conversation. But it does tell us, a little later, what Bezalel made with mirrors. And I will come to that.

* * *

So the work began.

And the book tells us one more thing about Bezalel and Oholiab that I find very moving. It says that the Lord had put it into their hearts to teach. Not only to make things, but to teach others how to make them. The two of them, the master of metal and the master of thread, gathered the skilful men and women of the camp around them, and showed them.

Imagine that workshop. It was not a building. It was a cluster of tents and awnings, and shelters of cloth to keep off the sun, and open spaces in between with fires and anvils and benches.

At one end, the looms. Oholiab's end. Big upright looms, with linen threads stretched on them, white and very fine, and women and men working the blue and purple and scarlet through them to make the patterns. Ten great curtains of linen, the book says, each twenty-eight cubits long and four cubits wide, which is about forty feet by six. With cherubim worked into them, skilfully. And along the edges, fifty loops of blue, so that the curtains could be joined together. And fifty clasps of gold to hold the loops. And then eleven more curtains of the dark goats' hair, each thirty cubits long, to go over the top.

At the other end, the fires. Bezalel's end. The smell of hot metal and charcoal. The hammering. The bellows going up and down like an animal breathing. Men beating gold into sheets thin enough to wrap round wood. Men cutting and smoothing the acacia boards, which had to stand upright and fit together into walls, each one with silver bases under its feet so that it would not sink into the sand.

And in the middle, Ammi, with his broom, trying to be everywhere and touch nothing.

I imagine he asked a great many questions. Boys of ten generally do.

"Why do the clasps have to be gold?"

"Because they're inside," said Oholiab. "Everything you can see from inside is gold or blue or linen. The goats' hair goes on the outside. Like a coat over your best clothes."

"Why do the curtains have angels on?"

"Because it's God's house," said Oholiab. "And angels are always round God. So when the priest goes in, he's walking in among the angels."

"Why does Bezalel get all the gold?"

And Oholiab, who was a patient man, put down his needle and said: "Because Bezalel is better at gold than me. And I am better at embroidery than Bezalel. And if you ever see him try to embroider anything, you have my permission to laugh."

From the other end of the workshop, a voice shouted: "I heard that."

* * *

And all this time, every morning, the people kept coming.

That is the thing the book makes a point of. Not just the first day. They kept bringing freewill offerings to Moses, it says, every morning. Every morning, more gold. More linen. More spun yarn. More planks of acacia, carried in from heaven knows where across the desert on somebody's shoulder.

The pile got bigger. The workshop got fuller. Ammi had to sweep a longer and longer path round the edge of it. There were bags of silver under the benches, and rolls of linen stacked on top of the bags of silver, and somebody's spare goats' hair spilling out over the top of the linen. Oholiab could not find his best needles. Bezalel kept tripping over a bundle of rams' skins that had not been there when he went to bed.

And so, one morning, the craftsmen came to a decision.

The book puts it like this. All the craftsmen who were doing every sort of work on the sanctuary came, each from the task that he was doing, and said to Moses: the people bring much more than enough for doing the work that the Lord has commanded us to do.

I love that. Each from the task that he was doing. They put down their hammers and their needles and their planes, and they walked across the camp together, a little delegation of tired, sooty, slightly baffled artisans, and they went to Moses with the strangest complaint any builder has ever made.

There's too much.

Not, the money has run out. Not, the materials are late. Not, the budget was underestimated. There's too much, please make them stop.

I have read a great many accounts of building projects, over the years. Cathedrals and bridges and town halls. I have read about bishops writing anxious letters asking for more money, and builders downing tools because they had not been paid, and towers that stood half finished for a hundred years because the funds ran out. I don't believe I have read of another one where the workmen went to the person in charge and asked him, please, to stop the people giving.

* * *

And Moses did.

Moses gave a command, the book says, and word was proclaimed throughout the camp. Let no man or woman do anything more for the offering for the sanctuary.

So a herald went through the camp. Down the lanes between the tents. Past the cooking fires and the goats and the women spinning at their doors. Calling it out. No more. No more offerings for the sanctuary. You have given enough. Thank you. No more.

And the book says: so the people were restrained from bringing. For the material they had was sufficient to do all the work, and more.

Restrained. They had to be held back. Like a crowd at a gate.

Ziva was sitting outside her tent when the herald came past, spinning goats' hair, as it happens, because she had decided that a mirror was a good start but not the end of it. She heard the call, and she stopped the spindle with her hand, and she looked down at the hank of dark yarn in her lap.

"Well," said her mother-in-law, from inside the tent. "That's a first."

"What do I do with this?" said Ziva.

"Mend the corner of the tent," said her mother-in-law. "It's been leaking for a month."

And she did. And I like to think it was the best-mended corner in the whole camp.

* * *

I want to say something about this, just for a minute, before we go on, because I think it is the heart of tonight's story.

Not so many weeks before, the same people had given their gold for a calf. And they had given it fast, without thinking, because they were frightened. Because Moses was gone and they wanted something they could see.

And now they were giving again. Just as fast. Just as much. More. But not because they were frightened. Because their hearts had been stirred. Because they had been forgiven, and the God they had let down had said, I still want to live in the middle of your camp. Build me a house.

The book does not say any of this out loud. It just puts the two stories side by side and lets you notice. The gold that went into the fire for the calf, and the gold that went to Bezalel's workshop. The same people. The same earrings. A different heart.

I think that is a hopeful thing to go to sleep on. That the very thing you got wrong yesterday, you can do right today. That God does not ask you to become a different person with different gifts. He asks you to bring the same gifts, the same earrings, the same mirror, the same hands, to a better place.

* * *

Now. What did they make?

They made everything. And the book of Exodus tells you, piece by piece, in chapter after chapter, and if I read you all of it we should still be here at breakfast. So here are a few of my favourite things.

First, Bezalel made the ark.

He made it himself, the book says. Of acacia wood. Two and a half cubits long, a cubit and a half wide and a cubit and a half high. That is about the size of a large chest, the kind you might keep blankets in at the end of a bed. He overlaid it with pure gold inside and out, and made a moulding of gold round the top edge. He cast four rings of gold for its four feet, and made poles of acacia wood covered in gold, and put the poles through the rings, so that it could be carried. The poles, the Lord had said, were never to be taken out. The ark was always ready to travel.

And he made the cover for it, the mercy seat, the place of atonement, out of pure gold. And on the two ends of it, he made two cherubim of hammered gold, beaten out of the same piece as the cover. One at each end. And the cherubim spread out their wings above, overshadowing the cover with their wings, and their faces were towards one another, looking down towards the mercy seat.

Later in the Book of Numbers we are told that when Moses went into the tent to speak with the Lord, he heard the voice speaking to him from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim. So the space between those two golden angels, that small space above a golden lid, was where the voice of God was heard.

Bezalel made that space. With a hammer.

* * *

Then he made the lampstand.

And I think this was where the boy with the broom would have stopped sweeping and just stood and watched. Because the lampstand was made of pure gold, all of it, hammered out of one piece. A central stem, with six branches coming out of its sides, three on one side and three on the other, curving up like the arms of a tree. And on each branch, three cups, made like almond blossoms, each with a little round bud and a flower. And on the stem itself, four more.

Almond blossom. You know the almond tree is the first tree to flower in that part of the world, at the very end of winter, when everything else is still bare. White flowers, with a little pink at the heart, on branches that have no leaves yet. In Hebrew, its name sounds like the word for watching, or waking, because it is the tree that wakes up first.

So the lamp that would burn all night in the house of God, in the dark, was shaped like the first tree to wake up in spring. And it was made of a single talent of pure gold, the book says, which is a great deal of gold, something like seventy pounds, though nobody is quite sure of the exact weight.

And at the top of each branch, a little lamp, filled with pure olive oil, so that the light would shine out in front of it.

* * *

He made the little golden altar for the incense. And he made the holy anointing oil, and the pure fragrant incense, blended, the book says, as by the perfumer. So Bezalel was a perfumer, too, on top of everything else. I imagine the workshop smelled extraordinary that week. Hot metal and charcoal and burning wood at one end, and myrrh and cinnamon and sweet cane at the other.

He made the altar of burnt offering, of acacia wood covered in bronze, with a grating of bronze like a net.

And then he made the basin.

The great bronze basin that stood in the courtyard, between the altar and the tent, where the priests would wash their hands and their feet before they went in to serve. And the book tells us what it was made of. I will read it to you as it is. He made the basin of bronze and its stand of bronze, from the mirrors of the ministering women who ministered at the entrance of the tent of meeting.

From the mirrors.

The book does not tell us who those women were, exactly, or what their service was. Only that there were women who served at the door of the tent, and that they gave their mirrors. Their polished bronze, the thing they had looked into every morning to see their own faces. And Bezalel melted them down and made them into the basin where the priests would wash before going in to God.

I think that is one of the most beautiful sentences in the Old Testament. A great many small vanities, given up, and made into one great cleanness.

So when Ziva walked past the courtyard of the tent, years later, as an old woman, she could see the basin shining in the sun. And somewhere in it, melted and hammered and changed beyond recognition, was the bronze that had once had a handle shaped like a little woman holding a flower above her head. She could not have pointed to it. But she knew it was there.

Ziva is our invention. The mirrors are not.

* * *

And when it was all finished, somebody counted.

This is another thing I love about the book of Exodus. It does not just say "they had a lot of gold". It gives you the inventory, as if somebody had stood there at the end with a tablet and added it all up, which is exactly what happened. The book says the account was made by the Levites, under the direction of Ithamar, Aaron's youngest son.

All the gold that was used for the work, it says, came to twenty-nine talents and seven hundred and thirty shekels. The silver came to a hundred talents and one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five shekels. The bronze came to seventy talents and two thousand four hundred shekels.

And the silver had been given in a particular way. Every man who was counted in the census of the people gave the same amount, half a shekel, no more and no less, the rich the same as the poor. And they made the silver bases for the boards of the tent out of it, a hundred of them, one talent of silver for each base.

So the walls of the house of God stood on the half-shekels of every family in the camp. Everyone the same. You could not have pointed to the bit that came from you. But it was holding up the building.

I am not going to tell you what all that gold would be worth in money today, partly because nobody is sure exactly how heavy a talent was, and partly because I think it would spoil it. It was a great deal. It was given freely. That is enough.

* * *

And then came the day the work was done.

The book says it very simply. Thus all the work of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting was finished. And the people of Israel did according to all that the Lord had commanded Moses. So they did.

And they brought it all to Moses. The tent and all its furnishings. The clasps and the boards and the bars and the pillars and the bases. The covering of rams' skins dyed red, and the covering of fine leather, and the veil. The ark and its poles and the mercy seat. The table and the bread. The pure lampstand with its lamps. The golden altar. The anointing oil and the sweet incense. The bronze altar and the basin and its stand. The hangings of the court. The holy garments for Aaron the priest.

It must have taken a long time. A long line of people, carrying things. And Moses, standing at the end of it, looking at each piece.

And the book says: and Moses saw all the work, and behold, they had done it. As the Lord had commanded, so had they done it.

And then Moses blessed them.

I would very much like to know what Moses said, and the book does not tell us, and I am not going to make it up. But I know what it felt like. Anyone who has ever made something with their own hands, and then had somebody they respect look at it slowly, all the way round, and nod, knows what it felt like.

I suspect Ammi was at the back of the crowd with his broom, and I suspect he had swept the path to Moses' tent twice that morning.

[pause]

On the first day of the first month of the second year after they came out of Egypt, the book says, the tabernacle was set up.

Moses put up the boards on their silver bases, and the bars, and the pillars. He spread the tent over the top, and the coverings over the tent. He put the tablets of the Law into the ark and set the mercy seat on top, and carried the ark into the innermost room, and hung the veil in front of it. He set the table and the lampstand and the golden altar in their places, and lit the lamps. He set up the bronze altar at the door, and put the basin between, with water in it for washing. He hung the curtains of the court all round.

So Moses finished the work.

And then the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle.

And Moses, who had climbed the mountain of God, and spoken with the Lord face to face as a man speaks with his friend, Moses whose face still shone, was not able to go into the tent. Because the cloud had settled on it, and the glory of the Lord filled it.

The God of the mountain had come down to live in the middle of the camp. In a house made of earrings and goats' hair and mirrors and the half-shekels of every family. In the work of the hands of Bezalel and Oholiab and all the skilful women and men.

And from then on, the book says, whenever the cloud was taken up from over the tabernacle, the people set out on their journeys. And if the cloud was not taken up, they did not set out, until the day that it was. For the cloud of the Lord was on the tabernacle by day, and fire was in it by night, in the sight of all the house of Israel, throughout all their journeys.

* * *

Bezalel and Oholiab do not appear again in the story of the wilderness. The book does not tell us how long they lived or where they died. They finished the work they were given, and they were blessed, and then they put their tools away.

But their work went on travelling.

The tent went with the people through all the years in the wilderness, folded up and carried on shoulders, unfolded and pitched again wherever the cloud stopped. The ark that Bezalel made with his own hands went into the river Jordan ahead of the people, on the shoulders of the priests, as we heard a few nights ago. And in the land, the Book of Joshua tells us, the whole congregation gathered at Shiloh and set up the tent of meeting there.

And much, much later, hundreds of years later, the Second Book of Chronicles tells us that when the young King Solomon went to the high place at Gibeon to pray, the bronze altar that Bezalel the son of Uri, son of Hur, had made was still there, before the tent of the Lord. Bezalel's altar. Still standing. Still in use. Still with his name on it, as it were. And it was on that night, at Gibeon, after Solomon had offered his sacrifices on Bezalel's altar, that the Lord appeared to him in a dream and said, ask what I shall give you.

So the work of a craftsman's hands lasted longer than he did. Which is, I suppose, what craftsmen hope for.

[pause]

The old Jewish teachers told a little story about Bezalel, which is in the Talmud, written down many centuries later. I will tell it to you as their story.

They said that when Moses passed on to Bezalel what the Lord had commanded, he gave him the things in this order. First the ark, then the furnishings, then the tent.

And Bezalel, the rabbis say, said, politely: Moses, our teacher, usually a man builds his house first, and then he brings the furniture into it. If I make the ark first, where shall I put it? Perhaps the Lord told you to make the tent first?

And Moses said: perhaps you were in the shadow of God, and you knew.

And if you look at the book of Exodus itself, it is true that when the work is told, in chapters thirty-six and thirty-seven, the curtains and the boards of the tent come first, and the ark after them. The rabbis noticed that, and made a story of it.

Which is a little joke, because that is what Bezalel's name means in Hebrew. Be-tsel-El. In the shadow of God.

I like that story because it is a story about a craftsman thinking. Not just doing what he was told, but asking the sort of sensible question a builder asks. And I like that the rabbis imagined Moses being pleased about it, rather than cross.

[pause]

And Christians, when they read this part of Exodus, have always seen something more in it.

The Letter to the Hebrews, in the New Testament, says that the tent in the wilderness was made as a copy and a shadow of heavenly things, just as Moses was told: see that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown you on the mountain. And the Book of Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, speaks of a copy of the holy tent which God prepared from the beginning.

And then the Gospel of Saint John, at the very beginning, says this. And the Word became flesh, and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory. The word that John uses for dwelt is, in Greek, the word for pitching a tent. The Word became flesh, and pitched his tent among us. The early Christians heard the whole story of tonight in that one line. The God who once lived in a tent of goats' hair in the middle of the camp came, at last, to live in the middle of us in a body of flesh and blood. And the glory that filled the tabernacle so that even Moses could not go in, the disciples saw in a human face.

That is a reading, a very old and very loved one, and I give it to you as that.

[pause]

So tonight, as you lie in the dark, I would like you to think about the things in your own house that you have looked after. The thing you polished. The thing you mended. The thing you made with your hands, however small, however lopsided.

And I would like you to know that, according to the book of Exodus, God thinks those hands are holy. That when the Spirit of God first filled a person, it was a person who worked in a workshop. That God is glad of the gold, and just as glad of the goats' hair, and gladdest of all of the willing heart. And that the most generous people in the whole Bible were, on one particular morning, a camp full of ordinary families who had got it badly wrong a few weeks before, and who had to be asked, kindly, to stop.

Somewhere at the edge of that camp, an old woman is sitting outside a tent with a very well-mended corner, spinning. And a young man who used to be a boy with a broom is walking past with a toolbag over his shoulder, because Oholiab taught him embroidery in the end, and he is on his way to mend a curtain with angels on it.

And in the middle of the camp, over the tent, the cloud is resting. And when the night comes down, there is fire in the cloud. Not a frightening fire. A steady one. Like a lamp left burning in a house, so that anyone who wakes in the night can see that someone is there.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Exodus, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The Lord's words calling Bezalel by name and appointing Oholiab are in chapter thirty-one, verses one to eleven, and the sabbath that follows is in verses twelve to seventeen. Moses' call for the offering, the people bringing their gold, yarn, skins and wood, the women spinning, and Moses naming the two craftsmen and their gift for teaching are in chapter thirty-five. The craftsmen saying there was more than enough, and the proclamation through the camp, are in chapter thirty-six, verses one to seven. The curtains, the ark, the cherubim, the lampstand, the incense and the anointing oil are in chapters thirty-six and thirty-seven. The basin made from the mirrors of the ministering women, the account kept under Ithamar, and the weights of gold, silver and bronze are in chapter thirty-eight. Moses seeing the work and blessing them is at the end of chapter thirty-nine, and the setting up of the tabernacle and the cloud filling it are in chapter forty. The gold from the Egyptians is in chapter twelve, and the calf, the broken tablets and the shining face are in chapters thirty-two to thirty-four, which we have only summarised.

The half-shekel, the same for rich and poor, is in chapter thirty. Bezalel's family is in the First Book of Chronicles, chapter two, and his altar at Gibeon in the Second Book of Chronicles, chapter one. The voice from between the cherubim is in Numbers, chapter seven, Dan's place at the rear of the march in Numbers, chapter ten, and the tent at Shiloh in Joshua, chapter eighteen. The Christian readings are from Hebrews, chapters eight and nine, Wisdom, chapter nine, and the first chapter of John.

The story of Bezalel's question is in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Berakhot, and the saying about Judah and Dan is in Rashi's commentary on Exodus. Both are later Jewish tradition, told as tradition. The identification of the tachash is genuinely uncertain, and the weight of a talent is an estimate.

And Ziva and her mirror, Ammi and his broom, and every word that Bezalel and Oholiab say to anyone, are our own invention.

It is the middle of the afternoon, and it is very hot, and a little boy of four is sitting in the thin shade of a tent with a pebble in his mouth.

His big sister gave it to him. She found it on the ground, a smooth one, about the size of a grape, and she wiped it on her sleeve and said, here, suck this, it helps. And it does help, a little. It makes your mouth think it has something to do.

Beside him, his father is holding up a water skin. It is a goatskin, the kind you tie at the neck and carry on your shoulder, and it should be fat and round and sloshing. It is not. It hangs from his father's hand like an empty sock. His father tips it up, and shakes it, and holds the neck to his lips for a long time.

Nothing.

And from further along the row, from all over the camp, there is a noise the little boy has not heard before. It is not the low hum of grumbling. It is louder than that, and sharper, and it is coming from the direction of the tent where Moses lives.

His sister looks at the noise. Then she looks at her father.

Stay here, says her father. Both of you. Stay here.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Let me tell you where we are.

If you were with us last time, you will remember the wilderness of Sin, and the morning the ground went white. The people of Israel were a month out of Egypt, and the flour had run out, and they grumbled, and the Lord sent them quails in the evening and, in the morning, the manna. Small and white and sweet, like wafers made with honey. A day's portion every day. Twice as much on the sixth day. None at all on the seventh, because the seventh day was a rest. And Ziva, our invented girl with the mended tambourine, gathered for her old neighbour Naamah, who walks with two sticks, and her little brother learned the hard way that manna kept overnight goes wriggly.

Well. The manna kept coming. That is the first thing to say tonight, and I want you to hold on to it, because it matters later. Every morning, except the sabbath, the ground went white, and they gathered, and they ate. The book tells us, in another place, that they ate it for forty years. So all through tonight's story, whatever else is going on, breakfast is arriving every morning on time.

But you cannot stay in the wilderness of Sin for ever, any more than you could stay at Elim. So the book says that the whole congregation of the people of Israel moved on from the wilderness of Sin by stages, according to the commandment of the Lord.

By stages. That is a lovely, honest phrase. It means they did not do it all at once. They walked a bit, and stopped, and walked a bit more. The Book of Numbers, which keeps a list of every place they camped, like a very old travel diary, gives us the stages. From the wilderness of Sin, it says, they camped at Dophkah. And from Dophkah they camped at Alush. And from Alush they camped at Rephidim.

Nobody now knows where Dophkah was, or Alush. They are just names, two little stepping stones in a list. I find that rather touching. Somebody once spent a night at Alush, and made a fire, and put the children to bed, and in the morning packed it all up again, and now it is a word in a book and nothing else.

And how did they know when to stop? According to the commandment of the Lord, the book says. Remember the pillar of cloud. Ever since Egypt there had been a cloud going in front of them by day, and a fire by night. And the Book of Numbers explains that whenever the cloud lifted, the people set out, and wherever the cloud settled, there the people camped. Sometimes it stayed a day. Sometimes it stayed longer. They did not choose. They followed.

Which is a very restful way to travel, in one way. Nobody has to argue about the map. But it does mean that sometimes the cloud stops somewhere you would not have chosen.

And the cloud stopped at Rephidim.

* * *

Now, where was Rephidim? I have to tell you honestly that nobody knows for certain. It depends on which way the people went through the wilderness, and that is an old and open question. For many centuries the monks and pilgrims of the Sinai have pointed to a long valley in the south of the Sinai peninsula, among the high red mountains, where there is a palm grove now. That is the traditional place. It may be right. Nobody can prove it.

But wherever it was, we know what it was like, because the book tells us in one short, dry sentence.

There was no water for the people to drink.

That is all. Eight words. But if you have ever been really thirsty, properly thirsty, you will know that those are some of the most frightening words in the world.

Hunger is hard. We saw that last time. But you can be hungry for a long time. People have gone weeks without food. Water is different. In the heat of a desert, a person without water is in danger in a day or two, and a small child sooner, and an animal is no better off. And there were a great many children in that camp, and a great many animals, sheep and goats and cattle, all needing to drink.

The country round Rephidim, if it is the place the monks say, is rocky and steep. There are dry stream beds running down between the mountains, the kind of channels that roar with water for an hour after a storm and then lie empty for months, full of round grey stones. You can walk up one of those stream beds and see exactly where the water would go, if there were any. The stones are smooth from it. The sand is ribbed from it. Everything about the place says river. And there is no river.

The people had carried water with them from the last stopping place, of course. Everybody did. Skins on their shoulders, jars on the donkeys. But a water skin is not a well. It runs out. And they had been on the road by stages for some days now.

In Ziva's tent, they have one skin left. Then half a skin. Then the little bit at the bottom that you have to tip right up to get.

Ziva's mother has been very careful with it. She gives the little boy a drink first, and then Naamah next door, because Naamah is old, and old people dry out quickly, and then herself and Ziva and her husband, a mouthful each, and she ties the neck very tight afterwards, as if the water might try to escape.

On the second afternoon, Ziva's little brother says, I'm thirsty.

On the third afternoon, he does not say it. He just leans against his mother with his eyes half shut. And that frightens Ziva more than all the saying.

So she goes out and finds the pebble.

It is an old trick. Shepherds and desert travellers have used it for as long as anyone can remember. You put a small clean stone in your mouth and suck it, and it keeps your mouth from going quite so dry. It does not give you any water. But it helps you wait.

And waiting, as it turns out, is what this story is about.

The people did not wait.

I am trying to be fair to them, the way I tried last time. They were thirsty. Their children were thirsty. Their animals were lowing and bleating in the heat. If you or I had been there, I do not know that we would have done any better.

But the book uses a stronger word this time than it used for the manna. Last time it said they murmured. This time it says the people quarrelled with Moses. Or, as some translations put it, they found fault with him. The Hebrew word is the sort of word you would use for bringing a case against someone in a court. Not just grumbling under your breath. Standing up and pointing.

And they said, give us water, that we may drink.

That is a perfectly reasonable thing to want. But it is a slightly strange thing to say to Moses. Moses did not have any water. Moses was just as thirsty as they were. He was not a man with a secret well behind his tent. And I think, deep down, the people knew that. They were not really talking to Moses. They were talking past him, to somebody else.

Moses saw that straight away. Listen to his answer.

Why do you quarrel with me? he said. Why do you put the Lord to the test?

Put the Lord to the test. Hold on to that phrase too. We will come back to it.

But the people were very thirsty, the book says, and they murmured against Moses, and they said, why did you bring us up out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our cattle with thirst?

Does Moses think we can drink sand? he is saying. Is that his plan? First the bitter water, now no water at all. What does he think we are, lizards?

Is the Lord among us, he says, or not?

Ziva's father has been sitting very still. He did not join in last time, and he is not joining in now. But he is not stopping it either. He has the empty water skin across his knees. And Ziva can see from his face that he is asking the very same question, very quietly, inside.

And here is the strange thing, and I want to say it gently, because it is the kind of thing that is easy to see in other people and very hard to see in ourselves.

That very morning, like every morning, the ground round the camp had gone white with manna. And over their heads, all that day, the pillar of cloud had stood in the sky, the cloud that had led them out of Egypt and through the sea and all the way to this place.

And they were asking, is the Lord among us, or not?

It is a bit like a child sitting in the back of the car on a long journey, in the dark, with the heater on and a blanket round them and a sandwich in their hand, saying, does anybody even know where we are going? Naamah, sitting at the door of her tent, has been listening to the loud man for some time. She has her two sticks across her lap.

Well, she says, to nobody in particular, I don't know about him. But I know who sent breakfast.

Ziva hears her. And she almost laughs. And then she looks at her brother leaning against their mother with his eyes half shut, and she does not.

Now let us go to Moses.

The book tells us what Moses did, and I love him for it. He did not argue with the people. He did not make a speech. He did not wave his staff about and hope for the best.

Moses cried to the Lord.

That is what you do, when you are at the end of yourself. You cry to the Lord. And the prayer he prayed is in the book, and it is not a polished prayer. It is not the sort of prayer you would put on a card. It is the sort of prayer you pray in a kitchen at two o'clock in the morning, when the baby will not stop crying and you have tried everything.

What shall I do with this people? said Moses. A little more, and they will stone me.

I want to point out how honest that is. Moses was the greatest prophet of the old covenant. He had stood in front of Pharaoh. He had held up his hand over the sea. And here he is saying to God, plainly, I do not know what to do. They are so angry they are nearly ready to throw stones at me. Help.

That is a perfectly good prayer. In fact, I think it is one of the best kinds. It is true.

And notice, too, what Moses did not do. He did not try to make the water himself. A saint is never a wizard, and neither is a prophet. Moses had seen wonders in Egypt, but he knew perfectly well that he had never done a single one of them. They were all the Lord's. So when the water ran out, he did not reach for his staff. He reached for God.

And the Lord answered him.

Pass on before the people, the Lord said, taking with you some of the elders of Israel. And take in your hand the staff with which you struck the Nile, and go.

Behold, I will stand before you there on the rock at Horeb. And you shall strike the rock, and water shall come out of it, that the people may drink.

Let me say a few things about that.

First, the elders. The elders were the senior men of the tribes, the grandfathers and heads of families, the ones people looked to. Moses was told to take some of them with him. Not to do anything, but to see. They were to be witnesses. So that afterwards nobody could say Moses had found a spring by himself and was pretending. The old men would see it with their own eyes.

Second, the staff. The staff with which you struck the Nile. Do you remember what happened that time? Back in Egypt, at the very first of the signs, the staff was lifted over the river, and the water of the Nile turned to blood, and the fish died, and the Egyptians could not drink from it. Water made undrinkable. Now the same staff, in the same hand, is going to do the opposite. It will bring drinkable water out of a place where there was none at all. The staff that took water away from Egypt will give water to Israel. I do not think that is an accident. I think the Lord likes that sort of thing.

And third, and most of all. I will stand before you there on the rock.

The Lord does not say, go and hit a rock and I will see what I can do from up here. He says, I will be there first. I will be standing there, waiting for you, on the rock. When you get there, you will not be alone.

So the answer to the people's question, is the Lord among us or not, is given before they have even finished asking it. Not to them, yet. To Moses. Yes. He is among them. He is so much among them that he has gone on ahead.

* * *

Now there is a small puzzle here, and I would not be honest if I skipped it.

The people are camped at Rephidim. But the Lord says the rock is at Horeb. And Horeb, everywhere else in the book, is the name of the mountain of God, the mountain where Moses first saw the burning bush, the mountain that is also called Sinai, where the people have not arrived yet.

So how can the rock be at Horeb, if they are at Rephidim?

People have suggested several answers. Perhaps Rephidim was close to the mountain, near enough that the rock could be said to be at Horeb. Perhaps Horeb was the name of a whole region, a range of mountains, and not just one peak. Perhaps the people were already in sight of it. The book does not explain, and I am not going to pretend that I can. It is enough to say that the rock was somewhere up the valley from the camp, in the direction of the mountain of God. And that is where Moses went.

He went, the book says, in the sight of the elders of Israel.

In our telling, Ziva sees them go.

She is at the edge of the camp with her brother, because her brother has wandered off, holding his pebble, and she has gone to get him. And she sees a little group of men walking away from the tents, up the dry stream bed, into the hills. Old men, mostly, with grey beards and walking sticks of their own. And in front of them, a little ahead, as the Lord said, an old man with a staff.

Ziva knows who that is. Everybody knows. She has seen him at the sea, with his hand held out. She has heard him say, it is the bread which the Lord has given you to eat.

Where are they going? says her brother, round the pebble.

I don't know, says Ziva.

There is a low ridge of rock at the edge of the camp, and she lifts her brother up on to it, and climbs up after him, and the two of them sit on top of it in the heat and watch the little group of men get smaller and smaller up the valley. Other people have noticed too. A few other children. A few women. Somebody's grandfather. They stand about at the edge of the camp, shading their eyes. The loud man has stopped talking.

I want to be clear that the book does not say any of this. It does not say who watched from the camp, or what they saw from there. It says the elders saw, and that is what mattered. But I like to think that Ziva was on that ridge. I think somebody probably was.

The men stop, a long way off, by a great rock at the side of the valley. It is hard to see from this distance. It is just a grey shape against the red mountainside.

And the old man with the staff lifts it.

And brings it down.

* * *

The Book of Exodus tells it in the plainest possible way. And Moses did so, in the sight of the elders of Israel. That is all. The Lord said strike the rock and water will come out, and Moses did so. The book does not stop to describe it. It trusts us to understand.

But the people never forgot it. And many hundreds of years later, when they sang about it in the psalms, they did describe it. They sang it over and over. He split rocks in the wilderness, says one psalm, and gave them drink abundantly, as from the deep. He made streams come out of the rock, and caused waters to flow down like rivers. And another, he opened the rock, and water gushed out. It flowed through the desert like a river. And the prophet Isaiah, much later still, remembered it too. They did not thirst when he led them through the deserts. He made water flow for them from the rock. He split the rock, and the water gushed out.

So Scripture itself, looking back, tells us that it was not a trickle. It was not a little damp patch that you had to dig for. It gushed. It flowed like a river through the dry land.

So in our telling, this is what Ziva sees from the ridge.

First, nothing. The old man has struck the rock, and nothing seems to happen, and for one long breath the whole valley is completely still.

Then a glint. Something catching the sun, at the foot of the grey rock. Then a sound, very faint and far away, that Ziva does not recognise for a moment, because she has not heard it for days. It is a sort of rushing, chuckling sound.

And then the stream bed, the dry stream bed full of smooth grey stones that she has been looking at for three days, the one that said river all over it and had no river in it, begins, very slowly, to darken. A dark line, creeping down the middle of the valley towards the camp. Wet stones. Then a gleam on the wet stones. Then a ripple. Then a little brown tongue of water, pushing sand and twigs ahead of it, feeling its way down between the boulders, like a puppy coming to find you.

Her little brother takes the pebble out of his mouth.

Then he drops it.

Then he says, very quietly, water.

And from all along the edge of the camp, from the women shading their eyes and the children and the grandfather and the loud man, there is a sound that is not a quarrel, and not a murmur, and not a shout either. It is a sort of gasp, and then a sort of laugh, and then everybody is running.

* * *

I will not try to tell you everything that happened at Rephidim that evening, because the book does not, and because I think you can imagine it better than I can describe it.

But I will tell you a few things, in our telling.

Ziva carries her little brother down from the ridge, and he does not want to be carried, he wants to run, and she will not let him, because the stones are slippery now. And by the time they get to the stream, it is not a little tongue of water any more. It is a proper stream, running fast and clear between the boulders, cold, much colder than you would think, because it has come out of the heart of a mountain. And the little boy kneels down at the edge and puts his whole face in it.

All along the stream bed, people are drinking. They are drinking with their hands, with cups, with their faces in it like the little boy. The animals have smelt it and are coming down from the camp in a great bleating, lowing tide, and the shepherds are running beside them shouting, not too fast, not too fast, let them drink slowly, and nobody is listening.

Ziva's mother arrives with every jar and skin and bowl she can carry, and her husband behind her with more. And Ziva's father kneels down at the stream with the empty goatskin, the one that hung from his hand like a sock, and holds its neck open in the water, and watches it fill. It gets fat and round and heavy. It sloshes. He holds it for a long time after it is full, just feeling the weight of it.

Then he says, quietly, to nobody, he is among us.

Ziva hears him. She does not say anything. But she remembers.

And then, because she is Ziva, she fills a skin for Naamah, and carries it all the way back up to Naamah's tent, which is not easy, because water is heavy, and a full goatskin is about as easy to carry as a large, unhappy dog.

Naamah is sitting where she was, at the door of her tent, with her two sticks. She could not go down to the stream. It is too far and too steep and too slippery for her. She has been sitting there listening to the sound of running water and laughing people coming up from the valley, and waiting.

Ziva kneels down and holds the skin for her, and Naamah drinks, a long, long drink, with her eyes shut.

When she has finished, she wipes her mouth, and looks at Ziva, and says, you are a good girl. And then, after a moment, the water came out of a rock, did it?

It did, says Ziva. I saw it. From the ridge.

Naamah nods slowly.

Well, she says. When I was a girl, in Egypt, we used to say that you could not get water out of a stone. It meant something was impossible. You'll get money out of him when you get water out of a stone, we used to say.

She smiles her slow crinkly smile.

I shall have to stop saying that, she says.

Late that night, the loud man three tents along comes to the door of Ziva's tent.

He is not loud now. He has a jar of water in his hands, which is a slightly odd present, since the whole camp now has more water than it knows what to do with. But I think he wanted to bring something, and it was what he had.

He stands at the door and does not quite know what to say. Ziva's father gets up and goes out to him.

I said some things today, says the man.

You did, says Ziva's father.

I said, is the Lord among us. He looks down at the jar. I said it a lot.

Ziva's father is quiet for a moment. Then he says, I didn't say it. But I thought it. All afternoon. Sitting right there. He nods at the place by the tent door where he sat with the empty skin. I thought it just as loud as you said it.

The man looks at him.

And then Ziva's father takes the jar of water, which they do not need, and says thank you, which is the right thing to say, and the two men stand at the door of the tent in the dark for a while, listening to the stream running down the valley, and do not say anything else at all.

* * *

And Moses, the book says, called the name of the place Massah and Meribah.

Massah means testing. And Meribah means quarrelling. Because of the quarrelling of the people of Israel, the book says, and because they put the Lord to the test, by saying, is the Lord among us, or not?

It seems a bit of a shame, really. Such a wonderful thing happened there. Water out of a rock, in a desert, for a whole thirsty people. And Moses did not call the place Fountain, or Gift, or Rock of Mercy. He called it Testing and Quarrelling. He named it after what they did, not after what God did.

But I think there was a reason for that. Places in the Bible are often named so that people will remember something. And Moses wanted them to remember this. Not just the water. The question.

Do you remember, last time, when the Lord gave the manna, he said, that I may test them? God was testing the people. And I said that it was not a trick and not a trap. It was the kind of test a parent gives a child when they hand them a little responsibility. Here is enough for today. Do you trust me for tomorrow?

Now, at Massah, it is the other way round. The people are testing God.

And those are two very different things.

When God tests us, it is to help us grow, the way you test a young tree by letting the wind blow on it, so that its roots go down deep. When we test God, it is something else. It is saying, prove it. Prove you are there. Do it now, the way I want, or I will not believe you. It is the child in the back of the car saying, if you really knew where we were going, we would be there by now.

Many years later, when Moses was very old and the people were about to go into the promised land, he said to them, in the Book of Deuteronomy, you shall not put the Lord your God to the test, as you tested him at Massah. And those words were remembered too.

Because once again, a very long time afterwards, a man was in the wilderness, fasting, for forty days. And the tempter took him up to Jerusalem, to the very highest point of the temple, and said, if you are the Son of God, throw yourself down. For it is written, he will give his angels charge of you, and they will bear you up. Go on. Prove it. Make God catch you.

And Jesus answered him with Moses' words, about Massah. It is written again, you shall not put the Lord your God to the test.

So the name of that dry place in the wilderness turns up on the lips of Our Lord himself. Massah. Testing. The place where the people said, is the Lord among us or not, and he was standing on the rock all along.

* * *

There is one more place where Massah and Meribah are remembered, and you may very well have heard it, without knowing.

It is a psalm. Psalm ninety-five. And it begins like this. O come, let us sing to the Lord. Let us make a joyful noise to the rock of our salvation.

The rock of our salvation. You can hear Rephidim in that, I think. The rock that the water came out of.

And then, further on, the psalm turns. O that today you would listen to his voice, it says. Harden not your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness. And then God himself speaks in it. When your fathers tested me, he says, and put me to the proof, though they had seen my work.

Though they had seen my work. The manna on the ground every morning. The cloud overhead. They had seen it, and they still asked.

Now here is the thing I want you to know about that psalm. In the Church's daily prayer, in the Catholic Church, Psalm ninety-five is very often the first psalm of the whole day. Monks and nuns and priests, and a great many ordinary people with prayer books, open their mouths in the morning and the first thing they pray is this psalm. O come, let us sing to the Lord. Today, if you hear his voice, harden not your hearts, as at Meribah.

Every morning. Before anything else. A reminder of a thirsty afternoon at Rephidim, a very long time ago.

And the Letter to the Hebrews, in the New Testament, takes up the same psalm and leans hard on one word in it. Today. Today, when you hear his voice. Not tomorrow, not when things are easier, not when the water skin is full. Today. Which is exactly what the manna was trying to teach them as well. Just today. Trust him for today.

And Christians have always seen something more in the rock.

Saint Paul, writing to the Christians at Corinth, looked back at the whole long journey through the wilderness, the cloud and the sea and the manna and the water, and said something remarkable about it. Our fathers, he said, all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank from the spiritual rock which followed them. And the rock was Christ.

The rock which followed them. There is an old Jewish tradition, written down by the rabbis long afterwards, that the water from the rock did not stay at Rephidim, but went with the people through the wilderness, all forty years, like a well that travelled. We met that tradition once before, in the episode about the other rock, at Kadesh, at the end of the forty years. It is a tradition, not something the Book of Exodus says. But Saint Paul seems to have known some form of it, and he turns it into something new. The rock that went with them, he says, was Christ. It was Christ who was with them all along.

Is the Lord among us or not? Yes. He was the rock.

The early Christian writer Tertullian, in North Africa, writing about baptism around the year two hundred, gathered up all the old stories of water in the Bible, and he counted this one among them. The water that flowed out of the rock which went with the people, he says, and then, if Christ is the rock, we see baptism blessed by the water in Christ.

And there is one more picture, which Christians have loved for a very long time. On Good Friday, on the cross, after Jesus had died, a soldier pierced his side with a spear. And at once, the Gospel of John says, there came out blood and water.

A rock struck, and water coming out of it, for a thirsty people. Many Christian writers, through the centuries, have looked at the pierced side of Christ and thought of Rephidim. I do not think it is too much to say that they were right to.

Jesus said it himself, in a way. On the last great day of a feast in Jerusalem, he stood up in the temple and cried out, if anyone thirst, let him come to me and drink.

So the people drank at Rephidim. And they stayed there, for a while.

And I should tell you, because you might be worrying, that the story does not end with everyone being good forever. It is not that kind of book, and they are not that kind of people. They are our kind of people. Almost straight away, at that very same place, at Rephidim, there was trouble of a different sort, when the people of Amalek came and attacked them. That is a story we have told before, about old Moses on the hill with his arms held up by his brother Aaron and his friend Hur, until the sun went down. And forty years later, at the very end of the wandering, at a place called Kadesh, the water ran out again, and there was another rock, and another quarrel, and Moses, old and tired, struck it twice. That is a story we have told before too. And in our telling of it, an old woman called Ziva was there, the girl who danced at the sea, grown up and grown old.

But that is all a long way off.

Tonight is just the first night at Rephidim. The first night with water.

In Ziva's tent, everybody is asleep. The full goatskins are hanging from the tent pole, fat and round, and every now and then one of them sways a little and sloshes. Ziva's father is asleep with his hand resting on one of them, as if he wants to be sure it is still there. Her mother is asleep beside him. Next door, Naamah is asleep with her two sticks laid neatly beside her, and a cup of water within reach.

And her little brother is asleep with his blanket pulled up to his chin. He went back to the ridge before bed, to look for his pebble. He could not find it. Ziva told him not to worry. He does not need it any more.

And Ziva is lying awake, a little longer than everybody else, listening.

Because there is a sound in the valley that was not there last night. A low, soft, endless sound, rushing and chuckling between the stones in the dark. Water, running down from the rock, past the sleeping tents and the sleeping animals and the old men who saw it happen.

And over the camp, the pillar of fire is shining, as it has shone every night since Egypt.

And somewhere, very quietly, the dew is beginning to fall. And in the morning, there will be manna.

Now, a few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Book of Exodus, chapter seventeen, verses one to seven, told straight, in a plain rendering of the old words. The stages of the road, Dophkah and Alush, come from the list in the Book of Numbers, chapter thirty-three. Where Rephidim was is not known for certain; the long valley in the south of Sinai that monks and pilgrims have pointed to for centuries is the traditional place, and it may be right, but nobody can prove it. Why the rock is said to be at Horeb, when the people were camped at Rephidim, is a real puzzle, and the book does not settle it.

What Moses said, what the people said, what the Lord said, the elders as witnesses, the staff that struck the Nile, and the two names, Massah and Meribah, are all in the book. The water gushing and flowing like a river comes from the psalms and the prophet Isaiah, looking back. The tradition of a well that followed the people is a later Jewish tradition, not something the Book of Exodus says, and the reading of the rock as Christ is Saint Paul's, in his First Letter to the Corinthians, and Tertullian's, and that of many Christian writers after them.

Ziva, her mother and father and little brother, old Naamah, and the man with the loud voice are our own invention. So is the pebble, the ridge, the water coming down the valley as the camp saw it, and every word they say to one another. We have tried not to add anything to what Moses, the elders, the Lord or the people do in the story. If you would like to read it for yourself, it is only seven verses long, and it is right after the manna.

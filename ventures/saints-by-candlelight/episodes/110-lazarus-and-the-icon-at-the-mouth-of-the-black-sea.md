There is a small stone church on a hill above the sea, and the wind is coming off the water, and inside, a monk is sitting on a stool in front of a board of wood.

His hands are wrapped in clean linen, from the wrist to the knuckles. Only the tips of his fingers show. Between two of those fingertips he is holding a brush, very carefully, the way you might hold a moth you were trying to carry outside without hurting it.

Beside him, sitting on the floor with his knees up, is a boy of about eleven, who smells strongly of fish. The boy has a little dish of something yellow in his lap, and he is guarding it with his life.

On the board, there is a face. It is not finished. It is mostly brown, and a bit of green, and there are two dark eyes in it, looking out. They are looking straight at the boy, and the boy is pretending not to notice.

"Is he cross?" the boy whispers at last.

"Who?"

"Him. The man in the picture. He looks cross."

The monk considers the face for a while.

"He is not cross," he says. "He is paying attention. It looks the same, on some people."

If you have been listening for the last few weeks, you will know where we are. We are in Constantinople, the great city on the Bosporus, the narrow strip of sea that runs between Europe and Asia. And we are in the middle of a long, strange quarrel about pictures.

For about a hundred years, on and off, the emperors in Constantinople had decided that holy pictures were a bad idea. Pictures of Christ, of His Mother, of the saints, painted on wood or set in gold mosaic on church walls. Icons, they are called, which is only the Greek word for an image. Some of the emperors thought that people were worshipping the wood and the paint. And so the icons were taken down, and painted over, and in some places smashed.

The Church, at a great council in the city of Nicaea, in seven hundred and eighty-seven, had answered all this. It said, more or less: we do not worship wood. We honour the person in the picture, the way you might kiss a photograph of your grandmother, not because you love the paper, but because you love her. And because Christ really did become a man, with a real face, it is right to paint that face. That is still what Catholics and Orthodox both believe, and it was decided at a council they both accept.

But then the emperors changed their minds again, in eight hundred and fifteen, and the icons came down a second time. We met Saint Theodore the Studite then, carrying icons round his monastery garden on Palm Sunday. And the last of those emperors, the last one who was against the icons, was a man called Theophilos.

Theophilos is a puzzle. He was clever, and well read, and he cared about justice. The story goes that he rode through the city once a week, and anybody at all could stop his horse and complain to him, and that he punished his own officials when they cheated the poor. He loved beautiful things. In his throne room, the old accounts say, and Theophilos usually gets the credit for it, there was a tree made of gold, with golden birds on its branches, and when the machinery under the floor was worked, the birds sang. Golden lions beside the throne, too, that could roar and swish their tails. Visitors from other countries went home and told everyone, and I think they were a bit frightened, which was rather the idea.

So here is the puzzle. A man who loves beautiful things, and who builds singing golden birds, and who sets himself against painters.

I don't think we will ever really understand Theophilos. People are complicated. Emperors more than most, because nobody ever tells them no.

Now. Before we meet our painter, I want to tell you how an icon is made. Because it is not like painting a fence, and it is not even quite like painting a picture to hang over the sofa.

You start with a board. A good flat piece of wood, well seasoned, so that it will not warp. Usually you carve the middle of it down a little, so that there is a raised edge all the way round, like a tray. Then you glue linen over the wood, and over that you put a white coating, made of fine chalk mixed with animal glue, many thin layers of it, each one dried and smoothed, until the surface is as white and hard and smooth as an eggshell. That alone can take days.

Then you draw your outline. Then, if you are lucky and somebody can afford it, gold leaf for the background, so thin that if you breathe on it, it floats away. Painters have been known to hold their breath for a very long time.

And then the paint. Now here is the strange part. The paint is made with eggs. You take an egg, and separate it, and keep the yolk. You hold the yolk in your fingers, very gently, and pierce its little skin, and let the yellow run out into a dish, and throw the skin away. Then you mix a little water with it, and that is your glue. You grind your colours, which are earths and rocks and minerals, into powder, and mix the powder with the yolk, a little at a time. And that is your paint. It dries quickly and it lasts, quite literally, for a thousand years.

And the painting goes, as the old painters do it, from dark to light. You start with the dark colours, the browns and the olive greens, and then lay lighter and lighter layers on top of them, each one a little smaller than the last. So that the face seems to come up out of the dark, towards you. As if the light were inside it.

Remember the eggs. They are going to be important.

* * *

We do not know very much about where Lazarus came from. The old books disagree, and I will not pretend to know better than they do. What we do know is that he was in Constantinople, and that he was a monk, and that he was a painter. A very good one. His name, in the Greek calendar of the saints, has the word Zographos after it, which simply means the painter. Of all the painters in the city, he was the one people called the painter.

Lazarus is a good name for a monk, if you think about it. There are two famous Lazaruses in the Gospels. One is the poor man in Our Lord's story, who lay at the rich man's gate. The other is the friend of Jesus at Bethany, who was dead four days and came out of the tomb when he was called, which we told some weeks ago. A name about coming back out of the dark. You will see in a moment why I mention it.

Let us imagine Lazarus as a young man, in his workshop in the monastery, with his boards and his dishes of colour, and a window for the light. Painters always want a window. They will fight you for it.

And let us imagine that people came to him, quietly, because by now it was a quiet trade. A widow, wanting a small picture of the Mother of God to keep at home. A ship's captain, wanting Saint Nicholas, because Saint Nicholas is good with ships. A family whose old icon had been broken, wanting a new one, exactly like the old one, please, exactly, the nose was a little bit to the left.

"I'll do my best," Lazarus would say. "But I can't paint your grandmother's icon. I can only paint Saint Nicholas again."

"Is that the same?"

"It's better. He doesn't change. Only the painters change."

And word got around, as word does. There was a painter at such-and-such a monastery who would not stop.

* * *

Sooner or later, in a city like that, someone tells the emperor.

The chronicle that tells us about Lazarus was written in Constantinople, about a hundred years after he lived, by writers who did not like Theophilos one bit, and who were glad to tell his worst stories. So we should hold it a little loosely. But it does tell the story plainly, and it is the story the Church has remembered on his feast day ever since.

The emperor sent for him. Theophilos, it seems, did not begin with threats. He began with talk. He was a clever man, and he was used to winning arguments, and I imagine the conversation went something like this.

"You are a sensible man," said the emperor. "You can see what this leads to. People kneeling to bits of wood. People kissing paint. Wouldn't God rather be worshipped in spirit and in truth?"

"He would," said Lazarus. "So would I. I don't worship the wood, your majesty. I'm a painter. I know exactly what the wood is. I bought it."

"Then why paint faces on it?"

"Because He had one."

And I suppose there was a long silence in the throne room, under the golden tree.

The chronicle says that, when talking did not work, the emperor had him punished, and hurt badly, and put in prison, and that for a while people thought he would not live.

That is all I am going to say about that part. It is enough to know it was hard, and that he was brave. You can sleep without the rest of it.

But here is the thing that the chronicle says next, and it is the thing I love. Lazarus got better. Slowly. In prison. And when he was well enough to sit up, he asked for, or found, or was smuggled, a board and some colours. And he started painting icons again. In the prison.

I do not know where he got the eggs. I would like to think a guard brought them, in his lunch, and looked the other way.

The emperor heard. Of course he heard. And the chronicle says that this time he gave orders that the painter's hands should be burned, so that he could never hold a brush again. And it was done.

Let us go gently here. There are some people, in some stories, who are told no and simply say it louder. The emperor was one of those. And there are some people who are hurt, and simply wait, and keep loving the thing they loved. Lazarus was one of those.

* * *

You may remember the empress Theodora, from episode one hundred and one. She was Theophilos's wife. And she had grown up in a family that kept icons, quietly, at home. The old story says her mother kept them in a chest and took them out for the children to kiss, and that she kept some herself, in her own rooms in the palace, though she had to be careful.

The chronicle says that it was Theodora, along with some of the emperor's friends, who went to him and pleaded for the painter. Enough, they said. He is a monk. He is half dead. Let him go.

And Theophilos, who was not a monster, only a man who had dug himself into a hole and kept on digging, let him go.

I wonder sometimes what was said in that room. I think Theodora was cleverer than people gave her credit for. I think she did not argue about icons with her husband at all. I think she said something like: you are an emperor who rides through the city every week so that the poor can complain to you. What would they say about this?

But that is me wondering. The chronicle only says she pleaded, and that it worked.

So Lazarus was set free. And he did the sensible thing, which was to get out of the city, as far as he could without actually leaving. He went north, up the Bosporus, along the Asian shore, to a place where the narrow sea opens out at last into the wide grey water of the Black Sea. There was a small monastery there, at a place called Phoberos, and in it a church of Saint John the Baptist. In the East they call him Saint John the Forerunner, because he ran on ahead of Christ to tell everybody to get ready.

And there, in the church of the Forerunner, Lazarus hid.

* * *

Now from here on, I am going to tell you some of it from the point of view of somebody who is not in the chronicle at all. We have made him up. His name is Andreas, and he is eleven, and his mother sells fish.

Every morning, Andreas walks up the hill from the little harbour with a basket of fish for the monastery kitchen. And every morning, the cook at the monastery, who is a large, kind, permanently out-of-breath monk, takes the basket, looks inside it, and says, "Is that all?"

"It's what the sea had," says Andreas.

"The sea is very mean to monks," says the cook.

This is their ritual. They have been doing it for about a year.

But one morning in the spring, something is different. There is a new monk. He is sitting on a bench in the sun outside the church, with his back against the warm wall, and his eyes shut. He is thin. And his hands are lying in his lap, both of them wrapped in clean white linen.

"Who's that?" Andreas asks the cook.

The cook looks around, as if the gulls might be listening. "That," he says, "is nobody. A brother who's come to stay with us for a bit. For his health. You haven't seen him."

"But he's right there."

"Then you've seen nobody. Off you go."

Of course, the next morning, Andreas comes up the hill early and sits down at the far end of the bench. He doesn't say anything. Neither does the monk. The gulls say a great deal, but gulls always do.

After about a week of this, the monk opens one eye.

"You're the fish boy," he says.

"Andreas."

"Lazarus."

"Like the man who was dead?"

"Like him, yes. Or the one at the rich man's gate. Either will do."

"Were you dead?"

Lazarus thinks about this, with his eyes shut.

"Not quite," he says. "But close enough to see the door."

Andreas looks at the hands. He is eleven, so he asks.

"What happened to your hands?"

"Somebody didn't like my work."

"What do you do?"

"I'm a painter."

Andreas considers the hands, and the linen, and the painter.

"That's not very good for a painter," he says.

"No," says Lazarus. And for the first time, he smiles. "No, it isn't, is it."

* * *

It took a long time for the hands to heal. The chronicle does not tell us how long, and I am not going to guess. Burns heal slowly. They heal from the edges inwards, a little at a time, and they itch, and they pull, and they don't like being used.

But the monastery had a garden, and herbs, and an old brother who knew about poultices, and the sea air, and every day the monk sat in the sun with his hands in his lap, and every day they were a tiny bit better.

And one day, he came out of the church with a board under his arm.

It was a plain board. Somebody had found it for him, a good piece of seasoned wood, and Lazarus had spent many days, slowly and clumsily, with his hands still wrapped, putting on the linen and the white chalk layers, one at a time, sanding each one smooth with a piece of pumice stone, until it was as white as an eggshell. It had taken him a long time, because he had to stop and rest.

"What's that?" said Andreas.

"That," said Lazarus, "is going to be Saint John the Forerunner. For his church. The old picture of him here was taken down years ago, and they've been looking at a blank wall ever since."

"Are you allowed?"

"No," said Lazarus. "Not yet."

"Then why are you doing it?"

Lazarus looked out at the sea for a while.

"Because the Forerunner," he said, "was a man who got ready for something before it came. That was his whole job. He stood in the river and told everyone that it was coming, before anybody could see it. So I thought I'd get ready too."

"Get ready for what?"

"For the day they can put him back up."

Andreas thought about this. "How do you know that day's coming?"

"I don't," said Lazarus. "That's the point of getting ready."

And then he said: "Can you grind colours?"

"I don't know," said Andreas. "What are colours?"

* * *

So began the apprenticeship of Andreas the fish boy, which I am sorry to say did not get off to a good start, because of the eggs.

The trouble was that the eggs belonged to the cook.

"Absolutely not," said the cook. "Do you know how many eggs I get in a week? Do you know what I am meant to feed thirty monks on? Fish, which the sea is mean about, and lentils, and eggs. And you want to put them on a board."

"Only the yolks," said Lazarus.

"Only the yolks," said the cook, to the ceiling. "Only the best part."

"You can have the whites."

"What am I supposed to do with thirty egg whites?"

Here, I am afraid, there is a gap in the historical record. Nobody wrote down what the cook did with thirty egg whites. I like to think that some time in the ninth century, at the mouth of the Black Sea, a large, kind, permanently out-of-breath monk invented something very much like meringue, out of spite, and never told anybody. But I cannot prove it.

In the end, they made a deal. One egg a day. Andreas would bring it up from the kitchen every morning, in both hands, and guard it from the gulls on the way, because there was one particular gull, a big grey one with a bad leg, who had worked out what the egg was for and wanted it very much.

"He's a terrible gull," said Andreas. "He tried to take it off my head yesterday. I wasn't even carrying it on my head."

"He's practising," said Lazarus.

* * *

And so they worked. Through the spring, and into the summer, in a corner of the little church where the light came in from the east in the morning.

Andreas learned to grind. You take a lump of earth or rock, and you crush it, and then you put it on a flat stone with a drop of water, and you grind it with a smaller stone, round and round and round and round, until your arm aches and the powder is as fine as flour. Finer. So fine that if you rub it between your fingers, you cannot feel it at all.

There was a dull yellow earth, called ochre, which you can dig out of the ground in a great many places, and a red one, and a brown one, and a dark green one that Lazarus said was for the shadows of faces.

"Faces aren't green," said Andreas.

"Underneath, they are a bit," said Lazarus. "Look at your mother in the evening, by the fire. Look under her chin."

Andreas went home and looked under his mother's chin, by the fire, and his mother asked him what on earth he was doing, and he said he was studying, and she gave him a look that mothers have been giving sons since long before the ninth century.

There was a black, made from burnt things, and a white, and for special occasions there were more precious colours, but this was a small monastery at the edge of the sea, and Lazarus said the Forerunner had lived on locusts and wild honey, and would not mind being painted in plain earth.

The painting itself went slowly. Lazarus could not hold the brush the way he used to. At first he could only hold it between two fingertips, and his hand shook, and he had to stop every little while and put both hands flat on his knees and wait. But he was patient. And the thing about icon painting, the way it is done in thin layers one on top of another, is that it suits patience. You do not have to be quick. You do not even have to be strong. You only have to keep going, a little bit at a time, from dark to light.

First the dark: the browns and greens of the face and the robe, the deep colour of the hair, which on the Forerunner is always a great wild mane, because he lived in the desert and did not have time for barbers.

"He looks like my uncle," said Andreas. "My uncle lives on a boat."

"He'd have got on well with your uncle," said Lazarus.

Then the lights. Ochre with a little white, laid on the forehead and the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose. Then a little lighter, in a slightly smaller patch. Then lighter again, smaller again. Each layer had to dry before the next. And slowly, very slowly, the face came up out of the dark board towards them. Day by day. As if somebody were walking towards them out of a dark room, into the light.

Andreas would come up the hill every morning, with the fish for the cook and the egg for the painter, and before he did anything else he would go into the church and look, to see how far the Forerunner had come.

That is the morning in the little scene I began with. The face mostly brown, a bit of green, the two dark eyes.

"Is he cross?"

"He is not cross. He is paying attention. It looks the same, on some people."

"What is he paying attention to?"

Lazarus put the brush down carefully.

"To Christ," he said. "That's what the Forerunner always does. In the old icons, he's often turned to one side, with his hands held out, like this, towards someone you can't see. He's saying, not me. Him. Look over there."

He sat back on his stool, slowly, and looked at the board.

"That's what all the icons are for, really," he said. "That's what the emperor doesn't understand. Nobody who loves icons is looking at the wood. The wood is pointing. Like the Forerunner. Look over there."

Andreas looked over there. There was nothing over there except the wall of the church, and the gull, which had got in through the door again.

"Out," said Lazarus, to the gull, in a voice that was not at all like the voice he used for theology. And the gull went.

* * *

The Forerunner was finished at the end of the summer. The chronicle does not say that, of course. I have put it at the end of the summer because it is a good time for finishing things.

It was not hung up on the church wall. It could not be, not yet. It was wrapped in a cloth and kept somewhere safe, and the monks, I imagine, took it out now and then, on feast days, with the door shut, and lit a lamp in front of it, and sang.

The chronicle says one thing more about this icon. It says that it was still there, at Phoberos, in the chronicler's own day, about a hundred years later, and that many people who prayed in front of it were healed.

Let me be careful about that, as we always try to be. An icon is not a magic object. Wood and egg and earth do not heal anybody. But God hears people when they pray, and sometimes, in His goodness, He heals them, and over the centuries, people have often found that it was in front of a particular image, in a particular church, that they were heard. The healing is God's. The icon only points. Look over there.

And it seems right to me that the icon that people remembered from all of Lazarus's work was this one. The one painted slowly, by a man who could barely hold the brush, in a church at the edge of the sea, at a time when it was not allowed. Getting ready.

* * *

And the day came.

In January of eight hundred and forty-two, the emperor Theophilos died. He was still quite young, not even thirty, and he had been ill. He left his wife Theodora to rule for their little son Michael, who was two.

We told that part of the story in episode one hundred and one. How Theodora, and the old patriarch Methodios, who had spent nine years in a prison on an island, gathered the bishops and monks together, and how on the first Sunday of Lent in eight hundred and forty-three, the icons came back into the churches of Constantinople, carried through the streets at night with candles, to Hagia Sophia. The Orthodox still keep that day every year, and so do the Byzantine Catholics, as the Sunday of Orthodoxy.

I don't know whether Lazarus was in that procession. The chronicle doesn't say. But somewhere, that spring, at the mouth of the Black Sea, a small monastery took a cloth off a board and hung up an icon of Saint John the Baptist on the wall of his own church, and lit a lamp in front of it, for everybody to see.

And I like to think that a fisher boy, a bit older now, a bit taller, stood at the back of the church with his mother, smelling faintly of fish, and pointed.

"I ground that brown," he said. "The bit on his hair."

"Hush," said his mother. But she looked.

* * *

There is one more story the chronicle tells, and it is a hard and a good one.

Some time after her husband died, Theodora went to see Lazarus. She had a favour to ask him. A big one.

She asked him to forgive her husband.

You can see why she asked. She had loved Theophilos. He was the father of her children. He had done a great wrong to a great many people, and to this man in particular, and he had died before putting it right. And she was afraid for him, and she wanted to hear someone say that it would be all right. That the painter, of all people, would pray for him.

The chronicle gives Lazarus's answer, and I am going to give it in my own plain words, not the chronicler's. It runs something like this.

"God is not unjust. He will not forget what we loved, and what we worked for, and He does not weigh one man's anger more heavily than all of that."

[pause]

That is a hard sentence to understand, and people have read it in more than one way, and I am not going to tell you which way is right. Some hear it as stern, as if he were saying: the emperor will have to answer for what he did. Some hear it as gentle, as if he were saying: do not be afraid, the last word is not the emperor's anger, the last word is God's, and God is good.

I think it might be both. That is what justice and mercy usually turn out to be, when you look at them closely. Both.

But I notice one more thing. He did not say no. He did not say, never. He did not curse the man. He left Theophilos in the hands of God, which is the best place anybody can be left.

And the Church has gone on praying for the dead ever since, emperors included, because none of us can see the whole story, and only God can. And if a man who had his hands burned can leave his emperor in the hands of God, so can the rest of us, I think, with the people who have hurt us. It may take a long time. It took Lazarus a long time. But it can be done.

* * *

Now here is the last part of the story, and it is the part I like best, because it is the most surprising.

A few years later, the little emperor Michael was grown up, or nearly. And the court needed to send an embassy to the pope in Rome, Pope Benedict the Third. That is a long journey. A ship across the sea, or down along the coast of Greece, round the bottom of Italy, up to Rome. Weeks and weeks.

And who did they send? A monk. A painter. A man named Lazarus.

We know this, not from the Greek chronicle, but from a completely different book, written in Rome: the Book of the Popes, the old record of the lives of the bishops of Rome. In the life of Benedict the Third, it says that the emperor Michael sent gifts to the pope, and that they were brought by a monk called Lazarus, who was highly skilled in the art of painting. Most scholars think he is our Lazarus. I think so too, though I cannot prove it, and you should know that.

I find that wonderful. A painter, whom one emperor tried to stop from painting, sent by the next emperor across half the world, to carry gifts to Rome. With the same hands. As if the whole empire were saying: we were wrong. We are sorry. Here is our best painter. Look at his hands.

I imagine him standing on the deck of a ship, sailing out of the Bosporus, past the mouth of the Black Sea where he had hidden. Holding the ship's rail with both hands. Hands that worked now, not perfectly, but well enough. Scarred, and stiff in the cold, and good enough to paint with. Good enough to hold on with.

And I imagine him getting to Rome, and walking into the old church of Saint Peter, which was full of pictures. Mosaics on the walls, and paintings, and lamps burning in front of them. Nobody had ever taken those down. And I think he must have stood there for a long time, looking up, with his scarred hands folded, the way painters do when they come into a church. They look at the pictures first, before anything else. They can't help it.

[pause]

Lazarus went west again, some years later, on another embassy. He was old by now. And somewhere on that journey, around eight hundred and sixty-five, or a little later, the sources are not quite sure, he died. He went home to God, as quietly as he had lived.

The old calendar of Constantinople says his body was brought back to the city, and that he was buried there, in a monastery in the city he had painted for.

[pause]

The Orthodox Church remembers him on the seventeenth of November, as Saint Lazarus the icon-painter. In the Catholic Church, the Roman Martyrology remembers him on the twenty-third of February, a monk of Constantinople who painted holy images, and suffered for it, and kept on painting. And icon-painters, as you might expect, are very fond of him.

The little monastery at Phoberos, at the mouth of the Black Sea, is long gone. The Bosporus is still there, of course. It is a very busy bit of water now, full of enormous ships, and bridges, and ferries going back and forth all day between Europe and Asia. But up at the northern end, where it opens out into the Black Sea, it is still quieter, and still windy, and there are still gulls. A great many gulls. Some of them, I am sorry to say, still have their eye on your lunch.

The icon of the Forerunner is gone too. We do not know what happened to it. Icons wear out, in the end, like everything else that is loved and handled and kissed for centuries.

But here is something to think about, as you go to sleep.

All over the world tonight, in a great many small rooms, there are people making icons the way Lazarus did. Boards and linen. Layers of white chalk, sanded smooth. A yolk held carefully in the fingers, and pierced, and run into a dish. Colours ground round and round on a stone until they are finer than flour. A face coming up out of the dark, a layer at a time, towards the light. The same way. More than a thousand years later. Nobody stopped it, in the end. Nobody ever really could.

[pause]

Andreas, of course, is our own invention. But if there had been an Andreas, I like to think that he did not become a painter. I like to think he became a fisherman, like his mother, and his uncle on the boat. But that he always looked under people's chins, by the fire, and saw the green.

And that when he was old, and had grandchildren, he would take them up the hill to the little church on a feast day. And they would stand in front of the Forerunner, with his wild hair and his dark eyes, pointing over there.

"Is he cross?" the smallest one would whisper.

"No," Andreas would say. "He's paying attention."

"To what?"

And Andreas would point, the way he had learned. "Look over there."

[pause]

So if tonight you are waiting for something good that has not come yet, and you are not sure it ever will, you can rest. You do not have to make it come. You only have to get ready, a little at a time, the way the Forerunner did, the way Lazarus did, from dark to light. One thin layer, and then let it dry. Then another, tomorrow.

And the face comes up out of the dark. It does not come quickly. But it comes.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source tonight is the chronicle known as Theophanes Continuatus, written in Constantinople in the tenth century, which tells the story of Lazarus in its book on the emperor Theophilos, and which you can read in English and Greek in the edition by Michael Featherstone and Juan Signes Codoner. Cyril Mango's collection, The Art of the Byzantine Empire, Sources and Documents, gathers old texts about Byzantine painters and pictures in English. The short entry for Lazarus in the old calendar of the saints of Constantinople, the Synaxarion, is in Hippolyte Delehaye's edition. The journey to Rome is in the life of Pope Benedict the Third in the Liber Pontificalis, the Book of the Popes, which Raymond Davis has translated. For the background of the whole quarrel about icons, the best modern history is Leslie Brubaker and John Haldon's Byzantium in the Iconoclast Era.

What is history. That Lazarus was a monk and a painter in Constantinople in the reign of Theophilos, and was punished for painting icons. That he was released at the pleading of the empress Theodora and others, and went to the church of Saint John the Forerunner at Phoberos, near where the Bosporus meets the Black Sea. That the icons were restored in eight hundred and forty-three. That a monk and painter called Lazarus carried gifts from the emperor Michael the Third to Pope Benedict the Third in Rome; most historians take him to be the same man. That he died some time in the eight hundred and sixties, on a later journey west, and was buried in Constantinople.

What is the report of the old stories. The details of his punishment, his painting in prison, the icon of the Forerunner he painted at Phoberos and the healings there, and his answer to Theodora, all come from a chronicle written about a hundred years after him, by authors who disliked Theophilos. They are told as that chronicle tells them.

What we invented. Andreas the fisher boy, his mother, his uncle on the boat, the cook and his egg whites, the gull with the bad leg, the season of the painting, the order of the colours on this particular board, and nearly all the words anybody says. Lazarus's answer to Theodora is our own plain paraphrase. We have also made the punishment much gentler in the telling than the chronicle does, and left out most of the politics.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Lazarus the icon-painter for tonight. He is remembered on the seventeenth of November in the Orthodox Church, and on the twenty-third of February in the Roman Martyrology.

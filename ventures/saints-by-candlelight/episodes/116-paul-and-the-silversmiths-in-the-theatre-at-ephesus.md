It is a hot afternoon in a city by the sea, and a boy of fourteen is standing in a theatre, shouting.

He has been shouting for a long time. His throat hurts. His voice has gone thin and squeaky, the way a voice does when it is fourteen and has been used too hard. All around him, above him and below him, on row after row of stone seats climbing up the side of a hill, there are thousands and thousands of other people, and they are all shouting the same five words, over and over, like waves coming in on a beach.

Great is Artemis of the Ephesians! Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!

The boy stops for a moment, to get his breath. He turns to the man next to him, a big, sweaty man with a fish basket on his arm, who is shouting louder than anyone.

"Excuse me," says the boy. "Sorry. What are we shouting about?"

The man stops. He thinks about it. He looks a little puzzled.

"No idea," he says. "But it's the goddess, isn't it? Can't go wrong with the goddess." And he goes back to shouting.

The boy looks down at the stage, far below, where two frightened men are standing in the middle of the crowd, with their cloaks half torn off. He does not know who they are either.

Last time, if you were with us, we were in the city of Ephesus, on the west coast of what is now Turkey, with two tentmakers called Priscilla and Aquila. They had opened a little workshop on a side street, rented from a sharp old widow called Doris, who had a great many bracelets and a family of sparrows in her rafters. And they had met a brilliant young preacher from Alexandria called Apollos, and taken him home for supper, and explained the way of God to him more accurately. And then they had sent him off across the sea to Corinth, with a letter and their blessing.

Tonight, Apollos is in Corinth, doing very well. And Saint Paul, who had sailed away from Ephesus promising to come back if God willed it, is on his way back. God, it turns out, did will it.

So before Paul arrives, let me tell you a little about the city he was coming back to. Because Ephesus was not just any city. Ephesus was, in its own opinion, the most important city in the world, and it had a fair case.

It was the biggest city in the Roman province of Asia, which in those days meant not the great continent we call Asia, but the western end of Turkey, a rich land of olive groves and river valleys and harbour towns. It had perhaps a couple of hundred thousand people, though nobody really knows. It had marble streets with columns down both sides, and fountains, and public baths, and a great market square where you could buy anything from a sack of wheat to a slave to a parrot. And it had a harbour, though, as we heard last time, the harbour had a habit of silting up with mud from the river, so that the city kept having to dig it out again, and the sea kept quietly moving further away. Today the sea is several miles from the ruins. The Ephesians lost that argument in the end.

But above all, Ephesus had the goddess.

Just outside the city walls, on the flat ground towards the river, stood the temple of Artemis. The Greeks counted it as one of the seven wonders of the world, alongside the pyramids of Egypt and the great lighthouse at Alexandria. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder, who loved measuring things, says it had a hundred and twenty-seven columns, each sixty feet high, and that it took a hundred and twenty years to build. That is a lot of columns. If you have ever stood next to a column sixty feet high, you will know it is the sort of thing that makes you feel you ought to have brushed your hair.

There is a story about that temple, which I cannot resist telling you, though it happened long before our story. The Greek writer Plutarch tells it. Nearly four hundred years before Paul, there was an earlier temple on the same spot, and one night a man called Herostratus set fire to it and burned it to the ground. When they caught him and asked him why, he said he had done it so that his name would be remembered for ever. The Ephesians, very sensibly, passed a law that nobody was ever to say his name again. This did not work. I have just said it to you. Plutarch adds that it was the very same night that Alexander the Great was born, and that someone explained that the goddess had been too busy helping at the birth to look after her own house. Which is the kind of thing people say.

Anyway, the Ephesians built the temple again, bigger and grander than before, and by Paul's day it had stood for three hundred years. Pilgrims came to it from all over the world. It was also a kind of bank, where people deposited their money for safekeeping, on the reasonable theory that nobody would dare rob a goddess. Inside stood the statue of Artemis of Ephesus. She did not look like the slim huntress with a bow and arrow that you might have seen in pictures of Greek myths. The Ephesian Artemis was something older and stranger: a tall, stiff figure, with a tall crown like a city wall on her head, animals carved up and down her robe, and rows and rows of round shapes across her front, which scholars have been arguing about for centuries and still do not agree on. The Ephesians said the statue had fallen from the sky.

And around the goddess, there was a great deal of business.

There were priests and priestesses. There were the temple guards and the temple bankers. There were the people who sold food to the pilgrims, and the people who rented rooms to the pilgrims, and the people who sold the pilgrims sacrificial animals, and the people who stood around in the market square explaining to the pilgrims which way the temple was, for a small tip. And there were the silversmiths, who made little silver shrines of Artemis, small enough to hold in your hand or set on a shelf at home, so that a pilgrim could take the goddess back with them to Smyrna or Pergamum or Rome, and show the neighbours. Nobody has ever dug up one of these silver shrines, which is not surprising, since silver things tend to get melted down. We do not know exactly what they looked like. Perhaps a little model of the temple, with the goddess standing inside. But we know there were a lot of them, and that people bought them, and that the men who made them did very nicely indeed.

And at the other end of the city, cut into the side of a hill called Mount Pion, there was the great theatre. It is still there. It could hold about twenty-five thousand people, sitting on curved rows of stone seats that rise up and up, so steep that from the top row the stage looks like a postage stamp. It was where plays were put on, and where the city's assembly met, when the citizens gathered to vote on things. And it was where, when something exciting happened, everybody went.

Hold on to the theatre. We will be needing it.

* * *

Paul came back to Ephesus overland. Luke, who wrote the Acts of the Apostles, says he passed through the upper country, which means the high, dry inland plateau of Turkey, and came down to the coast through the river valleys. If you have ever done a very long walk and come over the last hill and seen the sea, you will know what that moment was like.

I like to imagine that the first place he went was a narrow shop on a side street, with hides hanging from the beams and sparrows in the rafters. Luke does not tell us that. But Priscilla and Aquila were his oldest friends in that part of the world, and they had been in Ephesus for a while now, and a church was already meeting in their house. Paul says so himself, in a letter he wrote from Ephesus a little later: Aquila and Prisca, together with the church in their house, send you hearty greetings in the Lord.

So let us imagine it. The door of the shop opening. Aquila looking up from his bench. And a dusty, bald, short man in the doorway, with a pack on his back and a walking stick in his hand.

"You said if God wills," said Aquila.

"He willed," said Paul.

And then there was a great deal of hugging, and Priscilla came running from the back, and somebody put a pot on the fire. And the old widow Doris, who lived next door and had been watching from her own doorway, came round to inspect the new arrival.

She looked him up and down.

"Another tentmaker?" she said.

"Yes," said Paul.

"From where?"

"Tarsus," said Paul. "And before that, Antioch. And before that, Jerusalem. And lately, Corinth, Athens, Thessalonica, Philippi."

Doris narrowed her eyes. "You people really do move about a lot."

"I'm told I'm staying a while this time," said Paul.

"The sparrows stay," said Doris. "They were here before you."

And Paul, who had been stoned at Lystra and beaten at Philippi and laughed at in Athens, and was not easily frightened, promised to be very respectful to the sparrows.

* * *

Now, very soon after he arrived, Paul met some people. Luke calls them disciples. There were about twelve of them, all men, and they were believers of some kind. But something about them puzzled Paul. Perhaps it was the way they talked, or the way they prayed, or something they did not seem to know. And so he asked them a question.

"Did you receive the Holy Spirit when you believed?"

And they said, "No. We have never even heard that there is a Holy Spirit."

Which is a startling answer, when you think about it.

"Then into what were you baptized?" asked Paul.

"Into John's baptism," they said.

You may remember that this is exactly what was said about Apollos last time, that he knew only the baptism of John. John, of course, is Saint John the Baptist, who preached by the river Jordan and baptized the crowds, and told them that someone greater was coming after him. His preaching had spread a long way, it seems, carried by travellers and pilgrims, and here was a little group of his followers, hundreds of miles from the Jordan, who had heard John's message and taken it to heart, but who had somehow never heard the rest of the story. Luke does not tell us how they knew John's baptism, or how they came to be in Ephesus, and so we will not guess.

Paul explained it to them, gently.

"John baptized with a baptism of repentance," he said. "And he told the people to believe in the one who was coming after him. That is, in Jesus."

And when they heard this, Luke says, they were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus. And when Paul laid his hands on them, the Holy Spirit came upon them, and they spoke in tongues and prophesied.

I think there is something lovely about those twelve men. They had been faithful to what they knew. They had heard John's call to turn back to God, and they had turned, and they had waited for the one who was coming. And then one day a dusty tentmaker walked into their lives and told them: he has come. His name is Jesus. And here is the rest of the story.

Most of us, I think, are a bit like those twelve. We know some of it. We are waiting for the rest.

* * *

As he always did when he came to a new city, Paul began by going to the synagogue. Last time, when he had passed through briefly, the people there had asked him to stay longer. Now he did. For three months, Luke says, he spoke boldly in the synagogue, arguing and persuading about the kingdom of God.

Three months is a long time, for Paul. In some cities he had lasted three weeks before somebody threw him out. And even in Ephesus it ended the usual way. Some of the people in the synagogue grew stubborn and would not believe, and began speaking badly of the Way in front of the whole congregation. The Way, by the way, is what the first Christians often called their faith. I rather like it. It sounds less like a club and more like a road.

So Paul withdrew. He took the disciples with him. And he found a new place to teach: the lecture hall of a man called Tyrannus.

We know nothing at all about Tyrannus except his name, and his name is not encouraging. It means, more or less, the tyrant. It may have been a nickname. Perhaps he was a teacher of rhetoric, and his pupils called him that behind his back, which is the sort of thing pupils do. Or perhaps his parents just liked the sound of it. Either way, he had a hall, and he let Paul use it.

Now here is a little detail which I love. In one very old manuscript of Acts, called Codex Bezae, there are a few extra words in this verse. It says that Paul taught in the hall of Tyrannus every day, from the fifth hour to the tenth. In the way the ancients counted the hours of the day, that means from about eleven in the morning until about four in the afternoon. That is the hottest part of the day in an Ephesian summer, when most people stopped work, closed their shutters, ate their lunch and had a nap. The hall would have been empty. Tyrannus would have been at home with his feet up. And Paul, who had spent the cool of the morning working, would put down his tools and walk across town and teach right through the siesta, to anyone who would give up their nap to listen.

We cannot be sure about those hours, because they are only in that one manuscript. But we do know that Paul worked with his hands in Ephesus, because he said so himself, years later, to the leaders of the Ephesian church: you yourselves know that these hands ministered to my needs, and to the needs of those who were with me. So I like to imagine Paul at dawn on a bench in Priscilla and Aquila's shop, with an awl and a curved needle and a heap of goat-hair cloth, and Doris's sparrows hopping about on the floor looking for crumbs. And then at the fifth hour, putting down the needle, and wiping his hands, and going off to teach.

It went on like that, Luke says, for two years. Two whole years. Longer than Paul had stayed anywhere else on his journeys. And people came. People came from all over the province, because Ephesus was the great city where everybody came sooner or later, for business, or a lawsuit, or the festivals of the goddess. And they heard the word of the Lord, Luke says, Jews and Greeks alike, and some of them took it home with them. It was probably in those years that churches began in the smaller cities round about, at Colossae and Laodicea and Hierapolis, places Paul himself may never have visited. One of his helpers, a man called Epaphras, seems to have carried the gospel to Colossae. It was spreading the way a good smell spreads from a kitchen.

* * *

And during those two years, Luke tells us, God did extraordinary miracles by the hands of Paul.

Notice how he puts it. Not that Paul did miracles. God did them, by Paul's hands. Paul was not a wizard. He was a tentmaker who prayed.

And Luke tells us something odd and rather homely about those miracles. He says that people took handkerchiefs and aprons that had touched Paul's skin, and carried them away to the sick, and the sick were healed, and evil spirits went out of them.

Now the words Luke uses for those handkerchiefs and aprons are workmen's words. The handkerchief is the sort of cloth you would tie round your head, or keep in your belt, to mop the sweat off your face. The apron is the sort of thing a craftsman wears at his bench. In other words, they were Paul's work clothes. His sweat rags. The things he wore while he was cutting and stitching tents in the morning, before he went off to teach.

I find that very moving. God did not choose a golden cup, or a jewelled crown, or a holy relic in a silver box. He chose the old, sweaty, stained work apron of a man who made tents for a living. Whatever you are, it seems, and whatever you do all day, God is not too proud to use it.

And here, I think, Doris began to pay attention.

Because one day, so I like to imagine, Doris's grandson Hermon came to visit. Hermon was fourteen, and he was an apprentice to a silversmith in the street of the silversmiths, where he spent his days polishing little silver goddesses until they shone. And Hermon had a cough. It was a bad cough, a deep, rattling, winter cough that had gone on for weeks and would not shift.

And Doris, who had been listening at the door of the tentmakers' shop for a long time now, and pretending she wasn't, did something she would never have admitted to. She went next door and asked Priscilla, quite gruffly, if she could borrow one of the little bald man's sweat rags. Just for a day. Not that she believed in any of it, of course.

And Priscilla, who was a wise woman, did not say anything clever. She just gave Doris the cloth. And then she said, "Shall we pray for him together?"

And Doris said, "I suppose it can't hurt."

That part is our invention. Luke does not tell us about Doris, or about Hermon, or about his cough. But Luke does tell us that it happened, again and again, to people very much like them.

And Hermon's cough, I am happy to say, got better.

* * *

Now, whenever something real is going on, there will always be people who want to copy it without understanding it. And that brings us to the seven sons of Sceva. I think this story is meant to be a little bit funny, and I am going to tell it that way.

In those days there were people who went from town to town as wandering exorcists. They claimed they could drive out evil spirits, for a fee, using spells and names and magic words. Ephesus was full of that sort of thing. The city was famous for magic. All over the Greek world, people talked about the Ephesian letters, which were strings of strange words that were supposed to work as charms, and that people wrote on scraps of papyrus and wore round their necks.

And some of these wandering exorcists had noticed that Paul was having remarkable success. They noticed that when Paul prayed, he spoke the name of Jesus. And so they thought, very sensibly from their point of view, that the name of Jesus must be a particularly powerful magic word. And that they might as well try it.

So they began to say, to people who were troubled by evil spirits: I command you, by the Jesus whom Paul preaches.

Luke says that among those who did this were seven sons of a man called Sceva, who was a Jewish chief priest. We do not know anything else about Sceva. No chief priest of that name appears in any of the lists from Jerusalem, and he may have been a priest who used a grander title than he had, or the title may simply have been part of the family's advertising. Seven sons, though. All in the same line of work. It was a family business.

And one day, the seven brothers went to a house where there was a man with an evil spirit. And they stood round him, all seven of them, in a solemn circle, and said their words.

"We command you, by the Jesus whom Paul preaches."

And the evil spirit answered them. And what it said, I think, is one of the most memorable lines in the whole of Acts.

"Jesus I know," said the spirit. "And Paul I know about. But who are you?"

I like to imagine a long, uncomfortable silence after that. The kind of silence you get when somebody at a party says, I'm sorry, have we met?

And then the man with the evil spirit leapt on them. All seven of them. And he got the better of all of them, so that they ran out of that house, Luke says, without their clothes and with their bruises. Seven grown men, running down the street in their underthings, or less, as fast as their legs would carry them, while the neighbours came out to watch.

It was the sort of thing that gets talked about. And it was talked about. It became known, Luke says, to everyone who lived in Ephesus, Jews and Greeks. And a great awe fell on them all, and the name of the Lord Jesus was held in honour.

Because the point of the story, underneath the comedy, is a serious one. The name of Jesus is not a magic word. You cannot borrow it, like a key, to open a door you have no right to. It belongs to a person, a living person, who knows his friends. Paul did not command evil spirits by the name of Jesus because it was a powerful spell. He did it because he belonged to Jesus, and Jesus was with him. The seven sons of Sceva did not know Jesus. And it turned out that even the demons could tell.

* * *

And then something happened in Ephesus that nobody had expected.

People began to come forward. Many of those who had become believers came, Luke says, confessing and telling openly what they had been doing. And a good number of those who had practised magic, the people who wrote the Ephesian letters, and sold the charms, and told fortunes, and kept the spell books, brought their books together and burned them in front of everybody.

Imagine it. A bonfire in a public square, and people coming one after another, each with an armful of scrolls, the precious, secret, expensive books they had collected for years, and throwing them on the fire. The papyrus curling and blackening. The smoke going up into the evening sky. A whole city watching.

And then somebody, being an Ephesian, worked out how much it had all been worth. Luke says they counted up the value, and it came to fifty thousand pieces of silver.

That is a great deal of money. If those pieces of silver were drachmas, and a drachma was something like a day's wage for a working man, which is roughly right, then fifty thousand of them would be the wages of one man working every day for about a hundred and thirty-seven years. It was an enormous sacrifice. Those people did not have to burn their books. They could have sold them. Somebody would have bought them. But they wanted no part of them any more, and they did not want anyone else to have them either.

And so, Luke says, the word of the Lord grew mightily, and prevailed.

I imagine Doris came to watch the bonfire. I imagine she stood at the back, with her bracelets jangling, and said nothing. And I imagine she went home that night and took down from her shelf a little charm that her husband had bought her years ago, a strip of lead with strange words scratched on it, and looked at it for a long while. And then put it back. These things take time.

* * *

Now, after all this, Paul made up his mind, in the Spirit, Luke says, to go on. He would pass through Macedonia and Greece again, to visit the churches he had founded there, and then go to Jerusalem. And after I have been there, he said, I must see Rome as well.

That is a very large plan, said very simply. Rome was the capital of the world. And Paul, a tentmaker with a sweat rag, had decided he must go and see it. He did, in the end, though not in the way he expected. But that is a story for another night.

So he sent two of his helpers on ahead of him to Macedonia: Timothy, the young man who had joined him years before at Lystra, and a man called Erastus. And he himself stayed in Asia a while longer. In a letter he wrote around this time, to the Corinthians, he explains why. I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost, he writes, because a wide door for effective work has opened to me, and there are many adversaries.

A wide door, and many adversaries. Paul had a gift for putting the good news and the bad news in the same sentence.

And it was about this time, Luke says, that there arose no little stir concerning the Way.

No little stir. Luke is a master of understatement. What he means is: a riot.

* * *

In the street of the silversmiths, there was a man called Demetrius.

Demetrius was a silversmith himself, and he was evidently a big man in the trade. He made silver shrines of Artemis, and he employed a good many craftsmen and workmen, and he brought a good deal of business their way. He was, in our terms, the head of the guild, or the chairman of the local business association. And he had been watching the books.

In our story, Hermon the apprentice works for a different silversmith, a few doors down from Demetrius. And one evening, so we imagine, all the silversmiths in the street were called together, the masters and the craftsmen and the apprentices and the boys who swept the floors, and they all crowded into Demetrius's big workshop, among the furnaces and the anvils and the trays of half-finished goddesses. And Demetrius stood up on a bench, and made a speech.

The speech is in Acts. Here it is, in our own plain words, with nothing added.

"Men," said Demetrius, "you know that our prosperity comes from this business. And you see and hear that, not only in Ephesus, but in almost the whole of Asia, this man Paul has persuaded and turned away a great number of people, by saying that gods made with hands are not gods at all. And there is a danger, not only that this trade of ours will fall into disrepute, but also that the temple of the great goddess Artemis will be counted as nothing, and that she will be stripped of her majesty, she whom all Asia and all the world worship."

It is a very good speech, in its way. Notice the order of things. First, our prosperity. Then, our trade. Then, almost as an afterthought, the goddess. Demetrius knew his audience. He knew that the surest way to get a room full of craftsmen angry is to tell them their wages are at risk, and then to tell them that their religion is at risk too, so that they can feel righteous about being angry about their wages.

And I must say, in fairness to Demetrius, he was not wrong about the facts. Paul really was saying that gods made with hands are not gods. That is what the prophets of Israel had always said. And a great many people really had stopped buying silver shrines. Demetrius had noticed something true. He simply did not like it.

The speech worked. The silversmiths were filled with fury. And they began to shout.

"Great is Artemis of the Ephesians! Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!"

And Hermon, our apprentice, being fourteen, and being in a room full of shouting grown men, did what any fourteen-year-old would do. He shouted too.

* * *

The shouting spilled out of the workshop and into the street. And from the street of the silversmiths it spilled into the next street, and the next. And people came out of their houses to see what the noise was, and joined in, because everybody was shouting, and it sounded important. The whole city, Luke says, was filled with confusion.

And somebody spotted two of Paul's travelling companions, Gaius and Aristarchus, two men from Macedonia. Aristarchus came from Thessalonica. The crowd seized them, and dragged them along, and everybody rushed together, with one mind, into the theatre.

Of course they did. Where else would you go?

Twenty-five thousand seats, climbing up the side of the hill. And in a short time they were filling up, with silversmiths and their workmen, and fishmongers and porters, and housewives with baskets, and boys who should have been at work, and old men who had been dozing in the market square and woke up to find everyone running. And Gaius and Aristarchus, down on the floor of the theatre, in the middle of it all, with the whole city roaring above them.

When Paul heard what was happening, he wanted to go in. Of course he did. They were his friends, and the crowd was angry with him, not with them, and he wanted to go and stand in front of the people and speak. But the disciples would not let him. And then a message came from some of the Asiarchs.

The Asiarchs were among the most important men in the province. They were wealthy, leading citizens, who held a grand title and had charge of some of the great public festivals. And some of them, Luke says, were Paul's friends. Which tells you something about how widely Paul's two years in Ephesus had reached. These great men sent word to him, begging him not to venture into the theatre.

So Paul stayed away. I imagine it was the hardest thing he had done in a long time. I imagine him pacing up and down Priscilla and Aquila's shop, among the hides, while the roar of the crowd came over the rooftops from the other side of town, and Priscilla and Aquila stood in the doorway to make sure he did not slip out. Paul wrote later, in his letter to the Romans, that Prisca and Aquila had risked their necks for his life. He does not say when, or where. Some have wondered if it was in Ephesus. Nobody knows. But I like to think of them standing in that doorway.

* * *

And in the theatre, Luke says, some were shouting one thing, and some another. The assembly was in confusion. And most of them did not know why they had come together.

That is one of my favourite sentences in the whole Bible. Most of them did not know why they had come together. Twenty thousand people, shouting at the tops of their voices, and most of them had no idea what about.

This is where our cold open came from. Hermon, with his voice gone squeaky, asking the man with the fish basket what they were shouting about, and the man with the fish basket saying, no idea, but you can't go wrong with the goddess.

Then something else happened. Some of the Jews of Ephesus pushed forward a man called Alexander. We don't know who he was, exactly. Perhaps he was one of the leaders of the synagogue. And perhaps the Jewish community, seeing a great angry crowd shouting about people who did not worship Artemis, were worried that the crowd would turn on them as well, since they did not worship Artemis either. And perhaps they sent Alexander forward to explain to the crowd that the Jews of Ephesus had nothing to do with Paul and his friends. Luke does not tell us all that. He only says they put him forward. The rest is a reading.

Alexander climbed up where everyone could see him, and he raised his hand, asking for quiet, the way speakers did. He wanted to make a defence.

And the crowd looked at him. And they saw that he was a Jew.

And they drowned him out. With one voice, all of them together, for about two hours, they shouted:

"Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!"

Two hours.

I want you to imagine shouting the same five words for two hours. Not singing. Not chanting, nicely, in time. Shouting. In the heat of an Ephesian afternoon, with the sun beating down on the stone seats, and the dust, and no shade, and nothing to drink. Two hours. Your throat would be like sandpaper. Your voice would be gone. Your ears would be ringing.

Hermon's voice, by the end of it, had gone altogether. He was just opening and closing his mouth, like a goldfish, while the noise went on around him. And somewhere in the middle of the second hour, he stopped even doing that, and sat down on the hot stone, and put his chin in his hands, and looked down at the two frightened Macedonians on the floor of the theatre. And he thought: what are we doing? What is any of this for?

Which is a very good question to ask in the middle of a crowd. Most people never get round to it.

* * *

And then, at last, somebody important arrived.

Luke calls him the town clerk. That makes him sound like a little man with spectacles and a rubber stamp, but he was nothing of the kind. The town clerk of Ephesus was one of the chief officials of the whole city. He kept the public records. He drew up the decrees that the assembly voted on. He was the man the Roman authorities would hold responsible if the city got out of hand. His name, and the names of other men who held his office, appear on inscriptions found in the ruins of Ephesus. And he was a man who knew how to handle a crowd.

He came out onto the stage. And somehow, Luke says, he got them quiet. Perhaps they were simply too tired to go on. Two hours is a long time. And then he spoke. Here is his speech, in our own plain words, with nothing added.

"Men of Ephesus," he said, "who is there anywhere in the world who does not know that the city of the Ephesians is the temple keeper of the great Artemis, and of the sacred stone that fell from the sky? Since nobody can deny these things, you ought to keep calm, and do nothing rash. You have brought these men here, who have neither robbed temples nor blasphemed our goddess. If Demetrius and the craftsmen with him have a complaint against anyone, the courts are open, and there are proconsuls. Let them bring charges against one another. And if you want anything more than that, it will be settled in the lawful assembly. Because we are in danger of being charged with rioting over today's events, since there is no reason we can give to account for this commotion."

And having said this, he dismissed the assembly.

It is a wonderful speech. He does not argue with them about the goddess at all. He agrees with them. Of course Artemis is great, he says. Everyone knows that. Nobody could possibly doubt it. So there's no need to shout about it, is there? And then, very gently, he reminds them of the one thing every citizen of a Greek city under Roman rule was afraid of. The Romans did not like riots. A city that rioted could lose its privileges, its freedoms, its right to hold assemblies at all. And the town clerk was saying, in effect: there are proper ways of doing things. We have courts. We have judges. And if the Romans ask me what all this was about, what exactly am I supposed to tell them?

There is a little joke hidden in the Greek, by the way. The word Luke uses for the assembly, the gathering of the citizens of a Greek city, is ekklesia. And ekklesia is the very same word that Christians used, and still use, for the Church. So in this one chapter, Luke has two assemblies in Ephesus. One of them is a crowd of twenty thousand people who have been shouting for two hours and most of whom do not know why they came. The other is a little group of tentmakers and apprentices and former magicians, meeting in a workshop on a side street, who know exactly why they came. I do not think Luke chose that word by accident.

And so the great theatre emptied. People went home, hoarse and sunburnt and rather embarrassed, the way people are after they have got carried away. Gaius and Aristarchus, I am glad to say, were let go. And Demetrius went back to his workshop, and his furnaces, and his trays of little silver goddesses, and we never hear of him again.

* * *

Hermon came home that evening very late, and very quiet, with no voice at all.

His grandmother was waiting for him in the doorway with her arms folded, which was how Doris waited for everybody. And next door, in the tentmakers' shop, the lamp was lit, and there were people inside, quite a lot of people, talking in low voices. Gaius and Aristarchus were there, with bruises and torn cloaks. And Paul was there, with his arms round both of them.

Hermon looked in at the door. Nobody noticed him. He watched Paul for a while. He was smaller than Hermon had expected. And balder. He did not look like someone who could turn a whole city upside down.

Then Doris put her hand on Hermon's shoulder.

"Come home," she said. "I'll heat some honey and water for that throat. And then you can tell me what all the shouting was about."

Hermon opened his mouth. No sound came out at all. He shrugged.

"No," said Doris. "I didn't think you'd know."

And then, after a long pause, she said something she had never said before.

"Tomorrow," said Doris, "I think I might go next door, and ask that little bald man to tell me about his Jesus. From the beginning. Properly. Not through a keyhole."

And Hermon, who had no voice left, just nodded.

All of that is our invention, I should remind you. Luke tells us nothing about Doris. But Luke tells us that the word of the Lord grew mightily in Ephesus, in those years, and every word that grows has to grow in somebody. I like to think that some of it grew in a sharp old widow with too many bracelets, who had been listening at the door for two years, and finally knocked.

[pause]

After the uproar had stopped, Luke says, Paul sent for the disciples. He encouraged them. He said goodbye. And he set out for Macedonia.

He did not come back to Ephesus itself. But a year or so later, on his way to Jerusalem, his ship put in at the port of Miletus, a little way down the coast, and he sent a message asking the elders of the Ephesian church to come and see him there. And they came. And Paul spoke to them, a long, tender farewell speech, which is in Acts, chapter twenty. He reminded them of his years among them, of how he had taught them in public and from house to house, and of how he had worked with his own hands. He told them he did not think he would ever see them again. He told them to look after the flock, and to remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he himself said, it is more blessed to give than to receive. And when he had finished, he knelt down with them all, and prayed. And they wept, and put their arms round his neck, and kissed him, and walked with him down to the ship.

Aristarchus, the man who was dragged into the theatre, went on travelling with Paul. He was with him on the ship that was wrecked off Malta, which we told in an earlier episode. And later, when Paul was a prisoner, he wrote that Aristarchus was there too: Aristarchus, my fellow prisoner, greets you. Some people stay.

Paul wrote about Ephesus himself, in his letters, but not very much, and never quite plainly. In one place he says he fought with wild beasts at Ephesus, and people have argued ever since about whether he meant real animals in an arena, or whether he was talking about his adversaries in the way we might call someone an animal. Most think the second. In another letter he says that in Asia he was so utterly, unbearably crushed that he despaired of life itself, but that God delivered him. He does not say what happened. Perhaps the riot. Perhaps something else. Paul did not always tell the whole story. He was too busy getting on with the next part.

Ephesus went on being Ephesus for a long while. The temple of Artemis stood for another two hundred years, more or less. Then it was damaged by raiders from the north, and over the centuries that followed its marble was carried away to build other things, and the river mud crept over what was left, until nobody could even remember where it had been. An English engineer found its foundations again in the eighteen-hundreds, after years of digging. Today there is one column standing in a marshy field, put back together from broken pieces, with a stork's nest on the top.

And the great theatre is still there, and the summer tourists sit in it, and eat ice cream, and take photographs of each other, and sometimes, to test the echo, somebody stands on the stage and shouts.

There is an old tradition, too, that Saint John the Apostle came to live in Ephesus, in his old age, and that he brought with him the Mother of God, whom the Lord had given into his care from the cross. It is a tradition, and other places have their own traditions, and we will leave it as one. But Saint John is certainly remembered at Ephesus, and the ruins of a great church built over his tomb still stand on a hill above the town.

And there is one more thing. Nearly four hundred years after Paul, in the year four hundred and thirty-one, the bishops of the whole Church came to Ephesus for a great council. And at that council they declared that the Virgin Mary is rightly called the Mother of God, because the child she bore is God the Son made man. Catholic and Orthodox Christians both hold to that council to this day. And Saint Cyril of Alexandria, who was there, wrote home afterwards that when the decision was announced, the people of Ephesus came out into the streets with torches, and walked the bishops back to their lodgings in the dark.

So the city that once spent two hours shouting great is Artemis of the Ephesians ended up rejoicing, with torches, over the Mother of God. I do not think Demetrius would have been pleased. But I think the town clerk might have smiled.

And somewhere, I like to think, in a narrow street, behind a shop that smells of leather and lamp oil, there were sparrows in the rafters, and the sparrows stayed. They were there before all of it.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter nineteen, and the first verse of chapter twenty. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept, in our own plain rendering, and the speeches of Demetrius and the town clerk add nothing to what Luke reports.

The hours from the fifth to the tenth are in Codex Bezae only. That Paul worked with his hands at Ephesus is from his farewell at Miletus, in chapter twenty. Paul's greeting from Aquila and Prisca and the church in their house, the wide door and many adversaries, and the wild beasts at Ephesus are from the First Letter to the Corinthians; his despair in Asia is from the second. That Prisca and Aquila risked their necks for him is from Romans, and where it happened is unknown. Aristarchus as fellow prisoner is in Colossians.

Why the Jews put Alexander forward is a reading, not a fact. What Sceva's title meant, and how the twelve knew only John's baptism, Luke does not say. The sum of fifty thousand pieces of silver is Luke's; the hundred and thirty-seven years assumes a drachma for a day's wage, and is only a rough picture.

Pliny describes the temple's columns, Plutarch tells the story of Herostratus, and Strabo describes the city. The town clerk, the Asiarchs and the title temple keeper are known from inscriptions. John and the Mother of God at Ephesus is tradition. Cyril's torches come from his own letters from the council.

Paul's looks, short and bald, come from the second-century Acts of Paul and Thecla, which is a legend. The Ephesian letters are mentioned by ancient writers. Epaphras at Colossae is from Colossians.

Doris, Hermon, his cough, his master's shop, the bonfire watchers, the man with the fish basket, Paul at the tentmakers' bench in Ephesus, and every word not in Acts, are our own invention.

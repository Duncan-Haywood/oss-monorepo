Picture a stone, standing on its edge against the front of a cave, as round and flat as a wheel.

It is a large stone, and it has been there for four days. The grass at its foot has been trampled, because a great many people have stood around it, one way and another. Beside it, on the hillside, the olive trees are grey and quiet. And somewhere behind it, in the dark, is a man whom everybody in the village loved, wrapped in linen, lying very still.

Now somebody is walking up the path, and the whole crowd has gone quiet. A woman has said something, in a small flat voice that carries, and the walker has said something back. And then he says three words that none of them has ever heard anyone say in a place like this. They are the words, "Take away the stone."

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Gospel of John, chapter eleven, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is about Lazarus, and his sisters Martha and Mary, and the day their friend came to Bethany. A few of the small things around the edges, the road, the neighbours, the long days of waiting and what the sisters were doing in them, are our own invention, told in the spirit of John, and the notes at the end say which. What the messengers say, what is said to the disciples, what is said at the meetings on the road, the tears, the stone, the prayer and the cry at the door of the tomb, we have kept as close to John as we can. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let me tell you first where we are, and who these people are.

We are in a village called Bethany. It sits on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, a short walk from Jerusalem. John gives us the distance, which is unusual for him, and he says about fifteen stadia, which comes to roughly two miles. That is about the distance you might walk to the shops, if you did not mind a hill. You can see the Temple from the top of the ridge above the village. And you can walk from Bethany to the gates of the city in not much more than half an hour, and be back in time for supper.

That matters. It means that Bethany is a place where people who have business in Jerusalem can live a bit more quietly. It is a village of gardens, and olive trees, and low stone houses with flat roofs. The name is thought to mean something like house of figs, or perhaps house of the poor, and scholars like to argue about which, and I shall not.

In one of those houses live three people, a brother and two sisters. We never hear of parents. We never hear of husbands or wives or children. We hear only that there are three of them, and that they are devoted to one another, and that they have a particular friend.

The friend is Jesus. John says it more than once, so that we cannot miss it: Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. It is a plain statement, and it is one of the warmest sentences in the whole Gospel. This is a house where he can come and put his feet up. It is a house where the door is never locked, where a meal can be found, where he is not a teacher or a wonder-worker or a danger to the authorities, but simply a friend who has come to visit.

Let me introduce them, in the order the Gospel does.

[pause]

* * *

Martha you may already know. She is the one who, on another visit, was busy in the kitchen while her sister sat listening, and who said, a little crossly, that someone ought to help her. She is practical. She is the one who does the arranging. If there is a meal to be got on the table for thirteen hungry men, Martha is the person who will get it there, and who will notice, in the middle, that the salt is low. She is, I think, the kind of person who makes the world run, and who is never thanked enough for it.

But there is another side to her, which we shall see in a few minutes. Martha is not only practical. She is brave. She is the one who will say what she thinks, straight to his face, and she is the one who will, in this chapter, say one of the greatest things anyone says to Jesus in any Gospel. But you must wait for it.

Mary is the quiet one. She is the one who sat at his feet. She is the one who, we learn in the next chapter, will pour a jar of costly perfume over those same feet and wipe them with her hair. Mary is not a talker. When she feels something, she feels it all the way down, and it shows in her face. And she is, if you like, the one who stays where she is, and waits, and lets the sorrow come.

I should say here, because people sometimes mix them up, that this is Mary of Bethany. She is not Mary Magdalene, whom we met in another story, and she is not the unnamed woman of Luke's seventh chapter. The Gospels keep them apart, and so shall we.

And then there is Lazarus. Lazarus is the one about whom we know least. The name is a short form of Eleazar, which means God has helped, and I find that rather moving, in view of what is coming. He does not say one word in the whole of this chapter. Not one. He is ill, and he is dead, and he comes out of the tomb, and John never records anything he says. All we are told is that he is loved. That is his entire part in the story: he is the one that Jesus loves. I think there is something worth sitting with in that. Some people, it seems, are the centre of the story simply by being loved.

[pause]

* * *

So. One day, Lazarus falls ill.

We do not know what it is. John does not say, and I am not going to guess, because I do not want to put a name on anything and have you worry about it in the night. All we know is that it is serious, and that it is getting worse.

There is a house in Bethany where, I imagine, the shutters have been drawn a little. There is a cup of water by the bed. There is a sister sitting up, and another going back and forth to the well. There is the low murmur of a neighbour who has come to help, and has brought some soup, and is not sure what to say. Anyone who has looked after someone who is ill knows how that house sounds. It is very quiet, and every sound seems enormous.

And the sisters do what you do, when you have a friend who can help. They send word.

Now, where is Jesus? He is not in Bethany. He is across the Jordan, a day's journey or more away, in the place where John the Baptist used to baptize, where he has gone to stay for a while, because the authorities in Jerusalem had lately tried to stone him. It is a quiet place, a retreat. There are people there who have come to listen. And it is some distance from Bethany, perhaps a full day's walking, or a bit more.

So the sisters find a messenger. Perhaps a boy from the village, with a good pair of legs. And they give him a short message, because it is the kind of message that does not need to be long. It says: "Lord, the one you love is ill."

That is all. Look at it. They do not say, "Please come." They do not say, "Come quickly." They do not give directions, or list the symptoms, or remind him of the way. They say: the one you love is ill. They know that he loves him. That is all they need to say. They trust that if he knows, he will do whatever is right.

I find that a very moving prayer. It is the shortest sort of prayer there is. It is simply to tell the one who loves, what is happening to the one he loves. Many of us have prayed it, and have not known what else to say.

[pause]

* * *

The messenger arrives. He finds Jesus, and he says the sentence. And Jesus listens.

And what Jesus says, if I may put it like this, is not at all what anyone expected. He says: "This illness does not lead to death. It is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it."

Now. You and I are listening to this from the other side, so we know how it turns out. But think what the messenger must have thought. He hears the words, "does not lead to death," and he takes them as good news. He turns around and starts back, and perhaps he is half running, to tell the sisters. Lazarus will recover! The Lord has said so!

Hold on to that. We shall come back to it. The words are true, in a way nobody can see yet. But a good many people are going to have a bad few days before they find out how.

And then John tells us something I find truly strange. He says, "Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So, when he heard that Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer in the place where he was."

Two days longer. Because he loved them, he stayed. John puts those two things next to each other on purpose, with the little word "so" between them, and he does not explain. He simply sets them down, and lets us wince.

I think this is one of the things the Gospel most wants us to feel. There are times when the love of God looks, from where we stand, like a delay. We pray, and the answer does not come. We send word, and nobody turns up. The sisters in Bethany send for the friend who could have helped, and he does not come. And John does not hide this from us, or pretend it is not hard. He says: he loved them, and he stayed where he was.

We shall find out why. But not yet. For the sisters, there is only a long and awful wait.

[pause]

* * *

Let us go back, for a while, to Bethany.

The messenger has come back, and he has given them the answer, and they have listened to it, and there is nothing to do but wait. So they wait.

The first day goes by. Lazarus is no better. The second day, perhaps he is a little worse. Martha is up at dawn, doing what there is to do, boiling water, wringing out cloths, because that is what she does with fear. She puts it into her hands. Mary sits beside him, and does not say much, but watches his face.

At some point in these days, one of the sisters goes to the door and looks down the road. It is the road that leads east and down, to the valley of the Jordan. And she looks, and there is nothing on it, but a man with a donkey, and a woman carrying water, and the dust. And she goes back in.

I am only guessing, you understand. John does not tell us this. But I think anybody who has waited for someone who might not come will know that the road is the thing you watch. You watch it, and you tell yourself that you are not.

And then, sometime in the third day, Lazarus dies.

I will say it as plainly as John does, and as gently. He dies, in his own house, with his sisters beside him. And the house goes very still.

[pause]

In that place, in that time, the customs were quick. The dead were buried on the same day, if it could be done. The body was washed, and wrapped in linen strips, with spices between the folds, and carried to a tomb. For a family with a little money, the tomb was a cave cut into the rock of the hillside, with a low doorway, and a big flat stone to close it, set on its edge in a groove, so that it could be rolled across the entrance and rolled back.

And the whole village came. That is the other thing about mourning in those days. It was a community matter. The neighbours came and sat with the family for days, and wept with them, and brought food, and said the things that people say. John tells us that many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary to console them. It's a lovely detail. It means that the sisters were not left to grieve alone, in the first days. Bethany did what it should do.

And so the funeral is held, and the stone is rolled across, and the neighbours go back to the house. And the sisters sit down, and begin the days of mourning. And in all this time, there is no sign of Jesus.

[pause]

* * *

Now we go back across the river, to where he has been staying.

It is the third day since the message. After two days of waiting, Jesus says to his disciples, quite suddenly, "Let us go to Judea again."

And the disciples are alarmed, and they say so. They remind him, politely, that the last time he was in Judea, people tried to stone him. "Rabbi, the Jews were just now trying to stone you, and are you going there again?" You can see their point. If they were a travelling party with sensible friends, someone would be getting out a map and looking for the longest way round.

But Jesus gives them a short answer, about daylight. "Are there not twelve hours in the day? Those who walk during the day do not stumble, because they see the light of this world." He is telling them that there is a time to walk, and that he is walking in it. They do not quite follow, and that is all right. They rarely do.

And then he says something else. "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going there to awaken him."

Now the disciples are relieved. They say, in effect, well, that is good news. If he has fallen asleep, he will get better. Sleep is a sign that the fever has broken. There is no need to go into Judea and be stoned for a man who is getting a good night's rest.

You can almost hear the sigh of relief. But John tells us what Jesus was really saying. He had spoken about Lazarus's death, but they thought he meant ordinary sleep. So then he told them plainly: "Lazarus is dead. And for your sake I am glad I was not there, so that you may believe. But let us go to him."

And then there is a small moment that I love. One of the disciples speaks. It is Thomas, called the Twin. We met him on another night, in a locked room, and I think you will like him better here. Thomas looks around at the others, and he says: "Let us also go, that we may die with him."

It is a gloomy speech, and it is also remarkable. He does not argue. He does not say it is a bad idea, though he clearly thinks so. He says, if the master is going into danger, then we shall go with him, and if that means we shall die, then so be it. It is not a speech of great faith. But it is a speech of great loyalty. And I think the Lord, who knows what is in people, would have smiled.

And so they set out.

[pause]

* * *

The journey is a day or more. They arrive at last at the outskirts of Bethany, and John tells us, in the quietest way possible, how long it has been. Lazarus, he says, has already been in the tomb four days.

Four days. This number matters. There was a belief among the people of the time that the soul of a dead person lingered near the body for three days, and left on the fourth. Whether that belief is true or not, it was widely held, and John means us to feel that this is past all hope. It is not that someone has just stopped breathing. It is not a matter of a few minutes, or a mistake. Four days. The door has closed.

Jesus has not yet come into the village. He is waiting at the place where Martha met him, a little outside. And someone runs ahead to tell the sisters. Perhaps a child. And in the house, where the neighbours are sitting, Martha hears it, and she gets up at once.

It is just like her. She does not stop to ask anyone. She does not tidy her hair. She goes straight out of the house and down the road. And Mary, John tells us, remains sitting in the house.

[pause]

So here is Martha, on the road, and here is Jesus. And the first thing she says to him is the thing she has been saying to herself for four days. She says: "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."

I want you to hear it. It is not an accusation, exactly. It is not quite a complaint, either. It is the plain and honest truth as she sees it. You could have prevented this. You were not here. And the whole of her grief is in it, and the whole of her love. She has had four days to think it over, and she has come to the person she trusts most, and she has told him the truth.

I find this wonderful. We are sometimes taught that we ought to come to God with nice thoughts only. But Martha does not. She tells him what is on her heart, and it is a hard thing to say. And Jesus does not rebuke her. He listens.

And then, before he can answer, she says something more. And this is the part I wanted you to wait for. She says: "But even now I know that whatever you ask from God, God will give you."

Even now. After four days. After the stone, and the linen, and the mourners. Even now, she says. She does not say what she is asking for. She does not dare to. She simply says that she knows, and she leaves the rest to him. It is one of the bravest things in the Gospels, and it comes from a woman standing on a dusty road with her feet still sore from the funeral.

And Jesus says: "Your brother will rise again."

Martha says, "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day."

And that is a good answer. It is the right answer. It is what a faithful Jewish woman of her day would believe, and she believes it. There will be a day, at the end of all things, when the dead will be raised. She holds on to that. But I think you can hear that it is, for now, a long way off. It is a comfort that she has been given, but it is a comfort that is kept in a far room. What she wants, I think, is something nearer.

And now Jesus says the thing that has been quoted at funerals ever since. He says: "I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?"

I am the resurrection. He does not say, I can bring about the resurrection, or I will be there on the last day. He says, I am. The day she is waiting for is standing in front of her, on the road to her village, with dust on his sandals.

And Martha answers. She does not say that she understands, because she could not. She does not say that she is no longer sad, because she is. She says: "Yes, Lord. I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world."

That is Martha's confession. If you know the other Gospels, you may be reminded of another confession, made by a fisherman, on a hillside in the north, which the Church has treasured ever since. Martha's is not less. She says it with her brother four days dead, and she says it before she has seen anything happen at all.

[pause]

* * *

And then she does what she always does, which is to take the next practical step. She goes back to the house, and she calls her sister, quietly, so that the neighbours will not hear. She says: "The Teacher is here and is calling for you."

And Mary gets up at once.

Do you notice? John says she got up quickly. The whole of her stillness is gone. She had been sitting in the house for four days, and the moment she hears that he is calling, she is on her feet.

The neighbours see her go, and they think she is going to the tomb to weep, and they follow her. So now there is a whole crowd coming along behind, though nobody has meant to bring them.

She comes to the place where Jesus is. And she falls down at his feet, and she says the same thing. Exactly the same words. "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."

Isn't that remarkable? The two sisters have been in the same house for four days, and without a doubt they have said it to each other a hundred times. When each of them meets him, it is the same sentence, word for word. It is what they have been thinking. But Martha says it standing up, and goes on to talk, and Mary says it on the ground, and says nothing more. That is how different they are. And it is how alike.

And Jesus, who answered Martha with words, does not answer Mary with words at all.

John says, "When Jesus saw her weeping, and the Jews who came with her also weeping, he was greatly disturbed in spirit and deeply moved." And he asks, "Where have you laid him?"

They say, "Lord, come and see."

And then comes the shortest sentence in many of our translations, and one of the most famous in all the Bible. "Jesus wept."

[pause]

I would like to stop there for a moment, and be quiet with it.

Jesus knows what he is going to do. He has told the disciples. He has said, I am going to awaken him. In a few minutes Lazarus will be walking out of that cave. And still, Jesus weeps.

He does not weep because he is confused, or because he has failed. He weeps because Mary is weeping, and because the whole village is weeping, and because death is, as it has always been, a real grief. He does not tell them that it will be all right in ten minutes, and that they should wipe their eyes. He stands with them in the grief, and he feels it, and the tears come.

The old writers noticed this. Some of them said that it shows us that our Lord had a truly human heart, and that he was not above sorrow. Some said it shows us that he hates death, and that he is troubled by it, because God did not make us for it. Either way, it is a great comfort. When we are in grief, we are not alone in it, and the one we are praying to has been there.

The crowd takes it in different ways, as crowds do. Some say, "See how he loved him!" And they are right. But others, a little more doubtful, say, "Could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man have kept this man from dying?" And that, too, is a fair question. It is the question the sisters asked, put into the mouths of the neighbours. And Jesus does not answer it with words. He answers it at the tomb.

[pause]

* * *

Now they come to the cave.

John describes it in a few words. It was a cave, and a stone was lying against it. We know the sort of thing. A hillside, with a low door cut into the rock, and a big round stone on its edge. And there is a crowd of people, rather a lot of them, standing about at a respectful distance, not quite knowing where to put their hands.

Jesus is deeply moved again. And he says, "Take away the stone."

There is a silence. I think it must have been a very uncomfortable one. Because no one in the history of the world has ever asked that. You do not open a tomb. You do not undo what has been done. The entire point of a stone is that it stays where it is.

And it is Martha who speaks. Practical Martha. She is not rude, but she is certainly honest. She says: "Lord, already there is a stench, because he has been dead four days."

I am going to say that this is the funniest and the most human line in the chapter, and I mean it kindly. She has just made the greatest confession of her life, a few minutes ago, on the road. And now she is standing at the tomb and thinking: but the smell. She is not being faithless. She is being a woman who has actually washed and wrapped her own brother, and who knows exactly what four days means. She cannot help it. She is not going to pretend.

And Jesus says to her, very gently, "Did I not tell you that if you believed, you would see the glory of God?"

It is not a rebuke. It is a reminder. Do you remember, he is saying, what we talked about on the road? And she does. And she says nothing more.

And the stone is taken away.

[pause]

Someone does it. We do not know who. Perhaps some of the men from the village, who set their shoulders against it, and heave. It is heavy, as these stones are, and it moves slowly, with a grinding sound. And there is the opening, and the dark.

Everyone is still. Not a person in the crowd is saying anything.

And Jesus lifts up his eyes, and he prays. He says: "Father, I thank you for having heard me. I knew that you always hear me, but I have said this for the sake of the crowd standing here, so that they may believe that you sent me."

Do you see what he does? He gives thanks. He thanks the Father, aloud, before anything has happened. As if it were already done. And he says that he is saying it for the people standing around, so that they will hear, and know that this is not a trick, and not magic, and not something he does alone. It is the Father's doing. It is an answer to prayer. He asks, and the Father hears, and he is thankful.

That matters for how we tell this story. Our Lord is not a wizard. He prays. And the Father answers.

And then, when he has said this, he cries out in a loud voice. John gives us the words, and there are only three of them in the Greek.

"Lazarus, come out!"

[pause]

* * *

I think the crowd must have stopped breathing.

There is the opening of the tomb, and the dark behind it, and the quiet. A bird, perhaps, in an olive tree. Someone's cloak rustling. Someone standing very still with a hand over their mouth.

And then, in the dark, something moves.

John does not tell us the sound of it. He says only: "The dead man came out." He came out with his hands and feet bound in strips of cloth, and his face wrapped in a cloth. He is still exactly as they left him. He walks out of the tomb in the grave clothes, bound hand and foot, so that he can hardly move. It must have been, in a way, slightly absurd, and entirely astonishing, and I do not think anyone laughed, and I do not think anyone could have helped smiling.

And Jesus says to the crowd, who are standing there rooted to the spot: "Unbind him, and let him go."

That is the last thing he says. Not a speech. Not a sermon about what has happened. He says, in effect, well, don't just stand there. Get those bandages off him. Let him go.

I love that so much. He does the thing that only God can do, he calls a man back from the dead after four days, and then he hands the rest of the job to the neighbours. They are to unwrap him. They are to take part. Somebody has to go and put their hands on him, and begin to undo the cloth, and find that he is warm.

I like to think it was Martha who did it first. She is the practical one, after all, and she has been wanting something to do with her hands for four days. And I like to think that Mary stood, and looked at his face as it came free, and could not say a word.

And Lazarus, as I said, says nothing. John never records a single thing he says. Not then, not afterwards. And I think that is very right. What would one say? He is simply there, again, among those who love him, with the daylight on his face.

[pause]

* * *

Now. Let us slow down a little, and think about it, because there is a good deal in this story, and it is the sort of story that rewards being turned over in the dark.

I want to say three things.

The first is about the waiting. Jesus stayed two days where he was, and John tells us that it was because he loved them. It does not make it easy. I do not think it was meant to. There are times when the answer to a prayer does not come on the day we ask, and the sisters in Bethany teach us that this is not the same as being forgotten. They were not forgotten. They were loved, and the one they had sent for was already on his way to something much greater than a recovery. It is a story for anyone who has ever sent word, and watched the road.

The second is about the two sisters, and the same sentence. If you had been here, my brother would not have died. Each of them says it, in her own way. And Jesus receives each of them in her own way. To the one who stands and argues and believes, he gives a conversation, and a question that goes to the heart of things. To the one who falls down and weeps, he gives his tears. He does not need them to be the same. He meets Martha as Martha and Mary as Mary. And that is a comfort, for anyone who has ever worried about whether they were praying correctly. The Lord is not looking for a particular style of grief.

And the third is about the stone, and the bandages. There are two parts to what happens at that tomb. There is the part only God can do, which is the calling out of the dead man. And there is the part the people can do, which is to roll the stone and to unbind the cloths. Jesus asks them to do both. I think that is a quiet picture of how God often works with us. He does the thing we cannot do, and he asks us to do the thing we can. We cannot raise the dead. But we can take away a stone, and we can untie a knot, and we can let a person go.

[pause]

* * *

And that is the story. Now the wind-down, and I will go slowly.

What happens afterwards, John tells us briefly. Many of the people who had come to see Mary, and had seen what Jesus did, believed in him. But some of them went away to the Pharisees and told them what had happened. And the chief priests and the Pharisees called a council, and began to worry, and to plan. The raising of Lazarus, which was the greatest sign of all, became the thing that set the road toward the cross. That is a sad thing, and it is a true one, and the Gospel does not hide it. We shall not go into that tonight.

But there is one more scene at Bethany, which is a happier one, and John gives it in the next chapter. Six days before the Passover, Jesus comes back to the village. And they give a supper for him. Martha serves, of course. Lazarus is one of those at the table with him, sitting among the guests. And Mary takes a pound of costly perfume, and anoints his feet and wipes them with her hair, and the whole house is filled with the fragrance.

I love that picture. Lazarus at the table. John mentions him almost in passing, one of those at the table, as if it were the most natural thing in the world. He is eating dinner. He is sitting with his friend, in the lamplight, with his sisters in the room. And it is a little while after the tomb, and it is a very quiet scene, and nothing at all seems to be happening. A meal. A house that smells of perfume.

I think that is one of the best things in the Gospel. Not only that the man was raised, but that he went on, afterwards, to sit down to supper.

[pause]

What became of Lazarus after that, the Gospel does not say. Later traditions tell us different things. The Orthodox Church remembers an old tradition, which has been told since early centuries, that Lazarus went to the island of Cyprus and became the first bishop of the town of Kition, which is now called Larnaca, and that he lived there for another thirty years. There is a church in Larnaca, named for him, that stands over a tomb that is said to be his. There are also other stories, from the south of France, which carry him and his sisters across the sea to a different shore. These are traditions, told long after the Gospel, and we cannot check them, and we will not lean on them. What the Gospel gives us is enough.

And the Church has gone on keeping the memory in its own way. In the Orthodox Church, the day before Palm Sunday is called the Saturday of Lazarus, and the people sing about the raising of the four-days-dead, as a kind of promise of what is to come. In the Catholic Church, the three of them, Martha and Mary and Lazarus, have a feast together at the end of July, the household of Bethany, kept as one. I like that. They are not remembered apart. They are remembered, as they lived, as three people in a house, who were loved.

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Gospel according to John, chapter eleven, with a glance at the opening of chapter twelve. It is Scripture in the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and in Protestant Bibles too. It is the longest single miracle story in the Fourth Gospel, and the last of the signs that John records before the Passion. The other three Gospels do not tell it. Luke mentions Martha and Mary at another visit, in his tenth chapter, but does not name the village or say anything of a brother.

What we have kept from the text: the sisters' message, "Lord, the one you love is ill"; the answer that the illness does not lead to death but is for the glory of God; the two days' delay and John's word that Jesus loved them; the decision to go to Judea; the disciples' fear of stoning; the saying about the twelve hours of daylight; the sleep that was death; Thomas's words, "Let us also go, that we may die with him"; the four days; the distance of about fifteen stadia; the mourners; Martha's going out and Mary's staying; Martha's two sentences and Jesus's answer; the great saying, "I am the resurrection and the life"; her confession; Mary's being called quietly, and her rising quickly; her falling at his feet and the same sentence in her mouth; the weeping; the question, "Where have you laid him?"; the words, "Come and see"; "Jesus wept"; the two reactions of the crowd; the cave and the stone; "Take away the stone"; Martha's remark about the smell; the reminder of what he had told her; the prayer of thanks; the cry, "Lazarus, come out!"; the grave clothes; "Unbind him, and let him go"; the belief of many and the report to the Pharisees; and, from chapter twelve, the supper at Bethany.

What we flagged as uncertain or as a reading. The distance of fifteen stadia is John's; two miles is our conversion. The meaning of the name Bethany is debated, and we named two of the guesses. The belief that the soul stays near the body for three days and departs on the fourth appears in later Jewish writings, and many commentators think John's four days is meant to rule out any such lingering hope; but the Gospel does not say so, and we gave it as the background it probably is. The Greek words rendered "greatly disturbed in spirit and deeply moved" are strong and have been debated since the early centuries; some old writers heard a note of anger at death, and some heard simple sorrow, and we have given both. That the verse "Jesus wept" is the shortest in many English Bibles is true of the English verse divisions and does not hold in the Greek, so we did not lean on it. The Cyprus tradition, with Lazarus as first bishop of Kition and a tomb in Larnaca, is old and honoured in the Orthodox Church, but it is later than the Gospel, and so are the stories of his voyage to Provence; we named them as traditions and left them there. And the idea that the neighbours' unbinding of Lazarus is a picture of how God works with us is a reflection, and not something the chapter states.

What we invented: the weather, the sounds, the shutters and the soup, the sister at the door watching the road, the thought of the messenger running back, Martha's hands in the kitchen and the guess that she was the first to untie the cloths. None of it is in John. None of it changes what happens, or what it means. We told the death as plainly and as gently as the Gospel does, because a bedtime story should not frighten anyone, and because this one, in the end, is a story about being called by name.

For further reading, John chapter eleven is not hard to find. Read it slowly, in a quiet room, and see how much happens in the silences.

That's the story of Lazarus and the stone for tonight. The Catholic Church keeps Saints Martha, Mary and Lazarus together on the twenty-ninth of July, and the Orthodox Church keeps the Saturday of Lazarus before Palm Sunday, and remembers the righteous Lazarus on the seventeenth of October. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Lord Jesus, friend of Martha and Mary and Lazarus, you wept at the grave of one you loved, and you called him by name. Be with everyone tonight who is waiting by a road, or sitting beside a stone. Take away what we cannot lift, and teach us to unbind one another. May the God who calls us by name watch over you tonight, and keep you till the morning. Goodnight, and God bless.

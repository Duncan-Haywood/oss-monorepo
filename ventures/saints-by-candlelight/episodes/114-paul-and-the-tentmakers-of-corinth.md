It is a bright, windy morning in the middle of a big city, and a crowd is pushing its way into a wide stone square.

At one end of the square there is a raised platform, built of marble, with steps up to it, like a stage. On the platform, in a chair, sits a Roman governor. He is a calm, well-dressed man in his fifties, with the look of someone who has had a great many people shout at him and has not enjoyed any of it.

In front of the platform, a group of angry men are all talking at once. And in the middle of them, being held by the arm, is a short, tired man with a weather-beaten face, who has been making tents in a back street for the past year and a half.

The angry men finish their accusation. The short man draws a breath. He opens his mouth to answer.

And the governor raises one hand, and says, very politely, "No."

Somewhere at the edge of the crowd, a boy of about eleven, who has followed them all the way from the workshop, holds his breath.

Last time on Saints by Candlelight, we left Saint Paul on a rocky hill in Athens, talking to philosophers about an altar to an unknown god. A few people believed. Some laughed. And then, Luke tells us in one short line, Paul left Athens and went to Corinth.

So let me tell you about Corinth.

If you look at a map of Greece, you will see that the southern part of it, the big hand-shaped piece of land called the Peloponnese, is very nearly an island. It hangs on to the rest of Greece by a narrow neck of land, the isthmus, which at its thinnest is only about four miles across. On one side of the neck is a gulf that opens to the west, towards Italy. On the other side is a gulf that opens to the east, towards Athens and Asia and the islands.

Now, if you are a sailor in the old world, and you want to get from Italy to Asia, you have a choice. You can sail all the way round the bottom of the Peloponnese, past some capes that sailors were very frightened of, with a saying that went roughly, when you round that cape, forget your home. Or you can sail up to the isthmus, unload at one side, carry everything four miles over the land, and load it on to another ship on the other side.

Most people chose the second. And so, right at the neck, there grew up a city with two harbours, one on each sea, and a road between them, and a great deal of money passing along it. That city was Corinth.

The Corinthians even built a paved track across the isthmus, with grooves in it, so that small ships could be put on wheeled cradles and dragged over the land from one sea to the other. They called it the diolkos, which means something like the haul-across. Parts of it are still there. It is one of the stranger things to imagine: a ship, sails furled, rumbling along a road between olive trees, pulled by men and oxen, with sailors walking beside it looking slightly embarrassed.

Above the city stood a huge rock, almost a mountain, called the Acrocorinth, with a temple on top and a spring of fresh water near the summit. Below the rock were the market, the temples, the shops, the fountains and the theatre, and round them, in every direction, the streets full of workshops.

The Corinth that Paul walked into was not, strictly, the old Corinth. The old Greek city had been destroyed by a Roman army about two hundred years before. For a century the place had been mostly ruins. Then Julius Caesar, not long before he died, refounded it as a Roman colony, and sent settlers to live there, many of them freed slaves from Italy, people with nothing much except hard work and a chance. By Paul's time, about a hundred years after that, Corinth was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia, which was most of southern Greece. It had a Roman governor. It was loud, new, rich, crowded, full of sailors and traders and craftsmen from all over the world, and very proud of itself.

It was, in short, rather unlike Athens. Athens was a university town, full of old buildings and people talking about ideas. Corinth was a port. People there talked about prices.

Paul came into it, as far as we can tell, on foot, along the road from Athens. And he did not come in triumph. Years later, writing to the Corinthians, he reminded them how he had first arrived. I came to you, he says, in weakness, and in fear, and in much trembling.

I find that one of the most comforting lines he ever wrote. The great apostle, the man who argued with philosophers on the Hill of Ares, walked into Corinth worn out and frightened. He had been beaten in one city, chased out of two more, and laughed at in a fourth. He was alone. His friends Silas and Timothy were far away in the north. He had no money. And he was about to try again.

* * *

The first thing Paul needed, in Corinth, was work.

This surprises people sometimes, but Paul was a working man. Like a great many Jewish teachers of his time, he had learned a trade as well as the Scriptures, and he was proud of paying his own way. Luke tells us the trade. Paul was a tentmaker.

What exactly a tentmaker did is a lovely little puzzle, and scholars argue about it cheerfully. The Greek word means tent-maker, but tents in the Roman world were made mostly of leather, and also of a rough cloth woven from goat hair. Some people think Paul was really a leatherworker, cutting and stitching hides, making tents and awnings and perhaps other things besides. Others point out that the goat-hair cloth was called, in Latin, cilicium, after Cilicia, which was Paul's own home province, and wonder whether that was the family trade. Either way, it was the sort of work you can do sitting down, with a knife and an awl and a big needle and a strong thread, and a great deal of patience. And it was the sort of work there was plenty of, in a city with two harbours and a great many ships needing sails and awnings, and travellers needing tents.

So Paul went looking for a workshop. And somewhere in the streets of Corinth he found one, belonging to a married couple who were Jews like himself, and who had also only just arrived.

Their names were Aquila and Priscilla. Aquila was from Pontus, on the shore of the Black Sea, which is a very long way from Corinth. But they had come from Rome. And they had left Rome, Luke tells us, because the emperor Claudius had ordered all the Jews to leave the city.

We know a little about that order from outside the Bible. A Roman writer called Suetonius, who wrote lives of the emperors about seventy years later, says in one short, slightly baffling sentence that Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome because they were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus. Who Chrestus was, Suetonius does not say. A great many scholars think it is a garbled version of Christus, Christ, and that the disturbances were arguments among the Jews of Rome about whether Jesus was the Messiah. Others think Chrestus was just a troublemaker of that name. It is honestly not settled. A later Christian writer, Orosius, puts the order in the ninth year of Claudius, which is the year forty-nine. So Aquila and Priscilla had probably been in Corinth only a few months when Paul walked in.

We do not know whether they were Christians already when they met him. Luke does not say that Paul converted them, and he usually mentions that sort of thing, so many people think they had already heard the good news in Rome. Either way, Luke says, because Paul was of the same trade, he stayed with them, and they worked together.

Here, at last, is where our invented friend comes in.

Two doors down from the workshop, let us say, there lived a widow called Melitta, who sold bread and lentil soup from a counter on the street. And she had a son of about eleven called Nikon, who was supposed to be helping her, and who was mostly to be found in the tentmakers' doorway instead, because there was always something interesting going on there.

On the day Paul arrived, Nikon was sitting on an upturned basket, watching Priscilla cut a hide, when a dusty stranger appeared in the doorway and asked, in a Greek with a Cilician accent, whether they needed another pair of hands.

Aquila looked up from his bench. He was a broad, quiet man with big, careful fingers. "Depends," he said. "What can you do?"

"Most things," said the stranger. "Leather. Cloth. Seams that do not leak. I can cut a panel straight without a rule."

"Everybody says that," said Priscilla, not looking up.

The stranger smiled, a little wearily, and held out his hands. Nikon leaned over to look. They were the hands of somebody who had been doing this for years. Hard skin across the palm where the awl pressed. Little white scars on the left thumb, where the needle had slipped. A dark stain under the nails that never quite washed out.

Priscilla looked at the hands, and then at the man's face, and then at her husband. Something passed between them, the way it does between people who have been married a long time.

"Where are you staying?" said Aquila.

"Nowhere yet," said the stranger.

"Well," said Aquila, "you'd better stay here, then." And he pushed a stool across the floor with his foot.

The stranger sat down. And that, as far as Nikon was concerned, was how Paul came to live in their street.

* * *

The weeks that followed had a shape to them, and Nikon got to know it well.

Six days a week, the workshop worked. They started early, as soon as there was enough light to see a seam, and they worked late, by lamplight, often long after Nikon's mother had shut up her counter and called him in. Paul says as much himself, in a letter he wrote from Corinth to his friends in Thessalonica. You remember our labour and toil, he says, how we worked night and day, so as not to be a burden on any of you. He says it again, later, to the Corinthians. We labour, working with our own hands.

I like to imagine the workshop. A narrow room opening on to the street, with a shutter that came down at night. Hides hanging from the beams. Bales of rough cloth. A bench by the door for the light, and a smell of leather and lamp oil. Customers coming and going: a ship's mate who needed an awning mended before the evening tide, a farmer who wanted a cover for a cart, a man from the games who wanted a dozen tents and wanted them yesterday.

That last one may not be entirely my invention. Every two years, a few miles away at the isthmus, the Isthmian Games were held, one of the great athletic festivals of Greece, with runners and wrestlers and chariot racers, and enormous crowds who came from everywhere and needed somewhere to sleep. Some scholars have pointed out, rather charmingly, that the games seem to have fallen during the time Paul was in Corinth, and that a tentmaker would never have been busier. We cannot be sure. But it does make you wonder whether, when Paul later wrote to the Corinthians about runners in a race, all running but only one getting the prize, he was thinking of crowds he had seen from the door of a tent he had sewn himself.

And while they worked, they talked. That is the thing about sewing leather. Your hands are busy, and your mind is free, and the person beside you is right there, and the afternoon is very long.

Nikon heard a great deal of it, sitting on his basket. He did not understand most of it, at first. There was talk about Moses and the prophets, and about a man called Jesus, who had been crucified in Jerusalem, and whom Paul said had risen from the dead and spoken to him on a road. There was talk about the churches Paul had left behind in the north, at Philippi and Thessalonica and Beroea, and how worried he was about them. There were long, comfortable silences, with only the sound of the awl going in and the thread pulling through.

And there was Priscilla. She was, Nikon decided quite early on, the cleverest person he had ever met. She argued with Paul about the Scriptures as an equal, and sometimes won. She remembered everything. When Paul was low, which in those first weeks was quite often, it was usually Priscilla who noticed, and put a bowl of Melitta's lentil soup in front of him without saying anything, and waited.

Here is a small thing, and I think a lovely one. In the New Testament, this couple is mentioned six times. And in four of those six times, her name comes first. That was unusual, in a world where the husband was nearly always named before the wife. Paul does it. Luke does it. Nobody explains why. Saint John Chrysostom, preaching about the two of them three hundred years later, noticed it too, and was full of admiration for her, and thought it was because of her zeal and her faith. Paul, by the way, calls her Prisca, which is her proper name, while Luke calls her Priscilla, which is the friendly, everyday form of it, a bit like the difference between Elizabeth and Lizzie. So if you hear both names in church, they are the same woman.

* * *

On the seventh day, the workshop stopped.

On the Sabbath, the shutter stayed down, and Paul and Aquila and Priscilla went to the synagogue. Every Sabbath, Luke says, Paul reasoned there, and tried to persuade both Jews and Greeks. The Greeks were the God-fearers, people who were not Jews themselves but who loved the God of Israel and came to listen to the Scriptures being read.

Nikon went once, out of curiosity, and stood at the back. He did not understand it much better than the talk in the workshop. But he understood that there was a disagreement. Some of the people listened to Paul with shining faces. Some of them listened with folded arms. And some of them, as the weeks went on, stopped listening at all, and started talking over him instead.

Then, one evening, two travellers came down the street, looking for the tentmakers. They were dusty, and footsore, and one of them was very young. Paul, at his bench, looked up and saw them and dropped his awl and was out of the door before it had stopped rolling.

They were Silas and Timothy, his friends from the north, come down at last from Macedonia.

They brought two things with them. The first was news. The young church in Thessalonica, which Paul had had to leave in such a hurry, and which he had been worrying about every single night, was holding firm. They were standing fast in the faith. They loved him, and longed to see him. We know Paul's reaction, because he wrote it down, in a letter he sent them almost straight away, the one we now call the First Letter to the Thessalonians. Now that Timothy has come to us from you, he writes, and brought us the good news of your faith and love, we have been comforted. Now we live, he says, if you stand fast. You can hear the relief in it, the way you hear it in somebody who has finally had a phone call they were waiting for.

The second thing they brought, it seems, was money. Paul says, in a later letter to Corinth, that the brothers who came from Macedonia supplied what he needed. The Christians of the north, who were not rich, had sent a gift so that Paul could stop sewing for a while.

And so, Luke says, from then on Paul gave himself completely to the word, telling everyone that the Messiah was Jesus.

The workshop was rather quieter after that. Paul still came back to it at night, and sat at the bench and mended things, because his hands did not know how to be still. But in the day he was out, in the synagogue and the market and the houses of anyone who would listen.

Nikon missed him. But there were compensations. Timothy, who was much younger than Paul and liked children, taught him to tie a sailmaker's knot that would not slip, and then a second one that came undone with a single pull, which was much more useful for annoying people.

* * *

It came to a head on a Sabbath not long after.

Luke tells it very briefly. The people in the synagogue who did not agree with Paul opposed him, and spoke against him, and insulted him. And Paul stood up, and took hold of his cloak, and shook it out, the way you shake the dust out of a cloth. That was an old gesture, and everybody knew what it meant. It meant, I have done all I can, and I am leaving, and what happens now is not on me.

Your blood be on your own heads, he said. I am clear of it. From now on I will go to the Gentiles.

And he walked out.

Now, that sounds like the end of something. And then Luke tells us where Paul went, and I am sorry, but it is one of the funniest sentences in the whole book, and I think Luke knew it.

Paul went to the house of a man called Titius Justus, a God-fearer, a Gentile who worshipped the God of Israel. And his house, Luke says, was right next door to the synagogue.

Right next door.

So Paul, having dramatically shaken out his cloak and walked out of the synagogue for ever, walked about twenty steps down the street and in through the next door along. And from then on, every Sabbath, people going into the synagogue could hear him through the wall.

Nikon, who had followed the whole business at a safe distance, stood in the street with his mouth open. Then he ran all the way back to the workshop to tell Priscilla.

"He's moved next door," he said. "Right next door. Into the man with the garden. You can see the synagogue from the step."

Priscilla put down her knife. For a moment Nikon thought she might be cross. Then she started to laugh, and she laughed until she had to sit down, and Aquila, at the other bench, shook his head slowly and smiled into his beard.

"Well," Priscilla said at last, wiping her eyes. "He did say he was going to the Gentiles. He didn't say how far."

But the next part, Luke says, was not funny at all. It was astonishing.

The ruler of the synagogue was a man called Crispus. The ruler of a synagogue was an important person: the one who arranged the services, chose who would read and who would preach, and looked after the building. And Crispus, Luke says, believed in the Lord, together with his whole household.

The man in charge of the synagogue next door. With his wife, and his children, and his servants. You can imagine the conversations in the street.

And after him, Luke says, many of the Corinthians who heard believed, and were baptized. Paul himself tells us a little more, in his first letter to them. He did not usually do the baptizing himself; he left that to others. But he remembers baptizing Crispus, and a man called Gaius, and the whole household of a man called Stephanas. He has to stop and think about it, in the letter, and you can see him counting on his fingers. I baptized none of you, he says, except Crispus and Gaius. Oh, and the household of Stephanas. Beyond that, I don't remember whether I baptized anyone else. I have always loved that. It is the most human sentence in the Bible about baptism, and it is there, I think, partly to show us that Paul did not want anybody saying they belonged to Paul. They belonged to Christ.

So the church in Corinth began. It met in houses. Not in grand buildings, but in a dining room here and a courtyard there. The house of Titius Justus, next to the synagogue. The house of Gaius, who later had the whole church round, Paul says. And, we can be fairly sure, the house of Aquila and Priscilla, with the hides hanging from the beams, and the benches pushed back against the wall.

* * *

And yet Paul was still afraid.

We know he was, partly because of what he wrote later, about weakness and fear and trembling. And we know it, mostly, because of what happened one night.

I will tell this part very simply, because Luke does.

One night, Luke says, the Lord spoke to Paul in a vision. And he said:

Do not be afraid. Go on speaking, and do not be silent. For I am with you, and nobody will lay a hand on you to harm you. For I have many people in this city.

That is all we are told. We are not told where Paul was, or what he saw, or whether he was asleep or awake. So I will not tell you either.

But I will tell you what I notice about it. The Lord does not say, there is nothing to be afraid of. Paul had been beaten and stoned and thrown out of town after town; he knew perfectly well there were things to be afraid of. What the Lord says is, I am with you. And then he says something that I think must have astonished Paul. I have many people in this city.

Corinth. Loud, rough, money-grubbing Corinth, with its sailors and its taverns and its temples to every god you could name. The city that Paul had walked into in fear and trembling. And the Lord looks at it and says, I have many people here. Most of them do not know it yet.

The next morning, let us say, Nikon was on his basket in the doorway of the workshop, eating a heel of his mother's bread, when Paul came back down the street from wherever he had spent the night. And Nikon thought that something about him was different. He could not have said what. Paul looked just as tired as ever, and just as dusty. But he walked more slowly, and looked at the street, and the people in it, and the shutters going up, and the ships' masts showing over the roofs, as if he were seeing them for the first time.

"Are you all right?" said Nikon.

Paul looked down at him, and smiled.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, I think I am. I think I'm going to stay a while."

And so he did. He stayed, Luke tells us, a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them. That is longer than he had stayed anywhere since he began travelling. Long enough for a church to put down roots. Long enough for Nikon to grow a whole hand's breadth, and learn three more knots, and be allowed, at last, to cut a straight seam himself, under Priscilla's eye.

* * *

And now we come back to where we started, to the bright, windy morning and the marble platform.

In the summer, probably of the year fifty-one, a new Roman governor arrived in Corinth to rule the province of Achaia for the year. His name was Gallio.

And here is one of my favourite things in the whole of the Acts of the Apostles. We know who Gallio was. We know a great deal about him, from outside the Bible. His full name was Lucius Junius Gallio, and he was the elder brother of Seneca, the famous Roman philosopher, the one who wrote all those wise books and letters about anger and calm and how to live a good life. Seneca loved his brother, and wrote about him with real affection. No one, he said, is as sweet to a single person as Gallio is to everyone.

Better still, a little more than a hundred years ago, archaeologists at Delphi, not far from Corinth, pieced together fragments of a stone inscription. It is a letter from the emperor Claudius, carved on a wall, and in it the emperor mentions, by name, Junius Gallio, my friend, and proconsul. The letter can be dated, from the emperor's titles, to about the year fifty-two. And because of that one battered stone, Gallio's year in Corinth is one of the few dates in Paul's whole life that we can fix with real confidence. Whole books on Paul's travels are built outward from it, like a house from a cornerstone.

So. Gallio arrived. And the people who opposed Paul saw their chance. A new governor might be persuaded. They rose up together against Paul, Luke says, and brought him before the judgment seat.

The judgment seat was a raised platform in the forum, where the governor sat to hear cases in public. In the ruins of Corinth today, in the middle of the old market square, there is a big platform of stone, with steps, and many people think it is the very one. We cannot be completely sure. But it is in the right place, and of the right kind, and if you go there you can stand in front of it and look up.

And there they stood, and the accusers made their case. This man, they said, is persuading people to worship God in ways that are against the law.

Nikon was there. Of course he was. He had seen them come for Paul at the workshop, and he had followed them all the way, ducking between people's legs, and now he was at the edge of the crowd, with his heart going like a drum.

Paul drew a breath. He opened his mouth to answer.

And Gallio raised his hand, and spoke first.

Here is what he said, in our plain rendering of Luke. If this were a matter of some crime, or some serious villainy, he said, then I would be right to listen to you. But since it is a question about words and names and your own law, see to it yourselves. I do not intend to be a judge of these things.

And he had them driven away from the judgment seat.

Paul never got to say a word.

Nikon let out his breath all at once, so loudly that the man next to him looked round.

The governor of all Achaia, the brother of the great Seneca, had looked at the case and decided that it was simply not his business. To him, it was a quarrel among Jews about religion, and Rome did not judge such things. He was, if you like, bored. And because he was bored, Paul was free. It is not the most heroic way to be rescued. But it is exactly what the Lord had said. Nobody will lay a hand on you to harm you.

Luke adds one more sentence, and I will tell it quietly, because it is not a happy one. The crowd turned, and seized Sosthenes, who was now the ruler of the synagogue, and beat him there in front of the judgment seat. And Gallio paid no attention to any of it.

That is all Luke says. We do not know exactly who did the beating, or why. But here is something to set beside it. When Paul wrote his First Letter to the Corinthians, a few years later, he began it like this: Paul, called to be an apostle of Christ Jesus, and our brother Sosthenes. A Sosthenes, standing beside Paul, a brother in Christ. Whether he was the same man, nobody can say for certain; it was not a rare name. But many people, all down the centuries, have hoped that he was. That the man beaten in front of the judgment seat for being on the wrong side of that quarrel later found himself on Paul's side. And the Orthodox Church counts a Sosthenes, and a Crispus too, among the seventy disciples of the Lord. I like to think it is true.

As for Gallio, I will tell you what his brother Seneca says about him a few years later, in one of his letters. Gallio, he says, began to have a fever while he was in Achaia, and immediately got on a ship, saying that the illness was not in his body but in the place. So it seems Gallio did not like Corinth much either. Perhaps the Corinthians did not mind.

[pause]

That is nearly the end of the story.

After Gallio, Luke says, Paul stayed on in Corinth for many more days. And then, at last, he said goodbye to the brothers and sisters, and set out to sail for Syria, back towards the east, and home.

And he did not go alone. Priscilla and Aquila went with him.

They shut up the workshop, I suppose. They sold what they could not carry, and packed the knives and the awls and the needles, because wherever they went next, people would still need tents. And they walked, with Paul, the few miles east along the road to the other harbour, the one on the eastern sea, called Cenchreae.

Let us say that Nikon walked with them. All the way to the harbour, with his mother beside him, and a bag of bread for the journey that she had baked that morning, and a knot in his throat that would not come undone with a single pull.

At Cenchreae, Luke tells us one more odd little thing. Paul had his hair cut, because he had taken a vow. Luke does not say what kind of vow. It was probably something like the old vow in the Book of Numbers, where a person who wanted to give a time of their life especially to God let their hair grow, and then cut it at the end, as a sign that the vow was complete. Perhaps Paul had made it on the night of the vision. Perhaps it was a thank you, for the year and a half, and for the many people in that city. We do not know. The Greek is even a little unclear about whose hair it was, and a few scholars think it was Aquila's. But most people have always read it as Paul's, and I will too.

And then they went down to the water. The ship was waiting, with its sail loose and its ropes creaking. The sun was going down behind the Acrocorinth, and the sea in front of them was going grey and gold. Paul put his hand on Nikon's head, and said something to him that nobody else heard. Priscilla hugged him so hard his ribs creaked. Aquila, who never said very much, gave him his second-best awl.

And then the ship slid out of the harbour, and turned east, and got smaller, and smaller, and was gone.

* * *

They were bound for Syria, but they did not go there straight away. The ship put in at Ephesus, the great city across the sea in Asia, and there Priscilla and Aquila stayed, and set up another workshop, and Paul promised to come back, and did.

And Priscilla and Aquila kept on doing what they had done in Corinth. In Ephesus, a brilliant young preacher called Apollos turned up, eloquent and on fire, who knew a great deal but had a few things not quite right. And Luke says that Priscilla and Aquila took him aside, quietly, into their home, and explained the way of God to him more accurately. Not in public. Not to embarrass him. Over supper, I expect, with the hides hanging from the beams. Apollos went on to Corinth, and helped the church there enormously.

Later still, when the emperor's order had lapsed and Jews could go back to Rome, they went back to Rome, and there was a church that met in their house there too. And when Paul wrote his letter to the Romans, he sent greetings to them first of all. Greet Prisca and Aquila, he says, my fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their necks for my life, to whom not only I but all the churches of the Gentiles give thanks. We do not know when they risked their necks for him. They never made a fuss about it, it seems, and neither did he. Just, they did.

Saints Aquila and Priscilla are remembered on the eighth of July in the Roman Martyrology, and the Orthodox Church keeps their memory on the thirteenth of February. Craftspeople have always been rather fond of them, and so have married couples, which seems right.

And the church in Corinth? It grew, and squabbled, and grew again. It gave Paul a great deal of trouble. He wrote them at least two long letters, which we still have, full of scolding and worry and argument, and full of some of the most beautiful words ever written. One of them is the passage about love, the one that gets read at weddings. Love is patient, love is kind. Paul wrote that to Corinth. To the people from the back streets and the workshops and the houses round the corner from the synagogue. To people like Nikon.

The city is ruins now, and very quiet. The great rock of the Acrocorinth still stands over it. You can walk down the old road from the market to the harbour, and see the stone platform in the square, and the shops along the street where the craftsmen worked. Near the theatre there is a stretch of old pavement with an inscription cut into it, saying that a man called Erastus laid it at his own expense, in return for being made a city official. And at the end of his letter to the Romans, Paul sends greetings from Erastus, the city treasurer. It may be the same man. It may not. But there the name is, cut into the stone, under your feet.

And the isthmus, the narrow neck of land, is not a neck any more. The emperor Nero tried to dig a canal through it, a few years after Paul, and turned the first earth himself, and then the work was abandoned. It was finally cut at the end of the eighteen hundreds, a deep straight channel with sheer rock walls, and the ships go through it now, slowly, in a line.

But in the evening, when it is quiet, if you stand at the old harbour of Cenchreae, where some of the stones of the ancient quay are still there under the water, you can watch the sun go down behind the mountain. The sea goes grey and gold. And you can think about a tired man who walked into a city in fear and trembling, and found two friends, and a bench, and a needle and thread, and work to do, and many people in that city who did not yet know they were the Lord's.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter eighteen, verses one to eighteen, with a little from verses nineteen to twenty-six about Ephesus and Apollos. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened, and everything he says Paul, the Lord and Gallio said, is kept, in our own plain rendering, with nothing added.

Paul's weakness and fear at Corinth, his working with his own hands, the gift from Macedonia, and the people he remembered baptizing, are from his letters to the Corinthians. His relief at Timothy's news is from the First Letter to the Thessalonians, which most scholars think he wrote from Corinth. Priscilla and Aquila risking their necks, and Erastus the treasurer, are from the Letter to the Romans. Chrysostom's admiration for Priscilla is from his two sermons on Greet Priscilla and Aquila.

The expulsion from Rome is in Suetonius, and whether his Chrestus means Christ is still argued. The date is from Orosius. Gallio's office is fixed by the inscription from Delphi, and his fever is in Seneca's letter one hundred and four. Whether the platform in the ruins is the one in Acts, whether Paul's trade was leather or goat-hair cloth, whether the Isthmian Games fell while he was there, and whether the Sosthenes of the letters is the Sosthenes of Acts, are all open questions, and we have told them so.

Nikon, his mother Melitta, the workshop's customers, the knots, the awl, and the farewell at the harbour are our own invention, and so are everybody's thoughts.

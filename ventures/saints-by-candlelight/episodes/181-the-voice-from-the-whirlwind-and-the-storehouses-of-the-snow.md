There is a man sitting on the ground, outside a town, in the ashes, and the wind is getting up.

It has been getting up for a while. First it was just a stirring of the dust, the way a wind starts on a hot afternoon. Then the light changed. The sky to the north went the colour of a bruise, and then, strangely, along its edge, the colour of gold. Somewhere far off, there was thunder, rolling round the hills like a barrel down a flight of stairs.

Four men are sitting with him. One of them, the youngest, has been talking for a very long time, and has just noticed that nobody is listening to him any more. They are all looking up.

The dust lifts off the ground in a long twisting rope. The cloaks of the men flap and crack. And out of the middle of the wind, out of the noise and the gold and the dark, there comes a voice.

And the first thing it does is ask a question.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Book of Job, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is the great speech that God makes from the whirlwind, about the snow and the stars and the wild animals, and the pictures of what Job saw while he listened, and my descriptions of the animals, are our own, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Last time we visited the Book of Job, we went to the very last page. We met Job as an old man, after everything, naming three little daughters after a dove, and a spice, and a little horn of eye paint. And on the way to that last page, I said that God had answered Job out of the whirlwind, and then I hurried on, because we had a family to get to.

Tonight we are going back. Tonight we are going to stand in the wind and listen.

And I want to say, right at the start, that I think this is one of the best bedtime passages in the whole Bible, which may surprise you. It is a storm, after all. And it begins with God sounding, frankly, rather stern. But if you listen to what he actually talks about, it is not what you would expect. He does not give a lecture. He does not explain why bad things happened to Job. Instead, for two whole chapters, he talks about the weather. And the stars. And baby ravens, and mountain goats having their kids on the cliffs, and a donkey who refuses to live in town, and a bird who is not very good with eggs but can run faster than a horse.

It is the longest speech God makes anywhere in the Old Testament. And it is very nearly a nature documentary.

So let me remind you, quickly, how we got here.

There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job. Nobody now knows exactly where Uz was. Somewhere to the east or south of the land of Israel, out towards the desert, perhaps in the country of Edom, perhaps further. It does not much matter. It is a far-off country, the way that once upon a time is a far-off time.

Job was good. The book says so in the first line. He was blameless and upright, he feared God and turned away from evil. And he was rich, in the way people were rich in that world: seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred yoke of oxen, five hundred donkeys, and a great many servants. He had seven sons and three daughters. And he loved them so much that whenever they had a party, which was often, he got up early the next morning and offered a sacrifice for each of them, just in case one of them had done something silly and said something unkind about God. Which is, I think, the most father-like thing anybody has ever done.

And then, in a single day, he lost almost everything. The animals were stolen or killed. A great wind struck the house where his children were eating together, and they died. And then he fell ill, with sores from head to foot, so that he went and sat outside the town, on the heap of ashes where the rubbish was burned, and scraped himself with a piece of broken pot.

The book tells us, though Job does not know it, that all this was a test. That God allowed it, and that Satan, the accuser, had said that Job was only good because he was comfortable. Job never finds this out. Not once, in the whole book, does anybody tell him.

Then three friends came to see him. Eliphaz, and Bildad, and Zophar. And they did the best thing friends can do: they sat with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights and did not say a word, because they saw that his grief was very great.

And then they started talking. Which was less good.

* * *

Most of the Book of Job is talking. Chapter after chapter of it, in poetry, as beautiful as anything ever written. Job speaks, and a friend answers. Job speaks again, and the next friend answers. Round and round, three times over.

The friends say, more or less, this: God is fair. Good things happen to good people and bad things happen to bad people. So, Job, since all these bad things have happened to you, you must have done something. Think hard. Confess it. Say sorry. And everything will be all right.

And Job says: I have not. I have thought and thought, and I have not. I did not cheat anyone. I did not turn away the poor. I did not let the widow wait at my gate. If I had, I would say so. And still this has happened. And I do not understand it. And I want to ask God, to his face, why.

It is not that Job stops believing in God. That is the strange and wonderful thing about him. He never stops believing. He just will not pretend that what has happened makes sense. He wants a hearing. In one place he says he wishes he could write out his case and sign it, the way you sign a legal paper, and have God answer it. Here is my signature, he says. Let the Almighty answer me.

The friends are horrified. You cannot talk to God like that!

And then, near the end, a fourth man arrives. He is young, and his name is Elihu, and he has been waiting politely for the old men to finish, and now he cannot hold it in any longer. He says that he is so full of words that he feels like a new wineskin that is about to burst, which is a very honest thing to say about yourself, and then he talks for six chapters.

I will be fair to Elihu. He says some good things. And right at the end of his speech, he starts describing a storm. He talks about the thunder, about God's voice rolling in the clouds, about the snow and the rain, about the lightning that God sends out, about how a man cannot look at the sun when the wind has cleared the sky. And then he says something lovely. He says, out of the north comes golden splendour. Around God is terrible majesty.

And I always imagine that while he is saying it, he is describing what is actually happening. That a real storm is coming up over the hills behind him, while he talks. And that the others are looking past him, at the sky.

Because the very next line of the book, the first line of chapter thirty-eight, is this:

Then the Lord answered Job out of the whirlwind.

* * *

Not the friends. Not Elihu. Job.

That is the first thing to notice. Job asked for a hearing, and God has come. He came to the man who argued with him, not to the men who argued for him. Remember that. It will matter at the end.

And here is how he begins. I am going to give you the words in my own plain English, as close as I can to the old Hebrew.

Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge? Brace yourself like a man. I will question you, and you shall answer me.

That is stern, yes. Brace yourself, or, in the old translations, gird up your loins, means tuck up your long robe into your belt, the way a man did before he ran, or worked, or wrestled. Get ready. You wanted to ask me questions. Very well. I have a few of my own.

And then the questions begin. And from the very first one, they go somewhere Job could never have expected.

Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding. Who decided how big it should be? Surely you know. Who stretched the measuring line across it? What are its footings sunk in? Who laid its cornerstone, when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?

Now, I want you to picture it the way a man of that world would have pictured it. The earth as a building. A great house. God as the builder. He measures it out with a line, the way a builder pegs out the shape of a house on the ground with string. He sinks the footings. And then comes the moment, the moment every builder knows, when the cornerstone is laid. The first stone, the one that everything else lines up with.

In that part of the world, and in a great many others, laying the cornerstone was a party. People came. There was singing. And so, God says, there was singing at the laying of the world's cornerstone. The morning stars sang together. The sons of God shouted for joy.

The old Christian readers took the sons of God here to be the angels. And so this is one of the very few places in the whole Bible where we are told what the angels were doing at the beginning of the world. They were not standing about looking serious. They were cheering. They were shouting for joy, the way children shout when the first snow comes.

And Job, of course, was not there. Nobody was there. That is the point of the question. Where were you? You were not anywhere. You had not been thought of yet. Neither had I, and neither had you, listening to this in the dark. But the stars were singing.

I find that very comforting, actually. Whatever happened before any of us arrived, it began with singing.

* * *

The voice goes on. And now it turns to the sea.

Who shut in the sea with doors, when it burst out from the womb? When I made the clouds its clothes, and thick darkness its swaddling bands, and set a limit for it, and fixed bars and doors, and said, this far you may come, and no further, and here your proud waves shall stop?

Listen to what is happening there. The sea, in the old stories of that whole part of the world, was the great monster. The wild, roaring, frightening thing. The chaos that every god in every neighbouring nation had to fight. And here, in the Bible, God speaks of the sea as a baby. A newborn. It bursts out of the womb, and God wraps it up, the way a mother or a midwife wraps a baby, in swaddling bands, so that its arms do not flail. Except that the swaddling bands are the clouds, and the thick darkness.

And then he puts it to bed. He gives it a room with a door. This far, and no further.

If you have ever stood on a beach at night and listened to the waves come up the sand, and stop, and go back, and come up again, and stop, you have heard that sentence being obeyed. This far, and no further. Every night since the beginning.

And then the morning.

Have you ever, in your whole life, given orders to the morning? Have you shown the dawn its place, so that it takes hold of the edges of the earth and shakes the wicked out of it? The earth changes like clay under a seal, and stands out like a garment.

That is one of my favourite pictures in the whole book, and it needs a little explaining. In that world, people did not sign their names. They had seals. Some were little stone cylinders, carved all round with a picture, and you rolled the cylinder across a piece of soft wet clay, and the picture came up, raised, on the clay. Before you rolled it, the clay was just a flat grey lump. After you rolled it, there were people on it, and trees, and animals.

And that, God says, is what the dawn does to the earth. In the night, the land is all one grey flatness. You cannot see anything. And then the light comes, low and sideways, and rolls across the hills, and suddenly there are shapes. Trees. Walls. A flock of sheep. A road. The whole thing comes up out of the dark like a picture out of clay. Or, he says, like a garment being dyed, the colour coming into it.

And the wicked are shaken out of it, the way you would shake crumbs out of a tablecloth in the morning. Because the people who did their bad work in the dark do not like the light.

Have you done that, Job? Have you ever once made the sun come up?

No. Nor have I. It comes up anyway. It will come up tomorrow, while you are still asleep, and nobody will need to tell it.

* * *

And now the voice goes further. Down, under the sea. Up, past the clouds.

Have you gone down to the springs of the sea? Have you walked about in the depths? Have the gates of death been shown to you? Have you understood how wide the earth is? Tell me, if you know all this.

Where is the way to where the light lives? And the darkness, where is its home, so that you could take it back to its own country, and show it the path to its house?

And then, a little joke. Because there are jokes in this speech, if you listen for them. God says, you know, surely. You were born then. You are so very, very old.

Which is a kind way, really, of saying: you are not. You are a man sitting on an ash heap, and you are tired, and you are very young, compared with the light.

And then, the line that gave tonight's story its name.

Have you gone into the storehouses of the snow? Have you seen the storehouses of the hail?

I love this. I love the picture of the snow being kept somewhere. In a great barn, up in the sky, in heaps, like grain after a harvest. Waiting to be let out. Each flake ready. And the hail in another storehouse next door, in its own great bins, kept for a time of trouble.

Snow does fall in that part of the world, by the way. Not often in the low places. But in the hills it comes, some winters. Jerusalem gets snow, once every few years, and when it does the whole city stops and the children go out and throw it at each other and everybody takes pictures. So the people who first heard this knew what snow was. They had seen it come out of a grey sky, quietly, from nowhere, and lie on the hills. And they might well have asked: where was it all kept, before?

And God says, I know. I have been there. Have you?

What is the way to the place where the light is shared out, or where the east wind is scattered over the earth? Who has cut a channel for the downpour, and a road for the thunderbolt, to bring rain on a land where no one lives, on the desert where there is nobody at all, to soak the wild and empty ground, and make the grass spring up?

Now stop there for a moment, because this is one of the most important sentences in the whole speech, and it is very easy to miss.

Rain on a land where no one lives.

All the way through the book, Job and his friends have been arguing about people. About what God does to people, and why. About whether God is fair to people. And here God says: I also send rain where there are no people at all. On the desert. On the empty land. Just so that the grass will grow. Grass that nobody will ever graze, or cut, or walk on, or even see.

Why would he do that? There is no use in it, for us. Nobody gets anything out of it.

And the answer, I think, is: because he loves the grass. Because the world is not only about us. Because God is the kind of God who waters a hillside that nobody will ever visit, simply so that it can be green. There are flowers opening tonight, in places where no person will ever stand, and he made each of them, and he watered each of them, and he knows each of them.

That does not answer Job's question. But it does make the world a great deal larger than the question.

* * *

The voice goes on, and now it gets almost playful.

Does the rain have a father? Who is the father of the drops of dew? Out of whose womb did the ice come? And the frost of heaven, who gave birth to it? The waters hide themselves and go hard like stone, and the face of the deep is frozen.

If you have ever gone out on a cold morning and found every blade of grass coated in frost, every one, separately, with its own white fur, you will know why someone might ask who gave birth to it. It looks made. It looks as if somebody has been out in the night with a very small brush.

And then the stars.

Can you tie up the chains of the Pleiades, or loosen the cords of Orion? Can you lead out the constellations in their season, or guide the Bear with her children? Do you know the laws of the heavens? Can you set up their rule over the earth?

Some of you will know the Pleiades. They are the little cluster of stars, sometimes called the Seven Sisters, that you can see on a winter evening, like a small handful of sugar thrown on a dark table. And Orion is the hunter, with his belt of three stars in a row. The prophet Amos mentions the same two: seek him who made the Pleiades and Orion, he says, who turns deep darkness into the morning. So these were stars that shepherds and farmers in that country knew well. They watched them come up, season by season, and told the time of year by them.

I should be honest and say that the old Hebrew names of the stars here are not all certain. The word I have called the constellations is a word that appears only here, Mazzaroth, and nobody is quite sure what it meant. And the Bear with her children may be the Great Bear, the stars some people call the Plough or the Big Dipper, with the smaller stars around it. That is how many translators take it. But the scholars still argue, and I will not pretend they have settled it.

But the point is clear enough. Can you hold the stars together, Job? Can you take a handful of them, like a string of beads, and tie the knot? Can you take Orion's belt off him?

No. And yet there they are. Tonight, if the sky is clear where you are, they will be there. The same ones Job saw. Held together, still, by somebody.

* * *

Can you shout up to the clouds, so that a flood of water covers you? Can you send out the lightning bolts, so that they go, and come back to you, and say, here we are?

I love that. The lightning, reporting back. Here we are, sir. Like a dog coming back with a stick. Or a very bright and very obedient servant.

And then there is a line where the old Hebrew is difficult again. It says, more or less, who has put wisdom in the inward parts, or who has given understanding to the mind. But some of the words are rare, and some scholars think they are the names of birds. One of them may be the ibis, the long-legged bird of Egypt that was supposed to know when the Nile would flood. Another may be the rooster.

And here I have to tell you something charming. Saint Jerome, when he translated this line into Latin, sixteen hundred years ago, read it that way. His Latin says: who gave the rooster understanding? And the old Catholic English translation made from his Latin, the Douay, says the same: who gave the cock understanding?

Why would a rooster need understanding? Well. Because he knows when to crow. In the dark, before anybody else knows the morning is coming, he knows. He is the first to say so. And the old Christian writers loved that. Saint Ambrose of Milan wrote a hymn about the rooster's crow, a hymn for the very early morning, about how it wakes the sleeper and cheers the traveller and makes the sailor brave, and how it was the rooster's crow that made Saint Peter remember, and weep, and turn back. That hymn is still sung at morning prayer in some places today.

I cannot tell you for certain that Jerome was right about the Hebrew. But I am very glad he got the rooster in.

And then: who can count the clouds by wisdom? Who can tip over the water jars of the heavens, when the dust runs into lumps and the clods stick together?

That is a picture of rain after a long drought. The ground has gone to dust, and then the rain comes, and the dust turns into mud and clumps together. And the rain comes because someone has tipped the great water jars of the sky. Like a woman tipping a water jar over a garden. Like a gardener with a watering can.

* * *

And now, the animals.

This is where I always lean forward, because the speech, which has been all sky and sea and weather, suddenly turns to living things, and the voice seems to get warmer.

Can you hunt prey for the lioness, or fill up the hunger of the young lions, when they crouch in their dens, or lie in wait in the thicket?

Who gives the raven his food, when his young ones cry out to God, and wander about with nothing to eat?

Now, the raven. In the Law of Moses, the raven was an unclean bird. You were not to eat it. It was not a bird that anyone kept, or bred, or was fond of. It was the big black noisy bird that hung around the rubbish heaps, perhaps even the very rubbish heap where Job was sitting.

And God says: who feeds the raven's chicks? When they cry out, who are they crying to? To me. They cry out to God. And I hear them.

Many hundreds of years later, a young teacher from Nazareth stood on a hillside in Galilee and said to the crowd, consider the ravens. They neither sow nor reap. They have no storehouse and no barn. And God feeds them. How much more valuable are you than the birds? I think he knew this chapter of Job very well. I think he had sat with it, as a boy.

And the Psalms say the same, more than once. The young lions roar for their prey, seeking their food from God. He gives the beasts their food, and the young ravens that cry. It is one of the oldest and simplest things in the Bible. The world is fed. Every morning. Not by us.

* * *

Do you know when the mountain goats give birth? Have you watched the deer when she calves? Can you count the months they carry their young, and do you know the time when they give birth? They crouch down, and bring out their little ones, and are done with their labour. Their young grow strong, and grow up out in the open. They go off, and do not come back.

The mountain goats in that part of the world are the ibex. If you go to a place called En-gedi, by the Dead Sea, you can still see them today. They are tough, brown, sure-footed goats, the males with enormous curved horns like the handles of a bicycle turned backwards, and they live on the cliffs above the oasis, on ledges that look as if nothing heavier than a sparrow could stand on them. The Bible even mentions the place. When David was hiding from King Saul in the wilderness of En-gedi, the book says Saul went looking for him on the Rocks of the Wild Goats.

And the ibex have their kids up there, on the cliffs. Alone. No shepherd. No barn. No one to help. Within a very short time the kids are hopping about on the rock behind their mothers.

Do you know when that happens, Job? Did you count the months? Were you there to help?

No. But someone was watching. That is what the question means. Somebody knew the day. Somebody saw each little one come into the world on its ledge, and stand up on its wobbly legs, and look out over the Dead Sea for the first time. And it was not us.

And the young ones grow strong, and go off, and do not come back. Which is what young ones do. Which is, I suppose, what Job's own children had done, in a different and much sadder way. I do not think God says that by accident. But he says it very gently. They grow up in the open. They go off.

* * *

Now the voice turns to two animals that will not do as they are told.

Who let the wild donkey go free? Who untied the ropes of the swift donkey? I gave him the wilderness for his home, and the salt land for his dwelling. He laughs at the noise of the town. He does not hear the shouts of the driver. He ranges over the mountains for his pasture, and he searches for every green thing.

The wild donkey is the onager, the wild ass of the steppes. It is a lovely animal, pale and sandy-coloured, quick and light, and it has never in all of history agreed to be tamed. People tried. People in the old empires of that part of the world tried very hard. And the wild ass looked at them, and their carts, and their towns, and their drivers shouting giddy-up, and laughed, and went off into the salt desert, where nobody could follow, and ate whatever green things it could find.

And God says: I did that. I let him go. I gave him the desert. He is not yours, and he is not supposed to be.

I love the thought of the town donkey, with his bundles, plodding up the street with the driver shouting at him, and somewhere far away, out in the shimmer, his wild cousin standing on a ridge with the wind in his mane, not listening to anybody.

And then the second one.

Will the wild ox be willing to serve you? Will he spend the night at your feeding trough? Can you tie him in the furrow with a rope? Will he harrow the valleys behind you? Will you trust him, because his strength is great, and leave your heavy work to him? Will you rely on him to bring home your grain, and gather it to your threshing floor?

The wild ox was almost certainly the aurochs. The great wild cattle of the ancient world, from which all our cows are descended. But much, much bigger than a cow. A bull aurochs stood as tall as a man at the shoulder, black, with long forward-curving horns, and a temper to match. You did not put a yoke on an aurochs. You did not ask him to pull your plough. You stayed well away from him, and hoped he had not noticed you.

The aurochs is gone now, I am sorry to say. The very last one we know of was a cow who died in a forest in Poland in the year sixteen hundred and twenty-seven. But when the Book of Job was written, they were still out there, in the hills and the river valleys, enormous and free.

And here is the fun part. The old Greek translation of the Bible, made before the time of Christ, did not quite know what this animal was, and called it a unicorn. A one-horned beast. And the King James Bible in English followed the Greek, and so in the King James, God asks Job, will the unicorn be willing to serve thee? Saint Jerome's Latin, in this chapter, made yet another guess, and called it a rhinoceros. So depending on which old Bible you open, God is asking Job about a wild ox, a unicorn, or a rhinoceros.

I think the point is the same whichever you pick. There are strong things in the world that will never wear your harness. And they are still his.

* * *

And then comes the ostrich. And the ostrich, I think, is where God is very nearly smiling.

The wings of the ostrich flap with joy. But are they the wings and the feathers of a stork?

That line, too, is hard in the old Hebrew. But it seems to be a joke. The stork, in Hebrew, has a name that means something like the faithful one, or the loving one, because storks were known for being devoted parents. And the ostrich, God says, flaps her wings very cheerfully. But she is no stork.

For she leaves her eggs on the ground, and lets them be warmed in the dust, and forgets that a foot may crush them, or a wild animal may tread on them. She is hard with her young ones, as if they were not hers. She does not worry that her work might be wasted. Because God has made her forget wisdom, and has given her no share in understanding.

And then the turn.

But when she spreads her wings to run, she laughs at the horse and his rider.

That is wonderful. That is God, describing one of his creatures, saying more or less: well. She is not clever. She is not, by the standards of other birds, a very attentive mother. She lays her eggs in a hollow in the sand. She looks, to anyone watching, as if she has wandered off and left them. But oh, can she run.

And she can. An ostrich can run faster than a horse. On those great long legs, across open ground, she can leave a galloping horse and rider behind and look back at them over her shoulder. The people who first heard this would have seen it happen, and laughed.

I should say, to be fair to ostriches everywhere, that they are rather better parents than the poem makes them sound. People who study them say that both the mother and the father take turns sitting on the eggs, and the father often does the nights. The eggs are laid in a shallow scrape in the ground, and in the hot middle of the day the parents may leave them for a while in the sun. So to an onlooker in the old days, it may well have looked as though she had simply forgotten them. The poem is speaking the way the shepherds and travellers saw her. And it is a poem.

But the deeper point is lovely. God made the ostrich. Not clever. Not careful. A bit silly. And he made her joyful, and he made her fast, and he is plainly fond of her. You do not have to be wise for God to delight in you. You do not have to be the cleverest bird in the desert. You can be an ostrich, flapping your wings for no reason, and he will still tell the story of you, at length, with a smile, to a man on an ash heap.

* * *

And then the horse. And this is the grandest thing in the whole speech.

Do you give the horse his strength? Do you clothe his neck with a thundering mane? Do you make him leap like a locust? His proud snorting is terrible. He paws the ground in the valley, and delights in his strength. He goes out to meet the weapons. He laughs at fear. He is not afraid. He does not turn back from the sword. The quiver rattles against him, the flashing spear and the javelin. With trembling and rage he swallows the ground. He cannot stand still when the trumpet sounds. When the trumpet blows, he says, aha! He smells the battle from far away, the thunder of the captains, and the shouting.

Now, that is a war horse. And I am not going to dwell on the battle, because it is bedtime, and the poem does not dwell on it either. The poem is not interested in the fighting. It is interested in the horse. In how brave he is. In how he stamps, and snorts, and cannot keep still, and the moment he hears the trumpet he throws his head up and says, aha!

That aha is the real word in the Hebrew, more or less. It is the noise you make when you have just been told the thing you have been waiting all day for. And the horse says it.

If you have ever stood near a big horse when it is excited, you will know. The ground seems to shake a little. The whole animal hums, like a kettle about to boil. It is a magnificent and slightly alarming thing to stand beside.

Did you make that, Job? Did you put the thunder in his neck?

* * *

And then, last of all, the birds of the high air.

Is it by your understanding that the hawk soars, and spreads his wings towards the south? Is it at your command that the eagle mounts up, and makes his nest on high? He lives on the cliff, and stays on the crag, in the stronghold of the rock. From there he looks out for food, and his eyes see it from far away.

Spreads his wings towards the south. That is migration. Every autumn, in that part of the world, the hawks and eagles and buzzards and storks go south. Down the long valley of the Jordan, down past the Dead Sea, along the hills, towards Africa, in their thousands and thousands. Even today, the skies over that land are one of the great highways of the world for migrating birds. People come from all over to stand on the hills in the autumn and watch them go over, wave after wave, riding the warm air, not flapping, just turning in great slow circles and sliding away to the south.

Did you teach them that, Job? Did you give them the map?

And the eagle on the cliff, at the very top, where nobody can reach, looking out over the whole country with those eyes that can see a mouse move from a mile up. Did you build his nest? Did you tell him where?

And then the voice stops.

* * *

[pause]

And the Lord said to Job: shall a fault-finder argue with the Almighty? He who argues with God, let him answer.

And Job answered.

Here is what he said. It is very short, after all those chapters of speeches.

Look. I am small. What can I answer you? I lay my hand on my mouth. I have spoken once, and I will not answer. Twice, and I will go no further.

I lay my hand on my mouth.

Now, I want to be careful here, because it is very easy to read that wrongly. It is easy to think that God has simply shouted Job down. That a very big voice has told a very small man to be quiet, and he has been quiet. But that is not what has happened.

Think about what Job asked for. He asked for God to come. He asked for an answer. And God came. In person. And spoke to him, for a very long time, as you would speak to someone you were taking seriously. Not to the friends who had said all the right things about God. To Job, who had argued. That is a great honour. You do not walk out into a storm to talk to someone you do not care about.

And what did God say? He did not explain the test. He did not mention Satan, or the bet, or the reason. He did not tell Job, it was all for a good purpose, and here it is, in three points. Instead he took Job by the hand, as it were, and walked him round the whole of creation. Look at the sea, being put to bed. Look at the dawn coming up like a picture on clay. Look at the snow in its storehouses. Look at the rain falling on the desert where nobody lives. Look at the stars. Look at the raven's chicks, and the goats on the cliffs, and the wild donkey laughing at the town, and the ostrich running, and the horse saying aha, and the hawk going south.

I know all of these, God is saying. I made them. I feed them. I know when each one is born. I water places you will never see. I am looking after a great deal more than you can imagine.

And so, perhaps, I am looking after you.

He never says that last line out loud. But I think Job heard it. Because Job, who had been so full of words, chapter after chapter, suddenly has nothing to say. Not because he has been beaten. But because he has seen something too big to argue with. Like a man who has been complaining all day about the size of his room, and is taken outside at night, and looks up, and sees the Milky Way for the first time.

You do not argue with the Milky Way. You just stand there, with your hand over your mouth.

* * *

[pause]

There is more of the speech, after that. God speaks again, and tells Job about two more creatures, two enormous ones. One is called Behemoth, a great beast who lies under the lotus plants in the marsh, with bones like bars of iron, who is not bothered when the river rises. Many readers think he is the hippopotamus. And the other is Leviathan, the great creature of the sea, whom no one can catch with a fish hook, or lead about on a string like a pet for the girls. Some think of the crocodile. Some think of the sea monster of the old stories, tamed, and turned into one of God's creatures, a creature God made, he says in a psalm, to play in the sea.

And then Job speaks once more, at the beginning of chapter forty-two, and says the line that everybody remembers. I had heard of you with my ears. But now my eyes have seen you.

And after that comes the last page, which we told some time ago. Job prays for his friends, and God is angry with the friends, not with Job, because Job had spoken of God what was right, and they had not. And Job's brothers and sisters come to eat bread with him, and his fortunes come back twice over, and there are seven new sons, and three new daughters, with the most beautiful names in the Bible. And he lives to see his great-great-grandchildren, and dies an old man, full of days.

Many centuries later, Saint James wrote a letter to the first Christians, and in it he says: you have heard of the patience of Job, and you have seen what the Lord did in the end, how the Lord is very compassionate, and merciful. That is the whole book in one sentence.

And Saint Gregory the Great, who wrote an enormous commentary on Job, thirty-five books of it, said in one place that the Scripture is like a river, shallow enough for a lamb to wade in, and deep enough for an elephant to swim. I think the speech from the whirlwind is exactly like that. You could spend your life on it, and Gregory nearly did. Or you could just listen to it, the way a child listens, and hear about the snow, and the ravens, and the ostrich who laughs at the horse. And both would be right.

The Church still sings this sort of thing. In the Book of Daniel, which Catholics and Orthodox both read in full, there is a long hymn that three young men sang in a furnace, and it goes through the whole of creation, just like God does in Job. Bless the Lord, sun and moon. Bless the Lord, stars of heaven. Bless the Lord, frost and cold. Bless the Lord, ice and snow. Bless the Lord, nights and days. Bless the Lord, all birds of the air. Bless the Lord, all beasts, wild and tame. That hymn is sung at morning prayer, in both East and West, to this day. And whenever I hear it, I think of Job, with his hand over his mouth, listening to the storm.

And the storm, I imagine, passed. Storms do. The wind dropped. The dust settled on the ash heap. The gold went out of the northern sky, and the evening came on, very clear and clean, the way it does after rain. And perhaps Job looked up, that night, and saw the Pleiades come out, the little handful of sugar on the dark table, and Orion with his belt, still fastened. And knew who was holding them.

Tonight, while you sleep, all of it is still going on.

Somewhere, the snow is falling quietly on a mountain where nobody is.

Somewhere, rain is falling on a desert, and the grass is coming up, for no one, for God.

Somewhere, a raven chick is crying in a nest, and is about to be fed.

On the cliffs above En-gedi, the ibex are asleep on their ledges, with their kids tucked in against them.

Out on the salt flats, the wild donkey is standing in the starlight, listening to nobody.

The sea is coming up the sand, and stopping, and going back. This far, and no further.

And in a few hours, without anybody telling it to, the dawn will take hold of the edges of the earth, and roll across the hills, and everything will come up out of the dark like a picture out of clay.

And the rooster will know first.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Job, chapter thirty-eight to chapter forty, verse five, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. The story of Job before that, his losses, the friends, and Elihu, comes from chapters one to thirty-seven, summed up very briefly; Job's wish to sign his case is in chapter thirty-one, and Elihu's golden splendour from the north is in chapter thirty-seven. Behemoth and Leviathan are in chapters forty and forty-one, and Job's last answer and the end of the book in chapter forty-two. Some of the old Hebrew words in this speech are rare and uncertain, including the names of the stars, the line about wisdom in the inward parts, and the line about the ostrich and the stork, and I have said so where it matters. Saint Jerome's rooster is in his Latin, chapter thirty-eight, verse thirty-six, and the unicorn and the rhinoceros are the old Greek and Latin readings of chapter thirty-nine, verse nine. The ravens in the Gospels are in Luke, chapter twelve, and the birds of the air in Matthew, chapter six. The lions seeking their food from God are in Psalm one hundred and four, and the young ravens that cry in Psalm one hundred and forty-seven. The Pleiades and Orion also appear in Amos, chapter five. The Rocks of the Wild Goats are in First Samuel, chapter twenty-four. The Song of the Three Young Men is in Daniel, chapter three. Saint James on the patience of Job is in his letter, chapter five. The river shallow enough for a lamb is from the letter that opens Saint Gregory's Morals on Job. Saint Ambrose's rooster hymn begins, in Latin, Aeterne rerum conditor. What science says about ostriches, aurochs and migrating birds is told as plainly as I can.

The storm coming up while Elihu talks, what Job saw and felt, and the pictures of the animals and places are our own. Nothing has been added to what the Lord or Job says in the book.

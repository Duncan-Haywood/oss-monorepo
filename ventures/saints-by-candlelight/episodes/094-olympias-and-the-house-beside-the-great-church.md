Imagine a courtroom, a very long time ago, in the hottest part of the summer.

It smells of smoke. The whole city smells of smoke. Two nights ago the great church burned to the ground, and the senate house beside it, and the ashes are still warm, and the people of the city are frightened and angry and looking for someone to blame.

At the front of the room sits the prefect of the city, a hard man, in his robes of office. Around him, clerks, guards, men with tablets. And in front of him, standing very straight, is a woman of about forty. She is dressed in the plainest dark clothes you can imagine. She is not well; she has a bad stomach and she has not slept. And she is, or she was until very recently, one of the richest people in the Roman Empire.

The prefect leans forward. He asks her why she set fire to the church.

And she looks at him, and she does not raise her voice at all.

On our last episode, we followed Saint John Chrysostom on his long last road into exile. And all the way along that road, if you remember, he was writing letters. Seventeen of them, which survive, to one woman back in Constantinople. Tonight is her night.

Her name was Olympias. It is a grand name. It means something like the one from Olympus, where the old Greek gods were supposed to live, and her family were the kind of people who gave their daughters names like that. She was born in Constantinople, the new capital of the Roman Empire, in the three hundred and sixties. Her father was a count, a high official at court. Her grandfather, the old Life says, had been one of the great ministers of the emperor Constantine himself.

And her family was rich. I do not mean comfortable. I do not mean a nice house and a few servants. I mean that they owned estates in Thrace and Galatia and Cappadocia and Bithynia, whole districts of farms and villages, and rows of houses in the city, and baths, and bakeries, and warehouses of gold and silver. If you have ever seen one of those lists of the richest people in the world, and wondered what it would be like, Olympias would have been on that list. Near the top.

And then, when she was still a little girl, both her parents died.

* * *

So the little heiress was brought up by someone else. Her guardian put her in the care of a woman called Theodosia. And Theodosia was a very good choice. She was the sister of a holy bishop, Amphilochius of Iconium, and she was a cousin, or some close relation, of our friend Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, the shy man with the books. Gregory wrote of Theodosia that she was a model of goodness, and that Olympias should copy her in everything.

So Olympias grew up in a house where people prayed, and read Scripture, and were kind to servants. And she grew up knowing, also, that she was the richest girl in the city, and that every family with a son of the right age had their eye on her.

When she was about eighteen, they found her a husband. His name was Nebridius. He was young, and well-connected, a man who held the office of prefect of the city. It was a grand wedding.

Gregory of Nazianzus was invited. And here is a lovely thing that survives. Gregory did not go. He was old, and ill, and living in his garden in Cappadocia, and it was a very long way. But he sent a poem. A long poem of advice, written for the young bride, which we still have. And it is full of the advice of a kindly old uncle. Do not wear too much gold. Do not paint your face; God made it, and it was fine. Love your husband. Be gentle with him when he is angry, and wait for the right moment to tell him he is wrong. Pray. And he excused himself for not coming: he was too old, he said, and too ill, so the poem would have to come instead.

I like to think of Olympias reading that, on her wedding day. The old saint, far away, being sweet and a little bit bossy.

* * *

And then, less than two years later, Nebridius died.

We do not know how. The old writers do not say. He was young, and he was gone, and Olympias, at about twenty, was a widow. Just like John Chrysostom's mother, Anthusa, whom we met last time, who was widowed at twenty too.

Now, a young, rich, beautiful widow in Constantinople was not going to be left alone. The suitors came at once. And one of them was very important indeed. The emperor himself, Theodosius, the great soldier emperor, had a kinsman, a Spaniard called Elpidius, and the emperor decided that Elpidius should marry the widow Olympias, and that would keep her fortune nicely in the family.

Olympias said no.

The old writers give her answer, and I am going to give it to you in our own words, but this is very close to what they report. She said: if my King had wanted me to live with a husband, he would not have taken my first one away. And she did not mean the emperor. She meant the King of Heaven. God, she said, has shown me what he wants. I am going to stay as I am, and serve him.

The emperor was not used to being told no. Especially by a young woman. Especially in front of the whole court.

So he did something very nasty, in a very legal way. He took her fortune away from her. Not all of it, not for ever, but he put the whole of it, every estate and every house and every coin, under the management of the prefect of the city, until she reached the age of thirty. She was not allowed to touch it. The prefect, eager to please, went further, and would not even let her go to church to meet the bishops and the holy people she liked to talk to. The idea, of course, was that she would get tired of being poor, and lonely, and give in, and marry Elpidius.

She did not give in.

Instead, she wrote the emperor a letter. And it is one of the most splendid letters any subject has ever written to any ruler. She said, more or less, this. Thank you, Your Majesty. You have shown me the kindness that only a king, or a bishop, could show. You have taken off my shoulders the heavy, worrying burden of managing all that money, and I am grateful. And may I make one more request? Please, give it all away. Divide it among the poor, and among the churches. Then I will be free at last from the worry of it, and from the vanity of it, and from the temptation of it.

You can imagine the emperor reading that. You can imagine him not quite knowing whether he had been thanked or told off. It was, I think, both.

Not long after, Theodosius came back from a long war in the West. Someone, perhaps, told him the whole story properly. And he gave Olympias her fortune back. All of it. She was free.

And then, as he would soon find out, she did exactly what she had said she would do.

* * *

Here is where I would like to introduce someone who is not in the old books.

The old Life tells us that Olympias founded a community of women right beside the great church of Constantinople. Not in the desert, not out in the countryside, but in the very middle of the city, in a building next door to the cathedral, with its own passage into the church. And the Life says that, in time, two hundred and fifty women lived there. Some were her relations. Three are named: Elisanthia, Martyria and Palladia, her kinswomen. Others were ladies of rich families. And others, surely, were not.

So for tonight we are going to imagine one of the others. We will call her Zoe, and everything about her, from here on, is our own invention.

Zoe is about fifteen when she arrives. She comes from the docks. Her father was a porter who carried sacks off the grain ships and fell into the harbour one winter and did not come out. Her mother died of a fever. Zoe has been sleeping in doorways and stealing bread. And one morning, very early, she is sitting on the steps of the great church, in the cold, because sometimes the deacons hand out bread there, and a woman in plain dark clothes comes out of a side door and sits down beside her.

The woman asks her name. Then she asks whether Zoe has had breakfast.

Zoe says no, obviously. And the woman says, good, neither have I, come in.

And Zoe goes in through the side door, into a big, warm, busy kitchen, full of women in plain dark clothes, chopping and stirring and laughing, and she eats more bread than she has eaten in a week, and only afterwards does somebody tell her that the woman who brought her in is the lady Olympias. The richest woman in Constantinople.

Zoe does not believe it. Rich women do not wear clothes like that. Rich women do not sit on the steps.

* * *

Now, the Church in those days had an office for women called the deaconess. It is a very old office; Saint Paul mentions a woman called Phoebe, a deacon of the church at Cenchreae, in his letter to the Romans. In the Church of the fourth and fifth centuries, deaconesses served women in particular ways. They helped at the baptism of women, which in those days was done by full immersion, and needed women to help for modesty's sake. They visited sick women at home, where a man might not go. They taught, and cared for the poor, and kept order among the women in church.

The bishop of Constantinople, Nectarius, made Olympias a deaconess when she was still young, younger than the usual rule. He did it, I suspect, because it was very obvious that she was already doing the work.

And she gave. Oh, how she gave.

The old Life gives a list. And I will spare you most of it, because it is a very long list, and goes on like an inventory of a treasure ship. Ten thousand pounds of gold. Twenty thousand pounds of silver. Estates in four provinces. Houses in the city. Baths. A bakery. All of it went, piece by piece, to the Church, to the poor, to hospitals, to monasteries, to prisoners, to exiles, to anyone, it sometimes seemed, who knocked on the door.

She paid for churches and their repairs. She looked after monks who came to the city with nothing, and widows, and the sick. Bishops came from all over the East to visit her, and they went home with full saddlebags.

Zoe, in our story, is put to work on the doors.

Every morning, there is a queue. Widows. Orphans. Sailors who have lost their ships. Monks who need sandals. Priests from little villages whose churches have fallen down. A man who says he is a bishop but who, Zoe is fairly sure, is not. Zoe's job is to sort them out, and give each one a little token, and send them in to the lady one by one.

Zoe asks, one day, whether she should send the man who is not a bishop away.

And the lady says no. Send him in. If he is lying, that is between him and God. If he is hungry, that is between him and me.

* * *

In the year three hundred and ninety-eight, when Olympias was about thirty, a new bishop came to Constantinople. A thin, pale, intense man from Antioch, who had been put into a carriage by the government and driven to the capital without being asked. John. The one they would later call Chrysostom, golden mouth.

And Olympias and John became friends.

I want to say this carefully, because it is a precious thing. It was a friendship between a holy man and a holy woman, a bishop and a deaconess, in the service of the same Lord. It was close, and warm, and completely proper, and everyone who knew them knew it. They were both rather difficult people, I think, in their different ways. They were both stubborn. They were both completely sure that money was for giving away. And they seem to have understood each other perfectly.

John, as you may remember, had a ruined stomach from his years as a hermit in the mountains. He could eat hardly anything. He would forget to eat altogether if he was writing a sermon. And the old Life says, simply, that Olympias saw to his food. Every day, from her kitchen next door to the cathedral, a meal was sent across to the bishop's house. Plain food, the kind his stomach could manage.

In our story, it is Zoe who carries it.

Every day at the ninth hour, with a covered dish and a jug of warm water, she goes along the passage from the women's house to the bishop's house. The bishop is usually writing. He usually does not look up. She puts the dish down where he can see it. And on the way out she says, as the lady told her to say: the lady Olympias says you are to eat it while it is warm.

And the bishop always says: tell the lady Olympias that I am eating it.

And Zoe always waits, just outside the door, until she hears the spoon.

* * *

John, I should tell you, also told Olympias off.

The historian Sozomen says that John told her to be more careful with her giving. Not to give less, but to give more wisely. She was giving to everyone who asked, rich and poor alike, including people who did not need it at all, and people who were just taking advantage. And John said to her something like this: you are pouring water into the sea. The sea does not need it. Give to the ones who are thirsty.

I love that he told her that. And I love even more that she seems to have listened, and to have carried on giving anyway, just more carefully.

There is another friend of Olympias's we have met on this show. Saint Gregory of Nyssa, Basil's younger brother, the one with the confusing name. He wrote, in his old age, a set of sermons on the Song of Songs, that great love poem in the Old Testament, which Christians read as a song of love between God and the soul. And he dedicated those sermons to Olympias. He wrote at the top a little letter to her, saying that she had asked him to explain the Song, and here, at last, was his explanation. So when you hold that great book today, you are holding something that was written, first of all, because Olympias asked for it.

She was, I think, a woman people wanted to write for.

* * *

And then everything went wrong.

We told this part of the story last time, from John's side, so I will go quickly. John made enemies at court, and in the Church. The empress turned against him. A meeting of bishops deposed him. He was sent away, and called back, and sent away again. And at Easter of the year four hundred and four, soldiers broke into the church on the night of the baptisms.

And because Olympias's house was right next door to the church, with its own passage into it, she and her women were right in the middle of it all.

In June of that year, on the day John left for good, he went into the baptistery of the great church and sent for Olympias, and for the other deaconesses, Pentadia and Procla, and a widow called Silvina. And Palladius, who knew them all, tells us what he said, and I give it in our own words.

Come here, my daughters, and listen to me. I think things with me are nearly finished. I have finished my course, and perhaps you will not see my face again. But I ask this of you. Do not let your love for the Church grow less. And whoever is made bishop in my place, if he comes honestly, without scheming for it, then obey him as you have obeyed me. And remember me in your prayers.

And the women wept, and threw themselves down at his feet, and could not stop. And he told one of the priests to take them away gently, so that the crowds outside would not see, and riot.

In our story, Zoe is there, at the back, in the doorway. She is twenty-one now. She has never seen the lady cry before. And she watches Olympias get up off her knees, and wipe her face, and straighten her plain dark dress, and take the other women by the hand, one by one, and lead them home along the passage. And Zoe thinks: she is going to be strong for all of them. Somebody has to be.

That night, the great church burned.

[pause]

* * *

Nobody knows how the fire started. It began in the church, in the night, and the wind carried it across the square to the senate house, with all its ancient statues, and it burned for hours, and in the morning there was nothing left but black stone and ash.

John's enemies said that John's friends had started it, in revenge. John's friends said that John's enemies had started it, to blame them. Most likely, I think, it was an accident, on a hot night, in a building full of lamps and candles and very old wood. But a frightened city needs someone to blame. And the house beside the great church was full of John's friends.

And so the prefect of the city, a man called Optatus, who was a pagan and who had no fondness for the Christians at all, sent his soldiers to fetch them. The clergy. The monks. The women of the community. And Olympias.

And this is where we came in. The courtroom. The smell of smoke. The prefect leaning forward, asking her why she had burned the church.

The historian Sozomen tells us what she said. And again, I give it in our own words, but very close to his.

My way of life, she said, should be enough to answer that. I have spent a fortune, a very great fortune, building and repairing the churches of God. Do you really think I am the sort of person who burns them down?

There was no answer to that. Everyone in the city knew it. Half the churches in the eastern empire had her money in their walls. And so the prefect dropped that charge.

But he did not let her go. Instead he told her, and the other women, that they must accept the new bishop who had been put in John's place, and take communion with him, and then all would be forgiven.

And Olympias said no.

Not because she was proud. Because John had not been deposed honestly, and the new bishop had not come honestly, and she would not pretend that he had. So the prefect fined her. A huge fine. And she paid it. And then they fined her again, and seized her property, and auctioned off her goods, and broke up her community, and sent the women away. And still she would not change her mind.

And at last, they sent her away too. Into exile. First, the old historians say, to the city of Cyzicus, and later to Nicomedia, across the water in Asia.

* * *

In our story, Zoe goes with her.

Most of the two hundred and fifty women have had to go home to their families, or to other communities. But Zoe has no family. And so she packs one bag, and walks down to the harbour, and gets onto the boat behind the lady, without asking, and sits down on the deck. And the lady looks at her, and does not tell her to go home. She only says: did you bring the big pot?

And Zoe says: of course I brought the big pot.

They live, in exile, in a rented house. It is small and damp. The lady is often ill. Her stomach troubles her, and some days she cannot get out of bed at all. And worse than the stomach, there is something else. A heaviness. A sadness that sits on her like a stone and will not lift. The old word for it, the Greek word that John uses in his letters, means something like losing heart. We might call it something else today. Whatever we call it, it is real, and it is very hard, and saints are not spared it.

And then the letters start coming.

* * *

They came from Cucusus, in the mountains of Armenia, carried by friends and travellers and anyone who was going that way. Seventeen of them survive. And they are some of the most beautiful letters of the ancient world.

What is so remarkable is that they are almost all about her.

John is the one in exile at the edge of the empire, in the snow, with a fever, with raiders in the hills. And he writes, again and again, not about himself, but about Olympias. How is your health? Are you taking the medicine the doctors gave you? Are you eating? Do not be sad. Do not lose heart. Do not think that what has happened to you is wasted.

Here is one of the things he wrote, in our own words. I know that you are sad. And I want you to understand that sadness, when it gets out of hand, is a danger to the soul, more than any fever. So fight it. Not by pretending, but by remembering. Remember what you have done. Remember the women you clothed, and the prisoners you fed, and the churches you built. None of that can be taken away. It is laid up already, where no prefect can fine it.

And in another letter, he talks about a storm at sea. The waves high as mountains, the ships going down, the sailors in despair, and the whole sea dark. And he says: this is what the world looks like now. I know. But God is the pilot. Do not lose heart. He calms the sea, not always when we want him to, but always in time.

In our story, it is Zoe who reads them aloud. The lady likes to have them read to her, on the bad days, in bed. Zoe has learned to read in the community, slowly and carefully, with her finger under the words, and she is still not quick. And she reads them over and over, the same letters, until she almost has them by heart.

And one day, reading the one about the storm, Zoe gets to the end, and looks up, and the lady is asleep. Properly asleep, with her face quiet, for the first time in days.

And Zoe folds the letter, very carefully, and puts it under the lady's pillow, and goes and puts the big pot on.

* * *

John told her, too, in one of the letters, that she must not grieve that she could not be with him, and must not worry that he was lonely. He said he was well looked after, that the people of Cucusus were kind, that he had a house and a fire. Some of that was true. Some of it, I suspect, he was saying to make her feel better. They were both, by then, very good at being strong for each other.

And he sent her a little book. Its title is a whole sermon in itself. No one can be harmed except by himself. His point was that nothing that is done to us from outside, no exile, no fine, no illness, no lies told about us, can touch the part of us that belongs to God. Only our own choices can do that. And so the prefect, with all his soldiers, could not really harm Olympias at all. Only Olympias could, by giving in to despair. And she was not going to.

I think that is a hard teaching. I think it is a true one. And I think it is very moving that John wrote it, in the first place, for her.

* * *

And then, in the autumn of four hundred and seven, the letters stopped.

Word came, eventually, along the roads. The bishop had been moved, even further away. He had been made to walk. He had died at a little martyr's chapel beyond Comana, in white clothes, saying glory to God for all things.

In our story, it is a traveller who brings the news, a monk on his way to the capital, who stops at the door to ask for water, and says it without knowing who he is saying it to. And Zoe takes the water out to him, and comes back in, and stands for a long time in the doorway of the lady's room, not knowing how to say it.

And the lady, from the bed, says: he has gone, hasn't he?

And Zoe says: yes.

And the lady is quiet for a long time. And then she says, very softly, his words. Glory to God for all things.

Then she asks Zoe to bring the letters. All of them. And Zoe brings the box. And the lady holds it on her lap, not opening it. Just holding it. All afternoon.

* * *

We do not know very much about the rest of Olympias's life. She lived on in exile at Nicomedia, for a while longer. She was ill. She was poor, now, by her old standards, though she would have said she had always been poor in the way that mattered, and was rich in the way that mattered too. She went on praying, and she went on giving, whatever she had left. And some time after John's death, perhaps a year or two, perhaps a little more, she died. She was in her forties.

She went home to God, the friend of a saint and a saint herself, a long way from the house beside the great church.

There is a story about what happened next, and I will tell it to you, but I want to be clear: it is a later story. It was written down about two hundred years afterwards, in the seventh century, by an abbess called Sergia, who was the head of the community of women in Constantinople that still kept Olympias's memory. And according to Sergia's account, Olympias came in a dream to the bishop of Nicomedia, and asked that her coffin be put into the sea. And so they did. And the waves carried it, the account says, all the way down the coast and up the straits, and it came ashore near Constantinople, at a place called Brochthoi, where there was a church of the apostle Thomas. And the people there took her in, and buried her in the church.

Is it true? I do not know. It is the kind of thing people told about the saints they loved. But I like the picture very much. The lady, in exile, coming home on the sea, by herself, without asking anyone's permission. She was always very good at that.

And as for Zoe, who never existed, but who stands tonight for the hundreds of real, unnamed women of that house, the porters' daughters and the orphans and the widows whose names nobody wrote down. I like to think she went back to Constantinople. I like to think she found the other women. I like to think that, many years later, an old woman in a plain dark dress came out of a side door of a church one cold morning, and sat down on the steps beside a hungry girl, and asked whether she had had breakfast.

* * *

Olympias is honoured as a saint in both East and West. The Orthodox Church keeps her feast on the twenty-fifth of July. The Roman Martyrology of the Catholic Church remembers her on the seventeenth of December. In icons she is shown as a woman in the dark clothes of a deaconess, often holding a cross, sometimes a scroll.

And her memory lives on chiefly in the letters. Seventeen letters, from a sick man in the mountains to a sick woman by the sea, each of them trying to keep the other one going. We do not have her side of the correspondence. Not one of her letters survives. We know her only through what he wrote back. And that, somehow, seems right for her. She spent her whole life giving everything away. Even her own words, in the end, we only have in the shape of the gifts that came back to her.

So if you have had a long day. If you have given and given, and it does not seem to have made any difference. If you are tired, and sad, and the heaviness is sitting on you and will not lift. If you are a long way from where you want to be. You could think of Olympias. The richest woman in the city, sitting on the cold steps beside a hungry child. Standing very straight in front of the prefect. Lying in a damp room in exile, with a box of letters on her lap. Not lost. Not wasted. Held.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

The main source for Olympias's life is a short Life written in Greek, probably in the fifth century, not long after her death, by someone who knew her community. Elizabeth Clark has translated it into English. The church historian Sozomen tells the stories of John's advice about her giving and of her trial before the prefect. John's friend Palladius tells of the farewell in the baptistery and mentions her in his other book, the Lausiac History. And John's seventeen letters to her survive, though her letters to him do not. Gregory of Nazianzus's poem for her wedding and Gregory of Nyssa's dedication to her both survive too.

What is history. Her family and her wealth, her upbringing by Theodosia, Gregory's poem, her marriage and early widowhood, her refusal of Elpidius, the emperor's seizure of her fortune and her letter, the return of her property, her ordination as deaconess, the community of two hundred and fifty women, her gifts, her care for John, his advice about the sea, the farewell in the baptistery, the fire, her trial and fines, her exile, John's letters, and her death in exile.

What is later legend. The coffin carried by the sea to Brochthoi is from the seventh century account of the abbess Sergia, and is told as that account.

What we invented. Zoe, her whole life, the steps, the kitchen, the dish carried along the passage, the big pot, the reading aloud, and the news brought by the monk. The words of Olympias and of John are given in our own plain paraphrase, close to the sense the old writers report. The old Life also says some things about her marriage that we have left out, as they are hard to judge and are not needed for her story.

* * *

A midwife is a woman who helps other women bring their babies into the world. It is one of the oldest jobs there is. Older than kings, certainly. Older than bricks. The very first pages of the Bible already have midwives in them. When Rachel, the wife of Jacob, was having her last baby on the road to Bethlehem, it was a midwife who said to her, do not be afraid, for you will have this son too. When Tamar had twins, it was a midwife who tied a scarlet thread around the wrist of the baby whose hand came out first, so that there would be no argument later about who was the eldest. Which, if you have ever had brothers or sisters, you will recognise as a very sensible precaution.

In those days nobody went to a hospital to have a baby, because there were no hospitals. You had your baby at home, in your own house, with your mother and your sisters and your aunts all around you, and, if you were lucky, a midwife who knew what she was doing. And a good midwife was worth more than gold. She knew when to wait and when to hurry. She knew what to do when a baby was coming the wrong way round. She knew which herbs to boil, and how to cut the cord, and how to rub the baby with salt and wrap it tight in strips of linen, which was what people did then. She knew the words to say to a woman who was frightened. She had seen hundreds of births, maybe thousands, and when she walked into the room, everybody in it breathed out a little.

The Egyptians had midwives too, and they had all sorts of customs about birth. A woman in Egypt would often give birth sitting or crouching on two bricks, set a little apart, with the midwife kneeling in front of her to catch the baby. There is an old Egyptian story, written on a papyrus that you can still see today, about three babies who were going to become kings, and in that story the midwives, who turn out to be goddesses in disguise, which is the Egyptians' idea and not ours, come to the house, and catch each baby as it arrives, and wash it, and cut the cord. Archaeologists have even found one of those birth bricks, made of dried mud, painted on the side with a picture of a mother holding her new baby. Somebody's grandmother, probably, sat on it a very long time ago, and was very glad when it was over.

The Book of Exodus mentions those two stones as well. Keep them in mind. They come into the story.

And now the book does something it hardly ever does. It slows down, and it tells us two names.

The king of Egypt, it says, spoke to the Hebrew midwives. And the name of one was Shiphrah. And the name of the other was Puah.

Shiphrah, as far as anybody can tell, means something like beautiful, or bright, or fair. Puah is harder. Some people think it means a girl, or a little one, and some think it is the soft sound a mother makes to a crying baby, the sort of sound you make when you are rocking someone at three in the morning and you have run out of real words. I like that one, though I cannot promise it is right.

Now, you might be wondering, as people have wondered for a very long time, how two midwives could possibly look after a whole people, when the book has just told us the land was filled with them. Some of the old commentators guessed that these two were the head midwives, the ones in charge of all the others, which would explain why the king sent for them and not for everybody. That seems sensible to me. But the book does not say.

And there is another question it does not answer, which is whether they were Hebrews themselves or Egyptians. In the Hebrew, the words can mean the Hebrew midwives, or the midwives of the Hebrews, the women who looked after the Hebrew mothers. Josephus, the Jewish historian who wrote in the time of the apostles, thought they were Egyptians. The rabbis of the Talmud thought they were Hebrews. They are both sensible guesses, and the book, once again, lets us wonder. What it is very clear about is what they did.

So let us imagine a morning in Goshen, early, before it gets hot.

Shiphrah is sitting in the doorway of her house, with a bowl of barley porridge in her lap that she has been meaning to eat for half an hour. She has been out all night at a birth, a little girl, nine pounds if she was an ounce, and the mother is fine, and the baby is fine, and Shiphrah is very tired and very happy, which is how midwives usually feel at this time of the morning.

Sitting on the step below her is Zibiah. Zibiah is fourteen, and she is the newest apprentice, and she is our own invention, so you will not find her in the Bible, but there must have been girls like her, because every midwife was once an apprentice who knelt in the corner holding the cloth. Zibiah has been to eleven births so far. She has fainted at one of them. She does not like to talk about it.

And coming up the path, much faster than usual, is Puah.

Puah is not fast, as a rule. Puah is the sort of woman who believes that babies arrive when babies are ready, and that running about only makes everyone nervous. So when Shiphrah sees her coming up the path at something close to a trot, she puts her porridge down.

There is a man from the palace, says Puah, out of breath. With a staff, and a linen kilt with a very sharp pleat in it, and a face like a closed door. The king wants to see us.

Us? says Shiphrah.

Us, says Puah. You and me. Today.

Zibiah's mouth falls open. Shiphrah closes it for her, gently, with one finger.

Well, says Shiphrah, getting up and brushing the barley off her skirt. We had better wash our faces, then.

I do not know exactly where they went. The book does not tell us, and the kings of Egypt had palaces in more than one city. Pillars carved like bundles of papyrus. Men with fans of ostrich feathers. Floors so clean that Puah, who had walked a long way on a dusty road, felt suddenly very aware of her feet.

And then they were standing in front of him. The king of Egypt. The Lord of the Two Lands. Which, to him, they were.

He spoke to them. And here, because it is a hard thing, I will tell it the way the book tells it, plainly and quickly, and then we will move on.

When you help the Hebrew women in childbirth, he said, and you see them on the two stones, if it is a son, you shall put him to death. But if it is a daughter, she shall live.

That is all he said. And then they were sent away.

You might wonder why the king thought this would work, and why he thought he could ask it of them. And I think the answer is that he did not think of them as people at all. He thought of them as a tool. He had a problem, too many Hebrew babies, and he looked about for the nearest thing that touched the problem, and the nearest thing was two women with their sleeves rolled up. He thought, quite simply, that they would be too frightened to say no. And if anything went wrong, well, babies did sometimes die at birth, in those days, and who would ever know?

The king was, in his way, quite clever. But he had made one mistake. And the book tells us what it was, in one short sentence, which I am going to save for a minute, because I want you to hear it properly.

I do not want you to think that this was easy. And here is the sentence I was saving.

But the midwives feared God, says the Book of Exodus, and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them, but let the male children live.

The midwives feared God.

Now, that word, fear, can be confusing. When we say fear we usually mean being frightened, like being frightened of the dark, or of spiders, or of a big dog behind a gate. But that is not quite what the Bible means when it talks about fearing God. The fear of the Lord, says the Book of Proverbs, is the beginning of wisdom. It does not mean being scared of God, the way you might be scared of a bully. It means something more like knowing who is really in charge. Knowing that God is God, and you are not, and the king of Egypt is not either, whatever he has carved on his temple walls. It means a sort of deep, quiet, wide-awake respect, the feeling you might have standing at the edge of the sea at night, or holding a newborn baby for the first time.

And I think, actually, that midwives would understand that better than most people. Because a midwife, more than almost anyone, has seen where babies come from. She knows that she does not make them. She catches them. She helps. She wipes their faces and wraps them up and hands them to their mothers. But every single time, she has watched something come into the room that was not there before, a whole new person, with fingernails, and opinions, and a voice, and she knows perfectly well that she did not do that. Somebody else did.

The king of Egypt thought that a baby was his to decide about. The midwives knew better. They had been in the room.

So I like to think that, somewhere on that path, with the frogs creaking and the light going, Shiphrah stopped walking, and turned to Puah, and said, very simply: they are not his. They are God's. And we are God's. And that is the end of it.

And Puah, who was not fast, and who believed that babies arrive when babies are ready, nodded once, and took her friend's arm, and they walked the rest of the way home.

Which brings us back to the little house, and the one lamp, and the girl in the corner holding the cloth.

Let us say this was a few months later. The family is our own invention. They are brickmakers, like nearly everybody in Goshen by now. The father works in the brickfields from dawn until dark, treading clay with his feet. The mother, whose name we will say was Leah, like her many-times-great-grandmother, has two little girls already, who are asleep, or pretending to be asleep, behind a reed screen at the back of the house. And it is her third baby, and it has taken all night, and Shiphrah has been kneeling on that floor for so long that she has stopped feeling her knees.

And it's a boy, says Shiphrah.

And there is that moment in the room, when nobody breathes.

Because everybody knows. That is the thing. Something like this never stays secret for long, and by now the whispering has gone round every house in Goshen. The king has given an order. The midwives have been told. Every mother in the land of Goshen has lain awake at night wondering what the woman with the rolled-up sleeves will do when she comes.

Leah looks at Shiphrah, and her face is white.

And Shiphrah, who has done this hundreds of times, does exactly what she always does. She says, Zibiah, the cloth, please, and Zibiah, whose hands are shaking, gives it to her. And Shiphrah wipes the baby's face, and clears his nose, and he yells even louder, which is very satisfactory. And she ties the cord with a bit of linen thread and cuts it with the little knife. And she rubs him down, and wraps him up, round and round, tight as a parcel, the way it was done, until only his cross red face is showing. And then she leans forward and puts him into his mother's arms.

There, she says. There's your son. Look at the size of his hands. He's going to be a brickmaker like his father, and twice as loud.

Is he, she says. Are you. Did they not tell you.

They told us, says Shiphrah. And she tucks the end of the linen in, very neatly, under the baby's chin. And then she says, as if she were saying something perfectly ordinary, like the weather or the price of onions: but we fear God, Leah. And God gave you this boy. So there it is.

On the way home, as the sky was going grey, Zibiah said nothing at all for a very long time, which was unusual for Zibiah.

And then she said, aren't you frightened?

And Shiphrah thought about that. And then she said, yes. Of course I am. My stomach has been in knots since the day we stood in that palace. I don't sleep as well as I used to. I keep thinking I hear a man with a staff at the door.

Then how, said Zibiah, and she did not finish the question, but Shiphrah knew what she meant.

And Shiphrah said: being frightened is not the same as being told what to do by it. You can be frightened and still do the right thing. In fact, I think that is the only time doing the right thing counts for very much. If it were easy, everybody would do it.

And then, because she was a sensible woman and it was five o'clock in the morning, she said, now. Your knot on that cord was too loose. Let me show you again.

Of course, the king found out.

You can imagine it from his side. Months go by. His officials keep bringing him reports from Goshen, and the reports keep saying the same thing. More babies. Lots of them. Boys, girls, twins, crying in every house, as if nothing had happened at all. And the king, who is used to being obeyed, who has never in his life given an order that was not carried out, begins to get very, very cross.

So the king of Egypt called for the midwives again.

This time I imagine nobody offered them a seat. I imagine there were rather more guards. And the king leaned forward on his gilded chair, and looked down at two women from Goshen, and asked them a question. The book gives it to us in his own words. Why have you done this thing, and let the male children live?

And here I would like you to imagine the room. The painted pillars. The men with their fans. The king, who held the whole of Egypt in his hand, waiting for an answer. And two midwives with dusty feet, who had already decided, on a path home one evening, that they were not going to do what he said.

The book tells us their answer. I will give it to you as the book does, and then a little more, because I think they were brave enough to deserve a few extra words.

Because the Hebrew women are not like the Egyptian women, they said. They are lively. They give birth before the midwife can even get to them.

Lively. In the Hebrew the word is very close to the word for living, or for living things, and it carries a sense of vigour, of strength, of life that simply will not wait. And I like to imagine Puah saying it, slowly, in her unhurried way. You must understand, my lord, these women do not wait for anybody. We get the message, we come running, and by the time we are through the door, there is the baby already, on the floor, yelling. What are we supposed to do?

And I like to imagine the king, who had never been present at a birth in his life, and had certainly never been sent for at three in the morning, finding that he had nothing at all to say to that.

* * *

Now, I want to pause here, because people have argued for a very long time about this answer, and I think it is worth being honest about it.

Was it true?

The book does not tell us. It may well have been partly true. The Hebrew mothers were strong women, working in the fields and the brickyards, and some of them surely did have their babies quickly. And it may well be that, once the king's order was known, some of them did not send for the midwife until it was all over, just to be safe. You can imagine a mother saying, don't call her, not yet, not till he's here. So there may have been a good deal of truth in it. But it was not the whole truth. The whole truth was that the midwives feared God and would not do it. And that part they did not say.

So was it a lie? Some readers have thought so. And the Church has thought about that very carefully, because the Church teaches that we must not lie, not even for a good reason. Saint Augustine wrote about these very midwives, sixteen hundred years ago, in a little book called Against Lying. And Saint Thomas Aquinas, eight hundred years after that, asked the question straight out: if the midwives lied, why did God reward them? And his answer, which is Augustine's answer too, is a careful and rather beautiful one. God did not reward them for what they said to the king, said Thomas. He rewarded them for their fear of God, and for their kindness, and for their good will in saving the children. The book itself puts it the same way. It says that God dealt well with the midwives, and it says why. Because they feared God.

Which is a long way of saying that the story is not teaching us that it is fine to tell a fib to a king. What it is teaching us is something older and harder and much more important. When a ruler tells you to do something terribly wrong, you do not do it. You may be frightened. You may not have the right words. But you do not do it. Hundreds of years later, the apostles Peter and the others were standing in front of a different court, in Jerusalem, and were told to stop speaking about Jesus. And Peter said, we must obey God rather than men. Shiphrah and Puah would have understood that perfectly. They had said it, in their own way, a very long time before.

And so we come to the end of their part of the story, which is short, and, I think, one of the gentlest endings in the whole Book of Exodus.

God dealt well with the midwives, the book says. And the people multiplied, and grew very mighty. And because the midwives feared God, he made them houses.

He made them houses.

That is an odd phrase in English. It does not mean that God built them each a nice villa with a garden, though for all I know he may have. In the Bible, a house very often means a household. A family. Children, and children's children, the whole line of people who come after you. When God promised King David, much later on, that he would build him a house, he did not mean bricks. He meant a family that would last. And so most readers take this to mean that God gave the midwives families of their own. That the two women who had spent their lives catching other people's babies were given babies of their own to love, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, households full of noise.

I find that one of the kindest details in the whole Bible. God noticed. The king of Egypt never wrote their names on anything, but God gave them names that would be read aloud for thousands of years. And then he gave them houses full of children.

There is a psalm that says, unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labour in vain. And a little further on, it says, children are a heritage from the Lord. I have always thought that psalm would have made Shiphrah smile.

Now, I have to tell you that the story does not end happily for everybody, not yet. The very next verse, the last verse of chapter one, says that the king, having failed with the midwives, gave a new order, this time to all his people, that every baby boy born to the Hebrews was to be put into the river. I will not say more about it than that, and neither does the book. Because the very next thing it tells us, at the beginning of chapter two, is about a woman from the tribe of Levi who had a baby boy, and saw that he was a fine child, and hid him for three months, and then made him a little basket of papyrus, and daubed it with pitch, and set it among the reeds by the river's edge. And a girl stood a little way off to see what would happen to him.

You will have heard that story, I expect. We told it in our last episode. The baby was Moses. And the river that was meant to take him away is the river that gave him to the daughter of Pharaoh, who drew him out and brought him up in the palace of the very king who had given the order.

So the story of Exodus begins with women. Two midwives who would not obey. A mother who hid her son. A sister who watched from the reeds. And a princess who heard a baby crying and felt sorry for him. Not one of them had a sword. Not one of them had an army. And between them they began the rescue of a whole people.

* * *

Later on, the Jewish teachers told a lovely story of their own about the midwives. In the Talmud, which is a vast collection of their discussions written down some centuries after Christ, some of the rabbis say that Shiphrah was really another name for Jochebed, the mother of Moses, and that Puah was Miriam, his sister, who would have been a girl at the time. And the great Jewish commentator Rashi, who lived in France about nine hundred years ago, explained the names this way. Shiphrah, he said, because she would make the newborn baby beautiful, washing it and tidying it up. And Puah, because she would coo and murmur to the baby to soothe it, that soft little sound that mothers make. And the houses, said Rashi, were the great households that came from them. The priests and the Levites, from the family of Moses and Aaron. And the kings of Israel.

That is a tradition, not something the Book of Exodus says, and I cannot tell you whether it is true. It may be that the rabbis simply could not bear for two such brave women to be strangers. But I like that the old teachers wanted them in the family. And I like it even more that, whoever they were, the book was content to let them be just themselves. Two midwives. Shiphrah and Puah. Who feared God.

Let us go back, one last time, to the land of Goshen.

Let us say it is many years later. Shiphrah is very old now, and sits most days in the doorway of her house, in the shade, with a bowl of barley porridge that she has been meaning to eat for half an hour. Her knees, which spent so many years on so many floors, have given up kneeling altogether. Around her there is a great deal of noise. That is the house God made for her. Children, and grandchildren, a whole tribe of them, running in and out, falling over the goat, asking her for things.

And coming up the path, at a sensible, unhurried pace, is a woman of about forty, with her sleeves rolled up past the elbow and a bundle of clean cloths under her arm. It is Zibiah. She is the head midwife of the whole district now. She has caught more babies than she can count. And her knots on the cord are very, very tight.

She sits down on the step below Shiphrah, the way she used to, and she says, a boy, last night. And a girl before that. Twins next week, I think, if I'm any judge.

And Shiphrah smiles, and says, good. Good. And then, because she is old, and has earned the right to say the same thing more than once, she says, they are not his, you know. They never were.

I know, says Zibiah. You told me.

And Shiphrah says, I'm telling you again.

And they sit there together in the warm evening, while the egrets come in to roost, and the frogs start up in the ditches, a thousand tiny doors creaking. And somewhere in one of the houses, behind a reed screen, a baby begins to cry. A thin, cross, wonderful cry.

And neither of them gets up, because it is not their turn tonight. But they both listen, the way midwives always will, until it stops.

So tonight, as you lie in the dark, I would like you to think about those two women.

Most of us will never stand in front of a king. Nobody is going to send a man with a sharp-pleated kilt to fetch us to a palace. But every one of us, at some time or other, will be asked to go along with something we know is wrong. It might be very small. Leaving someone out. Laughing when we should not. Keeping quiet. And we will be frightened, or embarrassed, or tired, and we will not have the right words.

And on those days I hope we remember Shiphrah and Puah, walking home together on a dusty path in the evening. They did not have an army. They did not even have a very good speech. They just knew, quite simply, who was really in charge. And so they went on doing what they had always done. They knelt on the floor in the lamplight, and they caught the babies, and they wiped their faces, and they put them into their mothers' arms.

And God noticed. He always does.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Exodus, chapter one, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The new king who did not know Joseph, the store cities of Pithom and Raamses, the more they afflicted them the more they grew, the king's order to the midwives, the two stones, the fear of God, the king's question, the midwives' answer about the lively Hebrew women, God dealing well with them and making them houses, and the king's last order about the river, are all in verses eight to twenty-two. The king's words, his question and the midwives' answer are kept as the book gives them, in a plain rendering, and I have only added a few words of Puah's around the edges.

The book does not say whether the midwives were Hebrews or Egyptians; Josephus thought they were Egyptians, and the rabbis of the Talmud thought they were Hebrews. It does not say how many midwives there were, or which king this was. The meanings of the two names are guesses. The tradition that Shiphrah and Puah were Jochebed and Miriam is in the Talmud, tractate Sotah, and Rashi's readings of the names and the houses are in his commentary on these verses. Saint Augustine wrote about the midwives in Against Lying, and Saint Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae, where he says they were rewarded for their fear of God and their kindness, not for any untruth. The old Egyptian tale of the three royal births is in the Westcar Papyrus.

And Zibiah, Leah and her family, the walks to and from the palace, the porridge, and every word the midwives say to each other, are our own invention.

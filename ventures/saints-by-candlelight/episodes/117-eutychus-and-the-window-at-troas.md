It is the middle of the night, in a room at the top of a house, and the room is full of people and full of lamps.

There are lamps on the table and lamps on the shelves and lamps hanging from hooks in the ceiling, little clay lamps, each with one small flame, and the air is warm and thick and smells of olive oil. There are people on the benches, people on the floor, people leaning against the walls. And at the front, a short man with a tired, eager face is talking. He has been talking since supper. He shows no sign at all of stopping.

In the window, with his back against the frame and his feet tucked up, sits a young man. The window is open, because the room is so warm, and the night air is cool on the back of his neck, and that is very pleasant. His eyes close. He opens them. They close again. He opens them, a little more slowly. The voice at the front of the room goes on, and on, like water running in a stream, and the flames of the lamps go soft and blurry, and the young man's head tips gently to one side.

His name, as it happens, means Lucky.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Last time, if you were with us, we were in the great city of Ephesus, where a silversmith called Demetrius filled the theatre with twenty thousand people who shouted for two hours, most of whom had no idea why they were there. In the end the town clerk calmed them down, and sent them all home, and Saint Paul, who had been kept out of the theatre by his friends, gathered the disciples, and encouraged them, and said goodbye, and set off for Macedonia.

Tonight we follow him. It is a story about a journey, and a story about saying goodbye, which are often the same story. And in the middle of it, there is one very long night in a town called Troas.

But first, where Paul went, because Luke, who wrote the Acts of the Apostles, covers the next several months in about four sentences, the way you might sum up a long holiday to someone who has not asked. Paul went through Macedonia, which is northern Greece, visiting the churches he had founded there years before, at Philippi and Thessalonica and Beroea, and giving them much encouragement. Then he came down into Greece proper, and stayed there for three months.

Luke does not say where in Greece. But most people think it was Corinth, in the house of a man called Gaius, because Paul wrote a letter from there, and in it he says, Gaius, who is host to me and to the whole church, greets you. The letter he wrote is the one we call the Letter to the Romans, the longest letter he ever wrote, and it is a fair bet that Gaius ran short of lamp oil that winter. That, I should say, is the common view of the scholars and not something Luke tells us. But it is a nice thought: that one of the great letters of the world was written in somebody's spare room.

And then, just as Paul was about to set sail for Syria, there was a plot against him. Luke does not give the details. It may be that someone meant to do him harm on board a ship full of pilgrims, where a man can easily go over the side at night and nobody notice. So Paul changed his plans. He turned round, and went back the long way, by land, up through Macedonia again.

He was not alone. Luke lists the people who went with him, and I am going to read you the list, because each of them was a real person, and each of them came a very long way. There was Sopater, son of Pyrrhus, from Beroea. There were Aristarchus and Secundus, from Thessalonica. Aristarchus you may remember: he was the one who was dragged into the theatre at Ephesus, and he was evidently not put off. There was Gaius from Derbe, a little town far inland. There was Timothy, Paul's young helper, who by now was not so very young. And there were Tychicus and Trophimus, from the province of Asia, which is to say from around Ephesus.

Seven men from five or six different places. Why so many? Paul's letters tell us that for years he had been collecting money from the churches of Greece and Asia for the poor Christians in Jerusalem, and he had told the Corinthians that the money would go with people the churches themselves chose. So many scholars think that this little band were the delegates, carrying the gift, each one sent by his own church to see that it arrived. Luke does not say so in so many words. But it would explain a great deal, including why Paul was so keen not to be robbed at sea.

These seven went on ahead, and waited for Paul at Troas. And Paul stayed behind at Philippi for the days of Unleavened Bread, which is the week of Passover, in the spring.

And then something small happens in the text, which you would miss if you were not paying attention. Luke stops saying they. He starts saying we. We sailed away from Philippi, he says. We came to them at Troas.

Luke is back.

* * *

Now I should tell you about Luke, and about that word we, because it is one of the nicest small things in the whole of the New Testament.

Most of the Acts of the Apostles is told the way a historian tells a story, from the outside. Paul did this. Peter went there. They said that. But here and there, without any announcement at all, the story switches to we. We set sail. We stayed some days. We were gathered together. And then, a little later, just as quietly, it switches back again. The usual explanation, the one the early Church gave, is that these are the parts where Luke himself was there. He is not the sort of writer who tells you he was there. He simply slips into the boat with everyone else, and you notice his footprints in the sand.

The first time it happened was years before, at this same town of Troas. Paul had come there once before, not knowing where to go next, and in the night he had a vision of a man of Macedonia, standing and pleading with him: come over to Macedonia and help us. And immediately, Luke says, we sought to go on into Macedonia. That was where the we began. And the we stayed in Philippi when Paul left it. And now, all these years later, in the spring, at Philippi, the we picks up again, as though Luke had been waiting there all along with his bag packed. Perhaps he had.

So they set off from Philippi, from its little port, and sailed across to Troas. And it took them five days.

This is worth a moment. Because the first time they made that crossing, in the other direction, it took them two. Same sea, same distance, more or less. Two days one way, five days the other. Anyone who has ever travelled by sea, or by train for that matter, will recognise this. The wind was with them the first time and against them the second. Luke does not complain about it. He simply writes it down. Five days. I think you can hear, in the number, a certain amount of sitting on a deck looking at the same island for a long time.

* * *

Troas was a city on the coast of what is now north-western Turkey, looking out across the sea towards Greece. Its full name was Alexandria Troas, which tells you it was founded by one of the generals who came after Alexander the Great, and which also tells you that the world had rather a lot of cities called Alexandria, so you had to add something on the end to say which one you meant. By Paul's day it was a Roman colony, with a harbour, and walls, and baths, and a good deal of coming and going.

And it was near Troy. Just up the coast, a few hours' walk inland, were the ruins of the city of the old stories, where, the poems said, the Greeks had fought for ten years over a beautiful queen, and ended it with a wooden horse. People in Paul's day visited it the way we visit old castles, and local guides would show them the very spot where this hero or that hero had fallen, and charge them for it. I suspect some of the very spots moved about a bit, depending on how many visitors there were.

Paul had been to Troas more than once. In one of his letters to the Corinthians he says that he came to Troas to preach the gospel, and a door was opened for him in the Lord, but he had no rest in his spirit, because he could not find his friend Titus there, so he left and went on to Macedonia. I love that. A door opened, and Paul walked straight past it, because he was worried about a friend. Even the great apostle was not always perfectly organised.

And many years later, near the end of his life, writing to Timothy from prison, he asks: when you come, bring the cloak that I left at Troas with Carpus, and the books, and above all the parchments. So somebody at Troas, a man called Carpus, had Paul's coat. Which means, I suppose, that Paul left a coat behind on one of his visits and never quite got round to collecting it. If you have ever left a jumper at a friend's house, you are in excellent company.

So: Troas. A church had grown up there. Not a big one, perhaps, but a real one, with people who had been waiting a long time to see Paul again. And Paul and his friends stayed there for seven days.

* * *

Now let me introduce somebody who is not in the Acts of the Apostles at all, because I have made her up.

Her name is Melitta, and she sells lamps.

She has a stall just off the main street of Troas, under an awning, with a long shelf of little clay lamps in rows, all the same shape: round, flat, with a hole in the top for the oil, a spout at the front for the wick, and a little handle at the back. Some are plain. Some have pictures pressed into the top, a dolphin, or a bunch of grapes, or a gladiator, which is the ancient equivalent of a mug with a football team on it. She has been selling lamps for thirty years. She can tell you, by looking at the flame, whether your oil is good, whether your wick is cut right, and whether your daughter-in-law has been buying cheap oil from the man on the corner, which she has.

Melitta has been a Christian for a few years now. She belongs to the little church that meets in the upper room of a house near the harbour, a tall house, three storeys high, belonging to a family that does well out of shipping. And when she hears that Paul is in the town, and that on the first day of the week everyone will gather to break bread with him, she does what any lamp-seller would do. She counts how many lamps the room will need.

"It'll be packed," she says to her neighbour, the man who sells figs. "Everyone will come. And Paul talks, they say. Paul talks and talks. We'll be there after dark."

"How many lamps?" says the fig man.

"All of them," says Melitta.

* * *

Here is how Luke tells it. On the first day of the week, when we were gathered together to break bread, Paul talked with them, intending to depart on the next day. And he prolonged his speech until midnight.

There is a lot in that one sentence, so let us go slowly.

The first day of the week is Sunday. This is one of the very earliest places anywhere where we see Christians gathering on the first day of the week, the day of the Resurrection, rather than on the Sabbath. And they gathered to break bread. Christians from the earliest centuries, in East and West, have seen in that phrase the Eucharist, the Lord's Supper, the breaking of the bread that the Lord himself commanded on the night before he died. So this is not just a talk. This is the church at Troas at prayer, on the Lord's day, with the apostle among them, and the bread waiting.

They probably met in the evening. Sunday was an ordinary working day in the Roman Empire. Nobody got it off. If you were a dock worker, or a servant, or a slave, you worked all day, and then you came along afterwards, when it was getting dark, tired and hungry, to be with your brothers and sisters. Some of them had probably been on their feet since before dawn.

And Paul was leaving the next day. He knew it, and they knew it, and he had a great deal to say. So he talked. He talked, as Luke puts it, until midnight.

I want to be fair to Paul here. It is easy to make jokes about long sermons, and I am going to make one in a minute. But Paul had founded churches across half the Roman world, and he could not be in all of them at once, and he did not know when, if ever, he would see these people again. Everything he wanted them to remember had to fit into one evening. If you had one evening with people you loved and might never see again, I think you might talk until midnight too.

That said. Midnight.

* * *

Melitta was there. Of course she was. She had brought a basket of lamps, and she had lit them, and placed them, and she kept getting up quietly in the middle of things to trim a wick here, or top up the oil there, the way a good waiter tops up your glass without you noticing.

Luke, who was also there, noticed the lamps. He wrote: there were many lamps in the upper room where we were gathered. He does not say why he mentions it. Some people think it is simply the kind of detail an eyewitness remembers, the way you might remember, years later, that it was raining on the day of a wedding. Others think he is explaining what happens next: a room high up under the roof, crowded with people, with dozens of little oil flames burning away for hours, would get very warm, and rather stuffy, and very, very sleepy. And some think he mentions it so that no one could say the meeting was held in secret, in the dark. That last was a charge that people did sometimes throw at Christians. All three are readings. Luke just says there were many lamps.

But I know which reading Melitta would have chosen.

"Too many," she would have said, afterwards. "I said all of them, and I meant all of them, and I was wrong. You could have baked bread in there."

And sitting in the window, as you have already guessed, was a young man called Eutychus.

Luke calls him a young man, and later a boy, using a word that could mean anything from a lad to a young servant. So he was young. Perhaps fifteen, perhaps twenty. Perhaps he had worked all day on the docks. Perhaps he had carried sacks, or rowed a boat, or swept a courtyard. We do not know. We know that he came. He came, when he could have gone home to bed, and he squeezed in, and the only place left to sit was the window.

And his name, Eutychus, is a Greek name that means fortunate. Or lucky. It was quite a common name, especially among slaves and freedmen, because it was the kind of name you gave somebody in the hope that it would come true.

Hold on to that.

The window was open. Windows in those days had no glass, usually, only wooden shutters, and on a warm night, in a room full of lamps, the shutters would be wide. It was the coolest seat in the house. It was also, though nobody was thinking about this, three floors up.

And Paul talked. And the lamps burned. And the air grew warmer, and thicker, and smelled of oil and wool and people. And Eutychus, sitting in the window, sank, as Luke puts it, into a deep sleep. Luke actually says it twice, in a way, which is how you know he saw it happen. Eutychus was sinking into a deep sleep as Paul talked still longer. And then, overcome by sleep, he fell.

He fell from the third storey, down into the dark.

* * *

I am not going to describe the fall, because this is a bedtime story, and because Luke does not describe it either. He simply says what happened, in a few plain words, and so will we.

There was a noise from the courtyard below. Somebody at the window looked out, and shouted. And everybody in that room, every tired dock worker and every widow and every one of Paul's seven companions, jumped up at once and ran for the stairs.

They found him on the ground. And Luke, who was a physician, if the old tradition is right that he is the Luke whom Paul calls the beloved physician, says this: he was taken up dead.

Luke does not say he looked dead, or seemed dead, or was knocked out. He says, they took him up dead. People have argued about how to read that, and some have said that perhaps he was not quite dead after all, because of what Paul says next. But Luke's words are plain, and the church at Troas plainly understood it as something God had done. We will tell it the way Luke tells it.

And Paul went down.

He did not stop to finish his sentence. He came down the stairs, past all the people crowding the doorway, and out into the courtyard. And he bent over the boy, and lay down on him, and put his arms round him.

If you have been listening to this podcast for a while, you may know where else this happens. In the old stories of the prophets, Elijah once carried a widow's dead son up to his own room, and stretched himself upon the child three times, and cried to the Lord, and the Lord heard him, and the child lived. And Elisha, years later, did the same thing in the house of the woman of Shunem: he lay upon the child, mouth on mouth, and eyes on eyes, and hands on hands, and the child grew warm. Paul knew those stories by heart. He had grown up with them. And now here he was, on the cold stones of a courtyard in Troas in the middle of the night, doing what the prophets had done, and praying, as they had prayed, to the same God.

And then he looked up at all the frightened faces, and he said: Do not be alarmed. His life is in him.

* * *

What happened next is, I think, the most surprising thing in the whole story.

They went back upstairs.

All of them. Paul went back up, and the church went back up, and they sat down again in that hot little room with all the lamps, and Paul broke the bread, and they ate. And then, Luke says, Paul went on talking with them for a long while, until daybreak. And so he departed.

I find that wonderful. A young man has just fallen out of a window, and a miracle has just happened in the courtyard, and you might expect everybody to go home and lie down. But no. They go back up the stairs, and they do the thing they had come to do in the first place. They break the bread. The Lord's Supper does not get cancelled because of the excitement. If anything, I think they had more reason than ever to give thanks.

And they kept talking until dawn. Not a sermon any more, perhaps. Luke uses a different word here, a word that means something more like conversation, chatting, talking together. The speech was over. Now it was the kind of talk that happens at four in the morning among people who love each other and know they are about to say goodbye. Questions. Stories. Somebody laughing at something that would not be funny in daylight.

I do not know where Eutychus was during all this. I like to imagine that he was there, wrapped in somebody's cloak, sitting very carefully on the floor, a long way from any window. And that Melitta brought him something warm to drink. And that every so often somebody looked across at him, just to check.

Because the last thing Luke tells us about that night is this. They brought the lad home alive, and they were not a little comforted.

Not a little comforted. That is Luke's way of saying they were enormously comforted, and it is the sort of understatement people use when the feeling is too big for words. Eutychus went home alive. His name came true. He was lucky after all, though I think everybody in that room would have told you that luck had very little to do with it.

And Melitta, on the way home in the grey light, carrying her basket of empty lamps, said to herself: next time, I am bringing half as many.

* * *

In the morning, Paul did something rather odd.

The rest of them got on a ship. Luke and Timothy and the delegates and all the baggage went down to the harbour and sailed off along the coast, round a great headland, towards a town called Assos. But Paul did not go with them. Paul had arranged it, Luke says, intending to go by land himself.

Having talked all night. Having not been to bed. Paul set off to walk to Assos.

It is about twenty miles by the road. Twenty miles, after a night like that one. Luke does not say why. Perhaps he wanted some time on his own, to pray, after all those crowded days. Perhaps he wanted to visit somebody on the way. Perhaps he simply wanted to walk. I have known people who, after a very long night and a very big feeling, need to go for a long walk before they can sit still again. And the ship had to go the long way round, past the cape, so perhaps he knew he could get there in time on foot. Luke, who was on the ship, was probably very glad of a quiet morning.

Assos was a town on a high rock above the sea, with a temple of Athena on the top, and a harbour at the bottom, and a very steep road between the two. It had a famous visitor once, centuries before. Aristotle, the great philosopher, lived there for a few years as a guest of the local ruler, a man called Hermias, and married a young woman of Hermias's household. The old writers tell us so. So the town where Paul climbed aboard had once been home to the most famous thinker of the Greek world. I don't suppose Paul stopped to look for his house. He had a ship to catch.

And there, at Assos, they took him on board, and off they went.

* * *

Now Luke gives us the voyage the way a ship's log would, one stop a day, and I am going to give it to you the same way, because it has a lovely rhythm to it, like counting sheep.

From Assos they came to Mitylene, the chief town on the island of Lesbos.

The next day they sailed from there and came opposite Chios, another island, famous for its wine and its sticky resin, and they spent the night anchored off it.

The next day they touched at Samos, yet another island. One old manuscript adds that they stayed that night at a place called Trogyllium, on the mainland, across the narrow water. Most manuscripts do not. It is the sort of detail somebody may have added because he knew the coast, or may have been there all along. We will let it be.

And the day after that, they came to Miletus.

Mitylene. Chios. Samos. Miletus. One island after another, each a day apart, on a small ship in the spring. If you are still awake, you can count them again on your fingers. Mitylene. Chios. Samos. Miletus.

Notice what they did not do. They did not stop at Ephesus. Luke says Paul had decided to sail past Ephesus, so as not to lose time in Asia, because he was hurrying to be in Jerusalem, if he could, for the day of Pentecost.

You can understand that. If you have ever had to be somewhere by a certain day, and you were going to pass the house of someone who loves you very much and talks for a long time, you will know the problem. If Paul had stopped at Ephesus, there would have been a visit to every house, and a meal at every table, and someone would have been ill and needed praying for, and someone else would have had a question about the Letter to the Corinthians, and three weeks would have gone by. So he sailed past.

But he could not bear to sail past entirely.

* * *

Miletus was an old, old city, a little way down the coast from Ephesus, at the mouth of a river called the Maeander. That river wandered so much, looping back and forth across its valley, that its name has come down to us as a word. When a river wanders, or a conversation, or a long walk on a Sunday afternoon, we say it meanders. That is this river.

And Miletus was famous, in a way, for thinking. Six hundred years before Paul, a man called Thales lived there, and the Greeks counted him the first of all their philosophers. The historian Herodotus tells a story that Thales foretold an eclipse of the sun, and that when it came, in the middle of a battle, the two armies were so startled that they stopped fighting and made peace. If you ever want to end an argument, I recommend an eclipse. They are hard to arrange.

So Paul's ship came into the harbour at Miletus. And from there, Luke says, Paul sent a message to Ephesus, and called for the elders of the church to come to him.

It was a good thirty miles, and a ferry ride, and more, and so the messenger had to get there, and the elders had to get ready, and then they had to make the journey back. It must have taken several days. Paul waited.

And here, if you will let me, I am going to slip our own invented friends into the story. Because if a message came to the elders at Ephesus that Paul was at Miletus and wanted to see them, I think the news would have gone round the church like a bell. And I think that in a narrow street, behind a shop that smells of leather and lamp oil, an old widow called Doris, with a great many bracelets, would have heard it.

"Hermon," she says to her grandson. "You're going to Miletus."

Hermon, you may remember, is fifteen now, or nearly, and works for a silversmith, and spent a very confusing afternoon in the theatre last time we saw him. He has grown a little. His voice has mostly stopped squeaking.

"Me?" says Hermon. "Why me? I'm not an elder."

"You have legs," says Doris. "The elders are old. Somebody has to carry the water skins. And these." And she gives him a cloth bundle, warm and heavy, which smells of honey. "Honey cakes. For Paul. He never eats properly. Tell him they're from Doris, and tell him the sparrows are still there."

And so Hermon goes, walking behind a little group of grey-haired men, carrying water skins and honey cakes, down the coast road to Miletus.

* * *

They found Paul by the harbour. And when they had all come together, Paul spoke to them.

This speech is the only one in the whole of the Acts of the Apostles that Paul gives to Christians, rather than to people who have not yet believed. All his other speeches are to crowds, or synagogues, or philosophers, or governors. This one is to his friends. It is the nearest thing we have, in Acts, to hearing how Paul talked to people he loved. I am going to give it to you in plain words, close to what Luke wrote, adding nothing. Listen to it the way Hermon heard it, sitting on a coil of rope at the edge of the circle, with the sea behind him.

You know, Paul said, how I lived among you the whole time, from the first day I set foot in Asia. I served the Lord with all humility, and with tears, and with the trials that came to me through the plots of my enemies. I did not hold back anything that would help you. I taught you in public, and from house to house. I told everyone, Jews and Greeks alike, to turn back to God, and to have faith in our Lord Jesus.

And now, he said, I am going to Jerusalem, bound in the Spirit, not knowing what will happen to me there. Except that in every city the Holy Spirit warns me that chains and hardships are waiting for me. But I do not count my life as worth anything to myself, if only I can finish my course, and the work the Lord Jesus gave me, to bear witness to the good news of the grace of God.

And then he said the thing that made them start to cry.

And now, he said, I know that none of you, among whom I went about proclaiming the kingdom, will ever see my face again.

Hermon looked round the circle. Some of the old men had their eyes shut.

So I tell you today, Paul went on, that I am not to blame if any of you is lost. Because I did not hold back from telling you the whole purpose of God. Keep watch over yourselves, and over all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to shepherd the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood.

That word overseers is the Greek word from which, in time, we get our word bishop, and it has been much discussed. Paul uses it here for the same men Luke has just called elders. We will not try to settle tonight how the shepherds of the early Church were ordered. What matters in this speech is the shepherding.

I know, Paul said, that after I have gone, savage wolves will come in among you, and they will not spare the flock. And even from among your own selves men will arise speaking twisted things, to draw the disciples away after them. So stay awake. Remember that for three years, night and day, I never stopped warning each one of you, with tears.

Stay awake. I wonder whether anybody thought of Eutychus.

And now, Paul said, I commend you to God, and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up, and to give you the inheritance among all those who are made holy. I never wanted anyone's silver, or gold, or clothes. You know yourselves that these hands of mine worked to provide for my own needs, and for the people who were with me.

And here he held up his hands. Luke does not say that he did, but these hands is such a pointing kind of phrase that I think he must have. Tentmaker's hands. Rough, and marked from the needle and the thread, and from years of the leather and the goat hair. The hands that had worked mornings at the bench in Priscilla and Aquila's shop.

In everything, he said, I have shown you that by working hard in this way we must help the weak, and remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he himself said: it is more blessed to give than to receive.

* * *

That last line deserves its own moment.

It is more blessed to give than to receive. You have probably heard it. It is one of the most famous sayings of Jesus. And yet, if you go looking for it in the four Gospels, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, you will not find it there. It is not in any of them. It is here, and only here, on a beach at Miletus, in the mouth of Paul, who heard it from the people who had heard the Lord himself.

Saint John says at the end of his Gospel that Jesus did many other things, so many that if every one of them were written down, he supposed the world itself could not contain the books. This is one of those things that was nearly not written down. It came down by word of mouth, from the first disciples, to Paul, to the elders on the beach, to Luke, who wrote it into his book. One small saying, kept safe the way you keep a coin in your pocket.

I like to think that Hermon, sitting on his coil of rope, heard it and looked down at the bundle of honey cakes in his lap.

* * *

And when Paul had said these things, Luke writes, he knelt down with them all, and prayed.

Right there, by the harbour, on the stones. Paul, and the elders, and Luke, and Timothy, and the seven delegates, and, in our story, one boy with water skins. All of them kneeling.

And they all wept aloud, and they fell on Paul's neck, and kissed him.

That is how people said goodbye, in that part of the world, and in many parts of the world still. Not a handshake. Arms round the neck, and a kiss on each cheek, and not caring who was watching.

And Luke says what grieved them most of all was the word he had spoken, that they would never see his face again.

And then they brought him to the ship.

In our story, I think that somewhere in the middle of all that, Hermon pushed his way through the grey heads, a bit red in the face, and held out the bundle.

"These are from my grandmother," he said. "Doris. She says you don't eat properly. And she says to tell you that the sparrows are still there."

And Paul, I think, laughed, even with tears on his face. And took the honey cakes. And put one tentmaker's hand on the boy's head for a moment, the way you would bless a child.

"Tell Doris," he said, "that I'll eat every one. And tell her to look after the sparrows."

That is our invention, of course. Luke does not mention honey cakes, or sparrows, or boys with water skins. But it is the kind of thing that happens when people say goodbye on a beach, and I think Luke would forgive us.

* * *

The next line in Luke is one of my favourites in the whole book. In the plain English translations it usually says: and when we had parted from them, and set sail. But the Greek word Luke uses is stronger than parted. It is the word you would use for tearing something away, pulling it off by force, the way you might pull a bandage off, or a child off a door handle.

When we had torn ourselves away from them, Luke says.

That is how hard it was. They had to tear themselves away.

[pause]

So the ship sailed on, out of the harbour at Miletus, past the islands, east towards Cos and Rhodes and the long voyage to Tyre and Caesarea and Jerusalem. And the elders of Ephesus stood on the shore and watched it go, until it was a small dark shape on the water, and then a speck, and then nothing.

Paul did get to Jerusalem. And everything the Spirit had warned him of came true. There was a riot in the temple, and he was arrested, and he spent years in prison, at Caesarea and then at Rome, and he was in that shipwreck off Malta that we told in an earlier episode. And at the very end, in Rome, the tradition of the Church tells us, he gave his life for the Lord, and went home to God. He finished his course, the way he said he wanted to.

The seven delegates went on with him, at least some of the way. Trophimus, the man from Ephesus, was seen with Paul in Jerusalem, and that very sight helped to start the riot there, because some people wrongly thought Paul had taken a Greek into the temple. And much later, in his last letter, Paul writes to Timothy: Trophimus I left ill at Miletus. So Trophimus came back to the same harbour, years afterwards, and stayed a while to get better. I like to think the elders of Ephesus came down and brought him soup. Tychicus became one of Paul's trusted messengers, carrying his letters to the churches. Aristarchus was in prison with him. Timothy went on to look after the church at Ephesus itself. The Orthodox Church remembers several of these men among the Seventy, the wider circle of the Lord's disciples, and Timothy is a saint in East and West alike.

We do not know what became of Eutychus. Luke does not mention him again. He went home alive one morning in Troas, and the church was not a little comforted, and that is all we know. Which is, I think, quite enough. Most of us will not have our lives written down in books. It is good to know that the people in them were real, and that some of them were young, and tired, and fell asleep in the wrong place at the wrong time, and were loved anyway.

Troas is a ruin now. Its walls are tumbled and overgrown with oak scrub, and its harbour has silted up, and the farmers' goats wander among the broken columns. Assos still sits on its rock, with a few columns of its temple put back up on top, and the view across the water to Lesbos is the same view Aristotle had, and Paul, if he looked. And Miletus, like Ephesus, has lost the sea. The Maeander, wandering and wandering, has carried down so much mud over the centuries that the old harbour where Paul knelt is now a long way inland, among the cotton fields. The great theatre of Miletus is still there. In the wet months the low ground round it fills with water again, and the frogs sing in the marsh where the ships used to be.

And somewhere, I like to think, in Ephesus, a boy came home late, dusty and tired, with empty water skins, and an old woman with a great many bracelets asked him whether Paul liked the honey cakes. And he said yes. And he told her about the hands. And she nodded, and said nothing for a while, and then she said: well. More blessed to give. I could have told him that.

And up in the rafters, the sparrows settled down for the night.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter twenty, and the first verse of chapter twenty-one. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it as Scripture. Everything Luke says happened is kept, and Paul's words to Eutychus and his speech to the elders are in our own plain rendering, adding nothing to what Luke reports.

That Paul spent the three months at Corinth and wrote the Letter to the Romans there is the common view of scholars, built on Romans, chapter sixteen, where Gaius is host to Paul and the whole church. That the seven companions carried the collection for Jerusalem is a reading of Paul's letters, not something Acts says. That the we passages are where Luke himself was present is the early Church's understanding and still the usual one. Why Luke mentions the many lamps, and why Paul walked to Assos, are readings. Trogyllium is in one ancient manuscript only. The saying, it is more blessed to give than to receive, appears in no Gospel; Acts is where it is kept.

The cloak left with Carpus and Trophimus left ill at Miletus come from the Second Letter to Timothy. The open door at Troas is from the Second Letter to the Corinthians. Elijah and Elisha raising the children are in the First and Second Books of Kings. Aristotle at Assos comes from Diogenes Laertius and Strabo, and Thales and the eclipse from Herodotus.

Melitta and her lamps, Doris, Hermon, the water skins, the honey cakes, the sparrows, the shipping family's house, Eutychus wrapped in a cloak, and every word not in Acts, are our own invention.

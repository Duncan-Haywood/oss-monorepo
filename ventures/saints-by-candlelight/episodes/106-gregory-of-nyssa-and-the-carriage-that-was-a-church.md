It is a hot afternoon on a long straight road, somewhere far to the south of anywhere you have heard of, and a carriage is rolling along it.

It is not a grand carriage. It is a government carriage, from the imperial post, which means it is solid and plain and painted a colour that nobody chose, and it is pulled by mules who have been changed at every post station since the mountains, and who all, somehow, have the same expression.

Inside the carriage, a group of men are singing. They are singing a psalm, slowly, in Greek, in parts. One of them is a bishop, a tall, thin man in his late forties, with a long beard going grey and a book open on his knees that he is not really reading. He is singing with his eyes shut.

On the driver's bench outside, next to the official driver, sits a broad man with a sunburnt neck, who is not singing, and who is eating a dried fig in a meaningful way.

The carriage hits a hole in the road. The psalm wobbles, and recovers.

The broad man leans down to the little window and says, "Do you lot ever stop?"

And the bishop opens his eyes, and smiles, and says, "Theon. This is not a carriage. This is a church on wheels. You are sitting on the roof of a church."

"Then the church," says Theon, "needs new springs."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

If you have been listening to this show for a while, you have met this family already.

We have met Basil, the brilliant, busy, slightly bossy elder brother, who became bishop of Caesarea and built a whole new town of hospitals and kitchens outside its walls. We have met Macrina, the eldest sister, who baked the bread, and told Basil to stop showing off, and turned the family house by the river Iris into a home for anyone who needed one. We have met their friend, the other Gregory, Gregory of Nazianzus, who was a poet and hated crowds and ended up, against his will, in charge of the biggest city in the world.

Tonight it is the turn of the one in the middle. The one people forget.

There were ten children in that family, in the hill country of what is now northern Turkey, in the middle of the three hundreds. Five boys, five girls, as far as we can tell. Their father, also called Basil, was a famous teacher of public speaking. Their mother, Emmelia, was a quiet, strong woman whose own father had died a martyr. And their grandmother, Macrina the Elder, had hidden in the forests during the last great persecution, and taught them all their prayers.

Gregory was born about the year three hundred and thirty-five. We do not know the exact year. In families with ten children, people tend to stop writing down the exact year at about child number four.

He was younger than Basil by several years, and younger than Macrina by about ten, and older than Peter, the baby of the family, whom Macrina more or less raised herself. And, as far as we can tell, he was the dreamy one.

Every family has one. You know the type. The one who goes to fetch the bread and comes back an hour later without the bread, but with a very interesting idea about clouds. The one who reads at the dinner table. The one who agrees with whoever spoke last, because he can see that they have a point, and so did the person before them.

Gregory never went away to the great universities, the way Basil did. Basil went to Constantinople and then to Athens, which was the ancient equivalent of going to the best and most expensive university in the world and coming home with opinions. Gregory stayed home and was taught, mostly, by Basil. All his life he called his brother "my father and teacher", which tells you two things. It tells you that he loved him enormously. And it tells you something about what it was like to be Basil's younger brother.

And yet. Of all the children in that remarkable family, it was the quiet, dreamy, forgettable one who turned out to have the deepest mind. People are still reading him, sixteen centuries later, and finding things they had not noticed before. Which is a comfort, I think, to anyone who was ever the one in the middle.

* * *

So let us begin with Gregory as a young man, and with the first of his many small disasters.

As a boy he was made a reader in the church. That was a real job, a small one, with a blessing to go with it. The reader stood up in church and read the Scriptures aloud to the people, in a clear voice, without losing his place. It is a good job for a young man who loves words.

And then, in his twenties, Gregory did what his father had done. He became a teacher of rhetoric. Rhetoric was the art of speaking well in public: how to build an argument, how to move a crowd, how to make people cry at a funeral and laugh at a wedding and vote for you in the city council. It was the most fashionable education in the Roman world. It paid well. It was, in short, a perfectly respectable career.

But his family had other hopes for him, and so did his friends. And one of those friends, the other Gregory, Gregory of Nazianzus, sat down and wrote him a letter.

The letter still survives. In our own words, it goes something like this. My dear Gregory. People are saying things about you, and I do not like what they are saying. They say you have put down the holy books, which are sweet and good to drink, and picked up the salty, bitter books of the speech-makers. They say you would rather be called a professor of speaking than a Christian. Come to your senses. Come back. And then, because he is Gregory of Nazianzus, and cannot help himself, he adds a very elegant quotation and signs off.

I find this letter very touching. It is the kind of letter you only write to someone you love, and it is the kind of letter you only get from someone who loves you. Most of us have had one, at some point. Most of us did not enjoy it.

There is another story, which Gregory tells about himself, years later, in a sermon. His mother Emmelia had built a little chapel on the family estate for the relics of the forty martyrs of Sebaste, forty soldiers who died for the faith on a frozen lake, and there was to be a great night vigil to bring the relics into the chapel. And Gregory, by his own account, did not want to go. He went, grumbling, because his mother asked him to. And during the vigil, he slipped out into the garden, and lay down, and fell asleep.

And he dreamed, he says, that he was going into the garden, and that the soldier martyrs were standing at the gate with rods in their hands, and that they stopped him, and were about to give him a good beating, when one of them, kinder than the others, spoke up for him and let him off.

He woke up, he says, ashamed of himself, and he went back inside and wept, and asked the martyrs to forgive him.

Now, that is his own report of his own dream, and I tell it to you as his. But I love that he told it at all. Most people who become great saints and great bishops tidy up their younger years. Gregory told a church full of people that he once skived off a vigil and got told off by forty soldiers in his sleep.

* * *

Somewhere in these years, Gregory very probably married.

We have to be careful here, because the sources are a little shy about it. Gregory wrote a book called On Virginity, in his thirties, praising the life of monks and nuns who give up marriage for God. And right at the beginning of it, he says, rather wistfully, that he wishes he could have shared in that life himself, but that he is cut off from it, as if by a great gulf, and that he can only look at it from the other side, like someone who sees a beautiful harvest that belongs to someone else.

Most scholars read that as a married man speaking. And years later, Gregory of Nazianzus wrote Gregory a letter of comfort, when a woman called Theosebia died, a woman he calls holy, and truly a partner of a priest. Some historians think Theosebia was Gregory's wife. Some think she was one of his sisters. Nobody knows for certain, and I am not going to pretend to you that I do. Either way, she was someone dear to him, and she died before him, and his friend wrote to tell him that she was with God.

Married or not, Gregory was drawn back, slowly, from the speech-making to the church. Some say he spent a while at his brother Basil's little monastery in the hills of Pontus, across the river from Macrina's house. And it is from about this time that we have the next small disaster. This one is my favourite.

* * *

Here is the situation.

Basil, the elder brother, was by now a priest and then a bishop, and he had fallen out with their uncle. The uncle was also a bishop, and he was also, because this family had very little imagination with names, called Gregory. Uncle Gregory and Basil had quarrelled, over church politics, and they were not speaking. It was making the whole family miserable. You can imagine the family dinners.

And young Gregory, our Gregory, the peacemaker, the one who could always see both sides, decided he would fix it.

So he wrote a letter to Basil. And the letter was signed by Uncle Gregory. And it said, more or less, my dear nephew, let us be friends again. And when that did not quite work, he wrote another one.

Uncle Gregory had not written either of them.

Here our mule driver enters the story. You may remember Theon from our episode about Macrina. He drove Gregory's mules up the valley to her house, and he is our own invention, and so is everything he says. But somebody drove Gregory's mules, all those years, so it might as well be Theon. And I like to think that Theon was standing in the yard when Basil's reply arrived.

"Letter from your brother," says Theon, holding it out.

Gregory takes it, and breaks the seal, and reads it. And his face goes slowly pink, starting at the beard and working up.

"Good news?" says Theon.

"Not exactly," says Gregory.

Basil's real letter still survives. In our own plain words, it says something like this. My dear brother, how am I supposed to argue with you, when you are so simple-hearted? You sent me letters from our uncle, and I believed them, and I was delighted, and I showed them to people, and then it turned out our uncle had never written them, and he says so, and now I have been made ashamed in front of a great many people. And then, in effect, please do not help me like this again.

Theon, in my imagination, reads this over Gregory's shoulder, because he can read a little, and has always wanted to know what bishops write to each other.

"You forged a letter," says Theon. "From a bishop. To a bishop."

"I was trying to make peace," says Gregory.

"With a forged letter."

"It was a very kind forged letter."

Theon looks at him for a long moment.

"Father," he says, "I have been driving mules for twenty years. And I have learned one thing. You cannot trick a mule into going where it does not want to go. You have to walk in front, with a carrot, where it can see you."

And Gregory, who was, under all the dreaminess, a humble man, says, "Yes. Yes, I think that is right."

It all worked out, in the end. Basil and the uncle were reconciled, the proper way, with letters they had actually written. But it tells you something true about Gregory. He wanted peace so badly that once, just once, he tried to take a short cut to it. And he was found out, and he was told off, and he took it. A man who can be told off by his brother and by forty martyrs in a dream and by his mule driver, and learn something each time, is a man who is going to grow.

* * *

In the year three hundred and seventy-two, or thereabouts, Basil, who was now archbishop of Caesarea, had a problem.

The emperor at the time, Valens, favoured the Arian party, the people who said that the Son of God was not truly God in the same way as the Father, but something a little less, a sort of first and highest creature. Basil, like the Council of Nicaea before him, and like the whole Church after him, believed and taught that the Son is truly God, of one being with the Father. And the emperor, partly to annoy Basil, cut his province in two, so that half of Basil's bishops suddenly belonged to someone else.

Basil's answer was to make new bishops, quickly, in little towns, wherever he could, with men he could trust. He made his friend Gregory of Nazianzus bishop of a dusty crossroads called Sasima, which Gregory of Nazianzus never forgave him for. And he made his brother Gregory bishop of Nyssa.

Nyssa was a small town in Cappadocia, west of Caesarea, on the river Halys. It was not famous. It was not rich. It had a church and a market and a lot of sheep, and it was the kind of place you go through on the way to somewhere else. Today, nobody is entirely sure exactly where it was. There is a hill near a modern village where it probably stood. Which is, if you think about it, a very humble thing for the name of a great saint. He is known all over the world by the name of a town that has disappeared.

Gregory did not want to be a bishop. Almost nobody in these stories ever does. But he went, and he was consecrated, and he went to Nyssa, and he did his best.

And his best, at first, was not very good. Not at the politics, anyway.

There is another letter of Basil's, a little later, where someone has suggested sending Gregory as an envoy to Rome, to talk to the pope about the troubles of the Eastern churches. And Basil writes back, in our own words: I would not send my brother. He has no experience at all of church business. He would be a good man to send to a good man, who would treat him kindly. But to a proud and difficult man, sitting on a high seat, what use would he be? He does not know how to flatter. He is not that kind of person.

Which, if you read it twice, is both a put-down and the nicest thing anyone ever said about him. He does not know how to flatter.

* * *

And then the trouble came.

The emperor's party did not like Basil's new bishops, and the governor of the region of Pontus at that time, a man called Demosthenes, who was on the emperor's side, decided to get rid of Gregory. In about the year three hundred and seventy-five, Gregory was accused of two things. The first was that his consecration as bishop had not been done properly. The second was that he had mishandled the church's money.

Now, the second charge is the funny one, or it would be, if it had not been so serious for him. Because Gregory was the kind of man who would not have known how to steal money if you had given him written instructions and a map. He was, in his brother's word, simple. What is much more likely is that he was not very good at keeping accounts, which is not a crime, though anybody who has ever had to sort out a church's books will tell you it can feel like one.

Soldiers were sent to arrest him. They took him away. And on the road, Gregory fell ill, with a pain in his side and a fever, and in the confusion, he got away. We do not know exactly how. The letters only tell us that he was taken, and that he was ill, and that he escaped, and that Basil wrote to everyone he could think of to defend him.

And then, for about two years, Gregory was a bishop without a town. A meeting of bishops on the emperor's side declared him deposed, and put someone else in his place, and Gregory wandered. He went from house to house, from friend to friend, never long in one place. He wrote to his friends. He prayed. He waited.

I like to think Theon came with him.

Picture a small inn on a wet evening, somewhere in the hills. A bishop with no diocese sits at a table in the corner, writing by a little oil lamp, and a mule driver with no particular reason to be there sits opposite, mending a harness.

"You could go home," says Gregory, without looking up. "To your family. Nobody is paying you."

"Nobody was paying me before," says Theon. "Your brother pays me, sometimes, when he remembers. Which is never."

"Then why do you stay?"

Theon pulls the strap tight and bites off the thread. "Because somebody has to stop you giving the mules away," he says. "You gave the grey one away at the last place."

"The man needed a mule."

"The man needed a mule," agrees Theon. "And now you need a mule. And so it goes on. That's why I stay."

Gregory smiles, and goes back to his writing. Outside, it rains. And there is a feeling, in that little room, that you sometimes get in the hardest seasons of a life, that this, too, is somewhere God is.

* * *

In August of the year three hundred and seventy-eight, the Emperor Valens died, in a great battle against the Goths at Adrianople. It was a terrible day for the Roman world. And one of its quieter consequences was that the new emperors let the exiled bishops go home.

So Gregory went home to Nyssa.

And here we have, in his own hand, one of the loveliest letters he ever wrote. He wrote it to a fellow bishop, describing his return. In our own plain words: as we came near the town, the people came out to meet us. Some of them had heard we were coming, and some had not, and the ones who had not came running when they heard the noise. They came out along the road, and crowded round the carriage, so that we could hardly get down from it, and we were carried, more or less, into the church, and it was full, and there were lamps, and there were tears, mine as much as anyone's.

The town that did not want a bishop, and the bishop who did not want the town, had found out, in two years apart, that they loved each other.

I imagine a woman at the front of the crowd with a basket of onions she had been taking to market and has completely forgotten about, waving at him. She is our invention. But somebody was at the front of that crowd. Somebody always is.

And I imagine Theon, standing by the mules at the edge of it all, with his arms folded, saying to nobody in particular, "Well. That's more like it."

The next year was the hardest of Gregory's life, and also the year he became the man we remember.

At the very beginning of the year three hundred and seventy-nine, or perhaps a little before, Basil died. He was not yet fifty. He had worn himself out, building and preaching and arguing and caring for the poor, and his body simply stopped. We told that story in another episode, as much as we could.

And a few months later, Gregory went up the valley to see his sister Macrina, whom he had not seen for nearly eight years. We told that story too. He found her lying on a plank on the floor, very ill, and she talked to him for two days about the soul and the resurrection, and comforted him for Basil, when it ought to have been the other way round, and then, at the hour when the lamps are lit, she died.

So in one year, Gregory lost the brother who had taught him everything, and the sister who had taught his brother.

And here is the strange and wonderful thing. That was the year the dreamy younger brother grew up.

Basil had been in the middle of a great argument, in writing, with a clever man called Eunomius, who was the sharpest thinker on the Arian side. Basil had written a book against him. Eunomius had written a book back. And now Basil was dead, and there was nobody to answer.

So Gregory answered. He picked up his brother's pen, as it were, and he wrote a long, careful, brilliant reply, and then another, and then another. And people who had thought of him as Basil's little brother started to realise that he was something else. He was a thinker in his own right. Possibly a deeper one.

He wrote, too, about his sister, the Life of Macrina, which is one of the most tender books from the whole early Church. And he wrote the long conversation from her deathbed, On the Soul and the Resurrection, where he makes her the teacher and himself the pupil. Which, again, tells you something about him. He had become a great bishop, a great writer, and he still put himself in the second chair.

* * *

In the year three hundred and eighty-one, the new emperor, Theodosius, called the bishops of the East to a great council in Constantinople. It is the council that finished the Creed that most Christians still say on Sundays, the Creed of Nicaea and Constantinople, with its words about the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life.

Gregory was there. So was his friend Gregory of Nazianzus, who was presiding for part of it and hating every minute. And so was a holy old bishop called Meletius, from Antioch, who was presiding at the start, and who died, quite suddenly, while the council was still meeting.

The bishops held a great funeral for him, and Gregory of Nyssa was asked to give the funeral speech. It still survives. It is full of grief, and also of a kind of rich, sad beauty, the way funeral speeches were in those days. Among its many pictures, he speaks of the bishops as wedding guests whose bridegroom has been taken from them. He was not, perhaps, the most famous bishop at that council. But they asked him to speak for all of them.

And then, a few weeks after the council, the emperor issued a law. It still survives too, in the great book of Roman laws. It said, more or less, that churches should be handed over to bishops who believed the faith of Nicaea, and it named, for each part of the empire, a few bishops whose communion was the test. If you were in communion with these bishops, you were orthodox. And for the region of Pontus, one of the names on the list was Gregory, bishop of Nyssa.

Think about that. Ten years before, he was the man his brother would not send to Rome. Six years before, he was a wanted man, escaping from soldiers with a fever. And now his name was in the law code of the Roman Empire, as one of the bishops you could measure the faith against.

I imagine Theon hearing about it.

"They put you in a law," he says.

"Apparently."

"Does that mean you get paid?"

"No."

"Does it mean you get a better carriage?"

Gregory thinks about this. "Actually," he says, "it might."

* * *

It did.

After the council, the bishops sent Gregory on a mission, to sort out a quarrel in the churches of Arabia, which in those days meant the Roman province east of the river Jordan, in what is now Jordan and the south of Syria. It was a very long way. And since he was going on the emperor's business, the emperor gave him the use of the public post.

The public post was the Roman government's own travel network. Every so many miles along the great roads there was a station, with fresh animals and a carriage and a place to sleep, and if you had the right pass, signed by the right official, you could travel the whole length of the empire, changing mules at every station, without paying a penny. It was the ancient equivalent of a first-class rail pass. Gregory had never had one before.

And here is where we came in, at the beginning of the evening.

Gregory wrote about this journey afterwards, in a letter. And he says, in our own words: the emperor gave us a carriage of the public post, so that we had no trouble on the road at all. And the carriage was for us a church and a monastery the whole way. We sang psalms together. We kept the fasts. We prayed all the way there and all the way back.

A church and a monastery, on wheels, rattling down the Roman roads, through Cappadocia and Cilicia and Syria and down into Arabia, with a group of men inside singing psalms in parts. I find that a very happy picture. Many of us spend a lot of time in vehicles. Not many of us turn them into churches.

Theon, I imagine, sits outside, next to the official driver, and does not sing.

"You could join in," Gregory says, through the little window, somewhere in Syria.

"I don't know the words."

"You've heard them three hundred times since Caesarea."

Theon is quiet for a mile. And then, very low, under his breath, so that nobody inside could possibly hear, he starts to hum along.

* * *

When the business in Arabia was done, Gregory went on, since he was so near, to Jerusalem.

He went to the holy places. He saw Bethlehem, where Christ was born, and Golgotha, where He died, and the empty tomb, and the Mount of Olives, where He ascended. He was moved by them. He said so, in a letter to three holy women he knew in Cappadocia. He wrote that the places reminded him of the great things God had done there, and that his heart was full.

But he also saw that the Christians of Jerusalem were quarrelling with each other, rather bitterly. And he saw that the city was full of all the ordinary sins of any big city, cheating and drunkenness and worse. And he saw that the pilgrims who came there, some of them, were not made any holier by coming. Some of them were made worse, by the long journey and the crowded inns and the temptations of the road.

So, when he got home, someone wrote to him and asked: should I go to Jerusalem too? Isn't it a holy thing to do? And Gregory wrote back the letter that we now call his letter on pilgrimage.

It is a surprising letter, from a man who has just been on a pilgrimage, and has just written to his friends about how moving it was. In our own plain words, he says something like this. When the Lord gave us the Beatitudes, He did not say, blessed are those who go to Jerusalem. When He told us what to do to inherit eternal life, He did not include a trip to Palestine. If God's grace were thicker in Jerusalem than anywhere else, then there would be less sin there than anywhere else, and there isn't. Changing your place does not bring you nearer to God. Wherever you are, God will come to you, if the lodging of your soul is ready for Him. So stay where you are, and get the lodging ready.

Now, Gregory is not saying that pilgrimage is bad. The Church has always blessed pilgrims, and still does, and Gregory himself had just been, and was glad he went. What he is saying is that the journey that matters most is not the one on the Roman road. It is the one inside you. And the most holy place you will ever stand is the place where you are, when you are turned towards God.

I think Theon would have liked that letter.

"So all that way," he might say, "all those mules, all those post stations, all that singing, and the answer is, you could have stayed at home."

"The answer," says Gregory, "is that God was in the carriage the whole time. You were sitting on Him, more or less."

"Then He needs new springs too," says Theon. But he is smiling.

[pause]

Gregory lived about another fifteen years after that. They were busy years, and full ones.

He went to Constantinople several times, and he preached there, and in the middle of the three hundred and eighties he gave the funeral speeches for the emperor's little daughter Pulcheria, who died very young, and then for the emperor's wife, the Empress Flacilla, who died not long after. He comforted a grieving family who happened to be the most powerful family in the world, and he did it, by all accounts, gently.

He preached, at home in Nyssa, a set of sermons on the book of Ecclesiastes. And in one of them he did something that almost no one in the ancient world did. The book of Ecclesiastes has a line where the wise king says, I bought slaves, men and women. And Gregory, preaching on it, stops, and turns to his congregation, and says, in our own plain words: You bought slaves? You condemn to slavery a human being, whose nature is free? You set a price on the image of God? How much did you pay for it? What is the going rate, in silver, for a creature God made to rule the earth? He has no price. You cannot buy him.

He said that in a world where everybody owned slaves, where even good Christian households did, where hardly anybody had ever thought to question it. It took the world more than a thousand years to catch up with that sermon. But a quiet bishop in a small Cappadocian town said it, plainly, to a church full of people who probably did not want to hear it, in the three hundreds.

He wrote a letter to a fellow bishop, Amphilochius, about a church he hoped to build at Nyssa, a shrine for the martyrs. And he describes it in loving detail: it was to have eight sides, with arches and columns, and he goes through the measurements, and he asks his friend to send him builders, and he worries, in a very human way, about how much they are going to cost. It is the letter of a man who has learned to keep accounts after all. We do not know if the church was ever built. If it was, it is gone now, along with the rest of Nyssa. But the letter is still here, and builders and historians still read it.

And in his old age, he wrote his most beautiful book, The Life of Moses.

* * *

A younger friend had written to him and asked: how can I live a perfect life? What does a perfect Christian life look like?

And Gregory answered with the story of Moses. The baby in the basket on the river. The burning bush. The long walk through the desert. The climb up the mountain into the cloud, where Moses spoke with God. And then, at the end, the strange, beautiful moment when Moses, having already seen so much of God, asks to see more, and God puts him in a cleft of the rock, and covers him with His hand, and lets him see His back as He passes by.

And Gregory says, in our own plain words: do you see? Moses had already climbed the mountain. He had already gone into the cloud. He had already talked with God face to face, as a friend talks with a friend. And still he asked for more. Because the more you see of God, the more you want to see. God is without limit, and so the journey towards Him is without limit too. You never arrive and sit down and say, there, I'm finished. You always go on.

And then he says the thing that is, I think, the heart of everything he ever wrote. The perfection of a human life is not standing still at the top. It is never stopping growing towards the good.

Never stopping growing. Forever. Not because you are never good enough, but because God is always more, and more, and more, and there is always more of Him to love.

The old Greek word for it, which Gregory took from Saint Paul, is epektasis. It means stretching out, reaching forward. Saint Paul says, forgetting what lies behind, I stretch out to what lies ahead. Gregory thought that was what heaven would be like too. Not a still, finished, frozen happiness, but a happiness that grows forever, a journey without end into a light without end.

And at the very end of the book, Gregory tells his young friend what the point of it all is. Not to be afraid of punishment, like a slave. Not to hope for a reward, like a hired worker. But to become God's friend. That, he says, is the only thing truly worth doing, and the only thing worth being proud of. To be known by God, and to become His friend.

[pause]

* * *

The last time we hear of Gregory, he is at a meeting of bishops in Constantinople, in the year three hundred and ninety-four. He would have been about sixty. And after that, the letters stop, and the record goes quiet, and we think he died soon after, perhaps the next year, at home in Nyssa. We do not know the day. We do not know where he was buried.

Theon, our invented friend, would have been an old man by then too. I like to think he outlived his bishop by a little while. I like to think that on summer evenings, in the yard behind the church at Nyssa, with the mules dozing in the shade, he would tell the younger drivers about the carriage that was a church, and the bishop who gave away the grey mule, and the forged letter, and the woman with the onions, and the long road south. And I like to think that, every so often, when he thought nobody could hear him, he would hum a psalm.

The town of Nyssa is gone. Nobody is quite sure exactly where it stood. The eight-sided church, if it was ever built, is gone. The post stations along the great roads are gone, and the roads are mostly gone too, under fields and motorways.

But Gregory's books are not gone. They were copied by monks for a thousand years, in the East and in the West, and they are still being read, more now than for a long time. People who study the soul read him. People who pray read him. People who wonder what heaven will be like read him, and find a bishop from a vanished town telling them that it will never, ever get boring, because God never runs out.

The Church in the East counts him among its greatest teachers. The bishops at a great council four hundred years after his death called him, the tradition says, the father of the Fathers. His feast is on the tenth of January in the Orthodox Church, and on the ninth of March in the Roman Martyrology. In icons he is shown as an old bishop with a long grey beard, in the robes of a bishop of the East, with a book in his hand. Often he stands next to his brother Basil and his friend Gregory, the three of them together, the way they were in life, arguing.

So if, tonight, you are the one in the middle. If you are the dreamy one, the forgotten one, the one who got told off by his big brother, the one who once tried a short cut and got found out. Then perhaps you could think of him. Gregory, who was forgettable, and found the deepest things of all. Who was told he could not flatter, and never learned. Who rode in an emperor's carriage and turned it into a church, and came home to say that you do not need to travel to find God.

Because God is already in the carriage. Wherever you are. Stretching out ahead of you, always a little further, always more to love.

Rest now. You do not have to get there tonight. You only have to keep going, a little, tomorrow. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Most of tonight comes from Gregory's own letters, which have been gathered and translated into English by Anna Silvas, with very full notes. The carriage that was a church and the letter about not needing to go to Jerusalem are his second letter. His visit to Jerusalem and his sadness at the quarrels there are his third letter. His welcome home after the exile is his sixth, and the eight-sided church is his twenty-fifth. The forged letters and the remark about Rome come from his brother Basil's letters, and Gregory of Nazianzus's scolding and his letter about Theosebia are among that Gregory's letters. The law naming Gregory as a test of right belief is in the Theodosian Code, the great Roman law book. The sermon against slavery is the fourth of his sermons on Ecclesiastes. The Life of Moses has a lovely translation by Abraham Malherbe and Everett Ferguson. Anthony Meredith's short book is a good place to start on Gregory's life and thought.

What is history. His family, his work as a reader and then a teacher of speaking, his friend's letter, the forged letters and Basil's rebuke, his being made bishop of Nyssa, Basil's letter about Rome, his arrest, escape and exile, his return, his writings after Basil and Macrina died, the council of three hundred and eighty-one and the speech for Meletius, the emperor's law, the journey by the public post, the visit to Jerusalem, the letter on pilgrimage, the funeral speeches, the sermon against slavery, the eight-sided church, the Life of Moses, and his last appearance in three hundred and ninety-four. Many of the years are approximate and argued over.

What is uncertain. Whether Gregory married, and whether Theosebia was his wife or his sister. Exactly how he escaped from the soldiers. Where Nyssa stood.

What is told as his own report. His dream of the forty martyrs, from one of his sermons about them.

What we invented. Theon and everything he says, the grey mule, the inn, the woman with the onions, and the wording of nearly every conversation. Gregory's letters and books are given in our own plain paraphrase and much shortened.

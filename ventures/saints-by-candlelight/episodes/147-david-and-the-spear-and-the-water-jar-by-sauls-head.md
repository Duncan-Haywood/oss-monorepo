The same Hebrew word for a water jar, and the same little phrase, a jar of water at his head, turns up again much later in the Bible, in the story of the prophet Elijah. Elijah is running for his life too, into the wilderness, from another angry king and queen, and he lies down under a broom tree and asks God to let him die. And he falls asleep. And an angel wakes him, and he looks, and there at his head is a cake baked on hot stones, and a jar of water. And he eats and drinks and lies down again.

So in one story, a hunted man takes away the jar of water from his sleeping enemy, and gives his life back to him. And in the other, a hunted man wakes up and finds that God has put a jar of water by his own head. I do not want to make too much of that. But I think it is a lovely thing to notice, just before sleep.

[pause]

* * *

And now it is time to slow down.

That night on the hill of Hachilah was the last time David and Saul ever saw each other. After it, the book never brings them face to face again.

David did not trust Saul's promise. He said in his heart, one of these days I shall perish by the hand of Saul. And so he did the very thing he had been afraid of. He took his six hundred men and their families and went away, over the border, to live among the Philistines, at a town called Gath, and then at Ziklag, on the edge of the southern desert. And when Saul heard that David had gone, the book says, he did not look for him any more.

And some time after that, there was a great battle in the north, on the hills of Gilboa. And Saul's own words came true, or rather David's did. His day came, and he went down into battle, and there he died, with his son Jonathan, David's dearest friend, beside him. God had seen to it, in his own time, exactly as David had said. Nobody needed a spear in the night.

And when the news came to David, he did not celebrate. He tore his clothes, and he wept, and he fasted until evening. And he wrote a song for Saul and Jonathan, a lament, which is one of the most beautiful poems in the whole Bible. And in it he says, of the man who had chased him through the wilderness for years:

Saul and Jonathan, beloved and lovely. In life and in death they were not divided. They were swifter than eagles. They were stronger than lions. You daughters of Israel, weep over Saul.

Weep over Saul. That is what David wrote. Not good riddance. Weep.

* * *

As for Abishai, he never quite stopped offering to kill people for his uncle.

Years later, when David was king, and an old man, and his own son Absalom had rebelled against him, David had to flee from Jerusalem on foot, weeping, with his head covered. And on the road, a man called Shimei came out and walked along the ridge beside them, throwing stones and dust at the king and shouting insults.

And there was Abishai, at David's elbow again. Why should this dead dog curse my lord the king? Let me go over and take off his head.

And David said, what have I to do with you, you sons of Zeruiah? Leave him alone. Perhaps the Lord has told him to do it.

Some things never change. But I want to be fair to Abishai, because he was also a hero. Once, in a battle with the Philistines, when David was older and very tired, a great Philistine warrior came at him, and it was Abishai who rushed in and saved his uncle's life. So he was loyal, and brave, and a little too quick with his sword. Most of us have known somebody like that. And I think David loved him very much, and spent a great deal of his life saying, put it away, Abishai.

* * *

The Christians who came after loved this story very much.

Saint John Chrysostom, the great preacher of Antioch and Constantinople, preached a set of three sermons on David and Saul. And one of the things he wanted his people to notice was this. David lived a thousand years before Our Lord. He never heard the Sermon on the Mount. And yet, on that night on the hill, he did exactly what Our Lord would one day ask of all of us.

Love your enemies, said Our Lord. Do good to those who hate you. Bless those who curse you. Pray for those who mistreat you.

David had never heard those words. But somehow, standing in the middle of a sleeping army, with the spear in his hand, he knew them.

[pause]

So let us leave them there, at the end of the night.

The sun is coming up over the wasteland now, gold on the tops of the ridges, and the valleys still full of blue shadow. Down in the camp, the soldiers are rolling up their blankets and grumbling and not quite looking at Abner. A young man is walking slowly back across the valley, carrying the king's spear in both hands.

And up on the hill, David is sitting down on a rock, at last, and stretching out his legs, very tired. Perhaps he has the little water jar beside him. Perhaps he is having a drink.

He did not get his kingdom that night. He did not get it for a long time yet. But he went to sleep that morning, wherever he slept, with nothing on his conscience. And that is worth more than a crown.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the First Book of Samuel, chapter twenty-six, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. Its twin, David sparing Saul in the cave at En-gedi, is chapter twenty-four. The first visit of the men of Ziph is in chapter twenty-three, and the psalm about them is Psalm fifty-four, numbered fifty-three in the Greek and Orthodox psalter. Saul throwing his spear at David and at Jonathan is in chapters eighteen, nineteen and twenty.

The hill of Hachilah has never been identified. The word we have called the ring is uncertain, and is translated in several ways.

The rare Hebrew word for a deep sleep from the Lord is the same one used of Adam in Genesis, chapter two, and of Abraham in Genesis, chapter fifteen. Elijah's jar of water is in the First Book of Kings, chapter nineteen. The pun on partridge and caller is a suggestion some scholars make, not a settled reading.

David going to the Philistines is in chapter twenty-seven, Saul's death on Gilboa in chapter thirty-one, and David's lament in the Second Book of Samuel, chapter one. Abishai and Shimei are in Second Samuel, chapter sixteen, and Abishai saving David is in chapter twenty-one. Zeruiah is named as David's sister in First Chronicles, chapter two.

Saint John Chrysostom's three homilies on David and Saul are available in English. Our Lord's words are from Saint Luke, chapter six, and Saint Matthew, chapter five.

The walk through the dark, Ahimelech minding the bags, the snorer, the dawn, and my guesses about the water jar are our own invention.

It is late afternoon, somewhere in Germany, and it has been raining all day.

A small cart is coming down a muddy road towards a town. The town has a wall around it, and a gate in the wall, with a tower over the gate and a guard in the tower who is trying not to get wet. The cart is pulled by one patient brown horse. A boy is driving it. And sitting beside the boy, with a hood pulled up over his head and a book wrapped in oilcloth on his knees, is a thin man of about thirty-six, with a long, gentle face.

As the cart comes up to the gate, the man pushes back his hood, looks up at the tower, and says, quietly, "Good evening."

The boy looks up too. The guard in the tower is looking the other way. He has not heard anything.

"He can't hear you, Father," says the boy.

"Oh, I wasn't talking to him," says the man. "I was talking to the angel of the town."

The boy looks at the tower again. He looks at the gate. He looks at the rain. Then he looks at the priest beside him, for a long moment.

"Right," he says.

If you were listening a couple of nights ago, you met Saint Ignatius of Loyola. A Basque soldier with a broken leg, who read the lives of the saints because there was nothing else in the house, and walked out of his old life, and went to Paris in his late thirties to sit in a classroom with teenagers.

Tonight is the story of one of those teenagers. Or nearly. He was twenty-three when Ignatius walked into his room. And he is the first person Ignatius ever really made into a friend for life.

His name was Pierre Favre. In English we usually call him Peter Faber, and I am going to call him Peter Faber, because that is how most of the books do it, and because Pierre Favre is a name I would mispronounce in a different way every time.

He came from Savoy. Now, Savoy is not a country you will find on a map today. In fifteen hundred and six, when Peter was born, it was a duchy, a little country of its own, ruled by a duke, sitting in the high mountains where France and Switzerland and Italy all meet and lean on one another. Today most of it is in France. It is a place of very steep green fields, and dark pine woods, and enormous white mountains that look down on everything and are not interested in anything you are doing. Mont Blanc is not far away. If you have ever seen a picture of a wooden chalet with flowers in the window boxes and cows with bells round their necks, that is more or less the sort of place.

Peter was born in a village called Villaret, high up in a valley, on the thirteenth of April. His father was called Louis and his mother was called Marie. They were farmers. Not poor exactly, but not anything else either. They had fields and they had animals, and the animals had to be taken up the mountain in the summer to eat the grass on the high pastures, and somebody had to go with them.

That somebody, from the time he was quite small, was Peter.

So picture him. A little boy, maybe seven or eight, alone on the side of a mountain all day with the sheep. A stick. A bit of bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth. The sound of the bells. The clouds coming over the ridge and going away again. Nothing to do for hours and hours except keep an eye on the sheep and look at the sky.

Some children would find that very boring. Peter, I think, found it very boring too. But it also did something to him. Years later, when he was a grown man and a priest, he wrote in his diary that he could remember, as a very small boy, a kind of quiet awareness of God, there in the fields. Not visions. Nothing dramatic. Just a sense that he was not alone up there. That someone was keeping an eye on him the way he was keeping an eye on the sheep.

And he had a terrible longing to go to school.

* * *

Now, this was not the normal thing for a shepherd boy in the mountains of Savoy in the fifteen hundreds. School cost money. School took a boy away from the sheep. And school, as far as most farming families could see, led to nothing in particular, unless you wanted to be a priest, and even then, there were plenty of priests in the valleys who could barely read their own Mass book.

Peter's parents wanted him at home. He was useful. He was good with the animals. He was, as far as they could tell, going to be a farmer like his father, and a perfectly good one.

But Peter, according to his own diary, kept on and on about it. And at last, when he was about ten, he simply cried. He cried so much and so long about wanting to go to school that his parents gave in.

I find this very touching. Not a tantrum about sweets or a toy. A little boy in the mountains crying because he wanted to learn to read properly.

So they sent him. First to a priest in the next town, a place called Thônes, and then to a proper school in a town called La Roche, a little lower down the valleys, where there was a schoolmaster who was by all accounts very good, and very kind, and taught the boys Latin and Greek and the writers of Rome, and also how to be decent people. Peter loved him. He remembered him all his life.

And it was around this time, when he was about twelve, that something else happened, which Peter also wrote down in his diary much later.

He was back home in the holidays, out in the fields with the animals again. And he knelt down in the grass, by himself, and he made a promise to God. He promised to keep himself pure, and to belong to God, all his life.

He was twelve. He did not really know what he was promising. He said so himself, later, with a sort of rueful smile in the writing. But he meant it with his whole heart. And it was the first time, he thought, that God had really taken hold of him.

The sheep, as far as we know, did not notice anything. They went on eating.

* * *

Peter stayed at school in La Roche until he was nineteen. And by then it was clear to everybody, even to Louis and Marie, that this boy was not going to be a farmer. He was too clever, and too bookish, and too obviously in love with learning. His teachers said he should go to the university.

And there was only one university, really, if you were a clever boy from those parts who wanted to go a long way.

Paris.

So in fifteen twenty-five, at the age of nineteen, Peter Faber left the mountains and walked to Paris. It is a long way. It is several weeks on foot. You come down out of the high valleys, and the mountains get smaller behind you, and the fields get flatter and wider, and the towns get bigger, and then one day you come over a rise and there it is, the biggest city most people in that whole part of the world had ever seen. Towers and spires and smoke. Bells ringing from about a hundred churches at once. Mud. Noise. Students everywhere, shouting at each other in Latin.

The university of Paris was not one building. It was a whole quarter of the city, on the left bank of the river, full of separate colleges, each one a sort of small boarding house with lecture rooms attached. And Peter went to one called the College of Sainte-Barbe.

And at Sainte-Barbe, he was given a room. And he was given a roommate.

The roommate was a young man from the kingdom of Navarre, on the Spanish side of the Pyrenees. He was the same age as Peter, give or take a few days. He was tall, and handsome, and very good at sports, especially the high jump, and he came from a noble family that had seen better days, and he had every intention of becoming a famous professor and a rich churchman with a fine income, and he did not hide it.

His name was Francis Xavier.

Now, if you know anything about Francis Xavier, you know that he grew up to be one of the greatest missionaries who ever lived. He went to India, and to Japan, and he died on an island off the coast of China, trying to get in. But in fifteen twenty-five, all of that was a very long way off. In fifteen twenty-five, he was a cheerful, ambitious, rather vain young man who liked running and jumping and was not particularly interested in God.

And Peter, the shepherd boy, was quiet, and shy, and serious, and worried a great deal about his soul.

You would think they would have got on each other's nerves. They didn't. They became the best of friends. I do not know exactly why. I think sometimes the people who are least like us are the ones we end up loving best. Francis was the sun coming out. Peter was the steady ground. They studied together, and they ate together, and they walked about Paris together, and they were very happy.

For four years.

* * *

In the autumn of fifteen twenty-nine, there was a knock at the door.

All right, I do not actually know that there was a knock at the door. But somebody new arrived in the room. Rooms at Sainte-Barbe were shared, and a third student had been put in with them.

He was a Basque, from the north of Spain. He was much older than they were. He was thirty-eight, which for a student was ancient. He walked with a limp. He was not tall, and he was going a bit bald, and he was poorly dressed, and he had no money, because he had given it away, and he begged for his food. And he had very dark, very quiet eyes, that seemed to look straight through you and out the other side and come back again with a pretty good idea of what you were thinking.

This, of course, was Ignatius of Loyola.

Francis Xavier took one look at him and decided he did not like him. Too odd. Too pious. Too poor. A man like that would not help a young man's career one little bit. Francis was polite, but he kept his distance. He kept his distance for years, actually, and that is a story for another night.

Peter was different.

You see, the lectures at Sainte-Barbe were given in Latin, on the philosophy of Aristotle. And Ignatius had learned his Latin late, in a hurry, sitting in schoolrooms in Spain with boys of twelve, and he found the lectures very hard going. Aristotle is hard going even in your own language. In somebody else's language, at thirty-eight, with a head full of other things, it was a struggle.

And their teacher, a young lecturer called Juan de la Peña, had Peter in the same room and the same class. So it was arranged that Peter would help.

So that is how it started. Peter Faber, the shepherd boy from Savoy, became the tutor of Ignatius of Loyola. Every evening, or most evenings, the two of them would sit down together by the light of a candle, and go back over the day's lecture, and Peter would explain Aristotle. What a substance was. What a cause was. What the difference was between the stuff a thing is made of and the shape it takes.

And then, when they had finished with Aristotle, they would talk.

And here is the lovely thing. While Peter was teaching Ignatius philosophy, Ignatius was teaching Peter something else.

Because Peter, under that quiet surface, was in a terrible state.

He was full of scruples. If you have never had scruples, I hope you never do. It means worrying, all the time, that you have done something wrong, when you probably haven't. Going to confession and coming out and immediately worrying that you did not confess properly. Lying awake at night going over and over something you said at lunch. Peter was like that. He worried about his sins. He worried about his thoughts. He worried about his future. He did not know whether to be a doctor, or a lawyer, or a teacher, or a priest, or a monk, and every time he thought he had decided, he unsettled himself again. He wrote in his diary later that in those years he was blown about like a leaf.

And Ignatius, who had had scruples so badly himself at a place called Manresa that he had nearly lost his mind, knew exactly what this was. He knew all about it from the inside.

So he did not tell Peter to pull himself together. He did not tell him he was being silly. He listened. Night after night. For a very long time. And slowly, gently, he taught Peter how to notice what was going on inside him. Which thoughts left him peaceful and which left him churned up. Which desires came from God and which came from fear. How to make a good confession once and then leave it alone. How to trust that God was not a strict examiner waiting to catch him out, but a Father who wanted him home.

It took about four years.

I want to say that again, because I think it matters. It took about four years. Ignatius was not a magician. He did not say three wise words and fix everything. He just kept being there, being patient, being kind, for four years, until Peter could breathe again.

And at the end of those four years, Peter Faber had decided what to do with his life. He was going to do whatever Ignatius was doing.

* * *

There was one thing Ignatius made him wait for, though.

Ignatius had a little book. Well, it was not really a book yet. It was a set of notes. It was a month of prayers and meditations, worked out step by step, that he had put together from his own long struggle at Loyola and at Manresa. He called them the Spiritual Exercises. Today, you can buy them in any good religious bookshop, and people all over the world make them, in retreat houses and in their own homes. But in fifteen thirty-three, they existed in one copy, in Ignatius's own handwriting, and he did not give them to just anybody.

He made Peter wait. First, Peter went home to Savoy for some months, to see his father and his family, whom he had not seen for years. Then he came back. And then, at last, in the January of fifteen thirty-four, in the middle of a Paris winter, Ignatius gave him the Exercises.

Peter went off to a little house on the edge of the city, by himself, to make them. And because he was Peter, and because he did nothing by halves when it came to God, he went at it with a kind of terrible enthusiasm.

According to the early Jesuit accounts, he did not eat anything at all. For six days. In the depth of winter. And Peter, so the old story says, slept on the bundles of firewood that had been brought for him to burn, instead of burning them, and knelt to pray out in the yard in the snow.

When Ignatius came to visit him on the sixth day and found out what he had been doing, he was, let us say, not pleased. He did not think that God was asking Peter to freeze to death. Ignatius had done things like that himself, years before, at Manresa, and he had come to think they were a mistake. So he sat Peter down, and he lit the fire, and he got some food into him, and he told him, very firmly, that this was enough. That God wanted his heart, not his chilblains.

I like to picture that. Ignatius, the great saint of discipline, standing over his young friend with a bowl of soup, saying, eat this. Now.

And Peter ate it. And he finished the Exercises properly, the rest of the month, with a fire going. And at the end of them, he was a different man. Not perfect. He would struggle with scruples, on and off, for the rest of his life, and he says so in his diary with great honesty. But he was steadier. He had found the ground under his feet.

* * *

That spring, on the thirtieth of May, fifteen thirty-four, Peter Faber was ordained a priest. He was the first of Ignatius's companions to be ordained. Ignatius himself was not a priest yet, and would not be for another three years.

And by now there was a little group of them. Not just Peter and Ignatius. Francis Xavier had finally come round. Ignatius had worked on him slowly, kindly, and with a question that is supposed to have stuck in Francis's head like a splinter. It is a question from the Gospel. What does it profit a man to gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? Francis, who had been planning to gain at least a reasonable portion of the world, could not stop thinking about it.

And there were others. Two young Spaniards called Diego Laínez and Alfonso Salmerón, who had come all the way to Paris specially to meet Ignatius. A Portuguese called Simão Rodrigues. Another Spaniard called Nicolás Bobadilla, who was clever and argumentative and loud, and whom I think I would have liked very much.

Seven of them.

And on the fifteenth of August, fifteen thirty-four, which was the feast of the Assumption of Our Lady, the seven of them walked out of the city, early in the morning, and up the hill of Montmartre.

Today, Montmartre is in the middle of Paris, and there is a great white church on top of it, and painters and tourists and people selling ice cream. In fifteen thirty-four it was outside the city, a hill with vineyards and windmills on it, and on the side of the hill there was a little old chapel dedicated to the martyrs, to Saint Denis, the first bishop of Paris, and his companions.

They went into the chapel, down into its crypt. And because Peter Faber was the only priest among them, it was Peter who said the Mass.

And just before Communion, he turned round, holding the Host, and one by one, the other six knelt in front of him and made their vows. They promised to live in poverty. They promised to go to Jerusalem, to the Holy Land, and spend their lives there helping people, if they could. And if they could not get to Jerusalem within a year, they promised to go to Rome instead, and offer themselves to the Pope, to be sent wherever he thought best. Then Peter made the same promises himself, and gave them all Communion.

Afterwards, they went out on the hillside, and sat down by a spring, and had a picnic. Bread and water, mostly. They were very poor. But they were extremely happy, and they stayed out there all day, talking, until the sun went down.

There was no Society of Jesus yet. That would not exist for another six years. There was no plan, really, beyond Jerusalem. There were seven young men on a hill in the summer, with bread and water and a promise.

And the shepherd boy from Savoy, who had once knelt in a field and made a promise to God all by himself, was the one holding the Host.

* * *

I am going to skip ahead now, quite quickly, because Peter's life after Montmartre was mostly one long journey, and if I tell you every stop we will be here until Easter.

In the winter of fifteen thirty-six, the companions left Paris to meet Ignatius in Venice, where they were hoping to catch a ship to the Holy Land. Ignatius had gone ahead. So it was Peter who led them. Nine young men, by now, on foot, in the middle of winter, across a Europe that was at war, through Lorraine, and down the Rhine, and over the Alps in the snow. Peter, the mountain boy, was presumably the only one of them who knew what to do with snow. They made it.

The ship to the Holy Land never sailed. There was war at sea that year between Venice and the Turks, and no pilgrim ships went. So they kept the other half of the promise. They went to Rome, and offered themselves to the Pope, and in fifteen forty, Pope Paul the Third approved them as a new religious order. The Society of Jesus.

By then, Peter was already gone. The Pope had sent him away on a job.

And this is where his life really begins to look like the rest of his life, which is to say, like a man permanently on the road.

The Pope sent him to Germany.

* * *

Now, I need to tell you something about Germany in fifteen forty, and I am going to try to tell it very gently and very fairly, because it was a sad and painful time, and it is not tonight's job to take sides in it.

About twenty years before, a German monk called Martin Luther had begun to say that the Church had gone badly wrong in many things, and that it needed to be completely changed. Many people agreed with him. Many people did not. And by fifteen forty, the people who agreed with him and the people who did not had stopped talking to one another, in a great many places, and started shouting instead. Towns were divided. Families were divided. Princes were taking sides. And the emperor, Charles the Fifth, who ruled over most of Germany, was very worried that the whole country was going to tear itself apart.

So the emperor called meetings. Big, formal meetings, in German cities, where the leading theologians from both sides were supposed to sit down at a table and try to find some common ground. One was at a city called Worms. One was at a city called Regensburg.

And Peter Faber was sent to these meetings, as a helper to a Spanish theologian called Doctor Pedro Ortiz, who was representing the emperor's side.

Peter was not very good at the meetings.

I mean that kindly. He was a very learned man. He could argue. But he did not like arguing, and he did not think it was getting anyone anywhere. He sat in those long halls, listening to the clever men on both sides making clever speeches, and scoring points, and getting angrier, and he came away feeling that nothing had changed at all.

So he started doing something else. In the evenings, after the meetings, he went out into the town. He talked to people. Ordinary people. Priests who had lost heart. Shopkeepers. Soldiers. People's servants. He heard confessions. He sat with them and listened, the way Ignatius had once sat with him and listened. And with the ones who wanted it, he did what Ignatius had done for him. He gave them the Spiritual Exercises.

And it was in Germany, in the summer of fifteen forty-two, that he began to keep his diary.

* * *

We call it the Memoriale, which is just Latin for a book of things to remember.

It was not written to be read by anybody. It was Peter, writing to himself, at night, about what God had been doing in his life that day. What he had felt at Mass. What he had felt while praying. Things that had troubled him. Things he was thankful for. Little conversations he had had with God. It is a very private book, and very honest, and sometimes very funny, though I do not think he always meant it to be.

And it is full of angels.

Peter had an enormous love for the angels. Not angels as decorations, or angels as pretty children with wings on a Christmas card. Real angels, as the Church understands them. Messengers of God. Spirits who watch over us. The Church teaches that each of us has a guardian angel. And Peter believed, as many Christians have believed, that not only people have angels to watch over them, but places too. That every town, every village, every parish, every country, has an angel who cares for it and prays for it.

So whenever Peter came to a new town, on his long walks and rides across Germany, he would greet its guardian angel. He would pray, quietly, for the angel's help with the people of that town. And he would greet the guardian angels of the people he met, even the people who did not like him. Especially the people who did not like him. He writes in his diary that this had helped him a great deal, that many times when he had to go and talk to someone difficult, he would first ask that person's angel for help, and things would go better than he had feared.

He did this all his life. And it is, I think, the most Peter Faber thing about Peter Faber.

Which brings us back to the cart.

* * *

So now we are going to stay with that cart for a little while, on that rainy afternoon in Germany. And I need to say again, very clearly, that the boy driving it is our invention. Peter really did travel around the Rhineland in those years, sometimes on foot, sometimes on horseback, sometimes with Jesuit companions and sometimes with whoever was going his way. We do not know the names of all the people who drove him and walked with him. So we have given him one. We will call him Konrad. He is fourteen, and his father is a carter, and he has been hired to take this priest from one town to the next, and he thinks the priest is a bit odd.

"Right," says Konrad, at the gate, in the rain.

The guard in the tower waves them through, without much interest. The cart rattles under the arch, and out into the street on the other side, which is narrow and dark and full of puddles.

"You don't believe me," says Peter, mildly.

"I didn't say anything," says Konrad.

"No. That's how I can tell."

Konrad thinks about this for a while. The horse picks its way around a large puddle, without being asked.

"It's just," says Konrad, "my father says priests are always talking about angels and devils and that, and it's all to frighten people. So they do what they're told."

"Ah," says Peter. "Well. I can see why he'd think that. Some priests do frighten people. I've met a few. I used to be frightened myself, all the time." He is quiet for a moment. "But I don't greet the angels to frighten anyone. I greet them because they're kind, and they're here, and it seems rude not to."

"Rude," says Konrad.

"Well, think about it. If you came into my house, and there was somebody in my house who had been looking after it for years, and looking after the people in it, and you walked straight past them without saying hello, wouldn't that be a bit rude?"

Konrad thinks about this too.

"I suppose," he says. "If I could see them."

"Yes, well, that's the difficult part," says Peter. "I can't see them either."

And Konrad laughs, despite himself. He did not expect the priest to say that.

* * *

They stop for the night at an inn. It is a small, smoky, crowded place, and the innkeeper looks at Peter's black clothes and his book and his tired face, and does not look especially pleased to see him. Not everybody in this town is happy to see a Catholic priest, these days.

They get a corner of the big room, near the fire, and a bowl of soup each, and some bread. Peter eats slowly. Konrad eats as if the soup is trying to escape.

There are some men at the next table who are talking loudly. They are talking about the priests, and the Pope, and the bishops, and they are saying some very rude things, and they are saying them loudly enough to make sure the priest in the corner can hear.

Konrad looks at Peter, to see what he will do.

Peter does not seem to do anything. He goes on eating his soup. He goes on looking into the fire. He looks, if anything, a little sad.

When the men have finished their beer and gone out, still laughing, Konrad says, "Aren't you angry?"

"No," says Peter. "Not really."

"They were being horrible. About you. About all of you."

"Yes," says Peter. "I expect some of what they said was true, though. That's the hard part. There have been bad priests, and lazy bishops, and a great deal of greed, and they are right to be angry about it. I'm angry about it too." He wipes his bowl with a bit of bread. "I just don't think shouting at them back would help. It hasn't helped so far. Everybody's been shouting at each other for twenty years. Nobody's changed anybody's mind."

"So what do you do?"

"I pray for them," says Peter. "And I greet their angels. And if I get the chance to talk to one of them, really talk, not argue, I try to listen first. People don't come back to God because they lose an argument, Konrad. They come back because somebody loved them."

Konrad does not say anything to that. But he remembers it.

* * *

That last thing is our invention, in the sense that we do not have any record of Peter saying it to a carter's boy in an inn. But it is not our invention in the sense that matters, because Peter said something very like it in a letter. Near the end of his life, one of his old companions from Montmartre, Diego Laínez, asked him for advice on how to deal with the people in Germany who had left the Church. And Peter wrote back a short letter of advice. It is still read today.

He said, more or less, in our own plain words: if you want to help them, the first thing you need is a great deal of charity for them. You need to love them, truly, and not just say so. You need to clear out of your own heart anything that would make you look down on them. You need to win their good will, and their trust, by being friendly and by talking with them in a familiar, easy way, about the things you agree on, before you ever get to the things you disagree on. Begin with what unites you, he said, not with what divides you.

And in his diary, he wrote down the names of people he was praying for. And on that list, along with the Pope and the emperor and the kings of Europe, he put the names of Martin Luther, and of Luther's friend Philip Melanchthon, and of other leaders of the reform. He did not pray for them because he thought they were right. He did not think they were right. He prayed for them because he loved them, or was trying to, and because he thought God loved them, and because their angels were working very hard, and could use a little help.

I find that very moving. In a time when nearly everybody was choosing a side and digging in, there was this quiet man from the mountains going round the inns and the town squares, greeting everybody's angel.

* * *

There is one person Peter met in Germany whom I must tell you about, because he matters for what came after.

In the spring of fifteen forty-three, Peter was in the city of Mainz, on the Rhine. And a young man came to see him. He was twenty-one, a Dutchman from Nijmegen, very serious, very clever, studying at the university of Cologne. He had heard about this Savoyard priest and his strange month of prayers, and he wanted to try them.

His name was Peter Kanis. We call him Peter Canisius.

Peter Faber gave him the Exercises. And at the end of them, Peter Canisius wrote a letter to a friend. And in that letter, he said, in words to this effect: I have found the man I was looking for, if he is a man and not an angel of the Lord. He said that he had never seen or heard a more learned or deeper theologian, or a man of such holiness.

Peter Canisius joined the Jesuits, that same year, in Mainz, on his twenty-second birthday. He went on to spend his whole long life in Germany and Austria and Switzerland, teaching and preaching and founding schools. He wrote a little book of Christian teaching for children, a catechism, that was printed so many times that for a while in some parts of Germany people called any catechism "a Canisius". And many years later, the Church named him a saint and a Doctor of the Church.

So Peter Faber, the shepherd boy, who had been taught by Ignatius in a room in Paris, went on to teach Peter Canisius in a room in Mainz. And Peter Canisius went on to teach about half of Germany.

That is how it works, I think. One candle lights another. Nobody gets the credit. The light just goes on down the line.

* * *

The Pope and Ignatius kept moving Peter about, the way you might move a very useful piece on a chessboard.

After Germany, they sent him to Portugal. Then to Spain. He went to the court of the young Prince Philip, who would one day be king of Spain, and he gave the Exercises to courtiers and ladies-in-waiting and princesses and the prince's servants. He went to Valladolid, and to Madrid, and to Toledo, and to many places in between. And wherever he went, people asked for him. Bishops asked for him. Abbesses asked for him. Ordinary people stopped him in the street.

He was not a great preacher. He did not draw crowds. He did his best work one person at a time, in conversation, in confession, in quiet rooms. People said that just being near him made them want to be better. And he was always tired. Always on the road. Always a bit ill. He walked, or rode, thousands and thousands of miles, in every kind of weather, across mountains and rivers and plains, through France and Germany and Italy and Spain and Portugal, and back again, and back again.

And in every town, at every gate, he greeted the angel.

* * *

In the spring of fifteen forty-six, word came from Rome.

There was going to be a great council of the Church. Bishops from all over Europe were gathering at a town called Trent, in the mountains of northern Italy, to try to set the Church to rights, and to answer the questions that had been tearing Europe apart for thirty years. And the Pope wanted theologians there. He asked Ignatius for some of his best men.

Ignatius chose Laínez, and Salmerón, and Peter Faber.

Peter was in Spain. He was not well. He had been ill, on and off, for months, with fevers. His friends in Spain begged him not to go. They said he should rest first. But Peter had made a promise, a long time ago, on a hill in Paris, to go wherever the Pope sent him. And so he went.

It was summer by the time he got to Rome. It was July, and July in Rome is very hot. Ignatius was there, waiting for him. They had not seen each other for years, apart from in letters. I would like to tell you what they said to each other, but nobody wrote it down. I hope they were very glad.

Peter had arrived with a fever. And in the heat of the Roman summer, the fever got worse.

He died in Rome, on the first of August, fifteen forty-six. He was forty years old.

[pause]

He was buried in the little church the Jesuits had in Rome then, called Santa Maria della Strada, Our Lady of the Way. Which seems right, for a man who spent so much of his life on the road. Later, when the Jesuits built their great church of the Gesù on the same spot, his bones were moved, along with the bones of other early Jesuits, and today nobody knows exactly where in the church he lies. There is no grand tomb for Peter Faber. I think he would have liked that.

For a long time after he died, he was not very famous. Ignatius was famous. Francis Xavier was very famous. Peter Canisius was famous. Peter Faber was the quiet one, the one in between, the one people forgot.

But the people who had known him did not forget. And slowly, over the centuries, people found his diary, and read it, and loved him. In eighteen seventy-two, Pope Pius the Ninth declared him Blessed. And then, for a long time, nothing much happened.

And then, in twenty thirteen, there was a new Pope, the first Jesuit Pope there had ever been. And in an interview that year, someone asked him which Jesuits he most admired, and he named Peter Faber. He said he loved Peter's way of talking with everybody, even the people who disagreed with him most. His simple goodness. His ability to be gentle and firm at once.

And that December, on the seventeenth of December, which happened to be his own birthday, Pope Francis declared Peter Faber a saint.

Four hundred and sixty-seven years after he died, more or less, the shepherd boy from Villaret got his feast day.

[pause]

And Konrad? Konrad is our invention, so he can do whatever we like. So let us say that he drove the priest to the next town, and to the one after that, and then home again to his father. And let us say that he did not become a priest or a monk or anything like that, but a carter, like his father, and a good one. And that for the rest of his life, whenever he drove his cart up to the gate of a town, any town, in the rain or the sun or the snow, he would look up at the tower, and nod, very slightly. Just in case.

And now, let us go back up the mountain.

Back to Villaret, in Savoy, high in the valley, where the fields are green and steep and the bells ring on the necks of the animals. It is evening there now. The sun has gone down behind the ridge, and the big white mountains are turning pink, and then grey, and then blue. The animals are lying down in the grass. Somewhere a door closes. Somewhere a lamp is lit.

And in the dark field, where a little boy once knelt down by himself and made a promise to God, there is nobody at all. Just the grass, and the quiet, and the first star coming out over the mountain.

But Peter would say that the field is not empty. He would say that the angel of that valley is still there, keeping watch over it, as it has kept watch all along. And the angel of your town is there, too, tonight. Keeping watch over the dark streets, and the parked cars, and the houses with the lights going out one by one.

And your own angel is there. Right beside you. It has been beside you all day, at every gate you went through, every room you walked into, every difficult person you had to talk to. You did not see it. You did not have to. It saw you.

So you can rest now. You do not need to keep watch tonight. That is somebody else's job, and they are very good at it. You can let go of the day, and let your eyes close, and know that you are not alone up here on the mountain.

That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source tonight is Peter Faber's own spiritual diary, the Memoriale, which he began in Germany in fifteen forty-two and kept until about fifteen forty-six. There is a good English translation, with a selection of his letters and instructions, by Edmond Murphy and Martin Palmer. For Ignatius's side of the Paris years, his own Autobiography is the place to go. For the early Jesuits as a whole, John O'Malley's book The First Jesuits is excellent, and Mary Purcell wrote a warm, readable life of Peter called The Quiet Companion. Pope Francis's words about him come from his long interview with Antonio Spadaro, published in twenty thirteen.

What is history. His birth in Savoy in fifteen hundred and six, his shepherding, his tears to go to school and his schooling at Thônes and La Roche, the promise he made at about twelve, his years at Sainte-Barbe with Francis Xavier and then Ignatius, his tutoring Ignatius and Ignatius's help with his scruples, his ordination in fifteen thirty-four, the Mass and the vows at Montmartre that August, the winter journey to Venice, his work in Germany, Portugal and Spain, his giving the Exercises to Peter Canisius, his letter of advice to Laínez, his death in Rome in fifteen forty-six, his beatification in eighteen seventy-two, and his canonisation in twenty thirteen. His prayers to the guardian angels of places and people, and his prayers for Luther, Melanchthon and others, are in his own diary.

What is the old accounts' story. The six days without food, the firewood and the snow come from the early Jesuit accounts rather than from Peter's own pen, and may have grown a little in the telling.

What we invented. Konrad, his cart and his horse, the rainy gate, the inn and the men at the next table, and nearly every word anybody says out loud. Peter's letter to Laínez and Canisius's letter are given in our own plain words, not quoted. We have tried to tell the troubles of the Reformation fairly and briefly, and to stay off the arguments themselves, which are not tonight's story.

[pause]

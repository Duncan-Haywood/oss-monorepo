Picture a courtyard in early morning, with a great many baskets in it, and one tired man trying to count to ninety.

He is counting loaves. There are a lot of them, and they are still warm, and they smell the way bread smells at that hour, which is the best smell that has ever been invented. Beside him there is a row of jars of oil, and a sack of lentils the size of a small sheep, and a table made out of two doors laid across four stools. Behind him, outside the gate, a line of people is forming. And the line is quite long. And a good many of the people in it are old women, in dark head scarves, who have been up since before the sun.

He gets to ninety-one, and loses his place, and starts again.

And somewhere in the line, quite near the back, there is a woman who has stood in this same line every morning for a week, and who has gone home every morning with a little less than she needed, and who has not said a word about it to anybody. But she is going to. Today. Very quietly. And that quiet word is the beginning of the story of the first martyr of the Church.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapters six and seven, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is about Saint Stephen, whom both churches honour as the first martyr. A few of the small things around the edges, the morning bread, the line at the gate, the thoughts of the people, are our own invention, told in the spirit of Acts, and the notes at the end say which. What the apostles say, what the crowd says, and what Stephen says at the end, we have kept as close to Luke as we can. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let me tell you first where we are, and when.

We are in Jerusalem, a few years after the first Easter, and not very long after Pentecost, which is the day the Holy Spirit came down on the followers of Jesus and sent them out of the upper room and into the street. Since then, a great many things have happened, and most of them have happened in a hurry. Thousands of people have joined the little community of those who follow Jesus. The apostles have been teaching in the Temple courts. They have been arrested, and let out, and arrested again. And the whole city has been talking about it, in the way a city does when something has happened that it cannot explain.

Now, if you had walked into this community at that moment, and looked around, the first thing you would have noticed is not what you might expect. It is not the preaching, and it is not the miracles. It is the way they eat.

Luke, who wrote the Acts of the Apostles, tells us about it twice, in two different places, because he evidently could not help himself. He says that all the believers were together, and had everything in common. He says that people who owned a field or a house would sell it and bring the money and lay it at the feet of the apostles, and that it was shared out, to each as any had need. He says that they broke bread in their homes, and ate their food with glad and generous hearts. And he says, in a line I rather like, that there was not a needy person among them.

Imagine that. Imagine a community of thousands, in an ancient city, with no government help and no bank and no charity office, and not one person in need. It does not sound possible. And of course, the moment that you try to do it, you discover what everyone discovers whenever a lot of people try to share a lot of bread. It requires a rota.

I mean that quite seriously. It requires a list of names. It requires somebody to know who has a family of six and who lives alone, and who is too frail to carry a basket home, and whose turn it is to be served first, and whose turn it is to be served last. And it requires somebody to stand at a table in a courtyard at dawn and hand out the bread, and to do it fairly, and to do it every day. The Church is a very holy thing. But it is also, from the very first week, a thing with a catering department.

And in the first years, as the number of people grew from hundreds to thousands, the catering department was run by the apostles themselves. The Twelve. The men who had walked with Jesus for three years, and who had seen him die, and seen him alive again, and had been sent out to tell the world about it. And they were also, at the same time, counting loaves.

[pause]

There is one more thing that you need to know about the community in Jerusalem, and it is the thing that goes wrong. Because the people in that courtyard were not all the same kind of people.

There were two sorts of Jewish believers in the Church at that time. Luke gives them names. He calls them the Hebrews and the Hellenists. The Hebrews were Jews who had grown up in Jerusalem and Judea, who spoke Aramaic at home, and who had known the Temple all their lives. The Hellenists were Jews who had grown up somewhere else, in Alexandria, or Cyrene, or Asia, or Rome, who spoke Greek at home, and who read the Scriptures in the Greek translation, and who had come to live in Jerusalem, some of them quite late in life, because they wanted to die near the Temple. Many of these were widows. A woman who had lost her husband in a foreign city and had come to Jerusalem to end her days in the holy place did not usually have a son nearby, or a cousin, or a neighbour. She had the Church. And the Church, at dawn, had a courtyard.

Now, I want to be fair to everyone in this story, because nobody in it is a villain. The Hebrew men who ran the distribution were not cruel. They were doing a huge job, with no training, in a crowd. They knew the Aramaic-speaking widows, because they had grown up with them, and they knew their children, and they knew their houses. They knew who was hungry, because they could see them across the street. The Greek-speaking widows were newer, and quieter, and there were a lot of them, and they did not always push to the front. And so, little by little, without anybody ever deciding it, a few of them were being missed.

That is how most unfairness happens. Not on purpose. Just by not noticing.

[pause]

* * *

So here is the morning.

The sun has just come up over the Mount of Olives, and the courtyard is full of the long gold light you get in that country early in the day. The man at the table is a good man called Matthias. Not the apostle, a different one, a baker's son with a big voice, and the sort of memory that remembers the name of every child in the street, which is why they put him on the list. He is counting out portions with great speed and no malice at all. Beside him, a young man named Joel writes down the names on a wax tablet, and every so often puts down his stylus to rub his hand, because the wax is cold.

The line moves. A grandmother with a basket on her hip is served a loaf, a handful of lentils, and a ladle of oil, and given a smile, and told that her grandson's cough sounds better. A man with a wooden leg is served and teased. A girl of about seven, standing in for her sick mother, is served and told to hold the basket with both hands.

Then, at the very back, there is the woman.

I will not give her a name, because Luke does not, and because she stands in this story for a good many women. But I will say what she looks like. She is small, and she is old, and she is wearing a grey scarf pinned neatly under her chin. She came to Jerusalem from Alexandria nine years ago with her husband, who died the first winter, and she has lived since then in one small room on a stairway on the south side of the city. She speaks Aramaic, with a Greek accent that makes people lean in and say "pardon?" And she is, right now, in line.

When she reaches the table, the portions are nearly gone. Matthias looks down at the baskets, and then up at her, and he says, kindly, "Oh, you're late. Never mind. Here is what's left." And what is left is half a loaf, and a little oil, and no lentils at all.

And she says, "Thank you."

She says it perfectly nicely, and she takes the half loaf in both hands, and she goes home. And Matthias turns to the next person in line and forgets her completely, because he has a hundred more people to feed, and the sun is getting hot.

But here is the thing. She was not late. She was there at dawn. She had been there before the table was set up. It was only that the line had parted around her, as lines do, when a man is looking at a basket and not at a face. And it was the seventh morning in a row. And what she thought, walking home through the narrow street with her half loaf, was nothing angry at all. What she thought was, *I do not want to make a fuss. These are good people. They are tired.* And what she did, when she got home, was to sit down on the step outside her door, and wait for her neighbour to come by.

Her neighbour was another widow from Alexandria, a woman a little younger, with a sharper tongue. And the neighbour looked at the half loaf, and looked at the old woman's face, and said, in Greek, a sentence that I will not repeat, because this is a bedtime story. But I will tell you what it meant. It meant, *how long has this been going on?*

[pause]

* * *

By the end of the week, the whole of the Greek-speaking part of the Church knew about it.

Luke says, simply, that "the Hellenists complained against the Hebrews because their widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution." It is a short sentence. And I think it is one of the bravest sentences in the New Testament, because it would have been so easy for Luke to leave it out. He is writing the story of the glorious early Church, and he stops to tell us that in the first years, in the very first community, there was a complaint. That there was a quarrel about food. That somebody was being missed.

The Church has never been a place without problems. It has been a place where problems are told the truth about, and then fixed. And Luke wants us to know that from the very beginning.

So the complaint goes up. We do not know who carried it. It may have been a delegation of men from the synagogues of the Greek-speaking Jews. It may have been a group of women. It may have been the woman with the sharp tongue, standing in the courtyard with her hands on her hips. It goes up, and it reaches the Twelve.

And the Twelve do a thing that I think is lovely.

They do not say it is not true. They do not say there must be a mistake. They do not say, "who has been spreading these rumours?" They do not even say, "we are very busy, and we are doing our best." They call a meeting.

Luke says that "the Twelve summoned the whole community of the disciples." The whole community. Thousands of people, in the courtyard of some great house or on a hillside, listening to twelve men speak. And Peter, I imagine, is the one who stands up, because Peter is the one who always stands up. And he says something that Luke has kept for us, in his own plain words.

He says, "It is not right that we should neglect the word of God in order to wait on tables."

Now, I want to stop for a moment on that sentence, because it is easy to hear it wrongly. It can sound as though Peter thinks that waiting on tables is beneath him. It is not that at all. The apostles, after all, had been taught by a man who had washed their feet. They knew what table service was. They had seen what it meant. What Peter is saying is something much simpler, and much more humble. He is saying, *we are doing this badly.* The widows are being missed because the apostles are trying to do two enormous jobs at once, and they cannot. One of the jobs is preaching and praying, which is what they were sent to do. The other is feeding a city, which needs someone's full attention. And the only way that the widows are going to be fed properly is if someone is given that job, and only that job.

So Peter says, "Brothers, select from among you seven men of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom, whom we may appoint to this task. But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word."

And Luke says, "What they said pleased the whole community."

I love that. They were pleased. It is not often, in the history of committees, that everybody is pleased. But everyone in that courtyard could see that it was fair, and that it was honest, and that nobody was being blamed, and that the people who had been missed would now have people looking out for them. And that, I think, is why it worked.

[pause]

* * *

Now, here is a detail that I find very moving, if you look at it carefully.

The community chose seven men. And Luke gives us their names. And if you read the list, slowly, you will notice something. They are Stephen, and Philip, and Prochorus, and Nicanor, and Timon, and Parmenas, and Nicolaus.

Every one of those is a Greek name.

Not one of them is Peter, or James, or John, or Simon, or Judas, or any of the good old Hebrew names that fill up the rest of the New Testament. They are all Greek. And the seventh, Nicolaus, Luke tells us, was "a convert to Judaism from Antioch," which means that he was not even born Jewish.

Do you see what happened? The widows who were being overlooked were Greek-speaking widows. And when the community was asked to choose seven men to look after them, the community, the whole of it, Hebrews included, chose seven men from the Greek-speaking side. They gave the job of fairness to the people who knew what unfairness felt like. And they did it without being told to.

That is not the only thing that it tells us. It tells us that the Church was not afraid of the quarrel. It did not try to smooth it over, or to keep the Greek speakers quiet, or to say that the problem was only a misunderstanding. It looked at the people who had been hurt, and it handed them the keys.

And at the top of the list, first of the seven, with a little phrase that Luke gives to nobody else, is Stephen. "A man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit."

We know almost nothing about him before this moment. We do not know where he was born, or how old he was, or whether he was married. We do not know whether he had known Jesus, or heard him teach. Some people have guessed that he may have come from the Greek-speaking Jews of the Dispersion. His name, Stephanos, means a crown, or a wreath, the kind that was given to the winner of a race. We do not know anything more. And I find that I like that. Because it means that when he walks on stage, he does so with nothing, no title and no past, only the word that Luke gives us: *full*. Full of faith. Full of the Spirit.

[pause]

So let me tell you what I imagine, and I will be clear that it is imagining, because Luke says only that "they had these men stand before the apostles, who prayed and laid their hands on them."

It is the evening of the day when the community has chosen. The seven men are standing together in a room, a bit awkwardly, the way people do when they have been chosen for something and don't yet know what it is. Nicanor is looking at his sandals. Timon is trying to remember whether he has left a pot on the fire. Philip is quite calm, and quite cheerful, and thinking about the bread. And Stephen is standing in the middle of the room with his hands at his sides, perfectly still, and looking at the apostles with an expression that is hard to describe. It is the expression of a man who has been told that his job is to carry baskets, and who has understood that this is a holy thing.

The apostles come forward. They pray. And one by one, Luke says, they lay their hands on each of the seven. It is a very old gesture, from the oldest part of the Scriptures. It was how Moses commissioned Joshua. It means, *this is yours now, and God goes with you.* It is not a magic trick. It is a handing over.

And then they go out. And the next morning, before the sun comes up, there are seven men in the courtyard instead of two.

[pause]

* * *

I want to tell you about the next morning, because I think it is the best part of the story, and the part that people forget.

Luke says nothing at all about it. Not one word. He moves straight on. "So the word of God continued to spread," he writes, "and the number of the disciples increased greatly in Jerusalem, and a large group of priests became obedient to the faith." That is all he says. But I believe, from the shape of the thing, that something must have happened in that courtyard, and so I am going to tell you what I think.

The seven arrive before dawn. They bring tables. They move them to a better place, near the shade. They write down every name on a new list, and they ask each person, one by one, where they live and how many are in the house, and who cannot walk to the courtyard, and who needs the bread brought to them. They send two young men to the south side of the city to find the people who have not been coming. Philip, it turns out, knows a back way. Prochorus has a very good head for figures. Nicanor can be trusted with the lentils. And Stephen, who is quietest, goes and sits down on the step beside the line of old women, and just talks to them.

He asks them their names. He asks where they came from. He asks about the husbands that they lost, and about the cities that they left, and what the bread tasted like in Alexandria. And what he discovers, over about an hour, sitting on a step in the early sun, is a roomful of people who have not been listened to for years.

By the time the line has moved forward to the table, there is a woman in a grey scarf standing in the very front of it. Nobody has made a fuss. Nobody has said a word about the week that is past. But she is first, and she gets a whole loaf, and a ladle of lentils, and a generous measure of oil. And Stephen says to her, in Greek, "Peace be with you, mother. Will you tell me tomorrow what you thought of the bread?"

And she says, "It was the best bread in the world."

And then, I imagine, she goes home and sits on her step in the sun, and for the first time in nine years, in a city that was not hers, she is not hungry, and she is not alone, and somebody knows her name.

That is all it is. It is not a miracle. It is a rota, and a list, and a pair of ears. But I think it is the first sermon that Stephen ever preached.

[pause]

* * *

Now, I have been telling you about a quiet man with a ladle. But Luke goes on to tell us something else about Stephen, and it is that the quiet man did not stay quiet for very long.

He says: "Stephen, full of grace and power, did great wonders and signs among the people." And then, in the very next lines, "Some members of the synagogue called the Synagogue of the Freedmen, Cyrenians, Alexandrians, and others of Cilicia and Asia, stood up and argued with Stephen."

Notice who they are. They are the Greek-speaking Jews. The same people, more or less, whose widows Stephen had been feeding. They have their own synagogues in Jerusalem, for the people from Cyrene and Alexandria and Asia Minor, and Stephen, being a Greek speaker himself, was at home in them. So he went. And he talked. And I think we can imagine what the talk was like, because we know the sort of man that he was. He was not a fighter. He was a listener. He explained the Scriptures, and he told them what he believed, that Jesus of Nazareth was the Messiah, and that God had raised him from the dead, and he did it with that same open face that he had shown to the widows.

And they could not answer him.

Luke says it plainly. "They could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he spoke." And that, as far as it goes, is a very pleasing thing to read. A man who had been chosen to carry bread, who had nothing to his name but a good heart and a clear head, stood up in front of the learned men of three synagogues and could not be argued down.

But there is, in human beings, a particular kind of person who cannot bear to lose an argument. And a few of them, Luke tells us, went off quietly and found some men to say that Stephen had spoken against Moses and against God. They stirred up the people. They stirred up the elders. They stirred up the scribes. And together they came upon him, seized him, and brought him before the council.

Now, here is a small piece of history, and I want to be honest about what we know. Luke does not say, but some have guessed, that one of the people in the Synagogue of Cilicia might have been a young man from Tarsus, a student of Gamaliel, called Saul. Acts does not say it. It is a guess that people have made for a very long time, and it may be right and it may be wrong. But we will meet Saul in a minute, and he is not a guess at all.

[pause]

* * *

The council is the Sanhedrin, the highest court of the Jewish people in Jerusalem. It is the same court, or very nearly, that had tried Jesus a few years before. The high priest is presiding. The seats are arranged in a half circle. And Stephen is brought in and stood in the middle of it, with the people who accused him behind him, and the false witnesses who have been found for the purpose, saying their piece.

Luke tells us what they said. "This man never stops saying things against this holy place and against the law. For we have heard him say that Jesus of Nazareth will destroy this place and change the customs that Moses handed down to us."

Now, I will say something about that, because it matters. Luke tells us they were *false* witnesses. He does not mean that nothing they said was true. He means that they twisted what was true. Stephen had said that Jesus was greater than the Temple. He had not said that the Temple should be destroyed, or that the law of Moses was wrong. But the accusers had taken a true thing and bent it into a false one. And that, as anyone who has ever been misquoted will tell you, is a very particular kind of lie.

And then Luke gives us the most beautiful line in the whole of this story. He says, "And all who sat in the council looked intently at him, and they saw that his face was like the face of an angel."

Nobody knows exactly what that means. It is a figure of speech, and it is a lovely one. It does not mean that he had wings, or that he was shining like the sun, or that he was an angel himself. Angels, as we have said on this programme, are angels, and men are men. It means that his face was perfectly calm. It means that there was nothing in it of fear, or of anger, or of defence. It means that a man was standing before the highest court in the land, with his life in the balance, and he looked as though he had just been told the best news in the world.

I think that is a thing worth sitting with for a moment.

[pause]

The high priest asks him, "Are these things so?"

And Stephen answers. He gives a speech. It is the longest speech in the Acts of the Apostles, and it is almost entirely a history lesson. I am not going to read all of it to you, because it takes up nearly the whole of chapter seven, and it would keep you awake. But I will tell you the shape.

He starts at the beginning. He starts with Abraham, the man who was called out of Mesopotamia, and who went, not knowing where he was going. He goes on to Joseph, sold by his brothers, and raised up in Egypt. He goes on to Moses, rescued from the river, and sent to his people, and refused by them the first time, and sent again. He talks about the tent in the wilderness, and about David, and about Solomon, who built the Temple. And then he says the one thing that matters most to the people who are listening. "Yet the Most High does not dwell in houses made with human hands."

And that, I think, is what he has been saying all along.

His speech is a very old and very honest thing. It is saying: *look at our story. Look at every single time that God has been at work among us. He was never held in one place. He called Abraham outside the land. He was with Joseph in Egypt. He met Moses in a bush in the desert, on ground that was holy because he was there. God is bigger than any building, and he has always been so.* And it is also saying, more gently than it sounds, *and every time that God sent someone to rescue us, we pushed them away at first.* Joseph. Moses. And now another.

It is an old story, told by a man who loves it. And the moment that he comes to the end of it, in the last few lines, he says something sharper. He says: "You stiff-necked people, you are always resisting the Holy Spirit." And it is not unkind, quite. It is the language of the old prophets, who said the same thing of their own people, and loved them, and were hated for it. But it lands like a stone.

[pause]

* * *

Luke says that when they heard this, "they were enraged, and they ground their teeth at him."

Now, I do not want to dwell on the next part. This is a bedtime story, and it is meant to send you off to sleep, not to keep you awake. And Luke himself tells it in a very few lines, with a great deal of restraint. So I will tell it the way he does, and I will go slowly.

Stephen, Luke says, "full of the Holy Spirit, gazed into heaven and saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing at the right hand of God." And he says, aloud, to the whole room: "Look, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God."

Notice that word. *Standing.* In the rest of the New Testament, Jesus is seated at the right hand of the Father, as a king is seated on his throne. Here, for the only time, he is standing. And people have wondered about that for two thousand years. Some say it means that he has risen to welcome his first martyr. Some say it means he stands as a witness. Some say it means that he stands as an advocate, as a lawyer stands to speak for a client. Luke does not tell us. He tells us only what Stephen saw, and he leaves the rest to us.

And the council cannot bear it. They cry out with a loud voice. They stop their ears. They rush at him all together. And they drag him out of the city.

The law of that time said that a person condemned in such a way should be taken outside the walls, and Luke tells us that the witnesses, who were required to throw the first stones, took off their coats and laid them down at the feet of a young man named Saul.

That is the first time we meet him. A young man, standing guard over a pile of coats. And it is a small thing, one line in Acts. But in a few chapters, he is going to be knocked off his horse by a light on the road to Damascus, and he is going to change his name to Paul, and he is going to carry the message of Jesus across half the world. And I think it matters that the first thing we know about him is that he was there. That he saw it. That he heard what Stephen said.

[pause]

* * *

And so we come to the last words of Stephen. I will tell you them as Luke gives them, and then I will say nothing more for a moment.

As they threw their stones, Stephen prayed. He said, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."

And then he knelt down, and cried out in a loud voice, "Lord, do not hold this sin against them."

And when he had said this, Luke tells us, he fell asleep.

That is the word that Luke uses. Not *died*. Not *was killed*. *Fell asleep.* It is the same word the Church has used ever since for the death of a Christian, and the word the early Christians carved on their gravestones. He fell asleep.

If you were listening carefully, you will have noticed that those two prayers are not Stephen's own. They are echoes. "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit," Jesus said from the cross, in Luke's Gospel. And, "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing." Stephen has just prayed, nearly word for word, the two prayers that his Lord prayed in the hour of his own death. And that is no accident. Luke is telling us that Stephen had learned, from the very first day, how to die. Not by being brave, though he was. Not by being clever, though he was that too. But by watching the way that Jesus did it, and doing the same.

[pause]

* * *

I said that this was a bedtime story, so let me slow down now, and tell you what came after.

Luke tells us in a single sentence. "Devout men buried Stephen, and made loud lamentation over him."

Devout men. Not the apostles, who were in hiding. Not a great crowd. A few good men, who had loved him, and who came out to the place outside the walls, and picked him up, and carried him away, and wept. We do not know their names. We do not know where they put him. But we know that they wept. And we know that he was not left there.

And Luke tells us what happened next. A great persecution broke out against the Church in Jerusalem, and the believers were scattered through the countryside of Judea and Samaria. And that scattering turned out to be the best thing that could have happened. Because the believers who were scattered went on talking about Jesus wherever they went. Philip, who had been one of the seven, went down to Samaria, and then to the Gaza road. The Church that had been in one city was now in many. And the young man with the coats, Saul, who had approved of what had happened to Stephen, was on his way to Damascus.

Saint Augustine, who loved this story, preached on it more than once, four hundred years later, and the old thought attributed to him is that if Stephen had not prayed, the Church would not have had Paul. I will not give you his exact words, because I would not like to put words into the mouth of a saint, but the thought is one the Church has always liked. That a prayer for an enemy, made with the last of a man's breath, is never wasted. That God can take even the stones of the worst day and build something out of them.

Stephen did not live to see it. He saw heaven opened, and he went. But he left behind a courtyard where widows were fed fairly. And he left behind a prayer, and a young man who heard it, and who never forgot.

[pause]

* * *

Now, the wind-down. Let me go slowly.

The Church has loved Stephen from the very first. In the Western calendar, his feast is kept on the twenty-sixth of December, the day after Christmas. People sometimes find that odd. One day you are singing about a baby in a manger, and the very next day you are remembering a man who was stoned. But the Church has always seen a reason for it. The one who was born at Christmas is the one who died for us, and the first to follow him all the way is placed right next to the cradle, as though to say that the crib and the cross are made of the same wood.

You may know the old carol. It begins, "Good King Wenceslas looked out, on the Feast of Stephen." That is this day. The twenty-sixth of December, in the snow, and a king and his page going out into the dark with a bundle of wood and some wine and some meat for a poor man who lives by the forest fence. And it is, I think, a beautiful accident that the carol and the saint belong together, because it is the same thing that Stephen was doing in the courtyard. Seeing the person who was being missed. Taking the bread to them. And going out, even in the winter, even when it was dark.

The Orthodox Church keeps Stephen the day after the feast of the Nativity as well, by its own calendar, and calls him Protomartyr, the first martyr, and Archdeacon, the chief of the deacons. In the East he is painted in a deacon's long robe, holding a little censer, and often with a few small stones in his lap, or a pile of them at his feet. His icons are quiet. In most of them his face is calm.

A long time later, in the year four hundred and fifteen, a priest named Lucian, in a village near Jerusalem, is said to have been told in a dream where Stephen's body was buried, and the body was found, and was taken in procession to Jerusalem. I mention that story so that you will know it exists. It is a later tradition, and a beautiful one, and the early writers who tell it were certainly sincere. But it is not in Acts, and I have kept it out of the story, and you should hold it lightly. The relics of Saint Stephen are kept today in churches in many places, and a great many people have prayed beside them.

And if you ever go to Jerusalem, you will find a church named for Stephen, near the old walls. It is a quiet place. There is a garden in front of it, and in the evening, when the tour buses have gone, you can sit on a bench, and hear the doves in the olive trees, and the call of a distant bell. I like to think that somewhere not far from there, long ago, a woman in a grey scarf sat on her step in the sun and ate a whole loaf of bread.

[pause]

* * *

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter six, verses one to fifteen, and chapter seven, verses fifty-four to sixty, with a few lines from the start of chapter eight. It is Scripture, and in both the Catholic and the Orthodox churches it is read in the liturgy, especially on Saint Stephen's day.

What we have kept from the text: the complaint of the Hellenists; Peter's words about waiting on tables and the choosing of seven men; the seven names, every one of them Greek; the laying on of hands; the word of God spreading; the argument in the synagogues; the false witnesses; the face like the face of an angel; the long speech, which we only summarised; the vision of Jesus standing; the casting out of the city, the coats and the young man Saul; the two last prayers; the word "fell asleep"; and the devout men who buried him.

What we flagged as not in the text: Acts never calls the seven *deacons*. The word is used in the Church's later tradition, and the Greek word for service that Luke uses in this chapter is closely related to it, but he does not give them the title. We have used it as the Church does, as a name that came later. The guess that Saul may have been in the Synagogue of Cilicia is a guess, and we said so. The meaning of Jesus standing is open, and we gave the readings without choosing between them. The discovery of Stephen's relics in the year four hundred and fifteen is a later tradition, and we kept it out of the story.

What we invented: the morning in the courtyard, the baker's son, the scribe with the cold wax, the little girl with the basket, the woman in the grey scarf and her neighbour, the early morning of the seven, and the talk on the step. None of them is in Acts. None changes what happens, or what it means. And we left Stephen's trial and death told as plainly and as gently as Luke tells it, because a bedtime story should not frighten anyone.

For further reading, the sixth and seventh chapters of the Acts of the Apostles are not long, and they are very much worth reading slowly, in a quiet room, with a loaf of bread somewhere nearby.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Stephen for tonight. The Catholic Church keeps his feast on the twenty-sixth of December, the day after Christmas, and the Orthodox Church keeps him on the twenty-seventh by its own calendar, with the title Protomartyr. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Lord Jesus, receive our spirits, and do not hold our sins against us. May the God who sees those who are overlooked, and who fills the hungry with good things, watch over you tonight, and keep you till the morning. Goodnight, and God bless.

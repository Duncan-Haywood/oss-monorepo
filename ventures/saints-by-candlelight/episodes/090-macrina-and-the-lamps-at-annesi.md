Imagine a big farmhouse kitchen, a very long time ago, on a cold morning before the sun is up.

There is a stone oven in the wall, and it is already hot, and it is ticking quietly the way ovens do. There is a long wooden table dusted with flour. And at the table, with her sleeves pushed up and her hair tied back in a cloth, there is a young woman of about twenty, kneading dough.

She is not a servant. She is the eldest daughter of the house, and the house is one of the richest in the province. Her father was a famous teacher. Her family owns land in three provinces. She could be asleep upstairs under embroidered blankets, with somebody else's hands in the dough.

A sleepy kitchen girl comes in, rubbing her eyes, and stops dead.

"Lady," she says. "You're doing it again."

"I am," says the young woman, cheerfully, and slaps the dough over. "My mother likes bread made by someone who loves her. I happen to be the person who loves her most. So it falls to me. Pass me the salt, would you?"

The kitchen girl passes the salt. She has given up arguing. Everyone in the house has given up arguing.

Last time on this show we met Saint Basil, ladling soup in a famine year in the city of Caesarea, in what is now the middle of Turkey. Tonight we go back a little, and a little north, to meet the person who, according to their brother, made Basil into the kind of man who would tie on an apron in the first place.

Her name was Macrina. She was named after her grandmother, who was also called Macrina, so historians call our Macrina Macrina the Younger, which is the sort of name you get when your grandmother was formidable.

And the grandmother was formidable. There was a family story, told by Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus, that in the days when Christians were still persecuted by the Roman emperors, the grandmother and her husband had fled into the forests of the mountains of Pontus and lived there for years, hiding, rather than give up their faith. Families tell stories like that around the table for generations, and this family did.

So where is Pontus? Pontus is the strip of country along the southern shore of the Black Sea, in the north of what is now Turkey. It is green, which surprises people who think of Turkey as dusty. It rains. There are thick forests, steep hills, hazelnut trees, and rivers coming down from the mountains to the sea. One of those rivers is the Iris, and on the Iris, near a village called Annesi, the family had an estate.

I should say what an estate meant then. It did not mean a large house with a nice garden. It meant a whole working world: fields, orchards, vineyards, flocks of sheep, barns, a mill perhaps, cottages for the workers, and a great many servants and enslaved people, because the Roman world, sadly, ran on slavery, and Christian families of that time had not yet worked out what their own faith was quietly saying about it. Hold on to that thought. Macrina is going to do something about it, in her own way, and it is one of the best parts of the story.

The family was large. The father, also called Basil, was a famous teacher of rhetoric, which means public speaking and fine writing, the most prestigious subject a clever young man could study. The mother was Emmelia, a gentle, determined, very beautiful woman who had, Gregory tells us, wanted to stay unmarried herself, but who was so beautiful, and so rich, and so newly orphaned, that she married quickly to stop being pestered by suitors. They had a houseful of children. Macrina was the eldest. After her came Basil, the brilliant one, and Naucratius, the athletic one, and Gregory, the dreamy one, who is the one who later wrote all this down, and several sisters whose names we do not know, which is a shame, and at the very end, the baby, Peter.

And four of them, by the way, are saints: Macrina, Basil, Gregory, and Peter. So are their mother and their grandmother. It is a family where, if you went to a reunion, you would need to be careful about the seating plan in heaven.

Gregory wrote his book about Macrina a few months after she died, in about three hundred and eighty. It is short. You could read it in an evening. And it is unusual, because most lives of saints in those days were written by people who had never met the saint, a hundred years later, from stories. This one was written by her little brother, who was there at the end, and who loved her, and who keeps slipping out of the formal style into the voice of a younger brother who is still slightly in awe of his big sister. That voice is what we are going to try to hear tonight.

* * *

Gregory begins the story before Macrina is even born.

When Emmelia was expecting her first baby, he says, she had a dream. In the dream she was holding the baby in her arms, already born, and a figure of great majesty appeared and called the child by a name. Not Macrina. The name was Thecla. Thecla was a famous holy woman from the early days of the Church, much loved in that part of the world. The figure said the name three times, and then the baby was born, easily, and the mother woke.

So the baby was called Macrina, after her grandmother, in public. But in the family, Gregory says, there was a sort of secret name, Thecla, which told them what kind of life she might have. Gregory tells the dream as his mother told it, and we tell it as his report.

Macrina grew up in the family house, and her mother taught her herself. And here is something interesting. The normal education for a well-born child of the time meant reading the great pagan poets, Homer and the tragic playwrights, which were full of gods behaving badly and heroes behaving worse. Emmelia did not think much of that for a little girl. She taught Macrina from the Bible instead, especially the Wisdom of Solomon, and the Proverbs, and above all the Psalms.

Macrina learned the Psalms the way some children learn songs from the radio. Gregory says she went through the whole day with them. A psalm when she got up. A psalm when she started work. A psalm when she sat down to eat and when she got up again. A psalm when she went to bed. The Psalter was always with her, he says, like a good travelling companion who never ran out of things to say.

She also learned to spin and weave wool, beautifully. Every woman in a household like that did, but Macrina was very good at it, and she was going to need it.

* * *

When Macrina was twelve, her father arranged her marriage.

Now, I know. Twelve. In the Roman world girls were often betrothed very young, promised to a husband, with the wedding itself coming a few years later. The father chose carefully. Gregory says that the young man was from a good family, was studying law, was clever and modest, and was just beginning to make a name for himself in the courts. Everyone thought it was a fine match. The father was looking forward to having him in the family.

And then, before the wedding, the young man died.

Gregory does not tell us how. Just that he died young and suddenly, and that the family was very sad.

And the family did what families did. They waited a decent time, and then they started looking for another husband for Macrina, who was by now a very eligible young woman. Suitors came, Gregory says, in great numbers.

And Macrina said no.

She said something that her family found very strange at the time. She said that she had been promised in marriage, and that the young man she had been promised to was not, in her eyes, dead. He was alive in God, she said, waiting for the resurrection, simply gone on ahead, like someone who has set off on a journey first. And it would be absurd, she said, to break faith with someone who was only away.

It is worth stopping on that for a moment, because it would be easy to think it was just grief, or stubbornness. Gregory does not think so. He thinks that Macrina, at about thirteen or fourteen, had taken what she had been taught from the Psalms and the Gospels completely seriously. If the dead are really alive in God, then a promise to them still counts. And if she did not want another husband, then she had a very good reason to give her family, and one they could not easily argue with.

She decided she would never leave her mother. She would stay home, and work, and pray, and that would be her life.

And here is where we get to the kitchen.

Gregory says that Macrina did not simply stay at home and look holy. She worked. She made bread with her own hands for her mother, and he mentions this specifically, which I think means it became a family joke, the daughter of the house up before dawn with flour on her elbows. She did her share of the work alongside the servants. She looked after the accounts of the estate, which were complicated, because there were lands in three different provinces and taxes to pay in each of them. Her father had died by now, quite young, and the mother was left with a large estate and a large family to run, and Macrina became her right hand.

There is a lovely line in Gregory where he says that Macrina was a support to her mother in body and soul. She lightened the work, and she also, gently, began to lift her mother's eyes. It took years. But slowly, Emmelia began to see the world the way her daughter did.

* * *

Now. Basil.

In the last episode, we met Basil as a grown man, bishop, builder, a terror to emperors and a friend to the hungry. Tonight we meet him at about twenty-five, coming home from university, and I am sorry to say that he was insufferable.

Basil had studied at Constantinople, and then at Athens, which was the Oxford or Harvard of the ancient world. He had been the star student. He had made his friend Gregory of Nazianzus there, and the two of them had been famous for being clever. And he came home to Cappadocia with a reputation, and the city of Caesarea offered him a teaching post, and he was, in his little brother Gregory's carefully chosen words, excessively puffed up by his gift for public speaking. He looked down on everybody, Gregory says. He thought himself better than the leading men of the province.

Imagine the scene. The family dining table. Basil, home from the best university in the world, explaining things. Everyone else trying to get a word in. And Macrina, who has spent the last ten years baking bread and singing psalms and doing the estate accounts, listening.

Gregory does not give us her actual words. He says, simply, that she took him in hand, and drew him with such speed to the goal of philosophy that he gave up the fame of his public speaking and became a worker with his own hands.

Philosophy is a word worth explaining. When Gregory says philosophy, he does not mean reading books about ideas. In the Christian writers of that time, philosophy meant a whole way of living, simple, prayerful, disciplined, with very few possessions, the kind of life the monks and hermits in Egypt were famous for. The true lover of wisdom, they said, does not just think about God. He lives for Him.

So here is the scene as I imagine it. We are inventing it, of course. Gregory gives us only that she took him in hand.

It is evening. Basil has been telling the family, at length, about a speech he gave in Athens and how the audience wept.

Macrina waits until he takes a breath.

"Basil," she says. "That is a lovely speech. What was it for?"

"For?" says Basil. "It was about virtue."

"And did anybody become virtuous?"

Basil opens his mouth, and shuts it again.

"You are the cleverest person I know," says Macrina. "And you are wasting it on being clapped."

And that, more or less, is what happened over the next year or two. Basil did not mention it in his own letters, by the way. In a letter written many years later, he describes waking up, as if from a deep sleep, and seeing the uselessness of the wisdom of this world, and going off to find the monks of Egypt and Syria to learn how to live. He does not say that his big sister woke him. Gregory does. Brothers, in my experience, are not always the most reliable reporters of who woke whom.

Either way, Basil gave up his teaching, gave away his money, and went to live as a monk, first travelling to meet the monks of the East, and then on a lonely hillside across the river Iris from the family house. He wrote to his friend Gregory of Nazianzus describing it: a high mountain, a thick forest, a river rushing below, a waterfall, wild animals, peace and quiet. Gregory of Nazianzus came to stay, and afterwards wrote him teasing letters about the hardships of the place, and loved it.

So when you remember Basil building his whole new town of nurses and doctors outside Caesarea, remember there was an older sister in a kitchen in Pontus who once asked him what his speech was for.

* * *

Meanwhile, back at the house on the Iris, something quietly extraordinary was happening.

Macrina persuaded her mother to change how they lived.

Gregory says that she brought her mother round to giving up her accustomed way of life, her grand household and the deference of being served, and to living, as he puts it, on equal terms with her maids. They would share the same food, the same table, the same kind of bed, the same work. The servants and enslaved women of the household were made, Gregory says, into sisters and equals. And from his description, many of them freely chose to stay and share that life, praying and working together, with the lady of the house and her daughter.

Pause on that, because in the fourth century it is astonishing. A rich Roman household, where the line between those who served and those who were served was thick and high, and where you could tell who was who by their clothes and their food and where they slept. And one young woman simply walked across the line, took her mother by the hand, and sat down on the other side of it.

The house became what we would call a monastery, a community of women living a life of prayer and work. It was not a building with a bell tower and a cloister. It was the family home, gradually transformed. They prayed the Psalms together through the day and in the night. They worked together, spinning and weaving and farming. They had no wish for anything, Gregory says, except what they needed. Their only luxury was quiet.

And people came. Over the years, other women joined them, from all kinds of backgrounds. Gregory mentions that some of the community had first come in a time of famine, when Macrina had found them as girls, lying by the roadsides, and had brought them home, and fed them, and raised them. The narrator likes to think that some of them, years later, were the ones who sang her psalms at the very end.

Not far off, across the river, Basil founded a community of men. Later, the youngest brother Peter took charge of it. So there were two households of prayer facing each other across the Iris, the brothers on one side and the sisters on the other, like two choirs singing alternate verses of the same psalm.

* * *

Now, Naucratius. I promised you an athletic brother.

Naucratius was the second son, and Gregory, who is sometimes a little dazzled by his brothers, says he was the best of them all for strength and beauty and quickness and an ability to turn his hand to anything. He too was given a fine education, and he too gave a public speech when he was young that made everyone sit up. And then, at the age of about twenty-two, he did something very like what Macrina was doing at home. He walked away from the career everyone expected, and went to live as a hermit in the forests by the Iris, a few miles from the house.

He did not go alone. He took with him a servant called Chrysapius, who loved him, and who chose to share that life, and the two of them lived simply in the woods.

And what did a strong, quick, clever young man do with his days in the forest? He went hunting and fishing. Not for sport. Gregory says that there were some poor old men living nearby, too old and too sick to look after themselves, and Naucratius looked after them. He hunted and fished to feed them, and he did all their caring for them with his own hands. A young athlete, using his strength to carry fish and firewood to old men who could not do it themselves. I think that is one of the nicest pictures in the whole book.

He lived like that for five years. And then something happened, Gregory does not say exactly what, out in the woods. He went out on one of his errands, and he and Chrysapius were brought home dead.

The news came to the house when their mother was some way off, and she collapsed, Gregory says, as though struck. And it was Macrina, in that hour, who held her up. She did not pretend it did not hurt. She was grieving too. But she sat with her mother and reminded her of everything they believed, that Naucratius was not lost but gone on ahead, like the young man who was to have been her husband, and that they would see him again. Gregory says she was like a rock in the middle of a flood, steady while the water rushed round her.

* * *

Here, I am going to tell you one of the two little healings in Gregory's book. Gregory tells them as reports, and we will too.

The first happened to Macrina herself.

Once, Gregory says, she developed a painful swelling on her breast, the kind of thing we would call a tumour. It grew worse. Her mother begged her to let a doctor look at it. Doctors were mostly men, and it would have meant uncovering herself, and Macrina, being who she was, would not.

Instead, Gregory says, she went into the little church at night, and prayed all night long, and wept. And she took some of the earth from the floor, wet with her tears, and made a sort of paste of it, and pressed it against the place.

And in the morning she went to her mother and asked her to make the sign of the cross over it. And her mother did. And the swelling was gone.

Gregory says that when he came to dress her body after her death, the widow Vetiana showed him a tiny mark, like a small scar from a needle, at the place where it had been. And they kept that as a quiet sign of God's kindness to her.

I want to say one thing about this, because it matters. Macrina did not heal herself. She did not do magic with mud. She prayed, and she asked her mother to bless her, and then, Gregory believes, God answered. That is how miracles work in all of these stories. The saint prays. God acts.

* * *

The years went by, and the household by the river grew older.

Peter, the baby, grew up there. He had been born around the time that their father died, so he never really knew his father, and Gregory says that Macrina became all things to him: father, teacher, guide, mother, adviser in everything good. She raised him, and taught him, and he grew up in that household of prayer and never went away to university to get puffed up, which was perhaps a deliberate choice. Later on, when a famine came to the district, it was Peter who opened the stores and fed the crowds that came streaming to the community's door, so many that the place, Gregory says, seemed like a city. You can see where he learned it.

Their mother Emmelia lived to a good old age, in the community she had helped make. When she died, Macrina and Peter were with her. Gregory says that she held their hands, one on each side, and offered them both to God, as the first fruits and the last of her children, and then she went to God very peacefully, and was buried in the family chapel of the Forty Martyrs, beside their father.

And then, at the very beginning of the year three hundred and seventy-nine, or perhaps a little earlier, historians argue, Basil died, worn out, in Caesarea. We told that story last time, as much as we could.

And Gregory, the dreamy younger brother, who had become a bishop himself, of a small town called Nyssa, and who had had a very hard time of it, with an emperor who had sent him into exile, realised that he had not seen his eldest sister for nearly eight years.

* * *

Here our mule driver enters the story. He is our own invention, and so is everything he says, but someone was driving Gregory's mules, so it might as well be Theon.

Picture a road through the green hills of Pontus in late summer, about nine months after Basil's death. The trees are heavy. There has been rain in the night and everything smells of wet leaves and warm stone. A small carriage, or a cart with a cover over it, which in the fourth century amounts to the same thing as far as your back is concerned, is lurching along behind two mules.

Theon, the driver, is talking. Theon is always talking.

"Your sister," he says, "is the one who makes the bread? I heard she makes the bread."

"She used to," says Gregory, from inside. "I imagine she has let someone else do it by now. She must be past fifty."

"My aunt is past fifty," says Theon, "and if you tried to take her oven away from her she would hit you with a ladle."

Gregory smiles in spite of himself. He has been tired for weeks. A church council in the great city of Antioch, long meetings, long arguments, long faces. And under all of it, ever since January, a heavy grief for Basil, who was difficult, and brilliant, and his teacher, and his brother. He has been carrying it the way you carry a stone in your shoe, trying not to limp.

That night they stop at an inn, a day's journey short of Annesi. And Gregory, Gregory tells us himself, has a dream.

In the dream he is holding in his hands the relics of martyrs, the bones of holy people who died for Christ, and they are shining. Not glowing gently, but blazing, the way the sun blazes off a mirror, so bright that he cannot look at them. And the dream comes three times that night. And he wakes up with a heavy, uneasy feeling, as if something is about to happen, and he does not know what.

"Bad night?" says Theon in the morning, harnessing the mules.

"A strange one," says Gregory.

"Eat something," says Theon. "Everything is stranger on an empty stomach."

* * *

They come down into the valley of the Iris in the afternoon.

And as they get near, a crowd of men comes walking out to meet them, the brothers from the men's community, which is the custom when a bishop arrives. They greet Gregory warmly. But Gregory notices that they are quiet. And he notices, as they walk him across to the women's house, that the sisters are waiting in the church, and nobody is quite meeting his eye.

"How is my sister?" he asks.

"She is ill, Father," says one of the brothers. "She has been ill for some time."

Gregory goes in.

And there she is. Not in a bed, because Macrina had never slept in a bed, not for years. She is lying on the floor, on a plank of wood, with a sack spread under her, and another plank under her head, raised a little, so that she can breathe. That is how she has always slept. She sees no reason to change it just because she is dying.

She is thin, and very weak, and she has a fever. But when she sees him come in, she raises herself up on one elbow, because she has not the strength to get up, and she holds out her hand, and she says something like, "You have come. You have done me this kindness too."

And Gregory, Bishop Gregory, the famous preacher, the defender of the faith, who has argued with emperors, kneels on the floor beside his big sister and cannot say anything at all.

* * *

What follows is one of the most beautiful passages in all of the old Christian writing, and I am going to tell it slowly.

Macrina could see that Gregory was upset, and she did what she had done all her life. She took charge. She made him sit, and she made someone bring him something to eat, and then she began to talk to him, quietly, about Basil.

Gregory confesses that at the mention of Basil he broke down completely. He had been holding it in for nine months. And Macrina let him cry for a while, and then she began to talk to him about the resurrection. About what death is. About what the soul is, and where it goes, and why we need not be afraid. She did this, Gregory says, with such clarity and such ease that he felt himself being lifted, as if he were being carried up out of his grief. Later he wrote the whole conversation down, or at least his memory of it, as a separate book, called On the Soul and the Resurrection. In that book, he calls his sister, simply, the Teacher. A bishop calling his sister his teacher, at a time when not many men would.

Then, later, Gregory began to tell her about his own troubles. The exile. The emperor who had driven him out of his church. The people who had plotted against him. The councils, the travelling, the being misunderstood. It all came pouring out, the way it does when you finally sit down with someone from home.

And Macrina, lying on her plank, listened, and then she said, as Gregory reports it, something like this.

"Gregory. Will you not stop being ungrateful for the good things God has given you?"

He was startled.

"Think about it," she said. "Our father was famous in his day. But famous where? In his own province. People knew his name in the towns round about. And you? Churches all over the world call for you. Cities send for you to make peace in their quarrels. You are known everywhere. And did you do that yourself? No. It was our parents' prayers that lifted you up. So stop complaining, little brother, and say thank you."

The narrator would like to point out that this is a dying woman gently telling off a bishop. And that the bishop wrote it down, for everyone to read, for the next sixteen hundred years. That tells you something about both of them.

* * *

Then she sent him off to rest. There was a shady place in the garden, Gregory says, a sort of arbour, under vines, and she had them take him there, and lie him down, while she rested too. And Gregory lay under the leaves, half dozing, and could not really rest, because he knew, and she knew, and everyone in the house knew, what was coming.

In the evening, he went back in. And she began to tell him about her life. Not to boast. Gregory says she went through it all from childhood, the way you would look through a box of old letters, and in every part of it she gave thanks. For her parents. For the house. For the bread and the wool and the psalms. For Basil and his temper and his genius. For Naucratius and the old men in the woods. For Peter. For the girls from the roadsides. For the whole long, ordinary, extraordinary life.

She did not ask to be remembered. She asked to be forgiven, and she thanked God.

And she kept turning towards the east, Gregory says, as if she were watching for someone.

* * *

The next day, she was weaker. Her voice was going. The fever was burning.

And towards the end of the day, she began to pray aloud, very quietly, a long prayer. Gregory wrote it down, and it is one of the treasures of the old Church, and I am going to give you part of it, in our own plain words, and much shortened.

She prayed something like this:

"You, Lord, have taken away from us the fear of death. You have made the end of this life the beginning of the true life. You let our bodies rest in sleep for a while, and you will wake them again at the last trumpet. You gave the earth our bodies, which your hands made, and you will take back what you gave, and make it beautiful.

"You opened the way to paradise for the thief who was crucified with you. Remember me, too, in your kingdom, because I also have been crucified with you.

"You have been my God from my mother's womb. I have loved you with all my strength. I have given you my body and my soul from when I was young until now. Give me now an angel of light to lead me by the hand to the place of rest, where the water is cool, near the bosom of the holy fathers.

"And if I have sinned in word or deed or thought, forgive me, because you have power on earth to forgive. And may I be found before you, when I put off this body, without stain, and may my soul be received into your hands like incense before you."

And then she was quiet.

* * *

It was evening now. And in that house, as in all Christian houses of prayer in those days, there was a little ceremony every evening, as the light went. Someone would bring in the lamps, and light them, and as the flames caught, everyone would sing the old hymn of the evening, giving thanks for the light. Gregory does not tell us the words they sang, but the oldest hymn for that hour, which Basil himself mentions as already ancient, is still sung in the Orthodox Church at Vespers, every evening, all over the world. It begins, O gladsome light.

So the lamps were brought in.

And Macrina tried to join in the thanksgiving. Her voice had gone. But Gregory says that she moved her lips, and that you could see from her eyes, and from her hand, that she was praying with them. She made the sign of the cross over her eyes, and her mouth, and her heart.

And then, when the prayer of thanksgiving for the light was finished, she breathed in, very deeply, once, and her life went out with the breath.

It was the hour of the lamps. The lights had just been lit. And the eldest daughter of the house by the Iris went home to God.

* * *

Gregory does not hurry the next part, and neither will we.

He closed her eyes himself, and her mouth, which had fallen a little open, and folded her hands. And then, because there was work to do, the women of the house began to do it.

Two of them Gregory names. One was Vetiana, a young widow from a noble family who had come to live in the community. The other was Lampadion, a deaconess, who led the choir of sisters. Her name means little lamp, which, under the circumstances, is almost too perfect, but that is genuinely her name.

Gregory wanted to dress his sister in fine clothes for her burial, as you would for a bride. He asked Lampadion to bring out the best of her things.

And Lampadion looked at him, with tears running down her face, and said, more or less: "Her best things? You can see her best things. That cloak. That veil. Those worn shoes. That is all there is. She never kept anything. Her only treasure was in heaven."

They had to make do. In the end, Gregory says, he had something of his own brought for her. And then, Gregory says, so that the brightness of it would not be too much, they laid over her the dark cloak that had belonged to their mother. Which I think is the gentlest detail in the whole book. The daughter who never left her mother, going to her grave wrapped in her mother's cloak.

When they were dressing her, Vetiana found, round Macrina's neck, on a cord, a small iron cross and an iron ring. Nothing else. The ring had a little hollow in it, and inside, Gregory says, was a tiny fragment of the wood of the Cross. Gregory and Vetiana divided these between them, a cross for one, a ring for the other, as their inheritance. Everything else she owned had been given away long ago.

* * *

The funeral was on the next day.

The news had gone out overnight, across the hills, and people came from everywhere. Farmers and shepherds. The poor she had fed. Monks from the brothers' house. Women from the villages. By morning there were so many people that the singing of the sisters, which Gregory had arranged to be in two choirs, men on one side and women on the other, the psalms going back and forth like the two communities across the river, kept being drowned out by weeping.

The local bishop, a man called Araxius, came with his clergy. And then the procession set out, carrying Macrina to the church of the Holy Forty Martyrs, where their parents lay.

It was not far, less than a mile. But the crowds were so great, and the singing so long, and everyone wanting to be near, that Gregory says it took them nearly the whole day. And there, in the family chapel, they laid her beside her mother, as she would have wanted, the two of them, Gregory says, now joined in death as they had been in life.

* * *

There is one more story in the book, and it comes after the funeral. Gregory heard it on his way home, and he tells it as a report.

On his journey back, Gregory was given hospitality by a soldier, an officer in charge of a garrison at a little town called Sebastopolis. The soldier had heard of Macrina's death, and he was very sad, and over dinner, he told Gregory a story.

Some time before, he said, he and his wife had gone to visit Macrina's community, and they had taken their little daughter with them. And the little girl had a bad eye infection, so bad that the eye was clouded over, and they were frightened she would lose the sight in it.

The soldier had gone across to the men's house, and his wife and daughter had stayed with the sisters. And Macrina had taken the little girl on her knee, and kissed her, and noticed the poorly eye. And she said to the mother, Gregory reports: "If you stay and eat with us, I will give you some medicine for her eye. Something to make it better."

They stayed, and ate, and had a happy evening. And then, in all the bustle of leaving, they set off home, and forgot the medicine completely.

They were well on the road before the mother remembered. And she was cross with herself, and she turned round to look at her little girl.

And the eye was clear.

"The medicine," the soldier said to Gregory, "was her prayer. That was the medicine she meant. We just did not know it."

Gregory sat at that dinner table and listened, and wrote it down, and said that there were many other stories he could tell, but that he would not, in case people who did not know her thought he was making them up. Which is the kind of thing you say only when you are trying very hard to tell the truth.

[pause]

* * *

So what happened afterwards?

Gregory went home to Nyssa and wrote two books about his sister: the Life, which is our story tonight, and On the Soul and the Resurrection, which is the long conversation by her deathbed. He went on to become one of the great teachers of the Church, a deep thinker about God and the soul and the endless journey of the soul into God, which he thought went on forever, always growing, never bored. He is a saint in both the Catholic and the Orthodox Churches. In the East, he is sometimes called the father of the Fathers. And his first teacher, by his own account, was a woman on a plank on a floor by a river.

Peter became bishop of Sebaste, not far away, and he too is honoured as a saint.

And the community by the Iris went on for a while after them, and then, as these things do over many centuries, it disappeared. Today nobody is quite sure exactly where Annesi was. Historians have argued about it, and there are a few likely spots in the green hills of northern Turkey, near the river that is now called the Yeshilirmak, which means the green river. The house is gone. The church of the Forty Martyrs is gone. The bread oven is gone.

But Macrina did not leave a house. She left a way of living, with her brothers, and through them with everyone who learned from them. Basil wrote the rules that monks and nuns in the East still follow, and he learned how to live them first at his sister's house. When Christians talk about living together in community, sharing everything, with no one above anyone else, praying through the day, working with their hands, taking in the ones left by the roadside, a good deal of it flows from that house by the Iris.

Her feast day is the nineteenth of July, in both the East and the West. In the Orthodox Church she is called Righteous Mother Macrina. In paintings she is shown as a nun in dark robes, often holding a scroll or a cross, with her face very calm.

And every evening, all over the world, in monasteries and parish churches and in people's homes, someone lights the lamps as the light goes, and sings, O gladsome light, the old hymn of thanksgiving for the evening, very likely the same hymn that was sung in the room where Macrina died, because her brother Basil already called it old.

So if tonight you have a lamp on, or a candle, or the little glow of a nightlight, you could think of her. The eldest sister, who baked the bread, and told her brother to stop showing off, and sat down on the other side of the line, and went home at the hour of the lamps.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Almost everything tonight comes from one short book, The Life of Macrina, written by her brother Gregory of Nyssa soon after her death, about three hundred and eighty. Gregory was there at the end, which makes the book unusually close to its subject. It is still a book of praise, written by a loving brother, and he shapes it carefully. The long conversation about the soul is his separate book, On the Soul and the Resurrection, where Macrina is called the Teacher. The grandmother hiding in the forests comes from Gregory of Nazianzus's funeral speech for Basil. Basil's waking from sleep is his own letter two hundred and twenty-three, and his hillside across the river is letter fourteen. Kevin Corrigan's translation of the Life is short and readable, and Anna Silvas has a fuller study with all the sources.

What is history. The family by the Iris, Macrina's betrothal and the young man's death, her life of work with her mother, the household made into a community, Naucratius and the old men, the raising of Peter, Gregory's visit after Basil died, her death at lamp-lighting about three hundred and seventy-nine, and her burial with her mother. The exact years are argued over.

What is told as a report. The mother's dream and the name Thecla, Gregory's dream of the shining relics, the healing of Macrina's illness, and the soldier's little girl. Gregory tells all of these as his own report or as what he was told.

What we invented. Theon the driver and everything he says, the kitchen girl, the weather, the words at Basil's dinner table, and the wording of most conversations. Macrina's last prayer is Gregory's, in our own plain paraphrase and much shortened.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Macrina for tonight. Her feast is on the nineteenth of July, in both the Catholic and the Orthodox Churches, and her brother Gregory's is on the tenth of January in the East and the ninth of March in the West. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. And here, in our own plain words, is the beginning of the old evening hymn of the lamps, which may well have been sung when she died. O gladsome light of the holy glory of the immortal Father, heavenly, holy, blessed Jesus Christ: now that we have come to the setting of the sun, and see the evening light, we sing to God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Holy Mother Macrina, pray to God for us. Goodnight, and God bless.

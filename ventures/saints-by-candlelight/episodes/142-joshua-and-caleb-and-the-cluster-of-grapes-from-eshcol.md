Two men are walking down a hillside in the evening, very carefully, with a pole across their shoulders.

The pole is long, and it bends in the middle. And hanging from the middle of the pole, swinging gently as they walk, is a bunch of grapes.

Just one bunch.

It is the biggest bunch of grapes either of them has ever seen. It is so big that one man could not carry it by himself without squashing it, so they have cut a branch, and hung it from the branch, and put the branch on their shoulders, and now they are walking in step, front and back, like two men carrying a very small and very sticky sedan chair.

"Mind the rock," says the man at the back.

"I see the rock," says the man at the front.

"You stepped on the last rock."

"I saw that one too. I just stepped on it anyway."

Behind them come the others, with their arms full of figs and pomegranates, and they are all laughing at the two men with the pole. There is something else about Joshua that matters tonight. His name was not always Joshua.

Forty-five years later, of all the places in the whole land he could have asked for, the old man asks for that one. The hardest one. The one with the giants.

You know the Anakim were there, Caleb says, and the cities are great and fortified. It may be that the Lord will be with me, and I shall drive them out, as the Lord said.

And Joshua blessed him. And Joshua gave Hebron to Caleb, son of Jephunneh, for his inheritance. And the book says, and I find this very moving, that Hebron became the inheritance of Caleb to this day, because he wholly followed the Lord, the God of Israel.

* * *

There is one more little story about Caleb, and it is so gentle and so domestic that I have saved it for the very end, when you may be nearly asleep.

Caleb had a daughter, called Achsah. And when she was married, and going off to her new home with her husband, a brave young man called Othniel, Caleb gave her some land in the south country. Dry land. Land in the Negeb.

And Achsah came to her father, riding on a donkey. And she got down off the donkey. And Caleb, who knew his daughter, said to her, what is it? What do you want?

And Achsah said: Give me a blessing. You have given me the dry land of the south. Give me springs of water too.

And Caleb gave her the upper springs and the lower springs.

That is the whole story. It is in the Book of Joshua, and it is told again in the Book of Judges, as if somebody thought it was too lovely to tell only once. A father, an old soldier, standing by a donkey. A daughter who knows exactly what she needs, and asks for it. And a father who gives her more than one spring. The upper springs and the lower springs. Both.

I think Caleb, who had once stood in front of a whole crowd and said, let us go up, because we are well able, must have been a very good father to ask things of.

[pause]

So that is how it ended. The man who had been forty when he carried the fruit back from Hebron, and eighty-five when he asked for it, sitting in the shade in his own hill country, among his own vines, with the grandchildren running about. The hills around Hebron covered in grapes. And springs of water, upper and lower, running down to his daughter's dry fields.

The Scripture remembered him. Hundreds of years later, a wise man called Jesus son of Sirach wrote a long poem in praise of the great men of Israel, and he wrote of Joshua and Caleb. He said that in the days of Moses they did a loyal thing. They stood against the whole assembly, and held the people back from sin, and stilled their wicked grumbling. And he said that the Lord gave Caleb strength, which stayed with him even in his old age, so that he went up to the heights of the land, and his children kept it for their inheritance.

And an old priest called Mattathias, dying, in the days of the Maccabees, told his sons to remember the heroes who had gone before them. And he said: Joshua, because he fulfilled the command, became a judge in Israel. Caleb, because he testified in the assembly, received an inheritance in the land.

He testified in the assembly. That is all Caleb did, really. He stood up in front of a frightened crowd and said, we can do it. And it was enough.

And the Psalms remembered that whole generation in the wilderness, the forty years and the rest they never entered, and turned it into a prayer that many Christians pray at the start of the day, in the Church's morning prayer, to this day. Today, if you hear his voice, the psalm says, do not harden your hearts, as your fathers did in the wilderness. And the Letter to the Hebrews, in the New Testament, takes up the same psalm and says that there is still a promised rest for the people of God, and that the word is today. Today, if you hear his voice.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Numbers, chapters thirteen and fourteen, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. Moses' own retelling of it is in Deuteronomy, chapter one, and it is mentioned again in Numbers, chapter thirty-two. Caleb's words to Joshua at Gilgal, and his gift of Hebron, are from the Book of Joshua, chapter fourteen. His taking of Hebron, and Achsah and the springs, are from Joshua, chapter fifteen, and Achsah's story is told again in Judges, chapter one.

Joshua at the battle with Amalek, on the mountain and in the tent is from Exodus, chapters seventeen, twenty-four and thirty-three, and the Lord's name proclaimed to Moses, which Moses prays back, is from Exodus, chapter thirty-four. The praise of Joshua and Caleb is from Sirach, chapter forty-six, and Mattathias's words from First Maccabees, chapter two. The psalm is Psalm ninety-five, which speaks of the whole wilderness generation, taken up in Hebrews, chapters three and four. Hebron and the cave of Machpelah are from Genesis, chapters twenty-three, forty-nine and fifty; Aaron's death is in Numbers, chapter twenty; Joshua's commissioning in Numbers, chapter twenty-seven; and Moses' death on Nebo in Deuteronomy, chapter thirty-four.

That the names Joshua and Jesus are the same name, through Greek, is a plain fact of language. That Kenizzite means Caleb's family came into Judah from outside is a common reading, not a certain one. The distance of the spies' walk is our own rough estimate. The Nephilim are mentioned briefly in Genesis, chapter six, and what they were is not known. Christians from early times have seen in the cluster hanging from the wooden pole a picture of Christ on the cross; that is a way of reading the story, and it is lovely, but it is not part of what Numbers says.

The boy Eli on his rock, the conversation about front and back, Joshua and Caleb carrying the pole, and every word not given in the chapters are our own invention.

Imagine a very small boat in a very large amount of dark. There is no moon to speak of. The wind is coming from exactly the direction the boat wants to go. A dozen tired men are taking turns at the oars, and every one of them has blisters, and every one of them has stopped making jokes, which, in a boat full of fishermen, is a bad sign.

And then one of them looks up and says, in a voice he has never used before, that there is somebody out there.

Not in a boat. Out there. On the water.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves. Let us start on the shore, at the end of a long day.

[pause]

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the Gospel according to Matthew, chapter fourteen, which Christians of both traditions read as Scripture, and we will tell it as it is told there. A few small things, the weather, the talk in the boat, the jokes, and what a tired fisherman might have been thinking, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and we will say at the end which parts those were. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

It is evening, and the hillside is full of people.

If you have heard the story that comes just before this one, you know why. That afternoon, a very large crowd had followed Jesus out to a quiet place by the lake, and they had stayed so long that it was getting late, and nobody had brought anything to eat. The Gospel says there were about five thousand men, besides women and children, which means that the real number was a good deal larger. And Jesus had taken five loaves and two fish, and blessed them, and broken them, and given them to the disciples to hand out, and everybody had eaten, and there had been twelve baskets of pieces left over.

We will not tell that story tonight. It is a long one and a good one, and it deserves its own evening. But it matters for us, because it is the day the disciples are coming off. They have spent hours carrying bread through a crowd. They have been up since before dawn. They have watched something happen with their own hands that they do not have a word for. And now, at the end of it, everyone is standing about in the dusk, full and happy and a little dazed, and showing no sign of going home.

You may know a crowd like that. The party has been wonderful, and the host is exhausted, and the guests are on their third round of goodbyes at the door.

So here is what the Gospel tells us next. Immediately, it says, Jesus made the disciples get into the boat and go ahead of him to the other side, while he dismissed the crowd.

Immediately. Made them. It is one of those small phrases that tells you a great deal. The disciples did not want to go. Perhaps they wanted to stay and watch what would happen next. Perhaps they thought, rather sensibly, that it was a bad time of the evening to start a crossing. But he said go, and they went. And I would like you to notice that already, in the first sentence of the story, the disciples are in the boat because they were told to be. Hold on to that. It is going to matter when the wind comes.

[pause]

Let us say a word about the lake, since the lake is a character in this story, and not an especially friendly one.

The Sea of Galilee is not really a sea. It is a freshwater lake, about thirteen miles long and about seven wide, sitting in a hollow in the hills, a good deal below the level of the real sea. If you were to stand on its shore on a calm summer morning you might think you had come to the quietest place in the world. The water is flat. The hills are soft. A boat drifts with its sail hanging like a towel.

And then the afternoon wind comes down.

Because the lake sits in a bowl, with high hills on the sides and a gap or two where cold air can pour in, the weather on it can change in a very short time. Fishermen who have worked it all their lives say that a wind can arrive from nowhere, and by the time you have taken in the nets the whole surface is white. It is not an ocean. It is nothing you could not cross in half a day on a good morning. But it can turn on you, and the men in this boat know it, because they were born beside it. Several of them have lost friends to it. They are not frightened of the lake the way a tourist is. They are afraid of it the way you are afraid of a kind uncle who sometimes loses his temper.

So when the disciples push off from the shore that evening, the Gospel tells us nothing about a storm. Not yet. It tells us only that Jesus has sent them, and that they have gone.

[pause]

And now the hillside empties, and we see something quiet.

When Jesus had dismissed the crowds, Matthew says, he went up on the mountain by himself to pray. And when evening came, he was there alone.

I find that sentence very moving, partly because of how little it says. There is no sermon in it. There is no miracle. There is only a man who has spent the whole day giving himself away to a crowd, and who now, when everybody has gone, climbs a hill in the dark to be alone with his Father. Not a hermit's silence. A tired man's silence, the kind you go looking for at the end of a day of being needed.

And he is up there, praying, while down on the water, a good way from the shore, a boat is having a bad time.

Now we will come back to that, because I think it is the key to the whole story. The disciples are in trouble, and Jesus is not with them, and he is not hiding. He is on a mountain, praying. In Mark's version of this story there is a little line that says he saw them, straining at the oars, because the wind was against them. From the mountain he could see the boat. The disciples could not see him at all. But he could see them, all night, the whole time.

Keep that in mind as well. We will need it later.

[pause]

* * *

[pause]

Down on the lake, the boat is a long way out.

John's Gospel, which tells this story in only a few lines, gives us a measure. By the time the trouble began, John says, the disciples had rowed about three or four miles. The Greek says twenty-five or thirty stadia, and a stadium is a bit over a hundred and eighty metres, so that works out, near enough, to what I have said. If the crossing was meant to be about five or six miles, they were more than halfway there. They were as far from either shore as it was possible to be.

And the wind is against them.

Matthew puts it very plainly. The boat, he says, was by this time many stadia distant from the land, beaten by the waves, for the wind was against it.

Let me tell you what that is like, as best I can guess, because none of the Gospels does. Picture a rowing boat, a working boat, broad and heavy and made for hauling nets, not for speed. It has a sail, but a sail is no use when the wind is in your face, so the sail has been rolled up, and the men are rowing. Every stroke of the oars pushes the boat a little way. Every wave knocks it back almost as far. The boat is moving forwards in the way that a person is moving forwards on a staircase that is going down.

There are about a dozen of them. They take turns at the oars, two or three at a time, and the ones who are not rowing bail. Water comes over the side, and when it comes over the side it has to go back over the side, and so somebody sits in the bottom of the boat, scooping, with a bucket or a cooking pot or his own two hands.

At first, they talk. A fisherman will tell you that talking is part of the work. Someone says, it was not like this when we set out. Someone says, it was not like this at all. Someone points out that they have rowed this lake a thousand times. Someone else says that is exactly what they said last time.

And then, as the hours go by, they stop talking. It takes all your breath to row against a wind, and when you have no breath for anything else, you begin to notice what is in your head.

What is in their heads? The Gospels do not say, but I think we can guess one thing. Not one of them is thinking, I wish I had stayed on the hill. They are not thinking, why did he send us. Those are the thoughts of someone with spare energy. These men have none. They are thinking about the next stroke, and the one after that, and if you had asked them what they were afraid of, I think they would have told you quite simply: that the boat will fill. They have sailed this lake all their lives. They know how it goes.

I want to say, as gently as I can, that this is how most of us spend most of our nights of trouble. We are not rowing against the wind because we are foolish. We are rowing against the wind because we were told to cross, and so we are crossing, and it is hard, and nobody has told us how long it will last. And the one who sent us is, as far as we can tell, somewhere else.

[pause]

The night goes on.

A word about the way the ancients counted it, because Matthew is about to use their clock. The Romans, and many people under Roman rule, divided the night into four watches, as sentries did. The first watch began at sunset, and the fourth ended at sunrise. The fourth watch, then, is the last part of the night, the dark before dawn, roughly three o'clock to six in the morning. If you have ever been awake at four, you will know it is the loneliest hour there is. It is the hour when the worries come out. It is the hour when a person who has been rowing all night begins to think that perhaps the morning will not come.

And it is in the fourth watch, Matthew says, that he came to them, walking on the sea.

Not at the beginning of the trouble. Not in the first hour, when they might have turned back and been home by midnight. Not even in the middle of the night. Late. At the very last moment, nearly at morning. I do not know why. The Gospel does not say, and I will not pretend to. I will only say that anyone who has waited through a long night for help will recognise this part of the story, and may find it a comfort, or a puzzle, or both. The help came. It was not early. It was not too late.

So there they are. It is the fourth watch. They are grey with tiredness. The oars are heavy as logs. Somebody has stopped bailing, and somebody else has not noticed.

And one of them looks up.

I think it was one of the men at the back, the ones who were not rowing, who were sitting a little higher and so could see further. He is looking across the water for no reason, the way you look when you are too tired to look at anything in particular. And a long way off, in the dark, where there ought to be only black water and white waves, there is something pale.

At first he thinks it is a trick of the foam. Then it moves. Then he sees that it is moving the wrong way for foam, steadily, evenly, straight across the waves, as though they were not there. And it is coming closer.

He says something. Perhaps he only says, look. Perhaps he cannot say anything. But the others follow his eyes, and one by one the oars stop.

The Gospel says what happened next in one short, honest sentence. When the disciples saw him walking on the sea, they were terrified, and said, it is a ghost. And they cried out in fear.

I would like to say a kind word for the disciples here. Notice that they do not stand up and say, that's him. Notice that they do not think, ah, our teacher is coming to help. They have just spent a night in a boat in a storm, and they see a figure coming over the water, and every sensible bone in their bodies says: that cannot be a man. A man cannot do that. And so the only explanation they have is the one that their grandmothers gave them. A ghost. Something that walks on the water at night. Something that comes for sailors.

And they cried out in fear. Twelve grown men, who are not easily frightened by water, who have made their living from it, cry out like children at the sight of one figure approaching in the dark. I find this very reassuring. It means these were real men, and this was a real night, and the fear was real. Nobody is pretending.

Mark adds one detail that we should mention, because it is odd and honest. He says that Jesus meant to pass by them. That is the word he uses. It was as though he was going to walk past the boat. I will not explain that, and I do not think Mark does either. Some have said it means he was letting himself be seen, the way God let himself be seen going past in the old stories, in the days of Moses and of Elijah. Some have said it was simply that he was on his way to the other shore, and they were in his way. We cannot be sure. It is one of those little marks that tell you the story is true, because nobody would have made it up.

And now, over the wind and the slapping of the waves, there is a voice.

It is an ordinary voice. That is the first thing to say about it. It is not thunder. It is not a choir. It is the voice of a man the disciples have known for years, who has eaten with them and slept near them and been tired in front of them. And it says, in the plainest words:

Take heart. It is I. Do not be afraid.

In the Greek, the middle of that sentence is two words. I am. It is the same two words that, in the old books of Israel, God uses when he gives Moses his name beside a burning bush. We told that story on another night. I do not want to build too much on it, and the Gospel does not tell us what the disciples heard in it. But I think it is worth noticing that when he says it is I, he may be saying more than that.

And I would also like you to notice the order of the sentences. First, take heart. Then, it is I. Then, do not be afraid. The comfort comes before the explanation. He does not say, here is how I am doing this. He does not say, I am so sorry I was late. He says, take heart, and then he tells them who he is, and that is the whole of the reason for taking heart. It is I. Not it is a ghost. Not it is the storm. It is I.

The boat has gone quiet. The men are staring. And in the middle of the quiet, one of them speaks.

Of course it is Peter. If you know the Gospels at all, you will have guessed. Peter, the big fisherman from Bethsaida, who is brave and impulsive and always the first to open his mouth. Peter, who has not always been right but has never been dull. And what he says, in the middle of that rocking boat, with the water pouring in around his feet, is this:

Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the water.

Take a moment with that. Of all the things he could have said! He could have said, Lord, thank God, help us bail. He could have said, Lord, get in the boat. He could have said nothing, and sat very still. But what he asks is something no sane fisherman would ever ask. He asks to leave the boat. He asks to do what the figure on the water is doing.

I have heard Peter criticised for this, and I have heard him praised. I think both are right. It is certainly rash. It is also, in its way, the most hopeful sentence anyone says in this story. Peter does not ask for the storm to stop. He does not ask for proof. He says, if it is you, and then he asks to come. If it is you, then let me come where you are.

And notice, too, the way he asks. Command me. He does not say, I am going to come. He does not simply climb over the side. He asks to be told. Whatever Peter's faults, he knows that this is not something he can do by being brave. If it is going to happen, it will happen because Jesus says so.

And Jesus says one word.

Come.

[pause]

* * *

[pause]

I want to go slowly here, because this is the part of the story that everybody remembers, and it is a mistake to rush it.

Peter gets out of the boat.

He does not leap. I do not imagine he leaps. I imagine he swings a leg over the side, and sits there for a moment on the edge, holding on to the wood. The boat is bucking beneath him. The water is black and heaving, and he can see it, because the first grey of morning is coming, and it is not at all like a floor. It is exactly as wet as it has always been.

His friends are watching. Not one of them says a word. Perhaps one of them puts out a hand, and thinks better of it.

And Peter puts one foot down on the sea.

The Gospel is very simple. And Peter got out of the boat, it says, and walked on the water and came toward Jesus. That is all. It does not describe how it felt. It does not say what the water was like. But think of it. A man who has spent his whole life on this lake, whose whole trade depends on knowing exactly what the water will and will not do, steps out onto it, and finds that for this one moment it does something it has never done. He takes a step. And another.

I like to think there was a moment, and I think it was a short one, when he was simply happy. Nothing but a man walking toward his friend. The wind in his hair, the cold on his face, no boat under him, and none needed. Walking.

And then the Gospel says: but when he saw the wind, he was afraid.

Hear how plain that is. Not, when the wind got worse. Not, when a big wave came. When he saw the wind. The wind had been there all along. It had been blowing the whole time, from the first step. But for those first steps, he had been looking at Jesus. And at some point, the way it happens with all of us, his eyes slipped, and he saw what was around him. The height of the waves. The noise of the gale. The distance back to the boat, and the distance forward to the figure on the water, and nothing at all underneath him but the lake.

And he began to sink.

I do not think there was a long struggle, or a long speech. The Gospel gives us only three words of what he said, and it is the shortest prayer in the whole of Scripture, I think, and one of the best. It is this:

Lord, save me.

That is all. Not a long confession. Not an excuse. Not, I should not have tried. Not, it was your idea. Just those three words, from a man in the water in the dark. Lord, save me.

And the next line is the one I would like you to remember, if you remember nothing else of tonight. Matthew writes: Jesus immediately reached out his hand and took hold of him.

Immediately.

You remember we noticed the word at the beginning of the story, when Jesus made the disciples get into the boat immediately. Now it comes again. He does not wait. He does not first deliver a lecture. He does not let Peter go down once, to teach him a lesson, and then fish him out. At the very instant that Peter cries out, there is a hand. And I think it was already close. I think he had never been further away than the length of an arm. Peter went down, and the hand was already there.

And only then does he speak.

You of little faith, he says. Why did you doubt?

Now, I have heard this said in a sharp voice, as though it were a scolding. I do not read it that way, and I do not think the Gospel needs us to. It is said with the hand already holding him. It is a question, a real one, from someone who is standing in a gale, with a soaked fisherman clinging to his arm, and who is perfectly calm. Why did you doubt? It is almost tender. It is the way you might say to a child who has fallen off a bicycle after riding well for ten yards: but you were doing so well. What happened?

And notice what Jesus calls him. Little faith. Not no faith. Not false faith. Little. A man who has no faith does not get out of the boat. Peter's faith was small, but it was enough to take him over the side and several steps across a lake. It failed him at the point where it was tested, which is how it is with most faith, most of the time. But it was real, and the Lord says so, and I think the word little is almost a compliment.

They climb into the boat together, the two of them, the wet one and the dry one. I do not suppose Peter said anything. I suppose he sat down in the bottom of the boat and was given a blanket, or whatever passes for one, and one of his friends put a hand on his shoulder.

And the Gospel says, quite simply, that when they got into the boat, the wind ceased.

It ceased. Not it died down a little. Not gradually. It stopped. All night it has been against them, and the moment he steps into the boat, there is no more wind.

Mark adds, and John adds in his own way, that the boat reached the land at once. Where they were going. As though the whole long night of rowing, with all its aching, had been resolved in a moment. John says, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going. I will not try to explain that. I will only say that I think it is a kindness. Nobody gets to the far shore by their own hard rowing alone, and the Gospel makes sure we notice.

And then what do the men in the boat do?

They worship. That is Matthew's word. Those in the boat worshipped him, saying: truly you are the Son of God.

Now think about who is saying this. They are tired fishermen, not scholars. They have walked with him for some time. They have seen a good many wonders. But they have never said this, not like this, not all together. A short while ago, they thought they were seeing a ghost. And now they are on their knees in a boat, in the first light, and the words that come out of them are the words that the Church has used to name him ever since.

Mark, who is always the honest one, adds a note I should not leave out. He says that they were utterly astounded, for they had not understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened. It is not unkind, and it is not an insult. It means that even after the bread, even after the baskets, even after all of it, they had still not put it together. They needed the night on the lake as well. And I find that rather comforting, because it means that the first disciples were just as slow as the rest of us. It took them a while. It takes everybody a while.

[pause]

The sun is up now, and the lake is calm. The sail has been let out, the way you would on a gentle morning, and it hangs there in the light air. The men are tired beyond words. Some of them are already asleep against the side of the boat. I think one of them is humming.

And Peter is sitting very quietly near the front. He is thinking about how it felt to be in the water. About the three words he said. About the hand.

I do not suppose he will ever forget that morning. I do not suppose he will ever stop thinking about it. Years later, he will be the leader of the whole company of disciples, and he will go to Rome, and he will write letters, and in all of them there is a thread of the same thing, I think: a man who has learned, in the water, that he is held. A man who knows from the inside what it is to sink and to be lifted. That is a good thing for a shepherd to know.

But that is for another night. For now, he is simply a tired man in a boat in the morning, with the sun coming up over the hills of the far shore, and his friends around him, and the one who walked on the water sitting beside him. And all of them, for a little while, are not rowing.

[pause]

Let us be quiet for a moment.

Think of the last time you were in the middle of something hard, and could not see the other side. Think of the people who were with you, rowing. Think of the one, if there was one, who came to you when it was nearly morning. And think of what you did when the wind seemed to be everything.

You do not have to do anything about it tonight. The wind will do what it does. The boat is a good boat. The one who sent you across knows the way, and he can see you from where he is, and when the hour comes, he will come.

Some notes for the curious. The story we have told is in the Gospel according to Matthew, chapter fourteen, verses twenty-two to thirty-three, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. A shorter version is in Mark, chapter six, and a shorter one still in John, chapter six. Matthew alone has the part about Peter, and so the Peter of tonight's story is Matthew's. What the disciples say, what Jesus says, the cry of Peter, the command to come, the immediate hand, the question about doubt, the stilling of the wind and the worship in the boat, all follow the text. The weather in detail, the bailing, the talk in the boat, the humming, and every guess about what Peter felt along the way, are ours.

Three things are worth being careful about. First, the distance. John says about twenty-five or thirty stadia, which is three or four miles, and we have said so; the lake's size, thirteen miles by seven, is from the modern maps, and the idea of how the boat was rowed and sailed is a reasonable guess, not something the Gospel says. Second, the watches of the night. The fourth watch was the last part of the night before dawn, and we have used the ordinary Roman reckoning, which Matthew and Mark both seem to use. Third, the words I am. The Greek is the same short phrase used in several places in the Gospels, and in the Greek Old Testament for the divine name. Some readers have heard the echo in this verse and some have thought it only means, it is I. We have named both, and have claimed no more than that.

I should also say that I have not described how Jesus walked, or how the water held him, or how the wind stopped. Matthew does not, and I think the Church is wise to be modest here. And I have not made Peter's sinking into a lesson of my own. The Gospel gives Jesus's one question. I have tried to leave it where it is.

Peter is honoured as a saint in both the Catholic and the Orthodox traditions, and the story of the lake is Scripture for both. Where the two traditions differ about what came afterwards, in Rome and in the Church, we have said nothing, and we leave it alone.

That's the story of Peter and the night on the lake for tonight. And here is a blessing. May you be given, tonight, the grace to put down the oars for a little while, and to rest in a boat that someone else is steering. May the wind go down when it is time. May you find, in the dark hour before morning, that the thing you were afraid of was your friend. And if you should ever find yourself sinking, may the three words come easily to you, and may you find, as Peter did, that the hand was already there. May the Lord who sees from the mountain and comes across the water go with you, tonight and always.

Goodnight, and God bless.

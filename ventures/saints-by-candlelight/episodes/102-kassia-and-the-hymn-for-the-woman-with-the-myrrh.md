It is late at night, and the chapel is very nearly dark.

There are two candles. One is on a stand by the icon of the Mother of God, and the other is on the floor, beside a woman sitting cross-legged on a mat with a wax tablet on her knee. She is not young any more. She has ink on one finger, and a small frown, and she is humming. Very quietly. The same five notes, over and over, as if she is trying a key in a lock.

Behind her, in the doorway, there is a girl of about fourteen in a novice's plain grey dress. The girl is meant to be in bed. She has been meant to be in bed for some time.

"Mother," the girl says at last. "Are you stuck?"

The woman on the mat does not turn round. "Yes," she says. "Come and sit down. You can be stuck with me."

The girl sits. The woman holds up the tablet. On it, scratched into the wax, are a few lines of Greek, and above the words, a scattering of tiny marks like bird footprints in snow.

"I have a woman with a jar of myrrh," says the woman. "And I have her tears. And I have the Lord's feet. What I do not have is the middle."

Last time, if you were with us, we walked through the night in the spring of eight hundred and forty-three, with a little girl and her grandmother and a great many candles, as the holy icons came back into the churches of Constantinople. Tonight we go back a little, and then forward a little, and we meet a woman who was mentioned in that story only in passing. She was mentioned because she was clever, and because she said something sharp to an emperor. Both of those things are true about her. But they are not the most important things.

Let me set the scene.

Constantinople at the beginning of the eight hundreds is the biggest and richest city in the Christian world. It sits on a triangle of land with water on two sides, and it has walls on the third side so thick that you could drive a cart along the top of them, and people sometimes did. It has a great church, Hagia Sophia, the Holy Wisdom, whose dome seems to float. It has markets where you can buy silk and pepper and ivory and very small, very expensive birds in cages. It has an emperor, a palace full of officials with titles so long that I am not going to attempt them, and a great many monasteries, some of them enormous, some of them three old monks and a vegetable patch.

It also has, at this time, a quarrel. You may remember it. Should Christians make and honour pictures of Christ, and of His Mother, and of the saints? The Church had said yes, at a council in seven hundred and eighty-seven. And then in eight hundred and fifteen, a new emperor had said no again, and the icons were taken down a second time. So the girl at the centre of our story grows up in a city where the pictures are officially forbidden, and where a great many people quietly keep one at the bottom of a chest anyway.

Her name, in Greek, is Kassia. You will also hear it said Kassiani, or Kassiane, and one of the old chroniclers calls her Eikasia, because chroniclers are like that. It is a name that comes from a sweet-smelling bark, cassia, a little like cinnamon, the kind of thing you might burn as incense or put into a precious ointment. Which turns out to be a rather good name for her, as you will see.

She was born in the first years of the eight hundreds, probably somewhere around eight hundred and five or eight hundred and ten, in the city itself, to a family that was comfortably off and close enough to the court to be noticed. We do not know her parents' names. We do know, from everything she later wrote, that somebody in that house made sure she was educated, and educated very well. She read the Scriptures, of course. She also read the old Greek poets and philosophers, and she learned to write the old, difficult, elegant kind of Greek that only well-taught people could write.

Now, a girl in Constantinople at this time did not go off to school the way her brothers might. Girls of good families were mostly taught at home, by tutors, or by their mothers, or by a learned relative who had been cornered at a family dinner. So we should picture a young Kassia at a table in a cool room, with a book on a stand and a lamp, and a tutor who is gradually realising that his pupil is going to be cleverer than he is. This happens to tutors more often than they admit.

* * *

The first real glimpse we have of Kassia comes in letters, and the letters are not hers. They are written to her.

You may remember Saint Theodore the Studite, from episode one hundred. He was the abbot of the great monastery of Stoudios in Constantinople, and when the icons were banned again in eight hundred and fifteen, he refused to go along with it, very loudly, and was sent into exile, and spent the rest of his life writing letters from one cold place or another. He wrote hundreds of letters. Among them are a few to a young woman called Kassia.

We do not have her side of the conversation, which is a great pity. But we can tell a good deal from his. He writes to her as someone who is still young, and he is plainly impressed. He praises her learning and the way she writes. He praises her good sense, which he seems to think is beyond her years. And he writes to encourage her in something she has evidently told him: that she wants to give her life to God, as a nun.

I like to picture this. An old abbot, in exile, his monastery scattered, his beloved icons banned, sitting in some draughty room in a far province, and a letter arrives from a girl in the city, a girl who writes better Greek than most of his monks. And he sits down, and he writes back to her properly, as one serious person to another, and tells her that her wish is a good one. He did not have to do that. He was a busy man. He was also, by then, not a well man. But he did.

Theodore died in eight hundred and twenty-six. Kassia would have been perhaps in her late teens. She did not become a nun straight away. Something else happened first. Or at least, the old chronicles say it did.

* * *

Now we come to the story everybody knows. You may remember it from last time. I am going to tell it again, from her side this time, and I am going to tell it honestly, which means telling you first that it comes from chronicles written down about a hundred years later, and that a good many historians doubt it, at least in its details. It is the kind of story that people love so much that it grows. But here it is, the way the old books tell it.

In about eight hundred and thirty, the young emperor Theophilos needed a wife. And the court, so the chronicles say, arranged what was called a bride-show. The most beautiful and well-born young women of the empire were gathered into a great hall of the palace, and the young emperor walked among them, and he was to choose. And to make his choice plain, he carried a golden apple, and he would give it to the one he chose.

I do want to pause on the golden apple. Anyone who knew the old Greek stories, and everybody in that hall did, would have thought at once of a much older golden apple, the one that a Trojan prince gave to a goddess in a beauty contest, which led, as I recall, to an extremely long war. It was perhaps not the most tactful prop. But nobody asked me.

So Theophilos walks down the line. And he stops in front of Kassia. The chronicles say she was very beautiful, and also that he was struck by her. And because he is young and wants to say something clever, and because everybody in that city liked to talk about theology the way some people today talk about football, he says to her something like this:

"Through a woman came the worse things."

He means Eve. He means the old story of the garden, of the fruit, of the beginning of all the trouble. It is a test, perhaps, or a tease, or simply a young man showing off. And Kassia, who has read a great deal and who does not like being talked down to, answers him at once:

"But through a woman also came the better things."

She means Mary. She means that the same human race that fell through one woman's choice was lifted up through another woman's yes. Through the Mother of God came Christ, and through Christ came everything good. It is a perfect answer. It is, in fact, an answer the Fathers of the Church had been giving for centuries. It is also, if you are a young emperor holding a golden apple in front of a hall full of people, rather a lot to take.

And so, the chronicles say, Theophilos turned away from her. He walked on down the line. And he gave the golden apple to a young woman from Paphlagonia called Theodora, who was beautiful and devout and, perhaps, a little quieter. Theodora became empress. You met her last time. She turned out, in the end, to be a saint too, and the one who brought the icons home. So whatever else we say, God was not short of good outcomes that day.

[pause]

What did Kassia think, walking out of that hall without the apple? The chronicles do not tell us. Some people over the centuries have wanted to make it a love story, the girl who lost the emperor and spent her life pining. I do not think there is any evidence for that, and I rather think she would be annoyed by it. What the chronicles tell us next is simply that she went and founded a convent. Which is exactly what she had told Theodore the Studite, years before, that she wanted to do.

So perhaps she walked out of the hall a little red in the face. And perhaps, on the way home, she thought: well. That settles that.

* * *

We do not know exactly when Kassia founded her convent. It may have been in the eight hundred and thirties, while Theophilos was still emperor and the icons were still forbidden, or it may have been after they came back, in eight hundred and forty-three. Later tradition places it on the western side of the city, in the hills inside the walls. We do know that she became its abbess, the mother of a community of women living together under a rule, praying the services day and night, working with their hands, and copying books.

And here, for the rest of our story, I am going to bring in some people we have made up. I want you to see the convent from inside, and the old sources do not give us its daily life, so we will imagine it, carefully, in the spirit of what we know.

The first of our invented people is Agatha.

Agatha arrived at the convent gate one autumn morning, some years after the icons came back, with her uncle, a bundle of clothes, a small icon of Saint Agatha the martyr wrapped in a cloth, and a cold. She was thirteen. Her parents had died. Her uncle was a decent man with six children of his own and a shop that sold rope, and he had heard that the abbess at this convent took in girls who wanted the life, and taught them to read.

The gate was opened by our second invented person, Sister Martha, the portress. Sister Martha was about seventy, had been at the convent longer than anybody but the abbess, and had a face like a walnut and a voice like a gull. She looked Agatha up and down.

"Do you want to be here?" she said.

Agatha thought about it. She was a very honest girl, which would turn out to be both her best quality and an occasional difficulty.

"I want to be somewhere," she said. "And I would like to learn to read."

Sister Martha nodded, as if this was the right answer. "That will do to begin with," she said. "Wipe your nose. Mother Kassia will see you after the midday prayers."

* * *

The convent was not large. A chapel, with a small dome and, by now, its icons back on the walls. A courtyard with a well and a fig tree. A long building where the sisters slept, a kitchen, a bakehouse, a room where they worked at copying and sewing, and a garden at the back with vegetables, herbs and an extremely bad-tempered goose. And a cat. Every convent and monastery in this series seems to have a cat, and I see no reason to break with tradition. This one was large, grey, and called nothing at all, because the sisters could never agree on a name, and so they all called it something different, and the cat answered to none of them.

Mother Kassia, when Agatha met her, was a woman in middle age, plainly dressed like the rest of the sisters, with a quick look and a dry way of speaking. She asked Agatha a few questions. Could she read? No. Could she sew? A little. Could she sing?

Agatha hesitated. "Not well," she said.

"How not well?"

"My uncle says when I sing the dogs in the street join in to help."

Mother Kassia laughed, a short, surprised laugh. "Then you will be a great help in chapel," she said. "The dogs here are very lazy."

And so Agatha stayed.

The days went like this. Before dawn, the bell, and the sisters went into the chapel in the dark for the long morning service. Psalms, readings, hymns. Then work. Agatha was put first in the kitchen, then in the garden, where she lost a battle with the goose on her very first morning, and then, because she was quick, in the copying room, where an older sister began to teach her letters. Prayers again at midday. Food, which was plain. Bread, beans, vegetables, olives, fish on the days when fish was allowed, and on great feasts a little cheese and wine. More work. Evening prayers, as the lamps were lit. And then, after the last short service, silence, and bed.

Agatha learned to read in about a year. She learned that if you stood very still the goose lost interest. She learned that the cat liked to sleep on whatever page you were copying, preferably while the ink was wet. And she learned that she could not sing. Not well, anyway. She could hear the tune perfectly in her head. It was only somewhere between her head and her mouth that it got lost, and came out a little sideways.

* * *

Now I want to tell you some things about Kassia herself, the real woman, because Agatha is going to learn them too.

Kassia did not only write hymns. She also wrote short sayings in verse. Little sharp lines, a bit like proverbs, about wisdom and foolishness, friendship and money and how people behave. A good number of them survive. And many of them begin with the same two words. "I hate."

I am going to give you a few of them, in my own plain words, because they are some of the most human things we have from her. Something like:

I hate the man who agrees with everyone.

I hate the rich man who complains as though he were poor.

I hate silence when it is the time to speak.

You can hear her voice in those, can't you? The girl who would not keep quiet in front of an emperor. These are not holy-sounding words. They are the words of someone who has sat through a great many dinners, and has watched people carefully, and has opinions. There are others about fools, and how hard it is to teach them anything, and about how much better it is to be with wise people even if they are poor. I suspect some of them were written after particularly long meetings.

In our story, Agatha found a copy of these sayings in the copying room one afternoon, and read them aloud to herself, and laughed out loud at one of them, and then looked up to find Mother Kassia standing in the doorway.

"Which one?" said Mother Kassia.

Agatha went pink. "The one about the man who agrees with everyone."

"Ah," said Mother Kassia. "I met him at court. Several times. He was a different man each time, but he always agreed with everyone." She came and looked over Agatha's shoulder. "I wrote those when I was young and cross. Some of them I would write again. Some of them I would make a little kinder."

"Which ones?"

Mother Kassia considered. "Ask me when you are older," she said. "And you are holding that page upside down."

Agatha was not. But she checked anyway. Which, Mother Kassia said, was the beginning of wisdom.

* * *

But what Kassia is remembered for, more than anything, is her hymns.

I should explain a little about Byzantine hymns, because they are not quite like the hymns you may know. In the Greek churches of this time, almost everything in the services was sung. The psalms were chanted, the prayers were chanted, and between and around them were thousands of short poems, each written for a particular day or a particular saint or a particular feast. They have names like troparion and sticheron and kontakion, and I promise I will not test you on them.

Each hymn was sung to one of eight musical modes, called tones, which you might think of as eight different families of melody, each with its own mood. Some are bright, some are solemn, some sound almost like a lament. A hymn-writer had to fit her words to a tone, so that the stresses of the words fell in the right places for the music. And then, over the top of the words, the melody itself was written down with little marks, signs that told the singer whether to go up or down, and how far, and how long to hold the note. Those are the bird footprints we saw on the wax tablet at the beginning.

Kassia wrote both: the words and, it seems, the music. There are something like fifty hymns that have her name on them in the old service books and music manuscripts. Scholars argue about exactly how many of those are really hers. Something over twenty are generally accepted, and the rest are disputed, which is the usual state of affairs with anybody who wrote a long time ago and became famous, because people start putting your name on things. The music survives in manuscripts written down some centuries after her lifetime, so we cannot be sure the melodies we have are exactly the ones she sang. But she is one of the earliest composers in the whole history of Christian music whose work is still sung, by name, today. Which is not bad for a girl who did not get the golden apple.

One of her hymns is sung every Christmas Eve, at the evening service, in the Orthodox churches. It begins with the Roman emperor Augustus. And it goes something like this, in my own plain words:

When Augustus reigned alone upon the earth, the many kingdoms of men came to an end. And when You became man from the pure Virgin, the many gods of the idols were done away. The cities came under one worldly rule, and the nations came to believe in one divine Lordship. The peoples were counted by the decree of Caesar, and we, the faithful, were counted in the name of the Godhead, when You, our God, became man. Great is Your mercy. Glory to You.

You can see what she is doing. She takes a fact from history, the census in Saint Luke's Gospel, Caesar counting everybody, and she turns it over and finds something shining underneath. One emperor, one world, one God becoming one of us. It is the same trick as her answer at the bride-show, really. Eve, and then Mary. Caesar's census, and then God's. Something ordinary, and then something better, through it.

In our story, Agatha learned that Christmas hymn in her first winter. She learned the words very quickly. The tune took longer. A great deal longer. Sister Martha, who led the singing, would stop and look at her, and Agatha would go pink, and they would begin again.

"I can hear it," Agatha said to Mother Kassia one evening, nearly in tears. "I can hear it exactly. It just will not come out right."

Mother Kassia did not say anything comforting straight away. She was not the kind of person who said comforting things straight away. She thought about it.

"Do you know," she said at last, "most of what I write down never comes out the way I hear it either. I hear something, and I scratch it on the wax, and it is never quite as good on the wax as it was in my head. Every hymn I have ever written is a little bit sideways." She paused. "God does not seem to mind. He is used to us arriving a little sideways."

Agatha thought about that for a long time.

* * *

Now we come to Holy Week, and to the hymn.

Every year, in the week before Easter, the Church walks with the Lord through the last days before His Passion. And on the Wednesday of that week, the services remember a particular woman.

There are two Gospel stories behind her. In Saint Matthew's Gospel, a few days before the Passion, the Lord is at a supper in Bethany, and a woman comes in with an alabaster jar of very precious ointment and pours it over His head. The disciples grumble about the waste, and the Lord says, leave her alone, she has done a beautiful thing, she has prepared me for my burial. And in Saint Luke's Gospel, earlier on, there is a supper in the house of a Pharisee, and a woman of the town who is known as a sinner comes in and stands behind Him at His feet, weeping, and wets His feet with her tears, and wipes them with her hair, and kisses them, and anoints them with ointment. And the Lord says that her many sins are forgiven, because she has loved much.

Whether those are the same woman or two different women is something people have read in different ways for a very long time, and I am not going to settle it tonight. But the services of Holy Wednesday put them side by side. The woman who weeps. The woman with the myrrh. And on the same day, the services remember Judas, who sat at the same kind of supper, and went out, and sold the Lord for silver. The woman who gave away something precious, and the man who took money. That is Holy Wednesday.

Kassia wrote a hymn for that day. It is the hymn she is remembered for above everything else. It is sung near the end of the morning service of Holy Wednesday, which most parishes now celebrate on the Tuesday evening, so that the church is dark outside and full of candles inside. It is sung in the eighth tone, slowly, very slowly, and in some of its settings the chanters take a long time over it, each syllable drawn out and turned, so that the whole church goes quiet and simply listens. In Greek-speaking churches, people come specially that night to hear it. It is often simply called the hymn of Kassiani.

And here, in our story, is where we come back to the chapel at night, and the wax tablet, and the five notes.

* * *

It was the Lent of Agatha's third year. She was fourteen by now, and could read and write, and had won an uneasy peace with the goose. She still could not sing quite in tune, but she had stopped minding as much.

She could not sleep that night, which is how she came to be standing in the chapel doorway, watching the abbess sit on a mat with a tablet on her knee, humming.

"I have a woman with a jar of myrrh," said Mother Kassia. "And I have her tears. And I have the Lord's feet. What I do not have is the middle."

She showed Agatha what she had. In our story, which is invented, she has already written the beginning. And I am going to give you the beginning, and indeed the whole hymn, in my own plain words, because the hymn itself is real, and it is hers:

Lord, the woman who had fallen into many sins, sensing that You are God, takes up the part of the women who bring myrrh. Weeping, she brings You sweet ointment before Your burial. And she says: Alas for me, for my night is dark and without a moon, and full of the love of wrong things.

Agatha read it twice. "That's her talking," she said. "The woman. In the middle of the hymn, she starts talking."

"Yes."

"Why does her night not have a moon?"

Mother Kassia smiled a little. "Because when you have done wrong things for a long time," she said, "it feels like that. Like walking about at night with no moon. You cannot see where you are going, and you cannot see your own feet. And then you see a light, a long way off, and you walk towards it, and you are not sure whether you will be let in."

Agatha was quiet. Then she said, "And then what does she say?"

"That," said Mother Kassia, "is the middle."

* * *

They sat together for a long time. The candle by the icon burned down a little. The cat came in, looked at them both with contempt, and lay down on the edge of the mat. Mother Kassia hummed, and scratched, and smoothed the wax flat with the blunt end of her stylus, and scratched again.

At last she said, "What would you say? If you were her. If you were kneeling there with your jar, and you had come in off the street, and everybody in the room was looking at you, and you could not stop crying."

Agatha thought. She was, you remember, a very honest girl.

"I would say, please do not send me away," she said. "And I would say, I am sorry about the tears. They are going everywhere."

Mother Kassia laughed, very softly. And then she stopped laughing, and looked at the tablet, and began to write. And this is the middle, as it is in the real hymn:

Take the springs of my tears, You who draw the water of the sea up into the clouds. Bend down to the groaning of my heart, You who bent down the heavens when You came to us in Your great humility. I will kiss Your spotless feet, and I will wipe them with the hair of my head.

"There," said Mother Kassia. "The tears going everywhere. He made the sea and lifts it up into the clouds. He can manage a few tears."

"And He bent down the heavens," said Agatha slowly, "so He can bend down to her."

"Yes. That is the whole of it, really. He bent down. Everything else is just people noticing."

* * *

Now I must stop and tell you a story about this hymn that you will hear if you ever go to a Greek church on Holy Tuesday evening, and ask the person next to you about Kassiani. It is a lovely story. It is also, almost certainly, not true. It is a legend from much later, and I am telling it to you as a legend.

The story goes that the emperor Theophilos never forgot the girl who had answered him back. And years later, when he was emperor and she was an abbess, he wanted to see her one more time. So he rode out to her convent. And she heard he was coming, and she did not want to see him, so she hid. And she left on her desk the hymn she was writing, unfinished. And the emperor came into the room, and found it empty, and saw the hymn, and read it. And he picked up the pen and added a line of his own. And then he went away. And when Kassia came out of hiding and read what he had written, she left his line in, and finished the rest.

The line he is supposed to have added is the one that comes next in the hymn, about the Lord's feet:

Those feet whose sound Eve heard in Paradise in the cool of the evening, and hid herself in fear.

It is a beautiful line. It goes back to the story in Genesis where Adam and Eve hear God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and they hide. And the woman in the hymn is kneeling at those very same feet, and she is not hiding. She is holding on.

So you can see why people love the legend. The man who said through a woman came the worse things, writing a line about Eve, in a hymn about a woman who comes back. But the historians point out that the legend appears centuries after Kassia's time. That Theophilos was an iconoclast, and was not likely to be riding out to visit iconophile convents. And that the line about Eve is exactly the kind of thing Kassia herself did best, turning a woman's fall into a woman's return, the very same move as her answer at the bride-show. If anybody wrote that line, it sounds very much like her.

In our invented story, there is no emperor. There is just an abbess and a novice and a cat, in a chapel, in the middle of the night. And it was Agatha who said, after a while:

"When Adam and Eve heard Him walking in the garden, they hid. But she does not hide. She comes and finds His feet."

And Mother Kassia looked at her for a long moment. And then she smoothed out a space on the wax, and wrote the line about Eve. And I would like to think, though of course nobody knows, that something like that is how real hymns sometimes get written. Not alone. With somebody sitting beside you who asks the right question.

* * *

The end of the hymn came quickly after that. The way the ends of things sometimes do, once the middle is right. In my own plain words:

Who can count up the great number of my sins, or the depth of Your judgements, my Saviour, You who save souls? Do not turn away from me, Your servant, You whose mercy has no end.

Mother Kassia read it all through, under her breath, from the beginning. Then she hummed it, all of it, once, very quietly, with the cat purring along underneath like a small organ. Then she put down the tablet.

"Bed," she said.

"But how does the tune go?" said Agatha. "The real tune. Will you teach it?"

"Tomorrow," said Mother Kassia. "To everybody. And you will sing it."

Agatha's face fell. "I'll sing it sideways."

"Yes," said Mother Kassia. "So will I. So did she, I expect, that woman, crying all over His feet with her hair coming down. Nobody in that room thought she was doing it properly. He did."

* * *

In our story, the sisters learned the hymn in the days that followed. It is not an easy hymn. It goes up and down, it lingers on some words and hurries over others, and the long notes need a great deal of breath. Sister Martha, who had led the singing in that chapel for forty years, said it was the hardest thing she had ever been asked to sing, and then sang it beautifully, in her gull's voice, which turned out to be exactly right for it.

And on the Tuesday night of Holy Week, when the bell rang for the morning service of Holy Wednesday, and the chapel was full of candles, and the sisters were standing in their places, and the moment came, they sang it.

Agatha sang it too. Not quite in tune. A little sideways, in places. But she did not stop, and she did not drop her voice to a whisper so that nobody could hear her, which is what she would have done two years before. She sang it the way the woman in the hymn came into the Pharisee's house. Knowing she was not doing it properly. Coming anyway.

And when they reached the last line, do not turn away from me, Your servant, You whose mercy has no end, the chapel was very quiet. And somebody, somewhere near the back, was crying a little. It may have been Sister Martha. She always denied it.

[pause]

* * *

And now the candles are getting low, so let me tell you, quietly, how the story ends.

We do not know very much about the rest of Kassia's life. She stayed at her convent. She went on writing, and praying, and being the mother of her sisters. She lived through the return of the icons in eight hundred and forty-three, which we told last time, when Theodora, the girl who did get the golden apple, brought the holy images home. I like to think of the two of them, the empress and the abbess, both by then middle-aged, both having lived through the long years of the ban, both on the same side in the end. Whether they ever met again, nobody knows.

We do not know when Kassia died. Probably somewhere in the eight hundred and sixties, perhaps earlier. We do not know where she is buried. Her convent, like so many of the old buildings of Constantinople, is gone, and nobody can now point to the place where it stood.

But her hymns did not go anywhere. They were copied into the service books, and the service books were copied, and copied again, from one monastery to another, down through the centuries, across the Greek world and into the Slavic lands and beyond. And every year, in the week before Easter, in churches all over the world, in Greek and Arabic and Slavonic and Romanian and English and a great many other languages, on the Tuesday night of Holy Week, the lights are low and the candles are lit and somebody stands up and begins to sing, slowly, Lord, the woman who had fallen into many sins.

Saint Kassia is honoured as a saint in the Orthodox Church, and her feast is kept on the seventh of September. And her hymn for Holy Wednesday is sung in the Byzantine Catholic churches too, which use the same old service books. So the girl who answered back has been heard, every spring, for more than eleven hundred years.

And Agatha, our invented Agatha, grew up, and grew old, and in time became the portress, and opened the gate to other girls with colds and bundles. And when they told her that they could not sing, she would say: good. Neither can I. Come in.

[pause]

There is a reason, I think, why that hymn has lasted when so many others have been forgotten. It is not because it is clever, though it is. It is because nearly everybody, at some point, has felt like the woman in it. Like walking about at night with no moon. Like coming into a room where everyone is looking at you, carrying the one precious thing you have, and not knowing whether you will be sent away.

And the hymn says: He is the one who lifts the sea up into the clouds. He can manage your tears. He bent the heavens down to come to you. He can bend down a little further. You do not have to do it properly. You only have to come.

So if you are carrying something tonight, something you are sorry for, or something you are worried about, or just the tiredness of a long day, you can put it down at His feet. He will not turn away. His mercy has no end. That is the whole of it, really. Everything else is just people noticing.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

Our sources tonight are of three kinds. First, the letters of Saint Theodore the Studite, edited by Georgios Fatouros, which include his letters to a young woman called Kassia, written before his death in eight hundred and twenty-six. Most scholars take these to be our Kassia. Second, the tenth-century chronicles, especially the Chronicle of Symeon the Logothete, which tell the story of the bride-show. And third, Kassia's own hymns and verses, in the Byzantine service books and in modern editions. For the modern study of her, the classic work is Ilse Rochow's book in German from nineteen sixty-seven. In English, there is Antonia Tripolitis's book with translations, and a warm, careful essay by Anna Silvas. Warren Treadgold has written on the bride-shows in general. The services we mention are in the Lenten Triodion and the Festal Menaion, translated by Mother Mary and Kallistos Ware.

What is history. Kassia's birth in Constantinople early in the eight hundreds, her fine education, Theodore's letters to her, her founding of a convent and her life as its abbess, the restoration of the icons in eight hundred and forty-three, her hymns in the service books, including the hymn of Holy Wednesday and the Christmas hymn that begins with Augustus, her collection of verse sayings, and melodies under her name in later music manuscripts. How many of the hymns are really hers is debated. Something over twenty are generally accepted.

What is the report of the old stories. The bride-show, the golden apple and her answer about Eve and Mary are the chronicles' story, and many historians doubt the details. The emperor's visit to her convent and the line he is said to have added is a much later legend. We told it as one. The place of her convent in the west of the city is later tradition.

What we invented. Agatha, Sister Martha, the goose, the cat, the convent's daily life, the night in the chapel and the first singing, and nearly all the words anybody says. The words of the hymns and sayings are our own plain renderings of the Greek, not quotations of a published translation. Whether the woman in Matthew and the woman in Luke are the same is left open, as the Church's readings leave it.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Kassia for tonight. She is remembered on the seventh of September in the Orthodox Church, and her hymn is sung every year on Holy Tuesday evening, at the morning service of Holy Wednesday. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Lord, take the springs of our tears, You who draw the water of the sea up into the clouds. Bend down to the groaning of our hearts, You who bent down the heavens. Do not turn away from us, Your servants, You whose mercy has no end. Goodnight, and God bless.

There is a man standing in front of a king, and he is in the middle of a story, and he is enjoying himself very much.

He has told this story before. You can tell. He knows where to slow down. He knows where to lower his voice. He has just reached the part where a small boy is lying very still on a bed in a little room on a roof, and a prophet has shut the door, and the whole court has gone quiet, and even the guard by the pillar has stopped shifting his weight from one foot to the other.

"And then," says the man, "the boy sneezed. Seven times."

And at that very moment, at the far end of the hall, the door opens, and a woman walks in, with a tall young man beside her. She is dusty from the road. She has the look of someone who has rehearsed a speech for two days and is about to forget all of it. She opens her mouth to speak to the king.

And the storyteller turns round, and stares, and says, "My lord the king, here is the woman."

If you have listened to this show before, you may remember the woman of Shunem. If you have not, here is what you need to know, and I will be quick about it, because it is a good story and she deserves to have it told properly another night.

Shunem was a village in the north of the kingdom of Israel, on the slope of a low hill above the wide valley of Jezreel. It was farming country, wheat and barley and olives, the kind of country where the soil is dark and the sky is enormous and everybody knows how much rain fell last winter, to the nearest thumb's width.

In Shunem lived a woman whom the book calls a great woman. It does not mean she was tall. It means she was a person of substance, with a house and fields and servants, and, as it turns out, a great deal of character. The prophet Elisha used to pass through the village on his travels, and she kept inviting him to dinner, until it became a regular thing. Then she said to her husband, I think this is a holy man of God who keeps passing our way. Let us build him a little room on the roof, with a bed and a table and a chair and a lamp, so that when he comes he can turn in there.

So they did. And Elisha, touched, wanted to do something for her in return. He offered to put in a word for her with the king, or with the commander of the army. And she gave him an answer that I have always loved. She said, I dwell among my own people.

Which is to say: thank you, but no. I have everything I need. I live among my family and my neighbours, in my own village, on my own land. I do not need anyone in the capital to know my name.

Hold on to that sentence. We shall come back to it.

What happened next, briefly. Elisha promised her a son, and she did not believe it, and then she had one. The boy grew, and one hot day at harvest he cried out in the field, my head, my head, and by noon he had died on his mother's lap. And she laid him on the prophet's bed in the little room on the roof, and shut the door, and rode a donkey hard all the way to Mount Carmel, and took hold of Elisha's feet and would not let go. And Elisha came, and prayed, and the Lord gave the boy back. He sneezed seven times and opened his eyes, and his mother took him up and went out.

That is where we left them last time, a mother and a small boy coming down a flight of stairs. Tonight, the boy is a good deal bigger, and the stairs are a long way off, and the woman who dwelt among her own people is about to discover what it is like not to.

Because there is going to be a famine.

Famines in that country were not rare. The land depends on the winter rains. There is no great river to flood the fields every year, the way the Nile does in Egypt, only the sky, and when the sky stays shut for a season, the barley comes up thin, and when it stays shut for two or three, the barley does not come up at all. The Bible is full of famines. Abraham went down to Egypt because of one. Isaac went to live among the Philistines because of another. Jacob's whole family ended up in Egypt because of a third, the great famine of seven years that Joseph saw coming in a dream about cows. And in the Book of Ruth, a man from Bethlehem called Elimelech took his wife Naomi and their two sons to the land of Moab, because there was no bread in the place whose name, rather painfully, means House of Bread.

So when a famine came, people did what people have always done. The ones who could, went somewhere else. They packed up their households and walked to where the rain had fallen, and they lived there as strangers for as long as they had to, and then, if they could, they came home.

The trouble was the coming home. Because while you were away, your land did not stay empty and wait for you, like a dog by the door. Somebody would be farming it. And whether you got it back depended on a great many things, not all of them fair.

* * *

The book begins this part of the story in a slightly unusual way. It does not say, one day Elisha came to Shunem. It says, Elisha had spoken to the woman whose son he had restored to life. Had spoken. As if the story is stepping back a little, to tell you something that happened a while ago. I mention this because it is a small puzzle, and we will come to it in the notes, and for now we will simply picture the scene.

It is evening, perhaps, in the little room on the roof. The lamp is lit. The prophet is sitting on the chair at the table, and the woman of the house is standing in the doorway, because she has come up to see if he needs anything, as she always does. And Elisha says to her, plainly, the way he says most things:

"Arise, and go, you and your household, and stay wherever you can stay. For the Lord has called for a famine, and it will come upon the land for seven years."

The Lord has called for a famine. It is a striking way to put it, as though a famine were a servant who could be summoned and would come. The book does not say why. Other famines in the books of Kings come in the days of kings who had turned away from the Lord, and the Bible is not shy about that, but here it gives no reason at all. It simply gives a warning. And I find it worth noticing that the warning is the kindness. The famine is coming for everyone. Only this household is told in time.

Seven years.

I want you to imagine hearing that. Not seven weeks, or one bad harvest that you could tighten your belt and get through. Seven years. A child who is small when you leave will be nearly a man when you come back. Seven years is long enough for everyone in the village to forget which field was whose.

The book does not tell us what she said. It does not give her a single word in this whole part of the story, until she is standing in front of the king, and even then it only says that she told him. So I am going to give her a little silence on the roof, and then one practical question, because from what we know of her she was a woman of practical questions.

"Where?" she said.

"Wherever you can," said Elisha. And then, perhaps, more gently, "But go."

What she did next the book tells us in one line, and it is a line that I think says a great deal about her. The woman arose, and did according to the word of the man of God.

That is all. No argument. No asking for a sign. The last time a prophet promised her something, a son, she said, please do not lie to your maidservant. She was not a woman who believed things easily. But she had held her dead child on her lap, and she had watched him open his eyes, and after that, I think, when this particular man of God said go, she went.

* * *

Packing a whole household is a large undertaking at the best of times, and this was not the best of times.

I picture it taking some days. The household would have been the woman and her son, and whoever else lived under that roof and ate at that table, servants and their children, perhaps an old relative. The book does not mention her husband at all in this chapter. He was old already in the first story, and perhaps he had died, or perhaps he came along and simply had nothing to say, which, if you remember him from last time, would not have been out of character. The book does not tell us, and we will not pretend to know. In this story it is her household, and she is the one who leads it.

They took what could be carried. Grain, while there was still some. Oil. The good cooking pot. Blankets, and the boy's things, and whatever money there was. Donkeys, loaded until they gave the particular sideways look that donkeys give when they feel they are being taken advantage of.

And there was one old servant, who I am going to imagine was the same man who had saddled the donkey for her on the day she rode to Mount Carmel, and who had been told on that occasion not to slow down unless she said so. He had not entirely forgiven her for that ride, and he made a point of mentioning it at least once a year.

"Seven years," he said, tying a rope. "Among the Philistines."

"Among whoever has bread," she said.

"The Philistines have bread," he admitted. "They also have a great many opinions about pork."

"Then you will not eat pork," she said, "and you will not discuss it."

The boy, who was old enough now to be useful and young enough to think the whole thing was an adventure, climbed the outside stair to the roof one last time, to the little room that had been built for the prophet. The bed was there. The table and the chair. And the lamp, standing in its niche in the wall. He had been told, many times, that this was the room where he had died and been given back, though he did not remember any of it. All he remembered, he said later, was that he had woken up with a headache and a strong urge to sneeze, and that his mother had been crying and laughing at the same time, which was alarming.

He stood in the doorway of the empty room and looked at the lamp.

"Should we take it?" he called down.

His mother looked up at him from the courtyard for a long moment.

"No," she said. "Leave it where it is. It belongs to the room."

And then they set off, down the hill, past the fields that were already brown at the edges, and the boy turned round at the bend in the road to look back at the house, and the woman did not, because she had decided not to, and she was a woman who kept her decisions.

* * *

The book says she went with her household and stayed in the land of the Philistines for seven years. It does not say which town.

The Philistines lived along the coast to the south and west, in the flat country between the hills and the sea. They had five great cities, Gaza and Ashkelon and Ashdod on or near the shore, and Ekron and Gath a little way inland, and a great many villages besides. They were old enemies of Israel. You may remember a large Philistine called Goliath. But by this time, a good while after David, the Philistines were mostly just neighbours, the kind of neighbours you are suspicious of in principle and buy olive oil from in practice.

Why the Philistines? The book does not say. My own guess, and it is only a guess, is that the coast was the sensible place to go. Land beside the sea can sometimes catch a little more of the winter weather than the hills behind it, and there were ports there, and ships, and grain that could come in from elsewhere. And it was not so very far. You could walk there in a few days. Isaac had done the same thing, long, long before, in a famine of his own. He had gone to live among the Philistines at a place called Gerar, and sown fields there, and dug wells, and quarrelled about them, and grown rich. So there was a family precedent.

Picture, then, a household of Israelites arriving in a Philistine town. It is a town of mud brick and whitewash and narrow lanes, with a market by the gate, and the smell of fish, which is a smell they are not used to in Shunem. The people speak differently. They dress differently. Their gods are different, and their shrines are on the corners, and our household walks past them with their eyes politely on the ground.

And here, I think, is the first great change in the woman of Shunem's life since the day her son was given back. Because she was a great woman. In Shunem, everybody knew her. Everybody knew her husband's family, and her fields, and her house with the little room on the roof. When she walked down the street, people greeted her by name. She had said to a prophet, with a perfectly straight face, I dwell among my own people.

And now she dwelt among other people. Nobody in this town knew who she was. She was not a great woman here. She was a foreign woman with a tall boy and an old servant and some donkeys, renting a house at the end of a lane, paying too much for barley, and having to point at things in the market because she did not know their names.

I do not think that was easy for her. I think it may have been one of the hardest things she ever did, harder in its own way than the ride to Carmel. The ride to Carmel took a day. This took seven years.

* * *

But people are kind, sometimes, in unexpected places, and I would like to give her a neighbour.

Across the lane, I am going to imagine, there lived a Philistine widow who was a dyer. You could tell she was a dyer because her hands were blue, and on bad days purple, and on very bad days a sort of green that she said was intended. She had a loud voice and a large family of grandchildren and a habit of leaning on the wall of the woman's courtyard in the evenings and offering advice about everything.

The book does not mention her. She is our own invention. But somebody must have shown the woman of Shunem which well to draw water from, and which baker was honest, and how to say thank you in the local way, and I would like it to have been someone like this.

"You are from up in the hills," the dyer said, on the first evening.

"From Shunem," said the woman.

"Never heard of it," said the dyer, cheerfully. "Is it nice?"

The woman of Shunem thought about that for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "It is very nice. There is a valley below it, and in the spring the whole valley is green, and you can see all the way to the mountains on the other side. And our house is on the slope. And there is a room on the roof."

"What is in the room on the roof?"

"A bed. And a table, and a chair, and a lamp."

"Who sleeps in it?"

"A man of God, when he comes."

The dyer considered this. "Does he pay?"

"No," said the woman of Shunem. "He does not pay. That is the point."

The dyer looked at her for a moment, the way people look at someone who has said something they do not quite understand but suspect may be important, and then she said, "Well. You had better come and eat with us tomorrow. You look as if you have been eating your own cooking and worrying."

And so, over seven years, a strange and quiet friendship grew up across a lane in a Philistine town, between a woman who would not eat certain things and would not bow at certain shrines and said her prayers to the God of Israel in the early morning, and a woman with blue hands who thought all of that was very odd and said so, often, and kept inviting her to dinner anyway.

I do not want to make this sound sweeter than it was. Strangers are not always welcome, and a famine makes everyone careful with bread. There must have been days when the market price went up only for her, and days when the children in the lane said things about Israelites that their mothers had said at home. There were certainly days when she lay awake and thought about her fields.

But there is a line in the Law of Moses that I have always found very beautiful. It comes in a list of rules about justice, about not taking bribes and not twisting the case of the poor, and then it says: you shall not oppress a stranger. You know the heart of a stranger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt.

You know the heart of a stranger. The woman of Shunem had known that line all her life, I expect, the way you know lines you hear often. And now, for seven years, she learned what it meant.

* * *

The boy grew up in that town. That is the thing about seven years. A child who leaves at, let us say, nine or ten, and we do not know his age, the book does not tell us, comes back at sixteen or seventeen, with a deep voice and large feet and a fondness for fish that his mother finds faintly disloyal.

He learned the Philistine way of speaking, and spoke it better than she did, and was sent to the market for that reason. He learned to mend nets, a little, from one of the dyer's grandsons, though there are not many nets in Shunem and he would never need it. He learned, as boys do, to be embarrassed by his mother.

And every so often, I like to think, he would climb up onto the flat roof of their rented house in the evening, and look east, towards the hills where the sun came up, and try to remember the valley.

"Do you remember the room on the roof?" he asked his mother once.

"Of course."

"I don't. Not really. I remember the stairs. And I remember the lamp."

"That is a good thing to remember," she said.

News came, sometimes, along the road. Travellers, merchants, people going down to Egypt or coming back. The rains had failed again in the hills. The rains had come a little, but too late. The rains had failed. Things were very bad in Samaria. The book of Kings does tell, elsewhere, of terrible hunger in Elisha's days, of a siege of the capital so harsh that we will not tell it at bedtime. The household in the Philistine town heard these things and was grateful, and frightened, and grateful again, and prayed for the people they had left behind.

And the old servant counted. Every year at the time the barley should have been harvested at home, he would say, at supper, "That is two," or "That is five," and nobody would answer him, and he did not need them to.

One year, he said, "That is seven."

* * *

They came home in the spring, I think, though the book does not say. It would be the sensible time to travel, and the sensible time to arrive, with a harvest ahead of you instead of a winter.

The dyer cried, and said she was not crying, it was the dye, and gave them bread for the road and a small jar of something purple that she said was a present and that nobody ever found a use for. The boy, who was not a boy any more, shook hands with the grandson who had taught him about nets. And the household loaded the donkeys, rather fewer donkeys than they had come with, and set off up into the hills.

And as they walked, the land changed around them. The fields were green again. The famine was over. In the valleys there were people out working, and in the villages there were children, and on the hillsides there were vines that had been pruned that year by somebody who expected grapes.

And at last they came over the shoulder of the hill, and there was the great valley of Jezreel spread out below them, all green, all the way to the mountains on the other side. Exactly as she had said it was. And there on the slope was Shunem. And there, on the edge of Shunem, was her house.

There was smoke coming from its chimney. There were people in its fields. And they were not her people.

The book tells us what she found in only a few words, by telling us what she asked for. She went to appeal to the king for her house and for her land. So we know that both had been taken. It does not say who had them. It might have been a neighbour who had moved in when the house stood empty and had been farming the fields for years and had come to think of them as his. It might have been the crown, because in some places a king could take land that had been abandoned. It might have been someone who had simply bought it from someone who had no right to sell it. The book does not say, and so I am not going to give you a villain. It is quite possible that whoever was living in her house was not a wicked person at all. Only someone who had got used to it.

And this was a serious matter, more serious than it might sound to us. In Israel, land was not supposed to be simply bought and sold and lost. It was an inheritance. The Law said that the land belongs to the Lord, and that the people are like guests and tenants on it, and that each family's portion should stay in that family, generation after generation. You may remember a man named Naboth, who had a vineyard next to the palace of King Ahab. The king wanted it for a vegetable garden, and offered him a fair price, and Naboth said, the Lord forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers. That did not end well for Naboth, and it did not end well for Ahab either, but the point is that Naboth was right. A family's land was not just property. It was where they belonged.

So the woman of Shunem stood on the hill above her own house, with smoke coming from her own chimney, and somebody else's washing on her own roof, and she understood something rather bitter. She had said to the prophet, all those years ago, I dwell among my own people. I do not need anyone to speak to the king for me. And now she was going to have to go and speak to the king herself.

The old servant, standing beside her, did not say anything. This was, I think, the one occasion in his life when he did not.

"Well," she said, after a while. "We are going to Samaria."

* * *

Samaria was the capital of the northern kingdom, built on a hill a long day's walk south of Shunem, or two at a donkey's pace. It had walls and towers, and a palace on the top, and the palace had a hall where the king sat to hear his people, because in those days that was one of the things a king was for. Anyone could come and cry out to the king for justice. Whether the king listened was another matter.

The book does not tell us which king it was. It just says the king. Many readers have thought it was Jehoram, the son of Ahab, who was king in much of Elisha's time, but the book does not name him here, and the stories of Elisha in these chapters are not always in strict order, so we shall leave him as the book leaves him. The king.

Now here is where the story does something that makes me smile every time.

While the woman of Shunem was on the road to Samaria, rehearsing her speech, the king, up in his palace, was having a conversation. And the person he was having it with was Gehazi.

You may remember Gehazi. He was Elisha's servant, sharp, quick, a little too fond of the sound of his own voice. He was there in the first story of the woman of Shunem. It was Gehazi who pointed out to Elisha that she had no son and her husband was old. It was Gehazi who tried to push her away when she grabbed the prophet's feet. It was Gehazi who ran ahead with the prophet's staff and laid it on the child, and came back and said, the child has not woken. He was there for all of it.

And the king, the book says, was talking with Gehazi, the servant of the man of God, saying: "Tell me, please, all the great things that Elisha has done."

I find this delightful. Here is a king, with a kingdom to run and enemies on every border, who has, for one afternoon at least, set it all aside and said to a prophet's servant, tell me the stories. Tell me all of them. Kings in the Bible are often rather alarming people, and this king, elsewhere, can be very alarming indeed. But here he is just a man who wants to hear about wonders. I think we can all understand that. It is, after all, more or less what you are doing now.

So Gehazi told him. He told him about the widow and the jars of oil that did not run out until there were no more jars. He told him about the bitter stew at Gilgal, and the flour thrown into the pot. He told him, perhaps, about the general from Damascus who washed seven times in the Jordan, though that is a story with a hard ending for Gehazi himself, and we shall come to that in the notes. And then, because he knew how to tell a story and he had saved the best for last, he began to tell the king about the woman of Shunem, and the little room on the roof, and the boy in the barley field, and the ride to Carmel, and the door that was shut, and the prophet stretched out on the child, and the warmth coming back into the small body, and the boy who sneezed seven times and opened his eyes.

And while he was telling the king how Elisha had restored the dead to life, says the book, behold, the woman whose son he had restored to life came crying out to the king for her house and for her land.

Behold. The book does not often say behold for no reason. It is the book's way of nudging you with its elbow. Look at this. Look at the timing.

[pause]

I would like to tell you what Gehazi's face did. The book does not say. But I imagine the whole court turned to see who had interrupted the king's story, and Gehazi turned with them, rather annoyed, because he had been just at the good part. And then he saw her.

He had not seen her for seven years. She was older, and tired, and dusty from the road, and she was not dressed like a great woman any more, but like someone who had been living as a stranger in a Philistine town, which she had. And beside her was a tall young man with large feet whom Gehazi had last seen lying on a bed with his eyes shut.

But he knew them. Of course he knew them. And he said, in the words the book gives him:

"My lord the king, this is the woman, and this is her son, whom Elisha restored to life."

There is a moment, I think, after that, when nobody says anything at all.

The king looks at the woman. The woman, who has rehearsed her speech for two days on the road, has entirely forgotten it. The young man looks at the floor, because he has just been introduced to the king of Israel as a person who was once dead, and there is no good way to stand while that happens.

And then the king asks her. The book says simply that the king asked the woman, and she told him.

She told him. That is all we are given. We do not hear her words, and I am not going to invent them, because I think the book leaves them out on purpose. We have just heard the whole story, from Gehazi, in the king's hall. We do not need to hear it again. What matters is that the king has now heard it twice, once as a wonderful tale about a prophet, and once from the mouth of the woman it happened to, standing in front of him, with her son beside her, asking for her home.

It is one thing to enjoy a story. It is another when the story walks in through the door and asks you for something.

* * *

And the king, to his very great credit, did not hesitate.

He called one of his officials, the book says. The Hebrew word means a court officer, someone in the king's own service, and the king gave him an order in front of everyone. And here are his words, as the book gives them:

"Restore all that was hers, and all the produce of the field from the day that she left the land until now."

I want you to notice how generous that is. He did not say, give her back her house. He did not say, give her back her house and her fields. He said all that was hers, and then he went further. All the produce of the field. Every harvest that had been taken from her land while she was away, or its worth, from the day she left until that very day, was to be paid back to her.

He did not have to do that. Nobody would have blamed him if he had simply given her the land and let the past be the past. But he did. Perhaps because he had just been listening to stories of what the God of Israel had done for her, and felt that a king of Israel could hardly do less. Perhaps because she was standing there, and so was her son. Perhaps, and I like this best, because he had just been reminded, by a servant who did not mean to remind him of anything, that the story of her life was not finished, and that it was his turn to be in it.

Whatever the reason, the book does not tell us anything more. It does not tell us what happened to the people in her house, or how the officer went about his business, or what Gehazi said afterwards, or whether the old servant made a remark about the length of the queue. The story simply stops, there in the hall, with the order given. Six verses. That is the whole of it.

And I think it is one of the most quietly hopeful little stories in the whole of the books of Kings. Nobody is struck down. No fire falls from heaven. Nothing happens that could not happen on an ordinary afternoon in any court in the world. A woman comes home. A king listens. A wrong is put right, with interest.

And yet the book wants us to see that it was not just luck. A prophet warned her in time. A servant happened to be telling her story at the very moment she walked in. A king happened to be in the mood to listen. You can call that coincidence if you like. The book calls it behold.

[pause]

I like to imagine the journey home.

They walked back north from Samaria, the woman and her son and the old servant and the donkeys, and the king's officer rode along with them, rather grandly, with a document. And they came over the shoulder of the hill again, and there was the valley, green all the way to the mountains, and there was the house.

The people who had been living there had gone, or were going. I hope they went without too much grief. I hope somebody found them somewhere else to live. The book does not say, and I would rather not leave anyone out in the cold, even people I have invented.

And the woman of Shunem walked into her own courtyard for the first time in seven years.

It was different, of course. Things had been moved. Someone had built a new oven, in the wrong place. The fig tree had grown. But it was hers. The walls were the walls she knew. And on the outside of the house there was a stair, going up to the roof.

The young man went up it first. He took the steps two at a time, the way he used to when he was small. And at the top he pushed open the door of the little room, and stood there in the doorway, and was quiet for a long while.

His mother came up behind him, more slowly, and stood at his shoulder.

The room was dusty. Somebody had been using it to store onions. But the bed was still there, and the table, and the chair. And in its niche in the wall, where nobody had thought to move it, because it was only an old clay lamp and not worth anything to anyone, was the lamp.

"It's still here," he said.

"It belongs to the room," she said.

And that evening, when the onions had been taken down and the floor had been swept and the bed had a clean blanket on it, in case a man of God should ever pass that way again, she filled the lamp with oil and lit it, and set it back in its niche, and went downstairs to supper in her own house, among her own people, in her own village, on her own land.

[pause]

That is nearly all the Bible tells us about the woman of Shunem. After this, she does not appear again. We never learn her name. We never learn what became of her son, whether he farmed his father's fields, whether he married, whether he ever went back to visit the dyer's grandchildren in the town by the sea. We never learn whether Elisha came to stay in the little room again, though I like to think he did, at least once, and found the lamp lit.

Elisha himself lived on for a good many years, through the reigns of several kings. When he died, at last, he was buried, and the book tells one more strange and wonderful story about his grave that you may hear on another night. The Orthodox Church remembers him on the fourteenth of June, and so does the Catholic Church in the Roman Martyrology. The Carmelites, who take their name from the mountain where the woman of Shunem found him, honour him too, alongside Elijah.

And the village of Shunem is still there, more or less, on the slope of the same hill above the same valley. It is usually identified with the village called Sulam today, and it is a small place, and if you stand there in the spring you can see the valley green below you, all the way to the mountains on the other side.

[pause]

I think, if I had to say what this little story is about, I would say it is about home. About leaving it, and being a stranger, and coming back to find that home has moved on without you. And about the fact that the God of Israel, who gave this woman a son and gave him back to her, did not forget her in a Philistine town for seven years, or on a hill above a house with someone else's smoke coming out of the chimney.

She never asked for much. She told a prophet she did not need anyone to speak to the king for her. And in the end, she did not need anyone to. Her own story spoke for her, carried in through the door just ahead of her, on someone else's lips.

So rest now. The lamp is low. Somewhere on a hill above a valley there is a little room on a roof, swept and quiet, with a bed and a table and a chair, and a small clay lamp burning in the wall, in case anyone should come.

Now, the notes, for the curious.

Tonight's story is the Second Book of Kings, chapter eight, verses one to six. In the Greek numbering the Orthodox Church uses, this is the Fourth Book of Kingdoms. It is Scripture in the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and in Protestant Bibles too. The earlier story of the same woman, the room on the roof and her son restored to life, is in chapter four, verses eight to thirty-seven, and we told it in an earlier episode.

What we kept from the text: Elisha's words to the woman whose son he had restored to life, arise and go, you and your household, and stay wherever you can, for the Lord has called for a famine and it will come upon the land for seven years; her doing according to the word of the man of God; her going with her household to the land of the Philistines for seven years; her return at the end of the seven years; her going to cry out to the king for her house and her land; the king talking with Gehazi, the servant of the man of God, and asking him to tell all the great things Elisha had done; Gehazi telling how Elisha had restored the dead to life; the woman coming in at that moment; Gehazi's words, my lord the king, this is the woman and this is her son whom Elisha restored to life; the king asking her and her telling him; and the king appointing an officer, with the order to restore all that was hers and all the produce of the field from the day she left the land until now.

What is uncertain, or a reading. The book says Elisha had spoken to her, and some commentators take that as a flashback, which might make this famine the same one that is mentioned in chapter four, verse thirty-eight, at Gilgal. Others think it is a different famine. We have not settled it. The king is not named, and while many have thought of Jehoram son of Ahab, that is an identification, not something the text says. There is also a well-known puzzle about Gehazi. At the end of chapter five, after the story of Naaman, Gehazi takes silver he should not have taken, and is struck with Naaman's skin disease. Yet here he is in chapter eight, chatting with the king. Some readers think this scene happened earlier and is told out of order; some think he spoke to the king from a distance; some simply note that the stories of Elisha were gathered as stories and are not always arranged by date. The book does not say, and we have left it open.

The Letter to the Hebrews, chapter eleven, says that women received their dead by resurrection, and many readers have taken that as a reference to this woman and the widow of Zarephath. That is a reading of the letter, and a good one.

What we invented: the Philistine town, the dyer with blue hands, the old servant and his counting, the boy's age, every word the woman and her son say, the people living in her house, and the lamp left in the room. The book gives the woman not a single word of her own in these six verses, and we have tried to leave the moment in the king's hall as silent as the book does.

For further reading: the six verses take less than a minute to read. Read them straight after chapter four, and listen for the echo of I dwell among my own people.

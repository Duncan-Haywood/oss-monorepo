Imagine a forest in the hills of eastern France, about fourteen hundred and thirty years ago.

It is the end of a grey afternoon. The beech trees are very tall and very old, and the ground under them is soft with last year's leaves. There is no road. There is barely a path. Somewhere a stream is talking to itself.

And climbing up through the trees, with a stick in one hand and a book under his arm, is a tall Irishman of about fifty, with the front of his head shaved from ear to ear in the old Irish way, which made him look, to the local people, rather like a man whose hair had decided to start halfway back.

Behind him, out of breath, comes a boy.

They reach a great rock in the side of the hill. In the rock there is a cave. It is dark inside, and it smells very strongly of something that is not a person.

The boy stops. The boy says, in a very small voice, "Father. I think somebody lives here."

And from the dark at the back of the cave, somebody large turns over, and opens one eye.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is Saint Columbanus, the Irish monk who walked into the forests of Burgundy, from the Life written by Jonas of Bobbio about twenty-five years after he died, and from Columbanus's own letters and sermons; the bear, the spring and the other wonders are Jonas's report, and we will tell them that way, and the boy's grumbling and most of the small conversations are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

Let me take you first to Ireland, in the middle of the five hundreds.

Ireland at that time had no towns, not in the way we would mean it. It had no coins, very few roads, and an enormous number of kings, most of them in charge of an area you could walk across before lunch. It had cattle, which were the main way of being rich. It had poets, who were the main way of being famous, and who could, if you annoyed them, compose a poem about you so insulting that people believed it might bring you out in blisters.

And it had monasteries. In the hundred years since Saint Patrick, the Irish had taken to the monastic life the way some people take to gardening, which is to say completely, and with strong opinions. There were monasteries on islands in lakes, and on islands in the sea, and on rocks so far out in the Atlantic that you wonder how anyone ever got the building stones there. The monks prayed, and studied, and copied books, and fasted rather a lot, and argued about grammar, and some of them were the best Latin scholars in the whole western world, which was a surprise to everybody, because the Romans had never even bothered to conquer Ireland.

Our saint was born into this world, in Leinster, in the east of Ireland, around the year five hundred and forty. His name, Columbanus, means something like little dove, which, as we will see, is one of the great mismatches between a name and a person in the history of the Church. He was many things. He was holy, and brave, and clever, and he loved God with his whole heart. But he was not, at any point in his life, very much like a dove.

Jonas, who wrote his life, tells us that before he was born, his mother had a dream. She dreamed that a sun rose out of her heart and gave light to the whole world. Which is the sort of dream mothers of saints have in the old lives, and which I imagine most mothers privately have anyway, without anyone writing it down.

The boy grew up clever and good-looking, and Jonas, who is a monk writing for monks, says that the good looks were a problem, because the young women of the district noticed them. Columbanus was worried about this. So he went to visit a holy woman who lived alone as a hermit, which was a thing some Irish women did, and asked her what he should do.

And she told him, with what I imagine was great firmness, that she had gone out into the wild places to escape the world, and that he, as a young man, was in much more danger than she was, and that if he wanted to be safe, he should leave home.

So he did. Or rather, he tried to.

When he told his mother, she was so upset that she lay down across the doorway of the house, on the floor, so that he could not get out without stepping over her. And Jonas says that he did step over her. He asked her not to grieve, and he said goodbye, and he stepped over his own mother and walked away, and as far as we know, he never saw her again in this life.

I will be honest. It is a hard story. Jonas tells it as a sign of how completely Columbanus gave himself to God, and the old writers loved it for that, and it echoes the words of the Lord in the Gospel about leaving father and mother. But I do not think we are meant to take from it that stepping over our mothers is a good idea in general. I think we are meant to see a young man who knew what God was asking of him, and did it, and found it very hard. And I like to think his mother, later, was proud of him, although the old book does not say.

[pause]

He went first to a teacher called Sinell, who lived on an island called Cleenish, in Lough Erne, in the north, and studied the Scriptures with him. And then he went to the great monastery of Bangor, on the shore of Belfast Lough, where the abbot was a famous saint called Comgall.

Bangor was strict. I want to give you an idea of how strict. The monks there ate once a day, in the evening. They ate bread, and vegetables, and sometimes a little milk. They sang the psalms through the night in turns. In the rule Columbanus later wrote for his own monks, which was surely shaped by what he learned there, even a small fault, like forgetting to say amen at the blessing before a meal, had a fixed penance, written down. And Columbanus, by all accounts, loved it. He stayed at Bangor for many years, and he became one of its teachers, and he grew into a man who was very learned and very disciplined and very, very determined.

And then, when he was somewhere in his forties, he felt that God was asking something else of him.

The Irish had an idea, in those days, that the hardest and holiest thing a monk could do was to go into exile for Christ. Not because anybody had sent you away. But because you chose to leave the country you loved, and your family, and your own language, and go somewhere you would always be a stranger, for the love of God. They called it peregrinatio, which means pilgrimage, but a pilgrimage with no return ticket. You did not go to a holy place and come home. You went, and you kept going, and the road was the point.

Columbanus asked Comgall for permission to go. And Comgall, Jonas says, did not want him to go at all, because he was the best teacher at Bangor and Comgall did not want to lose him. But in the end, Comgall gave his blessing. And Columbanus chose twelve companions, which is a number with a good Gospel ring to it, and among them was a young man called Gall, whose own story we told in an earlier episode, with a different bear. And in about the year five hundred and ninety, they took a boat across the sea.

* * *

They landed, probably in Brittany, and walked into the kingdom of the Franks.

Now, the Franks at this time were Christians, at least officially. They had been baptised, their kings had been baptised, there were bishops in all the old Roman cities. But it would be fair to say that some parts of the Christian life were going better than others. The kings were very busy murdering each other, which they did with great energy and in a spirit of family togetherness. The bishops were often noblemen who had been given the job as a reward. And in the countryside, Jonas says, the faith had grown cold, which is a gentle way of putting it.

Into all this walked thirteen Irishmen with strange haircuts, strange Latin, strange ideas about the date of Easter, and long walking sticks. And they began to preach. Jonas says that wherever they went, people noticed. They noticed that these men were poor and wanted nothing. They noticed that they were cheerful. They noticed that they were kind to everyone and frightened of no one. And word of them reached the king.

Jonas says the king was Sigebert, but Sigebert had been dead for some years by then, so it was probably one of his relatives, Guntram or Childebert. This is the kind of mistake that happens when you write a book fifty years after the events and the Frankish royal family has a great many people in it with similar names and very short life expectancies. Whichever king it was, he liked Columbanus. And he offered him land, and money, and a comfortable place to settle.

And Columbanus said, thank you very much, but no.

He did not want comfort. He had not left Ireland to be comfortable. What he wanted, he told the king, was somewhere wild and quiet, a desert, the old monks would have said, where he and his brothers could live in poverty and pray.

So the king gave him one. In the hills of the Vosges, in the far east of Burgundy, there was a great wild forest, with very few people in it and a great many animals. And in that forest, on a little hill, there were the ruins of an old Roman fort, from the days when the Roman army had kept watch there. It was called Annegray. The walls were falling down. The roof, if there had ever been one, was gone. And there was, as far as anyone could see, nothing to eat for miles.

Columbanus looked at it, and was delighted.

[pause]

Now, I want to be fair to the twelve companions. We are not told what they thought. But I imagine at least one of them looked at the ruined fort, and the dark forest, and the thin grey sky, and thought, quietly, that Bangor had at least had a kitchen.

They set to work. They cleared the brambles out of the ruins. They patched up a little building to pray in, and some huts to sleep in. They dug the ground to plant. And while they were waiting for anything to grow, they were very hungry indeed.

Jonas tells us that for days at a time they had nothing to eat but the herbs of the field and the bark of trees. Which is not a diet I would recommend, and which even the old Irish monks, who were very hard to impress on the subject of fasting, seem to have found a bit much. And then, he says, one day, a cart came up through the forest, loaded with food.

It had been sent by a neighbouring abbot, a man Jonas calls Carantoc, who ran a monastery some distance away. Jonas says that Carantoc had been moved by God to think of the strangers in the forest, and to send them bread and supplies, and that the cart arrived at exactly the moment when the brothers had begun to wonder how much longer they could go on.

I would like to stop here and say a word for Carantoc. He does not get much attention in the story. He is not the saint. He has no bears. But I think there is something very holy about a man who sits in his own monastery, in his own warm refectory, and suddenly thinks: those Irishmen up in the forest, I wonder if they have enough to eat. And then, instead of only wondering, sends a cart. A great deal of the history of the Church has been kept going by people like Carantoc. And I like to think that when the cart came over the hill, thirteen very thin monks in a ruined fort said a prayer for him that was quite unusually heartfelt.

* * *

Columbanus was the abbot of Annegray. He was in charge of the monks, and their prayer, and their work, and their food, when there was any. But every so often, Jonas tells us, he would go away by himself, deeper into the forest, to be alone with God.

This was an old habit, older than the Irish. Jesus himself, in the Gospels, often went off alone to a lonely place to pray, and the monks of Egypt and the deserts had copied him, and the Irish had copied the monks of Egypt. The idea was simple. The community was good, and necessary, and holy. But sometimes, a man needed to go and sit somewhere with no one else in it, and no noise, and listen.

And on these journeys, Jonas says, Columbanus would usually take one companion with him: a boy called Domoal.

We know almost nothing about Domoal. Jonas gives us his name, and tells us he was a servant, or a young attendant, who went with the abbot to carry things and to fetch things and to keep him company. I have decided, for tonight, that he was about thirteen. I have also decided that he did not especially enjoy walking uphill. This is our own invention. But I think it is the kind of thing that might well have been true, because I have met boys of thirteen, and I was one once.

So picture them, on that grey afternoon at the beginning of our story. Columbanus striding ahead through the beech trees, with his staff and his book of psalms, saying nothing, because he is already praying. And behind him, Domoal, carrying a bag with some bread in it, and a cloak, and a little flask, and saying a great deal.

"Is it much further, Father?"

"A little."

"You said a little an hour ago."

"It was further then."

"Father, what are we looking for?"

"A place to pray."

"We have a place to pray. We built it. It has a roof. Nearly."

Columbanus does not answer this. He has seen something, up ahead, through the trees: a great grey rock in the side of the hill, taller than a house, with ferns growing out of its cracks. And in the bottom of the rock, a dark opening. A cave.

He stops. He looks at it for a long time. And he smiles, which, if you know Columbanus at all, is something you notice, because he did not do it very often.

"Here," he says.

* * *

Now. Let me tell you what Jonas says happened next, and then let me tell you how I imagine it.

Jonas says, very simply, that Columbanus found a cave in a rock, far from the monastery, and that when he went into it, he found that it was the den of a bear. And he told the bear to leave, and not to come back. And the bear went, gently, and never came back.

That is all. One or two sentences in an old Latin book. But I think we can let the scene breathe a little.

They step into the mouth of the cave. It is cooler inside, and it smells of earth, and leaves, and old bones, and very strongly of bear. And Domoal, who has been talking all afternoon, suddenly stops talking, which is the most surprising thing that has happened all day.

"Father," he whispers, "I think somebody lives here."

And at the back of the cave, somebody large turns over in the leaves. And opens one small brown eye.

It is a brown bear. Not, perhaps, the biggest bear in the forest, but quite big enough for a cave, and certainly quite big enough for a boy of thirteen. It lifts its head. It looks at the tall Irishman in the doorway of its house. And it makes a low, rumbling sound, which is not a growl exactly, but is the sort of noise a person might make if they had been woken from a nap by somebody ringing the doorbell.

Domoal does not move. Domoal is quite sure that he will never move again.

And Columbanus steps forward, quite calmly, and speaks to the bear.

We do not know what he said. Jonas only tells us that he commanded it to go. But I imagine that he spoke to it the way he spoke to everyone, kings included: plainly, firmly, without any fear, and without any unkindness either. Something like this.

"Brother bear. God made this forest for you, and for me, and there is room in it for both of us. But I need this place to pray. There are other caves. Go and find one, in God's name, and do not come back here."

And the bear looks at him.

And the bear, Jonas says, went.

It gets up, slowly, the way bears do, with a great deal of shrugging and stretching, as if it were deciding to go anyway and the Irishman had nothing to do with it. It ambles past them, out of the mouth of the cave, so close that Domoal could have touched its fur, if Domoal had been able to move his arm, which he could not. And it goes off into the trees, and up the hill, and out of the story. And Jonas says it never came back.

Domoal lets out a breath he has been holding for some time.

"Father," he says. "Father. That was a bear."

"Yes."

"You told a bear to move house."

"I asked it, in God's name. It was God who moved it. I only said please."

"You did not say please."

Columbanus thinks about this. "No," he admits. "I should have. Remind me, if we meet another one."

[pause]

I want to say something here about what this story means, and what it does not.

It does not mean that Columbanus had magic powers over animals. The old writers were very clear about this, even when they told the most wonderful stories. A saint was not a wizard. A saint was a person who belonged to God so completely that, sometimes, God let the world around them show a little of what the world was meant to be before it went wrong. In the Garden of Eden, the old Fathers said, Adam was at peace with the animals. And when a holy man walked into the forest, and the bear stepped aside, the monks who told the story saw in it a little glimpse of Eden, and a little glimpse of heaven, where, as the prophet Isaiah says, the wolf will live with the lamb, and nothing shall hurt or destroy on all the holy mountain.

That is what Jonas wanted us to see. Not a man who could boss bears about. A man so at peace with God that, for a moment, the bear was at peace with him.

And I do not know exactly what happened in that cave fourteen hundred years ago. Jonas was not there. He wrote it down from what the companions of Columbanus told him, and some of them were old men by then, telling stories about a great abbot they had loved. But I know this. The cave is still there, in the forest near Annegray. The people of the place still show it, and still call it Saint Columbanus's cave. And whether the bear moved quietly or grumbled all the way up the hill, I am quite sure that somebody, once, sat in that cave and prayed, and found it very quiet.

* * *

They stayed in the cave that night, and for some days after. And this is where we come to the second story Jonas tells, which is about water.

The trouble with the cave, Domoal discovered almost immediately, was that it was a long way from anything. In particular, it was a long way from the stream. And Columbanus, who was fasting, did not need very much, but he did need water, and he did need it fetched, and fetching it was Domoal's job.

So the next morning, Domoal took the little flask, and went down the hill to the stream, and filled it, and climbed back up the hill to the cave. And it was a steep hill. And the flask was small. And by the third time, he had some feelings about it.

Jonas tells us that the boy complained. He says that Domoal grumbled about how far it was to carry the water, and how hard the path was. Which I find very reassuring, because it means that even in the lives of the saints, the people around the saints were ordinary, and got tired, and said so.

So I imagine Domoal, on the fourth or fifth trip, coming into the cave very red in the face, and putting the flask down rather harder than he needed to, and saying something like this.

"Father. I am sorry. I know you are praying. I know it is holy. But the stream is at the bottom of the hill, and the cave is at the top of the hill, and in between them there is all of the hill. Could God not have put the cave nearer the water? Or the water nearer the cave? Either one. I am not fussy."

And Columbanus, who has been kneeling at the back of the cave with his eyes closed, opens them, and looks at the boy. And I like to think that this time, he did not tell him off. I like to think he remembered being thirteen, and carrying water at Bangor, and being tired.

He gets up. He goes to the back of the cave, where the rock is bare and dry. And he kneels down there, and prays.

And Jonas says that as he prayed, water began to come out of the rock. Not a great gush. A spring, clear and cold, running out of the stone at the back of the cave, enough for the two of them to drink, and enough, Jonas says, that it went on running long after they were gone.

Domoal looks at it for a long time.

"Father," he says at last. "I would like to say that I am sorry for complaining."

"You are forgiven."

"But I would also like to say that it would have been nice to know about this on the first trip."

[pause]

Jonas tells this story, I think, because it reminded him of Moses in the wilderness, striking the rock to give water to the grumbling people of Israel. And the Irish monks, who knew their Old Testament very well, would have caught the echo straight away. There is a grumbling boy in it, as there were grumbling people in the desert. And there is a God who hears grumbling, and does not only scold, but gives water.

I do not know about you. But I find that a comfort. I have done a fair amount of grumbling in my time, on a fair number of hills. And it is good to know that God has heard all of it before, from much holier people than me, and was kind about it.

There is a spring near the cave at Annegray still. The people of the place still call it Saint Columbanus's spring, and they still go there, and some of them pray there. Whether it is the very spring from the story, nobody can say for certain. But it is cold, and it is clear, and I am told that it is not very far to carry the water.

* * *

There are other animal stories in the Life of Columbanus, and since this is a bedtime episode, and you are getting sleepy, I will tell you three of them, slowly, one after the other, like the beads of a rosary.

The first is about wolves.

Once, Jonas says, Columbanus was walking alone in the forest, reading, as he often did, with his book open in his hands. And he looked up, and found that he was surrounded by wolves. They had come out of the trees, silently, the way wolves do, and they were standing in a ring around him, looking at him.

He did not run. He could not have outrun them anyway. He stood still, Jonas says, and prayed. I like to think he used the words that monks still sing at the beginning of every hour of prayer, and have for longer than anyone can remember, though that part is our own guess. O God, come to my aid. O Lord, make haste to help me.

And the wolves came close. They sniffed at his clothes. One of them, perhaps, put its nose against his hand. And then, one by one, Jonas says, they turned and went back into the forest, and left him alone, and he went on with his walk, and his reading.

I like to think he finished the page.

The second story is about another bear, and a deer, and some shoes.

The brothers at Annegray, Jonas tells us, found a deer one day in the forest that had been killed by wolves. The wolves had eaten some of it and gone. And the monks, who were very poor, and whose shoes were wearing out on the stony paths, saw that the hide of the deer was still good, and could be made into leather for sandals.

But when they went back to fetch it, they found that a bear had got there first, and was settling down to eat whatever the wolves had left.

So Columbanus went to the bear. And he told it, Jonas says, that it could eat the meat, but it was not to touch the hide, because the brothers needed it for their shoes.

And the bear ate the meat. And it left the hide.

I find this story very funny, and also very Irish. Columbanus does not tell the bear to go away and leave the dinner. That would be unfair to the bear, who found it fair and square. He simply negotiates. The meat for you, brother bear, the leather for us. A clear division of property. Everybody goes home happy. Somewhere, I suspect, there is an old Irish lawyer who would have been very proud.

And the third story is the smallest, and my favourite.

There was a young monk with Columbanus called Chagnoald, who later became a bishop, and who, Jonas says, was one of the people who told him these stories. And Chagnoald said that he had seen Columbanus, walking in the woods, call the birds and the small animals to him. And they came. And once, Chagnoald said, the abbot called a squirrel down from the top of a tall tree. And it came all the way down, and ran into his hand, and up his arm, and around his neck, and in and out of the hood of his habit, and Columbanus stood there, the fierce abbot of Annegray, letting a squirrel run around in his hood, and laughing.

Chagnoald says he saw it. And he remembered it all his life, long enough to tell it to Jonas.

I think that is the most important story of all. Not the bear, not the wolves, not even the spring. The fierce man who stood up to kings, and wrote stern rules for monks, and was frightened of nothing, standing in a wood with a squirrel in his hood. Laughing.

* * *

Now I want to tell you, more quickly, about what happened next, because the forest did not stay quiet for long.

People heard about the Irish monks at Annegray. They heard about how poor they were, and how happy, and how holy. And they began to come. First a few, then many. Some came to be healed. Some came to ask for advice. And a great many young men came to join. So many, in fact, that Annegray was soon too small. And so Columbanus founded a second monastery, a few miles away, at another old Roman place called Luxeuil, where there were warm springs that the Romans had used for baths, and the ruins of their temples in the woods. And then a third, at a place called Fontaine. And soon there were hundreds of monks living under his rule, Irish and Frankish together, praying and working and copying books, in the middle of what had been an empty forest.

Luxeuil became one of the great monasteries of Europe. Over the next hundred years, monks trained there went out and founded dozens more, all over what is now France, and Belgium, and Switzerland, and Germany. Many of the great abbeys of the Middle Ages can trace themselves, one way or another, back to that ruined Roman fort with nothing to eat.

But Columbanus was still Columbanus. He was still fierce. He kept the date of Easter in the old Irish way, which was different from the way the bishops of Gaul kept it, and when they objected, he did not back down. Instead, he wrote a letter to the Pope. To Gregory the Great, in fact, who listeners to our last episode will remember: the monk with the bad stomach who sent Augustine to Kent. The letter still survives. It is very respectful, and very learned, and also, in places, quite cheeky, and it asks the Pope, more or less, to please explain why the Roman way of reckoning Easter is right, because Columbanus is not convinced. We do not have Gregory's answer. I suspect, given how busy he was, that it may have stayed on the pile.

And then, after about twenty years in Burgundy, Columbanus quarrelled with the court.

[pause]

The quarrel was about a marriage, or rather about the lack of one. The young king of Burgundy, Theuderic, had several sons by women who were not his wife. His grandmother, Queen Brunhild, who was a very formidable old lady and who effectively ran the kingdom, brought the boys to Columbanus one day, and asked him to bless them, as the king's sons and heirs.

And Columbanus refused. He said, Jonas tells us, that they were not born of a true marriage, and that he would not bless them as heirs to the throne. He told the king, plainly, that he ought to marry properly and live faithfully. And he went on telling him, for some time, which kings, then and now, do not enjoy.

So in the year six hundred and ten, the king had him arrested, and ordered him out of the kingdom, and sent him, under guard, right across Gaul to the port of Nantes, to be put on a ship and sent back to Ireland.

And here, Jonas says, God took a hand. The ship was loaded, and the old abbot was put on board, and they set out to sea. And a great wave came, and drove the ship back onto the shore, and it stuck there, and would not move. And the captain, who was a practical man, decided that the problem was the Irishman, and put him and his things back on the beach, and the ship floated off quite happily without him.

So Columbanus, who had been told to go home, did not go home. He went the other way. Up the rivers, through the kingdoms of the other Frankish kings, who were happy to welcome anybody their cousin Theuderic disliked. Up the Rhine, with some of his old monks, in boats. To Lake Constance, at the foot of the Alps, where they stayed for a while, and where his old friend Gall fell ill, and stayed behind, and met another bear, as we told in our earlier episode. And then, an old man by now, over the Alps themselves, on foot, into Italy.

In Italy, the king of the Lombards, Agilulf, and his queen, Theodelinda, who was a devout Catholic and a friend of Pope Gregory's, welcomed him. And they gave him a place to found one last monastery: a valley in the mountains, by a river called the Trebbia, with a ruined church in it, at a place called Bobbio.

It was wild, and quiet, and far from anywhere. There was nothing to eat for miles.

Columbanus looked at it, and was delighted.

[pause]

Now I want to slow down.

He was about seventy, at Bobbio. He had walked, by the end, from the Irish Sea to the mountains of Italy. He had stepped over his mother at the door, and crossed the sea, and slept on the floors of ruined forts, and eaten bark, and argued with bishops and kings and very nearly with a Pope. He had built monasteries out of nothing in three countries. And he was tired.

But I do not think he had changed his habits. The people of the valley still show a cave in the mountains above Bobbio, and say that he used to go there by himself to pray, just as he had at Annegray. That is their tradition, and not something we can prove. But it sounds very like him.

I like to imagine him there, in that last cave, an old man, kneeling on the cold stone. Listening. The river below. The mountains around. No bear this time, perhaps. Or perhaps a bear somewhere up the hill, who knew to leave him be.

He wrote sermons, at Bobbio and before, for his monks, and some of them survive. And one of the things he says in them, again and again, is that this life is a road. It is not our home. It is the road to our home. We are travellers on it, he says, pilgrims, guests in the world, and we should not love the road so much that we forget where it is going. And he says, in another place, that he wants his heart to be like a lamp. Not a lamp that is lit once and then goes out. But a lamp that burns always in the temple of the Lord, fed by God's love, giving light to everyone in the house.

[pause]

Columbanus died at Bobbio, on the twenty-third of November, in the year six hundred and fifteen. He was buried there, in the monastery he had founded last, in the valley by the river. And the monastery of Bobbio went on after him for more than a thousand years, and became, like Saint Gall's, one of the great libraries of Europe, full of books copied by monks who had been taught by monks who had been taught by an Irishman who once told a bear to move house.

His tomb is still there, in the crypt of the abbey church at Bobbio. And people still come, from Italy, and France, and Switzerland, and from Ireland, to pray at it.

And in the forest in the hills of eastern France, near the ruins of Annegray, there is still a great grey rock with ferns growing out of its cracks. And in the bottom of the rock, there is a cave. And near the cave, there is a spring, and the water is cold and clear.

And the bear, as far as anyone knows, never came back.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes mostly from the Life of Columbanus written by Jonas of Bobbio, an Italian monk who joined the monastery of Bobbio a few years after Columbanus died, and who wrote the Life about twenty-five years later, around the year six hundred and forty. Jonas never met Columbanus, but he knew many of the monks who had, and he tells us he got his stories from them, by name, including Chagnoald, who later became bishop of Laon. It is a careful book for its time, and much closer to its subject than most saints' lives. Columbanus's own letters, sermons and rules also survive, edited by G. S. M. Walker, and they let us hear his real voice.

What is history. His birth in Leinster, his years under Sinell and under Comgall at Bangor, his leaving for Gaul with twelve companions around five hundred and ninety, the founding of Annegray, Luxeuil and Fontaine, his letter to Pope Gregory about Easter, his refusal to bless the king's sons, his expulsion in six hundred and ten, the ship at Nantes being driven back, though the reason for that is Jonas's, the journey to Lake Constance and over the Alps, the gift of Bobbio, and his death there in six hundred and fifteen are all well attested. Jonas names the king who first welcomed him as Sigebert, which cannot be right, and historians think it was Guntram or Childebert.

What is legend, or Jonas's report. The mother's dream, the bear leaving the cave, the spring for Domoal, the wolves, the bear and the deer's hide, the squirrel, and the cart from the abbot Carantoc are all wonders Jonas reports, and we have told them as his report. That the cave and the spring still shown near Annegray are the ones in the story is local tradition, and so is the cave above Bobbio. The Irish haircut in the cold open is described in different ways by the old writers; we gave the most common reading. The rock of the cave, as we described it, is our own picture.

What we invented. Domoal's age, his grumbling beyond what Jonas says, and every word he speaks; Columbanus's words to the bear and to the boy; the weather; the walk up the hill; and the thoughts of the twelve companions about the kitchen at Bangor. Jonas only tells us that Columbanus commanded the bear, and that it went. The rest is our own, told in the spirit of the original. The words from his sermons about the road and the lamp are given in our own plain wording.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Columbanus for tonight. His feast is on the twenty-third of November, the day he died at Bobbio, and it is kept in the Catholic calendar, and with special joy in Ireland, at Luxeuil, and at Bobbio, and by many Orthodox Christians who honour the saints of the West from before the Churches parted. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. And here, in our own plain words, is a prayer from one of Columbanus's own sermons. Lord, give me a lamp lit by your love, that never goes out, that burns in me and gives light to others. Let it shine in your house, and let me always be looking for you, its light. Holy Columbanus, pilgrim for Christ, pray for us. Goodnight, and God bless.

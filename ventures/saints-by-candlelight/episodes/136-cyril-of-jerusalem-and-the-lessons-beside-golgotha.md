It is early on a spring morning in Jerusalem, in the biggest church that anyone in the room has ever seen, and a young man is trying very hard not to be noticed.

This is difficult, because his face is covered with a veil. Everyone's face near him is covered with a veil. There are a great many of them, men on one side and women on the other, sitting on the floor of the great church with their heads wrapped in cloth, like a field of very quiet, very nervous parcels.

At the front, a priest is speaking. He is in his thirties, he has a clear, carrying voice, and he has just said, quite calmly, that some of the people in front of him have not come for God at all.

"It is possible," the priest says, "that a man is wishing to pay court to a woman, and came here on that account."

Under his veil, the young man goes very red. Three rows across, on the women's side, a girl under her own veil is trying not to laugh.

And the priest, who has done this before, and who knows exactly what he has just said, smiles, and says the most surprising thing of all. "I accept this bait for the hook. And I welcome you."

Let us start with the city.

It is about the year three hundred and fifty. Jerusalem is not, at this moment, a very large place. It is a Roman town in the hills, with Roman streets, Roman baths, and a Roman name, which for most of the last two hundred years has been Aelia Capitolina, a name nobody uses in songs. The big city of the province is Caesarea, down on the coast, where the governor lives and the ships come in. Jerusalem is a hill town. It is the sort of place where everybody knows the baker.

But in the last twenty-five years or so, something extraordinary has happened to Jerusalem. The emperor Constantine, the first emperor to become a Christian, sent orders to build. And in the middle of the town, on the spot that the Christians of Jerusalem had always pointed to as the place where the Lord was crucified and buried, there now stands a set of buildings so large and so splendid that people come from the other end of the world to look at them.

There is a great round church over the tomb, called the Anastasis, which is Greek for the Resurrection. Inside it, in the middle, is a small cave in the rock, the tomb itself, as the Jerusalem church has always taught. Then there is an open courtyard, with colonnades round it, like a cloister with the roof taken off. And in the corner of the courtyard there is a great lump of rock sticking up, the hill of Golgotha, with a cross on top. And then, on the other side, there is a huge basilica, a long hall with columns and a gilded ceiling, which is called the Martyrium, which means the place of witness.

If you are trying to picture it, imagine a cathedral, a cloister and a round chapel, all in a row, all brand new, all sparkling, all built in the space of a few years, in the middle of a small hill town. It is a little as if a small market town in the countryside woke up one morning to find that the emperor had built three of the finest buildings in the world on its high street, and now there were tourists everywhere asking for directions.

And there were. Pilgrims came from Spain and Gaul, from Egypt and Syria and Persia and Armenia. They stood in the courtyard and looked up at the rock of Golgotha and wept. The innkeepers of Jerusalem were very pleased about all this. So were the people who made the little clay oil lamps that the pilgrims bought, to carry home with them, as a keepsake of the holy places.

Which brings us to Kallistos.

Kallistos, I should say straight away, did not exist. He is ours. But a great many young men very like him did exist, in Jerusalem, in those years. He is nineteen. He works in his uncle's workshop just off the main street, making oil lamps out of clay. Little round lamps, with a hole in the top for the oil, a spout at the front for the wick, and a pattern pressed into the top with a mould. A cross, or a palm branch, or a little bunch of grapes. His uncle sells them to the pilgrims by the dozen. Kallistos has made, by his own estimate, about eleven thousand lamps, and he is starting to dream about them.

And Kallistos is, in the way of a great many families in those days, a half-and-half sort of Christian. His mother goes to church. His father went sometimes, when he was alive. Kallistos himself has been signed with the cross as a child and enrolled as what they called a catechumen, a learner, which meant he was allowed to come to church and listen to the readings and the sermon, and then he was politely shown out before the most sacred part of the service began.

A great many people stayed catechumens for years, in those days. Some stayed catechumens for their whole lives, and were only baptised on their deathbeds. It was a bit like being a member of a club who has paid the joining fee and never actually gone to a meeting.

Kallistos was perfectly happy to stay that way. Until the spring when Susanna enrolled.

* * *

Susanna was the weaver's daughter from two streets over. She was eighteen, she was clever, she was quick, and she had once told Kallistos that his lamps were very nice but that the grapes on them looked like a bunch of peas, which was true, and which he had never forgotten.

And that winter, just before Lent, Susanna told her mother that she wanted to be baptised at Easter.

In Jerusalem, the way you did that was this. At the start of Lent, you gave your name in. You went to the church, with someone to speak for you, and your name was written down on the list of those who would be baptised on the night before Easter Sunday. Then, for the forty days of Lent, you came every morning to the great church, the Martyrium, and a priest or the bishop taught you. Everything. The whole faith, from the beginning, so that when you stepped down into the water at Easter, you would know what you were saying yes to.

Kallistos's mother had been gently suggesting for about five years that he might give his name in. Kallistos had been gently not hearing her for about five years.

The day after he found out that Susanna had given her name in, Kallistos went to his mother and said, in a casual voice, that he had been thinking, and that perhaps this year might be the year.

His mother looked at him for a long moment. She was a wise woman. She had been young herself. She knew perfectly well that the weaver's daughter had given her name in, because the whole street knew.

"That is very good news," said his mother. "I'll come with you."

And that was how Kallistos found himself, on the first morning of Lent, sitting on the floor of the Martyrium, with his face wrapped in a veil, among the men on the left-hand side, while a priest called Cyril stood at the front and began to speak.

* * *

Now, let me tell you about Cyril, because we are lucky with him. Most of the saints in these stories, we know only from what other people wrote about them, often hundreds of years later. But Cyril, we can actually hear. Somebody in that great church, probably a scribe taking it down in shorthand, wrote out his lessons word for word, as he gave them, one Lent around the year three hundred and fifty. There is an introduction, and eighteen lessons for the candidates during Lent, and then five more for the week after Easter. And they have been copied and read ever since. You can read them tonight, in English, if you have a mind to. They are not hard. They were not meant to be hard. They were meant for lamp-makers.

Cyril was a Jerusalem man, very probably born in or near the city, around the year three hundred and fifteen. Which means that when he was a little boy, the great church he was now standing in did not exist. He would have watched it go up. He would have seen the workmen digging out the rock round the tomb, and the columns arriving, and the gold going onto the ceiling. And now, a grown man, a priest of the Jerusalem church, he stood in it every morning in Lent and taught.

And the first thing he said, in that very first lesson, was a warning, and the warning was about a man called Simon.

You may remember Simon from the Acts of the Apostles. He was a magician in Samaria, very famous, very impressive, who heard the apostle Philip preaching, and believed, and was baptised. And then, when he saw that the apostles laid their hands on people and the Holy Spirit came upon them, he offered them money, so that he could do the same trick. And Saint Peter told him, in very plain words, that the gift of God is not for sale.

"Even Simon the magician once came to the water," Cyril told the candidates. "He was baptised. But he was not enlightened. His body went down into the water and came up again. But his heart did not."

There was a silence in the great church. Under his veil, Kallistos shifted uncomfortably.

"Let none of you," Cyril went on, "come in saying, let's see what the faithful are doing. Let me go in and see what happens. Do you expect to see, and not expect to be seen? And do you think, that while you are busy finding out what's going on, God is not busy finding out your heart?"

Kallistos had a strong feeling that this lesson had been written specifically for him, which was silly, because Cyril had never met him. But it was a feeling that a great many people have had, sitting in church, over the centuries. It is one of the occupational hazards of sitting in church.

And then Cyril said the thing about the man who came because of a woman.

I want to be careful here. Cyril did not say it to embarrass anyone. He said it the way a good teacher says something he knows is true of at least half a dozen people in the room, without looking at any of them. Some of you, he said, may have come on some other pretext. Perhaps a man wants to court a woman, and came for that reason. And the same goes for the women, in their turn. Perhaps a servant wants to please his master. Perhaps a friend came because his friend did.

"I accept this bait for the hook," said Cyril. "And I welcome you. Even though you came with a mixed-up purpose, I welcome you, as someone who is going to be saved by a good hope. Perhaps you didn't know where you were coming. Perhaps you didn't know what kind of net you were being caught in."

And then he said this. "You have come inside the nets of the Church. Be caught alive. Don't run away. Jesus is fishing for you. Not to kill you, but to make you alive."

On the women's side of the church, three rows across, a veiled girl who had once told Kallistos that his grapes looked like peas made a small noise that might have been a cough, and might have been a laugh. And Kallistos, under his own veil, with his ears burning, found to his surprise that he was smiling too.

Because it is a very odd thing, to be found out, and then, instead of being told off, to be welcomed.

* * *

Cyril had some other rules for them, and they are lovely, and very practical, and I want to tell you them, because they tell you exactly what it was like to be in that room.

The first rule was about the waiting. Each morning, before the lesson, there were prayers over the candidates, which they called exorcisms, which is a word that sounds a good deal more dramatic than it was. It meant prayers of cleansing, prayers asking God to drive out from them everything that was not of Him. And while they waited for everyone's turn, the candidates sat about in the great church, and Cyril was a realistic man, and knew what happens when you leave a lot of young people sitting about in a room together.

So. Men with men, he said, and women with women. Like Noah's ark, he said, which had one door, and everyone inside, but everyone suitably arranged.

And while you wait, he said, let the men have a useful book. Let one read, and another listen. And if there is no book, let one pray, and another say something useful. And let the young women sit together, and sing, or read quietly, so quietly that their lips move, but nobody else can hear.

So that was what the waiting looked like. A great hall, full of veiled people, the men with their books, one reading in a murmur, the others listening, and on the other side, a whole sea of women whispering psalms so softly that you could only see the veils move.

Kallistos, it has to be said, could not read. Not well, anyway. He could read the word Jerusalem, because it was pressed into some of the lamps, backwards, in the mould. But his neighbour on the floor, on the very first day, was an old man called Ammonius, who had driven donkeys up and down the road from the coast for fifty years and who, at the age of sixty-eight, had decided it was time to stop being a catechumen and get on with it. And Ammonius, astonishingly, could read beautifully, because a priest had taught him as a boy, in exchange for help with a donkey.

So every morning, while they waited, old Ammonius took out a battered little book of the psalms and read it aloud, in a low, rumbling voice, and Kallistos listened, and a few other young men round about listened too, and by about the second week of Lent Kallistos found that he was looking forward to it.

The second rule was a secret.

"When the lesson is over," Cyril told them, "if a catechumen who has not given his name in asks you what the teachers said, tell nothing to anyone outside."

This was not because the faith was shameful, or because it was a secret society. Cyril was very clear about that. It was because, he said, some things must be heard at the right time, in the right order. If you give wine to a sick man at the wrong moment, it does him harm. If a man hears the deepest things of the faith before he is ready, he does not understand them, and laughs at them, and the friend who told him is blamed. You are standing on the border now, Cyril said. When you have crossed it, you will understand.

Kallistos's cousin Demas, who was not enrolled, and who also worked in the lamp workshop, spent most of that Lent trying to get Kallistos to tell him what the priest said.

"Is it about magic?" said Demas. "Is it the secret names of the angels? Does he tell you how to get rich?"

"He says," said Kallistos, with great dignity, "that I am standing on the border."

"What border?"

"I can't tell you."

Which was, in its way, the best possible advertisement. Demas gave his name in the following year.

* * *

So Lent went on. Every morning, the prayers, the waiting, old Ammonius reading the psalms, and then Cyril's voice, carrying down the long hall of the Martyrium, under the gilded ceiling.

He taught them everything. One day it was about God, the one God, who made everything, and who is not to be imagined as a large old man sitting on a cloud, because God is greater than anything we could picture. One day it was about sin, and how it is not a thing that happens to you, like the weather, but a thing you choose, and which can therefore also be repented of, which is good news. One day it was about the Father, one day about the Son, one day about the Holy Spirit, and how the Son is true God from true God, and how the Holy Spirit is not a sort of energy or a mood, but the living God, who teaches and comforts and makes holy.

And the creed. That was the great thing. Each candidate was to learn the creed of the Jerusalem church, the statement of the faith, by heart.

Not written on paper, Cyril told them. On your heart. Learn it by memory. Say it over to yourself. Because, he said, not everyone can read the Scriptures, some because they have never learned to read, and some because they are too busy, and so the whole faith has been put in a few short lines, so that nobody need go without it.

Kallistos, who was one of the ones who had never learned to read properly, found this enormously comforting. He learned the creed in the lamp workshop. He said it over to himself, line by line, while he pressed the clay into the moulds. I believe in one God, the Father almighty, press, maker of heaven and earth, lift, and of all things visible and invisible, press. His uncle thought he had gone strange. His uncle was partly right.

And then, one morning, in about the middle of Lent, Cyril talked about the Cross.

You have to remember where they were. They were sitting in the great hall of the Martyrium, and just outside, through the doors at the end, in the corner of the courtyard, was the rock of Golgotha. And beyond that, the round church over the tomb. Cyril could, very nearly, have pointed out of the door.

And he did, in a way. "He was really crucified," Cyril told them, "and we are not ashamed of it. If I wanted to deny it, here is Golgotha to prove me wrong, near which we are all sitting now. Here is the wood of the Cross to prove me wrong, which has since been sent out from here, a little piece at a time, to the whole world."

The Jerusalem church, you see, kept a relic which it believed to be the wood of the Cross itself, and Cyril believed it too, and taught it, and already in his day little fragments of it had been given to churches far away. That is the tradition of the Jerusalem church, and I tell it to you as their tradition.

And then Cyril began to list the witnesses. Everything in Jerusalem, he said, bears witness to what happened. The garden of Gethsemane bears witness. The Mount of Olives bears witness. This holy Golgotha bears witness, which stands up above us here, and still shows how the rocks were split. The tomb nearby bears witness, where He was laid. And the stone that was rolled across the door, which, he said, is still lying by the tomb to this day.

Kallistos had walked past that stone a hundred times. Everybody had. It was just there. And now, sitting on the floor with his veil over his face, he found that he was thinking about it as if he had never seen it before. A stone. A real stone. That had been rolled away.

And then Cyril said the part that Kallistos remembered for the rest of his life.

"So let us not be ashamed," said Cyril, "to confess the Crucified. Let the Cross be our seal, made boldly with our fingers on our forehead, and on everything. Over the bread we eat, and the cups we drink. When we come in, and when we go out. Before we sleep, when we lie down, and when we rise up. When we are on the road, and when we are still."

It costs nothing, Cyril said. That's the beauty of it. It is free, for the sake of the poor. It is no effort, for the sake of the sick. Its grace is from God.

That night, lying on his mat in the little room above the workshop, Kallistos made the sign of the cross on his forehead before he went to sleep. He had done it as a child, when his mother made him. He had not done it for years. It felt strange, and small, and very, very old.

And it occurred to him, lying there in the dark, that a few streets away, Susanna was very likely doing exactly the same thing. And then it occurred to him, a moment later, that it was not actually Susanna he was thinking about, just at that moment. Which was new.

* * *

The forty days ran out, the way forty days do.

The Great Week came, the week before Easter, which some of you will remember from our story with Egeria the pilgrim, when the whole of Jerusalem walked from one holy place to another, singing, and the bishop read the Gospel in the very places where it had happened.

And then came the night before Easter Sunday. The Easter Vigil.

We have to be a little careful here, because Cyril told his candidates, very firmly, not to tell outsiders what happened in the baptism before they had been through it. But after Easter, in the week that followed, someone, perhaps Cyril himself, or perhaps his successor as bishop, a man called John, gave five more lessons to the newly baptised, explaining what they had just done. Scholars still argue about which of the two gave those lessons. But they come from Jerusalem, and from around this time, and they tell us what it was like. And since you and I are on the far side of the border, many centuries later, I think we may listen in.

First, they went into the outer hall of the baptistery, in the dark. And they turned to face the west. The west, where the sun goes down, which stood for darkness. And each of them stretched out a hand, and said, out loud, I renounce you, Satan. And all your works. And all your pomp. And all your service.

And then they turned round. A whole crowd of people, turning round in the dark, to face the east. Where the sun comes up. And they said that they believed in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Spirit, and in one baptism of repentance.

Then they went into the inner room. They took off their old clothes, the old clothes standing for the old life. They were anointed with oil. And they went down into the water, three times, at each name of the Trinity, and came up again. And then they were anointed once more, and dressed in white, and led, all shining and dripping and new, into the great church, where the faithful were waiting, with the lamps lit, for the first Eucharist of Easter.

Kallistos came up out of the water the third time, gasping, because it was cold, and because it was dark, and because the deacon had been rather enthusiastic. And standing at the edge in his new white robe, with water running out of his hair, he found that he was crying, and could not have told you why, and did not mind.

On the other side of the baptistery, behind a curtain, where the women were baptised, Susanna came up out of the water too.

And in the week after Easter, the newly baptised came every day to the round church, the Anastasis, around the tomb itself, still in their white robes, and the bishop explained to them everything they had done. And on the last day, he taught them how to receive Holy Communion.

Don't come with your wrists stuck out, he said, and your fingers spread. Make your left hand a throne for your right hand. As if it is going to receive a King. And hollow your palm. And receive the Body of Christ. And say, Amen.

Kallistos, who worked with his hands all day, who had made eleven thousand clay lamps, looked down at his own two hands, cupped one in the other, and thought that he had never in his life seen them look like that.

A throne for a King.

* * *

Now, I could stop the story there. That would be a perfectly nice place to stop. A young man came to church because of a girl, and found God, and also, in due course, the girl. They were married the following winter, if you were wondering, in the round church, and Cyril was there, and Susanna told Kallistos afterwards that he had looked like a lamp that somebody had remembered to light.

But Cyril's story was only just beginning. And it did not go smoothly.

Not long after these lessons, Cyril became bishop of Jerusalem. And about a year after that, in the year three hundred and fifty-one, in the fifty days after Easter, on the seventh of May, at about nine o'clock in the morning, something happened over Jerusalem that Cyril wrote to the emperor about.

According to Cyril's own letter, and to the historian Sozomen, who wrote about a hundred years later, a great cross of light appeared in the sky. It stretched, they say, all the way from the hill of Golgotha to the Mount of Olives, which is the best part of two miles. It was not like a comet, Sozomen says, with rays going off in all directions. It was a single, dense, shining shape, brighter than the sun, and it stayed there for hours.

And the whole city stopped. Men, women and children came running out of their houses, out of the market, out of their workshops, and ran to the church. And they sang hymns to Christ, together, all of them, the faithful and the catechumens and the pilgrims and the shopkeepers, all in the great church at once.

Kallistos, I am quite sure, was among them, with clay still on his hands.

Cyril wrote to the emperor Constantius, Constantine's son, telling him what had been seen, and his letter still survives. And the Orthodox Church still remembers that morning every year, on the seventh of May. What exactly it was, I cannot tell you. Some people since have suggested a rare kind of halo round the sun, which happens when the light shines through ice high up in the air, and which can make bright bands and crosses in the sky. Others have been quite content to call it a sign. The people of Jerusalem that morning did not argue about it. They went to church.

* * *

And now the trouble.

You remember I said that Jerusalem was a small hill town, and that the big city of the province was Caesarea, down on the coast. Well. In the Church, as in everything else in the Roman Empire, the bishop of the big city of a province was in charge of the other bishops in the province. So the bishop of Caesarea was Cyril's superior.

But Jerusalem was not just any small hill town, was it? It was where the Lord had died and risen. It was where the Church began. And Cyril felt, quite strongly, that this ought to count for something. He said that Jerusalem was an apostolic see, a church founded by the apostles themselves, and that it should not simply take orders from Caesarea.

The bishop of Caesarea, a clever, forceful, and rather political man named Acacius, did not agree.

And so, Sozomen tells us, the two bishops fell out. And like a great many people who fall out about one thing, they soon found other things to fall out about too. Each accused the other of getting the faith wrong. This was the time of the great arguments about the Arians, the people who said that the Son of God was not truly God, and every bishop in the East was being watched by every other bishop, to see which side he was on. I will not take you into all that tonight. It is not bedtime material. It is not really anyone's-time material. Let me just say that Acacius leaned towards the Arian side, and that Cyril, in the end, was vouched for as sound in the faith by a great council of the whole Church, as you will hear.

But Acacius needed a charge that would stick. And he found one.

There had been a famine in Jerusalem and the country round about. A bad one. The poor came in crowds to their bishop, asking for food. And Cyril had no money to buy any. So, Sozomen says, he sold the church's treasures. The precious hangings and the sacred ornaments. And with the money, he fed the poor.

Now. You and I may think, listening to this, that this is one of the most Christian things a bishop could possibly do. And you would be in good company, because a great many saints, before and after, did exactly the same, and said that the Church's gold was for the hungry before it was for anything else.

But Acacius said that Cyril had sold off the holy things of the Church. And he told a story about it which Theodoret, another old historian, writes down. Among the things sold, Acacius said, was a splendid robe, woven with gold thread, which the emperor Constantine himself had given to the bishop of Jerusalem, to wear when he baptised. And it had ended up, he said, being worn on the stage, by a dancer at the theatre.

The story, as Acacius told it, has an unhappy ending for the dancer, which I will leave in the history books. But notice who was telling it. It was Cyril's enemy. Sozomen tells a rather different version, about a piece of cloth that someone recognised on an actress. Whether any of it happened, nobody knows. What we do know is that it worked.

In about the year three hundred and fifty-seven, a council of bishops under Acacius deposed Cyril, and he was driven out of Jerusalem.

Kallistos and Susanna, by now, had a small son, and a slightly larger workshop. They stood in the street with half the town and watched their bishop go. Susanna was furious. Kallistos was quiet. That evening, he made the sign of the cross over the bread, the way Cyril had told them, before they ate. And then over the cup. And then, a little defiantly, over the baby.

* * *

Where do you go, when your fellow bishops have thrown you out of your own city?

Cyril went north, to the city of Tarsus, on the far side of the mountains, where Saint Paul had been born. And there, Theodoret tells us, the bishop of Tarsus, a good man called Silvanus, took him in.

Acacius, of course, wrote to Silvanus at once, to tell him that Cyril had been deposed, and that he was not to be allowed to do anything. And Silvanus, Theodoret tells us, refused. He let Cyril go on preaching in the churches of Tarsus. Partly out of regard for Cyril. And partly, Theodoret says, because he was a bit nervous of his own congregation. Because the people of Tarsus greatly enjoyed the stranger's teaching.

I love that. Cyril, thrown out of his own city, sent off over the mountains in disgrace, arrives in a strange town, and within a few weeks the people of Tarsus are so fond of his sermons that their own bishop daren't stop him. It turns out that a man who could make the faith clear to a room full of lamp-makers and donkey-drivers could make it clear to anyone.

And then began a long, wearying, back and forth, of the kind that happened to a great many good bishops in those years. In three hundred and fifty-nine, a big council at a town called Seleucia took his side, and he went home. In three hundred and sixty, another council, in Constantinople, with Acacius pulling the strings, took the other side, and he was sent away again.

Then a new emperor came, Julian, who was not a Christian at all, and who had no wish to help the Church. And one of the first things Julian did was to let every exiled bishop go home, all at once, whichever side they were on, perhaps in the hope that they would all go back to quarrelling. So Cyril went home again.

It was in Julian's short reign that the emperor gave orders to rebuild the old Temple in Jerusalem, as a way of showing up the Christians. The work stopped, after an earthquake and outbreaks of fire at the site. Even a pagan historian of the time, Ammianus, records the fires. The Christian historians add that Cyril had said, calmly, beforehand, that it would not be finished. They also wrote about it with an unkindness towards the Jewish people of Jerusalem which we will not repeat, and which no Christian should.

And then Julian died, and after a while another emperor, Valens, came. Valens favoured the Arian side. And in three hundred and sixty-seven, Cyril was sent away for the third time.

This time, it was for eleven years.

* * *

[pause]

Let us slow down now.

Eleven years is a long time. Kallistos's little son grew up, and learned the lamp trade, and made lamps of his own, rather better ones than his father's, with grapes that looked like grapes. Old Ammonius the donkey-driver died, peacefully, in his bed, with his little book of psalms on the shelf, and left it to Kallistos, who could, by now, after many years of trying, read it.

And every year, in Lent, a new crowd of candidates sat on the floor of the great church, with their faces veiled, and somebody taught them. Not Cyril. But they learned the same creed, and turned to the west in the dark, and then turned round to face the east.

And then, in the year three hundred and seventy-eight, the emperor Valens was killed in a great battle, far away in the north, and the new emperors let the exiles go home. And Cyril came back to Jerusalem.

He was in his sixties now. He had spent about sixteen years of his life in exile, in all, if you add it up. And when he came back, the city was in a sorry state. It was crowded, and quarrelsome, and full of rival groups who would not speak to each other, and pilgrims, and people who made money from pilgrims. A visiting bishop from far away, Saint Gregory of Nyssa, came to see it, a year or two later, and was rather shocked, and said so.

And Cyril, an old man, simply went back to work. Setting the church in order. Teaching. Every Lent.

In the year three hundred and eighty-one, the bishops of the whole East met in a great council in Constantinople, the council which gave us the creed that is still said every Sunday in a great many churches of East and West. And Cyril was there. And the next year, the bishops wrote a letter, which the historian Theodoret preserved. In it, they called the church of Jerusalem the mother of all the churches. And they said that its bishop was the most reverend and most religious Cyril, who had been properly ordained long ago, and who had, in many places, fought a good fight against the Arians.

So that was settled. After thirty years.

And it was around this time, very probably, that a pilgrim woman from the far west, from Spain, called Egeria, came to Jerusalem, and wrote home to her sisters about how the bishop taught the candidates in Lent, every morning, for three hours, explaining the whole of the Scriptures, beginning with Genesis, and how the people called out aloud when they heard it explained. She does not say the bishop's name. But the dates fit Cyril. If they are right, then the old man was still doing, in his last years, exactly what he had done as a young priest. Standing at the front of the great church. Explaining. To whoever had come in. For whatever reason they had come.

Kallistos, by then, was a grandfather. His hair was grey, and his hands were stiff from the clay. And one Lent, his granddaughter gave her name in.

She did not do it because of a boy. Or, at least, she said she did not, and her grandmother Susanna looked at her for a long moment, and said, "That is very good news."

And Kallistos went with her, on the first morning, to speak for her. And stood at the back of the great hall, among the faithful, and listened to the old bishop's voice carrying down under the gilded ceiling, and heard him say, once again, to a new crowd of nervous, veiled young people, that some of them had perhaps come for some other reason, and that he accepted this bait for the hook, and welcomed them.

And Kallistos laughed out loud, at the back of the church, and several people turned round and looked at him, and he did not mind at all.

Cyril died in Jerusalem, in about the year three hundred and eighty-six. He had been bishop for something like thirty-five years, and away for nearly half of them. He is remembered on the eighteenth of March, by Catholics and Orthodox alike, and in the eighteen-eighties the Pope named him a Doctor of the Church, which is the title the Catholic Church gives to its greatest teachers.

The great church of the Martyrium, where he taught, is gone now. It was destroyed, centuries later, and the buildings that stand over the tomb in Jerusalem today are newer and smaller and very different. But his lessons are still read. Every year, all over the world, in Lent, there are people preparing for baptism at Easter. And a great many of the people who teach them have, at some point, sat down with Cyril's lessons, and found that he got there first.

And somewhere tonight, I should think, there is a person lying down to sleep who will, before they close their eyes, make a small cross on their forehead. Freely. For the sake of the poor. Without effort, for the sake of the sick. When we lie down, and when we rise up.

Exactly as a priest in Jerusalem told a room full of lamp-makers, sixteen hundred and seventy years ago.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes mostly from Cyril's own Catechetical Lectures, an introductory lecture and eighteen lessons given in Lent in the great basilica beside Golgotha, taken down by a listener, probably in the year three hundred and forty-eight or three hundred and fifty. Scholars disagree about the year, and about whether Cyril was still a priest or already bishop. The Simon the magician warning, the people who come in to see what the faithful do, the man who came for a woman and the bait for the hook, the nets, the veils, the men with a useful book and the women whose lips move without a sound, the secret kept from outsiders, Golgotha near which they sat, the wood of the Cross, the stone by the tomb, the sign of the Cross before sleep, the creed learned by heart, are all his, in our own plain and shortened rendering. The five lessons after Easter, with the turn to the west and the east and the hand made a throne for a King, are given either to Cyril or to his successor John, and we have said so.

The cross of light is in Cyril's Letter to Constantius and in Sozomen. The quarrel with Acacius, the famine and the sold treasures are in Sozomen. The robe and the dancer are Acacius's accusation, as Theodoret reports it. Tarsus and Silvanus are in Theodoret. The letter of three hundred and eighty-two is in Theodoret. That the tomb and the Cross are the very ones is the tradition of the Jerusalem church, which Cyril held. The ice halo is a modern suggestion, not a finding.

Kallistos, Susanna, Ammonius, Demas, their families, and every word they say, are our own invention.

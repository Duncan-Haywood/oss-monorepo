It is late in the afternoon, on the side of a mountain in the wilderness, and an old man is walking uphill with a stick.

He has been walking for a very long time. His sandals are worn through at the heel. His water skin is nearly flat. His beard is full of dust, and his knees, which are more than eighty years old, have been saying unkind things to him since about the middle of the morning.

And then he stops. Because further up the slope, coming down a goat track between the rocks, there is another old man. This one has a shepherd's staff in his hand, and a shepherd's cloak, and a face burnt dark by forty years of desert sun. And he has stopped too.

For a long moment, neither of them moves. They are too far apart to be sure. It has been such a very long time.

And then the old man with the stick drops it, and starts to run. Uphill. At eighty-three. Which is not a thing anyone should try, and which he does anyway.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is the meeting of Moses and his brother Aaron at the mountain of God, from the Book of Exodus, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and Aaron's evening at home, his road across the wilderness and most of what the brothers said to each other are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

[pause]

If you have been listening to this show for a while, you have met Moses several times already, at different ages, which is a little like flicking through someone's photograph album out of order.

We have seen him as a young man at a well in Midian, standing up for seven sisters against some very rude shepherds. We have seen him as a shepherd himself, with his shoes in his hand, in front of a bush that was on fire and did not burn up. We have seen him as an old man on Mount Nebo, looking out at a country he would not walk into. And we have seen him, in between, sitting from morning till night judging the people, until his father-in-law told him, kindly but firmly, to stop it.

Tonight we fill in a gap. Tonight is the day, or rather the few weeks, between the bush and Egypt. And for once, Moses is not quite the main character.

Tonight's story belongs, at least partly, to his brother.

So let's set the scene.

* * *

Aaron is the older brother. The book tells us, a few chapters on, that when the two of them first stood in front of Pharaoh, Moses was eighty years old and Aaron was eighty-three. So Aaron was born three years before Moses. Which means, if you think about it, that Aaron was born before the worst of Pharaoh's orders, the one about the baby boys that we do not dwell on at bedtime. Aaron did not need to be hidden in a basket in the reeds. He grew up at home.

There was a big sister too. Her name was Miriam. She was the quick-thinking girl who watched the basket from the reeds and popped out to offer the princess a nurse. And their parents, the book tells us, were called Amram and Jochebed, of the tribe of Levi.

So picture a family in Egypt. A father and a mother, a clever older daughter, a little boy called Aaron. And then a baby, born in the worst possible year, who is hidden at home for three months, and then put in a basket, and then fished out of the river by a princess, and then, after he is weaned, taken away to grow up in a palace.

I do sometimes wonder what that was like for Aaron. To be a small boy, and to have a baby brother, and then for the baby brother to go and live with the king's daughter. You would hear about him, I suppose, now and then. You might see him, from a distance, in fine linen, in a chariot. He would be learning to read, and to speak properly in front of important people. And you would be out in the brickfields, with everyone else, making bricks.

The book does not say any of that. It does not tell us anything at all about Aaron's childhood. But it does tell us one thing about how he turned out, and it is the Lord himself who says it. He says, "I know that he can speak well."

Keep that in mind. It is the whole reason he is in tonight's story.

* * *

Aaron grew up, and he married. His wife's name was Elisheba, which is the Hebrew form of the name we know as Elizabeth, and she was the daughter of a man called Amminadab, and the sister of a man called Nahshon, who will turn out later to be rather important in the tribe of Judah. And Aaron and Elisheba had four sons, Nadab and Abihu, and Eleazar and Ithamar.

So by the time our story begins, Aaron is a grandfather, or old enough to be one. He is in his eighties. He lives in Egypt, among his own people, the Hebrews, who are still slaves. They are still building store cities out of brick for a Pharaoh who does not care about them. And they have been crying out, the book says, because of their slavery, and their cry has gone up to God.

And as for the baby brother, Moses: he has been gone for forty years.

Saint Stephen, telling this story in the Acts of the Apostles, says that Moses was forty when he went out to visit his people and it all went wrong, and that he was in Midian for forty years after that. So for forty years nobody in Egypt has heard anything from him. He killed an Egyptian, and Pharaoh tried to have him killed, and he ran away. And that was that.

For all Aaron knows, his brother is dead. Forty years is a long time in the desert. I think, if you had asked Aaron about Moses, he might have said, "We had a brother. He went away." And then changed the subject.

* * *

Now. Let's go to the other end of the wilderness.

You may remember, from the night of the burning bush, how Moses argued. God called to him from the bush, and told him his name, and told him to go back to Egypt and bring his people out. And Moses said, who am I to do that? And what will I tell them your name is? And what if they don't believe me? And God answered every question. He gave him signs to do: a staff that turned into a snake, a hand that went white and then was healed, water from the river that would turn to blood on the ground.

And then Moses said, "Oh, my Lord, I am not a man of words. I never have been, not before and not since you started speaking to me. I am slow of speech and slow of tongue."

And God said, who made the human mouth? Is it not I, the Lord? Now go, and I will be with your mouth, and I will teach you what to say.

And then Moses said the thing that I think every one of us has said at some point, in one form or another. He said, "Oh, my Lord, please, send someone else."

And the book says that the anger of the Lord was kindled against Moses. Which is not a small thing to read. But God did not take the call back. He did not say, fine, I will find a better prophet. Instead he said this, and I want you to listen closely to it, because it is the heart of tonight's story.

He said, "Is there not Aaron your brother, the Levite? I know that he can speak well. And look, he is coming out to meet you. And when he sees you, he will be glad in his heart."

He will be glad in his heart.

Notice something about that. God tells Moses that Aaron is coming out to meet him, and the book does not tell us about the word coming to Aaron until much later in the chapter. Whichever way round it happened, God is arranging both ends of the meeting at once, the way a good host at a party introduces two people who do not yet know they are going to be friends. Before Aaron has even put on his sandals, God already knows that he will be glad.

And then God went on. He said, you will speak to him, and put the words in his mouth. And I will be with your mouth and with his mouth, and I will teach you both what to do. He will speak for you to the people. He will be a mouth for you, and you will be to him as God. And take this staff in your hand, the one you will do the signs with.

So that was the plan. Moses would hear the word, and Aaron would say it. Two brothers, one message. The slow tongue and the quick one.

* * *

Moses went home to Midian first. He did the polite thing, which I always like. He went to Jethro, his father-in-law, whose sheep he had been looking after for forty years, and said, "Please let me go back to my brothers in Egypt, to see whether they are still alive."

And Jethro said, "Go in peace."

And the Lord said to Moses in Midian, go back to Egypt, because all the men who were trying to kill you are dead. Which must have been a relief. Forty years is long enough for most grudges to die out, and in this case the people holding them had too.

So Moses took his wife, Zipporah, and his sons, and set them on a donkey, and set out for Egypt. And in his hand he carried the staff, which the book now calls the staff of God. It was the same plain shepherd's staff it had always been. But it had been somewhere, and heard something, and it was not going to be just a stick any more.

On the way, the Lord spoke to Moses again, about Pharaoh, and about the wonders, and about how stubborn Pharaoh would be. And he gave him a message for Pharaoh that is one of the most tender sentences in the whole book. He said, tell Pharaoh, "Israel is my son, my firstborn. Let my son go, so that he may serve me."

My son. Not my slaves, or my workers, or my nation. My son. That is how God thinks of a crowd of tired brickmakers in Egypt.

And then the chapter tells of a strange and difficult night at a lodging place on the road, involving Moses and Zipporah and one of their sons. It is three short verses long, and people who have studied it all their lives still read it in different ways. It is not a passage for bedtime, and I am not going to pretend to explain it in a sentence. It is in the Bible, and we are not hiding it. We are simply leaving it for daylight.

The book does tell us, much later, that at some point Moses sent Zipporah and the boys back to her father, and that they came out to meet him again in the wilderness after the Exodus. It does not say exactly when he sent them back. So tonight, when Moses reaches the mountain, I am not going to say whether they are with him, because the book does not say.

What the book does say is this.

* * *

The Lord said to Aaron, "Go into the wilderness to meet Moses."

That is the whole of it. One sentence. The book does not tell us how the word came. It does not say whether it was a dream, or a voice, or a sudden certainty while he was praying, or something else entirely. It just says that the Lord said it, and that Aaron went.

So from here, for a little while, I am going to imagine. Everything in this next part is our own invention, and I will tell you when we are back on the book's ground.

Let's say it is evening, in a mud-brick house in Egypt. The lamp is lit. Elisheba is putting bread away for the morning. Miriam, the big sister, who is nearly ninety now and still the quickest person in any room, is sitting by the door. And Aaron comes in from outside, and sits down, and does not say anything for a while.

And Miriam, who has known him his whole life, says, "Well? What is it?"

And Aaron says, "I have to go into the wilderness."

"Why?"

"To meet Moses."

And there is a silence in the little house. Because nobody has said that name out loud in a long time.

And Elisheba says, very carefully, "Moses is alive?"

And Aaron says, "I think so. I think he must be. Because I've been told to go and meet him."

And Miriam says, "Meet him where?"

And Aaron opens his mouth, and closes it again. And then he says, a bit sheepishly, "In the wilderness."

"The wilderness is quite big, Aaron."

"I know."

"Do you know which way?"

"East," says Aaron. "I think. Probably."

And I like to think that his big sister looks at him for a long time, the way big sisters do. And then she gets up and starts filling a water skin.

* * *

So off he goes. An old man, setting out across the wilderness to find a brother he has not seen for forty years, with a water skin, and a stick, and a bag of bread, and not very much in the way of directions.

I want to stop for a moment and point out how brave that is. Not brave the way a soldier is brave, charging at someone. Brave the way an old man is brave, leaving home. Aaron does not know if he will find his brother. He does not know what his brother will be like. He does not know what is going to happen next, except that he has been told to go, and so he goes.

The road out of Egypt to the east is hard country. There is sand, and then there is stone, and then there is more stone. The days are hot and the nights are cold, which seems unfair, since you would think the desert could at least make up its mind. There are wells, here and there, if you know where to look, and Aaron does not, so he has to ask.

Let's say that on the fourth or fifth day he meets a shepherd, a young lad with a few goats, sitting on a rock.

And Aaron says, "Excuse me. I'm looking for my brother."

And the lad says, "What does he look like?"

And Aaron says, "I don't know. I haven't seen him for forty years."

And the lad looks at him for a bit, and then he says, "Well, that narrows it down."

Aaron laughs at that, because he has a sense of humour, and because he is tired. And then he says, "He's a shepherd, I think. He used to be a prince. He might be with the priest of Midian."

And the lad brightens up. "Oh, Jethro's son-in-law! The Egyptian. He took the flock right out past the far side of the wilderness this year, out to the mountain."

"Which mountain?"

And the lad points, east and south, to a high grey shape on the edge of the sky. "That one. The mountain of God. My grandmother says something happened there. She won't say what."

That is all our invention, I must say again. The book does not tell us how Aaron found the way. But it does tell us where he found him.

* * *

Now we are back on the book's ground.

The book calls it the mountain of God. It is the same mountain where Moses saw the bush, and the same mountain where, a little later on, the whole people of Israel would camp at the bottom while God gave them the law at the top. In some chapters it is called Horeb, and in others it is called Sinai.

Where exactly it is, people still argue about, and I am not going to settle it tonight. The old Christian pilgrims, Egeria among them, the lady from the far west whose travels we have followed on this show, were shown a mountain in the south of the Sinai peninsula, with a church on the top and monks all round the bottom, and she climbed it. That is the old tradition, and it is a lovely one. But the book itself gives no map.

So. Picture the mountain, however you like. Grey rock, and steep goat tracks, and a few thorny bushes, one of which, perhaps, you would not mind having a quiet look at. And an old man from Egypt, coming up the slope in the late afternoon.

And the book says, very simply, that he went, and met him at the mountain of God, and kissed him.

That is all. Five or six words in most translations. He went. He met him. He kissed him.

And I want to sit with that for a moment, because the book does not usually waste words on feelings. It does not tell us that Aaron wept, or that Moses wept, or what either of them said first. It just tells us what Aaron did with his face when he saw his brother. He kissed him.

Do you remember what God had said? When he sees you, he will be glad in his heart.

He was.

* * *

From here, for a while, I am going to imagine again, and then come back.

Let's say they sat down together on a flat rock near the bottom of the mountain, out of the wind, as the sun went down. Let's say Aaron had a little bread left, and Moses had some goat's cheese, which is the sort of thing a shepherd always has. And they made a small fire out of dry thorn, because the night was coming, and the desert gets cold.

And for a while they did not say very much. What do you say, after forty years? Moses would ask about their mother and father, and Aaron would have to tell him, gently, whatever there was to tell. Moses would ask about Miriam, and Aaron would say, oh, she's the same, she's exactly the same, she filled my water skin and told me I didn't know which way east was. And Moses would laugh, because he remembered her.

And then Aaron would ask, "Why am I here? I was told to come and meet you. I didn't know why. I just came."

And here we are back on the book's ground. Because the book says that Moses told Aaron all the words of the Lord with which he had sent him, and all the signs that he had commanded him to do.

All of it. The bush that burned and was not consumed. The voice that called him by his name, twice. The shoes off, because the ground was holy. The name of God, I am who I am. The promise to bring the people out of Egypt into a good and broad land, flowing with milk and honey. The staff that became a snake, and the hand, and the water from the river. All the words, and all the signs.

I imagine it took a long time to tell. I imagine the fire burnt down and had to be fed again. And I imagine that, near the end, Moses got to the part that was hardest to tell.

Let's say he said it like this. "And then I asked him to send someone else."

And Aaron said, "Did you?"

"I did. I said I couldn't speak. I said I was slow of tongue."

And Aaron, who had spent his whole life being good with words, and had never once, as far as he knew, been any use to anybody because of it, said, "And what did he say?"

And Moses looked at him across the fire. And he said, "He said you."

That is our invention, of course. The book does not tell us what they said by the fire. But it does tell us that Moses told him all the words. And all the words included that part. "Is there not Aaron your brother? I know that he can speak well."

I like to think that it was the first time in eighty-three years that anybody had told Aaron that God knew him by name. And I like to think that he did not say anything at all for a while after that.

* * *

The next morning, or some morning soon after, they set off back to Egypt together. And everything about this walk, until they reach the elders, is our own invention again.

It is a long way. Many days. And I like to think that they spent a good deal of it practising.

Because think about the plan. Moses has heard the words. Aaron has to say them. So Aaron has to know them, not roughly, not more or less, but properly, the way you would want to know a message you were carrying from the king, except that this message comes from a great deal higher up than any king.

So let's say that Moses would tell him a part, slowly, the way he said everything. "He said, I have seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt. I have heard their cry. I know their sufferings. And I have come down to deliver them."

And Aaron would say it back. And it would come out of him beautifully, the way words always came out of Aaron, smooth and clear, so that a goat by the side of the track looked up to listen.

And Moses would stop walking, and say, "No. That's lovely. But that isn't quite what he said."

"What did I get wrong?"

"You said, I have come down to rescue them. He said deliver."

"Is there a difference?"

And Moses would think about it, for a long time, the way slow speakers do, because they have to be careful with every word they use. And then he would say, "I don't know. But he said deliver. So we say deliver."

And Aaron, who could have talked his way round anybody in Egypt, would nod, and say it again, with deliver in it.

I think that is a very good picture of what a messenger is for. Not to improve the message. Not to make it sound cleverer. Just to carry it whole, from the one who spoke it to the ones who need to hear it. Aaron had the gift of words, and Moses had the words, and on that long walk west, I like to think, each of them learned a little of what the other one had.

And at night they would sleep under the same cloak, and in the morning they would get up and do it again. Two old men, walking across the wilderness, repeating to each other the most important message in the world, until they both knew it by heart.

* * *

And then, at last, they came to Egypt. Two old men walking west, with a staff of God, and a very large piece of news.

And the book says that Moses and Aaron went and gathered together all the elders of the people of Israel.

I do not know where they gathered. The book does not say. Let's say it was in the evening, after the day's bricks were made, in a courtyard somewhere, with the elders sitting along the walls and lamps set out on the ground. Tired old men, most of them, with brick dust in the creases of their hands. Men who had been crying out to God for a very long time, and had begun to wonder whether anybody was listening.

And Aaron stood up. Not Moses. Aaron. Exactly as God had said he would.

And the book says that Aaron spoke all the words which the Lord had spoken to Moses, and did the signs in the sight of the people.

Notice that. The words were Moses' to hear, and Aaron's to say. And the signs were done in front of everyone. The slow tongue and the quick one, working together, the way God had planned it on the mountain before either of them knew.

And this is the last verse of the chapter, and it is one of the loveliest verses in the whole book of Exodus.

It says, and the people believed. And when they heard that the Lord had visited the people of Israel, and that he had seen their affliction, they bowed their heads and worshipped.

The Lord had seen. That was the news. Not yet that they were free. Not yet the sea, or the cloud, or the manna, or the mountain. Just this. He has seen you. He knows. He has come.

And a courtful of tired old brickmakers bowed their heads to the ground in the lamplight, and worshipped.

[pause]

I would like to tell you that everything was easy after that. It was not.

The very next chapter of the book tells how Moses and Aaron went in to Pharaoh, and Pharaoh said, who is the Lord, that I should listen to him? And then he made the work harder, and took away the straw for the bricks, and the Hebrew foremen, who had to answer for the bricks, came out and complained bitterly at the two brothers. And a little after that, the book says, the people would not listen to Moses at all, because their spirits were broken and their slavery was cruel.

The Bible is very honest like that. It does not pretend that one good evening fixes everything. But it does not take the evening back either. The Lord had seen. That was still true, on the bad days as much as on the good one. And in the end, as you probably know, he brought them out.

And Aaron was with his brother the whole way.

He stood beside him in front of Pharaoh. He held up his brother's hands, on a hilltop, through a long day's battle, with a man called Hur holding up the other side, which is a story for another night. He became the first priest of Israel, and wore the robes, and was anointed with oil. And the Psalms, much later, remembered the two of them together. You led your people like a flock, the psalm says to God, by the hand of Moses and Aaron. And the prophet Micah has God say, I sent before you Moses, and Aaron, and Miriam. All three of them. The whole family.

Aaron was not a perfect man. The book does not hide that. There was a terrible day, down at the bottom of the mountain, with a calf made of gold, and Aaron was right in the middle of it, and he did not come out of it well. The book tells it plainly, and so should we. But the book also tells us that God did not stop calling him. The Letter to the Hebrews, many centuries later, says that nobody takes the honour of the priesthood for himself. You have to be called by God, it says, as Aaron was.

As Aaron was. Called by name, by a God who knew that he could speak well.

When Aaron was very old, a hundred and twenty-three years old, the Book of Numbers says, he climbed another mountain, Mount Hor, on the edge of the land of Edom, with his brother Moses and his son Eleazar. And at the top, Moses took the priest's robes off his brother, gently, and put them on Eleazar. And Aaron died there, on the top of the mountain, and went home to God. And when the people saw that Aaron was gone, the whole house of Israel wept for him for thirty days.

So the story of the two brothers begins on one mountain and ends on another. And in between, they walked together.

[pause]

But let's not leave them there tonight. Let's go back to the first mountain, the mountain of God, on the evening they met.

The fire of thorn is burning low. The stars are coming out over the wilderness, more of them than you have ever seen, because there is no town for a hundred miles to wash them out. Somewhere up the slope, a few of Jethro's sheep are settling down for the night, grumbling to each other in the dark.

And two old men are sitting side by side on a flat rock, with a single cloak pulled over both their knees, because Aaron did not bring a warm enough one, which is exactly the sort of thing his big sister would have mentioned.

They have stopped talking now. They have said a great deal, and there is a great deal more to say, and it can wait until the morning. For now it is enough to be sitting next to each other.

And Moses, who has been slow of speech all his life, says, very quietly, "I'm glad you came."

And Aaron, who has always been good with words, finds that for once he has none. So he just puts his hand on his brother's shoulder, and leaves it there.

And the fire burns down. And the sheep go quiet. And the mountain of God stands over them both in the dark, very still, the way it has always stood, the way it was standing on the night the bush burned and on the morning before the law was given.

And the two brothers sleep.

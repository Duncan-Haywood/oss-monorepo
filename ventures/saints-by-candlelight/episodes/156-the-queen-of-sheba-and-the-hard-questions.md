There is a woman standing very still in the middle of a king's house, and she has forgotten how to breathe.

She is not a small woman, in any sense. She is a queen. She has ridden on camels for weeks to get here. She has brought more gold than most people in this city will see in their whole lives, and enough spices to make the air in the courtyard smell like a garden after rain. She has asked the king every hard question she could think of, and some of them she had been saving up for years.

And now she has stopped asking.

She is looking at the food on the king's table, and the way his servants sit, and the way his ministers stand, and the clothes they wear, and the cupbearers, and the steps by which the king goes up to the house of his God. She opens her mouth. And what she says, after all that way, and all those questions, is something like this.

They told me. I didn't believe them. And they didn't tell me the half of it.

* * *

First, where is Sheba?

If you take a map of the world and find the Red Sea, that long thin finger of water between Africa and Arabia, and you follow it all the way down to the bottom, to the place where it narrows and then opens out into the ocean, you will find on the Arabian side a country of mountains and high valleys. Today we call it Yemen. And most scholars think that this is where Sheba was. In the old inscriptions of that country, carved in a beautiful square alphabet that looks a little like a row of tiny buildings, the people call their kingdom Saba, and themselves the Sabaeans.

It is a long way from Jerusalem. A very long way. As the crow flies, it is something like two thousand kilometres, and nobody goes as the crow flies, because between the two lies a great deal of Arabia, which is mostly sand, rock and the absence of water. By the caravan roads it is further still. A caravan of camels might manage thirty or forty kilometres on a good day. So you are looking at two months on the road, perhaps more, each way.

Now, why would anybody live down there, at the bottom of the map, so far from everything?

Because of the trees.

In the south of Arabia, and across the water in the Horn of Africa, there grow some small, scraggly, rather unimpressive trees. They cling to dry hillsides. They have papery bark and twisted branches, and if you saw one by the road you would not look twice. But if you take a knife and make a little cut in the bark, the tree weeps. It bleeds a milky sap, which slowly hardens in the sun into pale golden tears. And those tears are frankincense.

There is another tree, thornier and even less handsome, whose sap gives myrrh.

And the ancient world could not get enough of either. Frankincense was burned in temples from Egypt to Babylon. It was burned in the house of the Lord in Jerusalem, as part of the holy incense. Myrrh went into perfumes and anointing oils and medicines. Kings wanted them. Priests needed them. And they grew in only a few places on earth, and Sheba was one of them, or sat right next to them, which comes to the same thing if you are a merchant.

So the Sabaeans did what any sensible people would do, sitting next to the only supply of something everyone wants. They got rich. They loaded camels with incense and sent them north, up the long chain of wells and oases along the western edge of Arabia, all the way to the markets of the north. In later centuries this road was famous enough to have a name: the Incense Road. And Sheba sat at the bottom of it, like a well-to-do family at the top of a very long driveway.

I should say, honestly, that we know a great deal more about Saba a few centuries after Solomon than we do in his own time. The inscriptions with the kings' names, the great dam at their capital, Marib, the temples, most of that is later. Nobody has yet dug up a stone that says, here reigned the queen who went to see Solomon. But Assyrian records from a couple of hundred years after Solomon do mention tribute from Saba, and they name queens of Arab peoples, too, women who ruled tribes and sent camels to the great king of Assyria. So a queen from the south of Arabia, at the head of a caravan, is not an outlandish idea at all. It is the kind of thing that happened.

And at the other end of the road, there was Jerusalem.

Jerusalem in Solomon's day was not a vast city. If you could walk through it, you might find it smaller than you expected, a city on a ridge, with stone walls and stone houses and narrow streets, and on the high ground to the north, the king's new buildings. The house of the Lord, with its cedar and its gold. The king's own house, which took him thirteen years. A hall of pillars made from cedar of Lebanon, so many cedar pillars that people called it the House of the Forest of Lebanon, which I think is a lovely name for a building. Imagine telling a visitor that the king will see you now, in the forest.

It was a small kingdom by the standards of Egypt or Assyria. But for a few years, under Solomon, it was at peace, it was rich, and it had something nobody else had. It had a king whose wisdom, the Bible says, was greater than all the wisdom of the people of the east and all the wisdom of Egypt. He spoke three thousand proverbs. He wrote more than a thousand songs. He talked about trees, from the cedar on the mountain to the little hyssop that grows out of a crack in a wall, and about animals and birds and creeping things and fish. And people came from every nation, the Bible says, to hear him.

One of them came from a very long way.

* * *

The book begins very simply. The queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon, concerning the name of the Lord.

Notice that last part. Concerning the name of the Lord. She did not only hear that there was a clever king up north. She heard something about his God. Somehow, along with the gossip and the prices and the camel news, word came down the Incense Road that this king's wisdom had something to do with the name of the God he worshipped.

How did she hear? The Bible does not say. But you can imagine.

Imagine the caravans coming home. Imagine, at the end of the season, a long string of camels coming down out of the north into the green valleys of Saba, and the merchants climbing down, stiff and dusty and very pleased with themselves, and going to report to the palace on how business had been.

And imagine one of them, an old man who had made the journey thirty times and thought he had seen everything, standing in front of the queen, and saying, Your Majesty, the frankincense sold well, the myrrh sold very well, and also, there is something you should hear about.

And she says, go on.

And he says, there is a king in Jerusalem, in the hill country, the son of the old warrior David. And people are saying he is the wisest man in the world.

And she raises an eyebrow, because she has heard that sort of thing before. Every king has courtiers who say he is the wisest man in the world. It is practically in the job description.

But the old merchant shakes his head. No, he says, it is not his courtiers saying it. It is everybody. It is the Egyptians. It is the Phoenicians in Tyre. It is the people in the markets. And they say his wisdom is not his own. They say he asked his God for it, and his God gave it to him.

That, I think, is what would have caught her. Not the cleverness. The God.

And then, the book says, she came to prove him with hard questions.

The word for hard questions in the Hebrew is a word that also means riddles. Dark sayings. The kind of question that has a twist in it. In the ancient world, riddles were not only a game for children at parties. They were how wise people tested one another. The Book of Proverbs says, at its very beginning, that it was written so that a person could understand a proverb and a figure, the words of the wise and their riddles. A riddle was a little locked box, and wisdom was the key.

The most famous riddle in the Bible, by the way, belongs to Samson, who is not otherwise famous for his brains. At his wedding feast, he made a bet with his guests that they could not answer this. Out of the eater came something to eat. Out of the strong came something sweet. And nobody could, because the answer was a lion he had killed, and a swarm of bees that had made honey in it afterwards, and he was the only one who had seen it. Which is, I think we can all agree, cheating. That is not a riddle. That is just knowing something nobody else knows.

The queen of Sheba, I suspect, did not cheat.

And here is what I find wonderful about her. She did not send a messenger. She did not send a letter with a list of questions and wait a year for the answers. She did not send an ambassador to take notes. She decided to go herself. All that way. Two months of sand and wells and wind. Because some questions you can only ask face to face. You need to see the person's eyes while they answer, to know whether they mean it.

So she called for the camels.

* * *

The Bible says she came to Jerusalem with a very great company. A very great train, the old translations say. Camels that bore spices, and very much gold, and precious stones.

Let's go with them for a little while.

Imagine the caravan setting out from the high valleys of Saba in the cool of the early morning. The camels groaning as they get up, the way camels do, front half and back half separately, as if each half has to be persuaded on its own. The bells. The packs of frankincense, wrapped and tied, the whole caravan carrying a faint sweet smell of resin wherever it went, so that people in the villages along the way could probably smell it coming before they saw it.

And the gold, packed very carefully, and watched very carefully, by men who had been told what would happen to them if any of it went missing.

And somewhere in the middle of it all, the queen.

I like to imagine that she did not spend the whole journey hidden behind curtains. I like to think that some evenings, when they had made camp by a well and the cooking fires were lit, she walked out a little way beyond the tents and looked up. Because if you have never seen the sky over the Arabian desert at night, far from any town, I can tell you that it is not like the sky you know. There are so many stars that the familiar shapes get lost among them. The Milky Way is not a faint smudge. It is a river.

And I like to imagine her standing out there, under that river of stars, going over her questions. Saying them over to herself. Changing a word here and there. Wondering if the king in the north would see the twist in this one, or that one.

And perhaps, some nights, wondering about the other questions. The ones she had not written down. The ones that were not clever at all.

Day after day they went north. Past wells with names that the caravan masters knew the way you know the names of the stations on your train line. Past oases where there were date palms and water and small towns that lived by selling water and dates to people like them. Past long stretches where there was nothing at all, and the camels walked, and the men walked beside them, and nobody talked very much because talking makes you thirsty.

The old merchant, if he came, would have pointed things out. That rock there, Your Majesty, looks like a sleeping lion, if you squint. That well is sweet. That well is not sweet, do not let the camels drink from it, it gives them ideas.

And then, at last, after weeks and weeks, the land began to change. A little more green. Hills instead of plains. Villages closer together. Olive trees. And one morning, someone at the front of the caravan pointed, and there, on its ridge, catching the sun, was Jerusalem.

* * *

Now imagine being in Jerusalem that day.

Imagine you are a small boy, say, minding your family's goats on the slope outside the city wall, and you look up and see coming down the road from the south the longest line of camels you have ever seen in your life. And the wind changes, and suddenly the whole hillside smells like the inside of the temple on a feast day.

You would run. You would leave the goats, I'm afraid. You would run in through the gate shouting, and within the hour, the whole city would be on the walls and the rooftops, watching.

Everyone would have heard of Sheba. It was the land of incense, the land at the bottom of the world. But almost nobody in Jerusalem would ever have seen anyone from there. And now here they were. And at their head, not a king. A queen.

I imagine there was a great deal of talking on the rooftops.

Is that her? Which one is her? That one, with the guards. No, that one. How much gold do you suppose is in those packs? Don't point, it's rude. I'm not pointing, I'm gesturing.

And the queen came to Solomon, the book says. And she communed with him of all that was in her heart.

* * *

Here is the part of the story that I most want to tell you, and it is the part the Bible tells us least about.

It says she asked him hard questions. And it says that Solomon told her all her questions. There was not anything hidden from the king which he did not tell her.

And that is all. It does not tell us a single one of the questions.

Isn't that maddening? Here is the greatest riddle contest of the ancient world, the wisest woman of the south against the wisest man of the north, and the book does not record one question. Not one. Later storytellers could not bear it, and they made up riddles for her, and we will come to them at the end. But the Bible keeps quiet. And I want to respect that, so I am not going to put invented riddles in her mouth and pretend they were hers.

But I think the quietness might be the point. Listen again to how the book puts it. She communed with him of all that was in her heart.

Not all that was in her notebook. All that was in her heart.

It is possible to start out asking someone clever questions, to test them, to see if they are as good as people say. And it is possible that somewhere along the way, if the person is really listening, the questions change. You stop asking the questions designed to trip them up, and start asking the questions you actually want answered. The ones you have been carrying around for years. The ones you have never asked anyone, because there was nobody you trusted to answer.

What would a queen's heart questions have been? We do not know. But we can guess at the kinds of things anyone might ask. How do you rule people justly, when every choice hurts someone? How do you know when someone is telling you the truth? What do you do when you are surrounded by people who only tell you what you want to hear? What is your God like? Why does he care about a small kingdom in the hills? Does he care about people at the bottom of the map, too?

And we know what wisdom Solomon had to answer with, because we watched him ask for it. When God appeared to him at Gibeon and said, ask what I shall give you, he did not ask to be clever. He asked for a listening heart, an understanding heart, to judge the people and to tell good from evil. And God gave him a wise and understanding heart.

So when the queen of Sheba asked him all that was in her heart, the thing on the other side of the table was not only a brilliant mind. It was a listening heart. And I think that was what she had come all that way to find, without perhaps quite knowing it herself.

There was nothing hidden from the king, the book says, which he did not explain to her.

I picture them talking for days. Not in one great dramatic session, with trumpets, but over many conversations, in the cool of the mornings and in the evenings, with the lamps lit. Her questions getting quieter. His answers getting simpler. The court officials, who had been very excited at first, gradually getting bored and drifting off to have their lunch, because it was no longer a contest. It was just two people talking, about things that mattered.

That, at least, is how I picture it. The Bible leaves us room.

* * *

And then the book does something interesting. It stops talking about what she heard, and starts talking about what she saw.

When the queen of Sheba had seen all the wisdom of Solomon, it says, and the house that he had built, and the food of his table, and the sitting of his servants, and the attendance of his ministers, and their clothing, and his cupbearers, and the way he went up to the house of the Lord, there was no more breath in her.

That is a strange list, isn't it? The wisdom, yes. The house, fine, it was a very fine house. But the food on the table? The way the servants sat? What they wore? The cupbearers?

I think what she saw was not only clever answers in a conversation. She saw wisdom running all the way through a place. Into the kitchens. Into the way the servants were seated, and treated. Into the way the officials did their jobs. The way a household works tells you a great deal about the person at the head of it. You can be very charming in conversation and run a household where everyone is frightened and miserable. And she looked, and she saw that this was not that kind of house. At least, not then. Not yet.

And at the end of the list, the last thing. The way he went up to the house of the Lord. The Hebrew here can be read two ways, and translations differ. It might mean the stairway, the ascent by which the king went up from his palace to the temple. Or it might mean the burnt offerings that he offered there. Either way, it points in the same direction. Up. To the God whose name she had heard about at the very beginning. After the table and the servants and the clothes, the last thing on the list is the king going to pray.

And there was no more breath in her. She was, as we would say, breathless. Her breath was taken away.

And she said to the king, it was a true report that I heard in my own land, of your words and of your wisdom. But I did not believe the words until I came and my own eyes had seen it. And look. The half was not told me. Your wisdom and your prosperity are greater than the report that I heard.

I love that. The half was not told me. She came all that way to find out whether the stories were exaggerated, and she found out that they were. They were exaggerated downwards.

And then she said something even better.

Happy are your men. Happy are these your servants, who stand continually before you, and hear your wisdom.

Isn't that a generous thing for a visiting queen to say? She does not say, happy are you, King Solomon, with all your gold. She says, happy are the people who get to work for you. Happy are the servants. Happy are the ones who stand by the door all day and get to overhear.

And then, finally, the most important thing she says. Blessed be the Lord your God, who delighted in you, to set you on the throne of Israel. Because the Lord loved Israel for ever, therefore he made you king, to do justice and righteousness.

There it is. She came because she heard about the name of the Lord, and she ends by blessing the name of the Lord. And notice what she says the king is for. Not for gold. Not for buildings, or even for wisdom for its own sake. He was made king because God loved his people, so that he would do justice and righteousness. A foreign queen from the bottom of the map has understood, in a few days, what a king is for, better than some of the kings of Israel ever did.

* * *

Then came the presents, because in the ancient world, a visit between royals without presents would have been like turning up to a birthday party with nothing in your hands.

She gave the king a hundred and twenty talents of gold. Now, nobody is completely sure how heavy a talent was, and the scholars argue about it, but if it was something like thirty kilograms, which is a common guess, then that is around three and a half tonnes of gold. Picture a small truck. Made of gold. That is roughly what the queen left on the king's doorstep.

And precious stones. And spices. And here the book adds a lovely line. There never again came such an abundance of spices as these which the queen of Sheba gave to King Solomon.

I like to think of the storerooms of Jerusalem smelling of her visit for years afterwards. That long after she had gone home, someone would open a cedar chest in the palace and a breath of frankincense would come out, and an old servant would say, ah, that's from the queen.

And then, right in the middle of the story, the book does a funny thing. It wanders off, the way I do. It says, by the way, the ships of Hiram, the king of Tyre, Solomon's friend, who brought gold from a place called Ophir, also brought great quantities of almug wood and precious stones. And the king made the almug wood into supports for the house of the Lord and for the king's house, and into lyres and harps for the singers. And no such almug wood has ever come again, or been seen, to this day.

Nobody today is quite sure what almug wood was. Some think it was a kind of sandalwood. Some think something else. But I love that, in the middle of a story about gold and spices, the Bible stops to tell us about the wood they made the harps from. As if, in all that treasure, the thing worth remembering was that the singers in the house of the Lord got beautiful new instruments.

And then the king gave the queen of Sheba everything she wanted, whatever she asked, besides what he gave her out of his royal bounty.

So she left with her hands full, too. The book does not tell us what she asked for. Perhaps gifts for her people. Perhaps something from the house of the Lord, though I would not dare to guess what. Perhaps only more answers. Whatever she asked, she got.

And then she turned, the book says, and went to her own country, she and her servants.

That is all. One sentence. No farewell speech. No tearful goodbyes at the gate. She turned and went home.

Imagine the caravan going south again.

Lighter now, the camels, without all that gold and spice. Lighter, I like to think, the queen, too. The same wells, in the opposite order. The same rock that looks like a sleeping lion, if you squint. The same river of stars at night over the desert.

And the queen standing out there again, some evening, a little way beyond the tents. Not going over her questions this time. She has asked them. She is going over the answers.

We do not know what happened to her after that. The Bible never mentions her again by herself. She goes home, and she goes out of the story, and we are not told whether she reigned for a long time or a short one, whether she told her people about the God of Israel, or what she thought about, in her old age, when she remembered the king's house and the food on the table and the way he went up to pray.

The people who came after could not leave it there, of course. A thousand years later, the Jewish historian Josephus, writing in Rome, said she was queen of Egypt and Ethiopia, and gave her a name, Nikaule. Jewish storytellers in later centuries filled in the riddles the Bible left out, and made a great game of them. And in Ethiopia, there grew up a long and beloved national story, written down in a book called the Kebra Nagast, the Glory of the Kings, which calls her Makeda, and tells how she and Solomon had a son called Menelik, who became the first of a line of Ethiopian kings. That story means a great deal to the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and to the people of Ethiopia. But it is a tradition from long after the Bible, and we should call it what it is, a legend, and a much-loved one, not history. The Bible says only that she came, and asked, and saw, and blessed the Lord, and went home.

But the Bible is not quite finished with her.

There is a psalm, Psalm seventy-two, a prayer for the king, that says, the kings of Tarshish and of the islands shall bring presents, the kings of Sheba and Seba shall offer gifts. All kings shall fall down before him, all nations shall serve him. And to him shall be given of the gold of Sheba.

And the prophet Isaiah, looking far ahead to a day when the light of the Lord would rise over Jerusalem and all the nations would come to it, says this. Gold and frankincense. Carried on camels. From the far country. To praise the Lord.

Does that remind you of anyone?

Because hundreds of years after that, according to Saint Matthew's Gospel, there came some wise men from the east, following a star. And when they found the child with Mary his mother, in a house in Bethlehem, they knelt down, and opened their treasures, and gave him gifts. Gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.

Christians have always heard Isaiah and the psalm in that story. In the Catholic Church, on the feast of the Epiphany, the first reading at Mass is that very passage from Isaiah, the camels and the gold and the frankincense, and the psalm that is sung is Psalm seventy-two, the gifts from Sheba. The queen is not named in either of them. But it is hard to hear them and not see her caravan, a long way off, coming up the road from the south, smelling of incense.

And there is one more thing, and it is the most important of all.

Our Lord himself talked about her.

Once, when people were crowding round him and demanding that he prove himself with a sign, Jesus said this. The queen of the south will rise up at the judgment with the people of this generation, and condemn them. Because she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon. And look, something greater than Solomon is here.

Think about that. Of all the people in the Old Testament he might have held up as an example, he chose her. A foreign queen. A woman from the bottom of the map. Not because she was perfect, but because she wanted to know. She heard a rumour about the wisdom of God, and she did not sit at home and say, well, that's probably exaggerated. And Jesus said to the people standing in front of him, who had not had to travel anywhere at all, she went all that way for Solomon. And someone greater than Solomon is standing right here in front of you.

So I think, if the queen of Sheba has a lesson for us at bedtime, it is something like this. Bring your hard questions. All of them. The clever ones and the quiet ones. All that is in your heart. You do not need a caravan, or two months in the desert, or three and a half tonnes of gold. Someone greater than Solomon is here. And there is nothing hidden from him.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Her coming and her hard questions are verses one to three. What she saw is verses four and five. Her speech is verses six to nine. The gifts are verse ten, Hiram's ships and the almug wood are verses eleven and twelve, and the king's gifts and her going home are verse thirteen. Solomon's wisdom and the people who came to hear him are from chapter four of the same book, and the listening heart from chapter three. Samson's riddle is in Judges fourteen.

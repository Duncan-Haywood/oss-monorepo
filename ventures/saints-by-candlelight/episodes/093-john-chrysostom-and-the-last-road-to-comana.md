Imagine a small stone chapel beside a road, in the hills of what is now northern Turkey, very early on a September morning.

It is quiet. There is a smell of dust and wax and wet grass. Outside, two soldiers are arguing, in low voices, about whether to leave now or in an hour. Inside, on a bench, a very thin old man is sitting very straight. He is not really old, not by our reckoning. He is about sixty. But he has walked for three months, bareheaded, in sun and rain, and he looks a hundred.

He is taking off his travelling clothes, slowly, one piece at a time. And he is putting on clean white ones. Then he takes the clothes he has been wearing, the dusty ones, and gives them away, every last thing, to the people standing by. Even his shoes.

The soldiers, outside, stop arguing. Something about the quiet in the chapel makes them stop.

A few nights ago on this show we met Saint Basil the Great, and his sister Macrina, and his best friend Gregory, the one who only wanted a quiet room. Tonight we meet the man who completes the set. In the Orthodox Church there is a feast at the end of January called the Three Holy Hierarchs, and it keeps three bishops together: Basil, and Gregory, and John.

The story goes, and it is a lovely story whether or not it happened quite like this, that in the eleventh century the people of Constantinople got into a terrific argument about which of the three was the greatest. Some said Basil, for his wisdom and his soup kitchens. Some said Gregory, for his theology. Some said John, for his preaching. People formed parties about it. They called each other names. And at last, the story says, the three saints appeared together in a dream to a bishop and told him, more or less, will you all please stop it, we are equal before God, and we are not arguing, so why are you? And the feast was made so that the three would be kept together.

Which tells you something about the people of Constantinople. They could argue about anything. John found that out.

John did not come from Cappadocia, like the other two. He came from Antioch. And Antioch, in the three hundred and forties, when John was born, was one of the great cities of the world. It sat in what is now the very south of Turkey, near the Syrian border, on a river called the Orontes, with mountains behind it and the sea a day's ride away. It had a great colonnaded street, miles long, with covered walks on both sides, so you could shop in the rain. It had baths and theatres and a racecourse and a palace. It had street lamps, which very few cities had. People in Antioch liked to point that out.

It was a lively, loud, clever, quarrelsome city. The people loved horse races and the theatre and nicknames. They made up rude songs about emperors. It was also one of the first great Christian cities. It was in Antioch, the Acts of the Apostles tells us, that the followers of Jesus were first called Christians.

John's father was called Secundus, and he served on the staff of the army's commander in the East, a man of good position. He died when John was a baby. John's mother, Anthusa, was a widow at about twenty years old, with a little daughter and a newborn son.

Now, a young, well-off widow in Antioch was expected to marry again. Everybody expected it. It was the sensible thing. Anthusa did not. She stayed a widow, and she brought up her children herself, and she managed the family property herself, which John later said was very hard, with stewards cheating her and tax men bullying her. She stayed a widow for the rest of her life.

* * *

John was clever. Very clever. And he had a voice.

When he was a young man, his mother sent him to study with the most famous teacher in Antioch, a man called Libanius. Libanius was not a Christian. He was one of the last great pagan teachers of the ancient world, a master of rhetoric, which is the art of speaking well in public. If you wanted to be a lawyer, or a politician, or anything at all in the Roman world where you had to stand up and persuade people, you went to a teacher like Libanius.

John tells a little story about his teacher. Libanius once asked his students about their families, and when he heard that John's mother had been a widow since she was twenty, and had never married again, the old pagan threw up his hands and said, goodness me, what women these Christians have.

I like Libanius for that. He was not a Christian and never became one. But he knew something remarkable when he saw it.

There is another story, told by the historian Sozomen about fifty years later, that when Libanius was dying, his friends asked him which of his students should take over his school. And he said John. If the Christians had not stolen him.

We do not know if he really said that. But it is the kind of thing people remembered.

Because the Christians did steal him. Or rather, God did. John was baptised as a young man, which was normal then, and something in him changed. He gave up the law courts. He began to study the Scriptures with a teacher called Diodore. And he wanted, more than anything, to go up into the hills outside Antioch, where there were monks living in caves and huts, and pray.

And here comes one of the most touching scenes in all of John's writings. He tells it himself, in a book he wrote about the priesthood.

His mother knew what he was planning. And one day she took him by the hand, and led him into her own room, and sat him down beside the bed where she had given birth to him. And she wept. And she told him, in John's telling, everything that a widow had to go through alone. And then she said: I ask only one thing of you. Do not make me a widow a second time. Wait until I am gone. It may not be long. When I am buried, beside your father, then go wherever you like. But while I am alive, stay with me.

And John stayed.

He lived at home, very quietly, praying and fasting and studying, until his mother died. And only then did he go up into the hills.

* * *

He spent about six years up there. Four years with an old monk from the Syrian hills, learning how to pray, and then two years alone, in a cave.

Now, I have to tell you that John overdid it. He fasted too hard, and he hardly slept, and the old writers say he barely lay down at all. It was very cold up there in the winter, and he did not keep warm. And at the end of the two years his stomach and his kidneys were so damaged that he had to come down to the city to be looked after. He was never really well again for the rest of his life. He ate very little, always. He could not eat rich food. He could not drink wine. He was always thin and always pale, and he got cold easily.

Which, as we will see, became important later. People thought he was being unsociable when he would not come to their dinners. Really, his stomach would not let him.

Back in Antioch, the bishop, Meletius, made him a deacon. That was in three hundred and eighty-one, the same year Meletius went to Constantinople and died there, during the great council where Gregory, from our last episode, was so unhappy. Then a few years later the next bishop of Antioch, Flavian, made John a priest. And Flavian gave him a job.

Preach.

* * *

For twelve years, John preached in Antioch. And the city had never heard anything like it.

He preached long sermons. An hour, sometimes two. He preached on whole books of the Bible, verse by verse, sermon after sermon, week after week, so that the people of Antioch went through all of Genesis with him, and Matthew, and John, and Paul's letters. People came to hear him the way people today go to hear a great singer. They clapped. In church. He told them off for clapping, and they clapped at that.

What made him so good? Partly the voice, and the training, all those years with Libanius. But mostly it was that he talked about real life. He did not just talk about doctrine, although he knew his doctrine. He talked about how people actually lived. How husbands should treat their wives. How parents should bring up their children, which he thought was more important than almost anything. How the rich spent money on silver chamber pots while poor people froze on the steps of the church. He was very funny about the silver chamber pots. He was not at all funny about the poor people on the steps.

He said things like this. You honour the body of Christ in church, on the altar, with silk and gold. Good. But do not then walk out past the body of Christ shivering in the porch, without a coat, and do nothing. It is the same Lord who said, this is my body, and who said, I was hungry and you gave me no food. That is our own paraphrase, but it is very close to what he said, and it still stops people in their tracks.

And then, in three hundred and eighty-seven, something happened that made John famous in a different way.

The emperor, Theodosius, the same soldierly emperor who led Gregory into the great church, announced a new tax. The people of Antioch were furious. A crowd gathered. It got out of hand. And the crowd pulled down the statues of the emperor and his family, the bronze statues that stood in the public squares, and dragged them through the streets.

Now, in the Roman Empire, that was not vandalism. That was treason. The statue of the emperor was, in law, a bit like the emperor himself. To drag it in the street was to insult him to his face. And the morning after, the people of Antioch woke up and realised what they had done, and they were terrified. Soldiers came. Arrests began. There was talk that the whole city would be punished. People fled into the hills.

The old bishop, Flavian, set off in the middle of winter, ill as he was, to ride hundreds of miles to Constantinople and beg the emperor for mercy.

And John, left behind, preached. It was Lent. And every day or so, through that whole frightened Lent, he stood up in front of a church full of terrified people and spoke to them. Not about the statues, mostly. About fear, and about death, and about what is truly worth being afraid of, and what is not. He told them that the soldiers could take their houses but not their souls. He told them to stop swearing oaths, which was one of his favourite subjects, and the people of Antioch were dreadful for it. He kept them going.

Monks came down from the hills, too, men who had not been in the city for years, and stood in front of the imperial officials and pleaded for the people. One of them, an old hermit called Macedonius, who could barely speak Greek, told the officials to tell the emperor that statues can be made again, but men cannot.

And at Easter the news came. Flavian had reached the emperor. The emperor had forgiven the city.

John's sermons from that Lent are still read. They are called the Homilies on the Statues. And after that Lent, everyone in the eastern half of the empire knew the name of the preacher of Antioch.

That turned out to be a mixed blessing.

* * *

In the autumn of three hundred and ninety-seven, the bishop of Constantinople died. Constantinople was the capital, the emperor's city, the New Rome on the Bosphorus. Choosing its bishop was a matter of state. And the most powerful man at court, the emperor's chief chamberlain, a man called Eutropius, decided that the new bishop should be the famous preacher from Antioch.

There was one difficulty. The people of Antioch would never let him go. They would riot. And they had form.

So the government did something that sounds like the plot of a comedy. The governor in Antioch sent John a polite message asking him to come and meet him at a little martyr's shrine just outside the city gates. John went. The governor put him in a carriage. And the carriage did not stop. It drove, with John inside it, all the way to the capital, changing horses as it went. Nobody in Antioch knew where he was until he was gone.

I like to picture John looking out of the window of that carriage, somewhere on the second day, saying, I don't suppose there is any chance of turning round?

He was consecrated bishop of Constantinople in February of three hundred and ninety-eight. The bishop who consecrated him was the bishop of Alexandria, a strong and clever man called Theophilus, who had wanted a different candidate, and who was not pleased. Remember that name. It comes back.

* * *

So now John was bishop of the capital of the world. And he started, straight away, doing things that bishops of the capital did not usually do.

He looked at the household accounts. The bishop's palace, he found, was spending a fortune. Fine dinners for important guests. Expensive furniture. Silver and marble. John sold a good deal of it. He cut the dinners. He ate alone, very simply, partly because of his poor stomach, partly because he did not want to spend the Church's money on feasts. And he took the money he had saved and built hospitals for the sick, and houses for strangers, and gave to the poor.

The poor of the city loved him. Many of the rich did not. And the clergy who had enjoyed the dinners were very, very annoyed. He also told the clergy to stop living with unrelated women who kept house for them, and told the monks who wandered around the city to go back to their monasteries, and told the wealthy widows to stop wearing so much makeup. He was not, I think it is fair to say, a diplomat.

But he was loved. The crowds came to hear him, just as they had in Antioch, and he had to move his preaching chair down into the middle of the church so that everyone could hear. He started night processions, with hymns and candles, through the streets of the city. And the empress, Eudoxia, who in those early days admired him very much, gave silver crosses to carry in the processions, with candles on them, so the night streets of Constantinople were full of little moving lights and singing.

That is a lovely picture. Hold on to it. It did not last, but it was real.

* * *

Then, in the year three hundred and ninety-nine, the chamberlain Eutropius, the man who had had John carried off in the carriage, fell from power.

He had made many enemies, and the empress was one of them, and one day it all came crashing down. Eutropius ran for his life. And where did he run? To the great church. To the altar. In those days a person who held on to the altar could claim sanctuary, and the law could not touch him. Which was a little awkward, because Eutropius himself had tried to have that law weakened, not long before.

Soldiers came to the church to drag him away. And John stood in front of the altar and would not let them.

The next day, a Sunday, the church was packed. Eutropius was still there, crouched under the altar, holding on to one of its pillars, shaking. And John climbed up into his chair and began to preach, with the fallen man in full view of everyone. And his first words were from the book of Ecclesiastes. Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.

He talked about what power is worth. How yesterday this man was the second most powerful person in the world, and people bowed to him in the street, and today, look at him. Where are the friends now? Where are the flatterers? And then, when the people had been well and truly frightened by that, he turned it round. And now, he said, show mercy. This is a church. Christ forgave the people who crucified him. Will you not forgive this man?

Eutropius was not killed that day. Later on, he left the church, and was exiled, and in the end he was executed anyway, which is a sad story. But John had kept his promise. Nobody was taken from the altar while he stood in front of it.

* * *

Now. I am going to go quickly through the next part, because it is a sad and tangled part, full of quarrelling, and it is nearly bedtime.

John made enemies. Some of them were people at court, who did not like being preached at. Some of them were bishops, who did not like being told off. And one of them, I am sorry to say, was the empress. She had admired him. But he preached about the vanity of rich women, and the greed of the powerful, and she came to believe that he meant her. Perhaps he did. Perhaps he did not. People told her he did, at any rate, and that was enough.

And then the bishop of Alexandria, Theophilus, the one who had wanted a different candidate, came to Constantinople. There had been a quarrel between Theophilus and some monks of Egypt, and the monks had come to John for help, and Theophilus came to deal with it. Theophilus is honoured as a saint in the Coptic Church of Egypt, and we will be fair to him and say only that he was a strong man used to winning, and he had no love for John, and he found a great many people in Constantinople who felt the same.

In the summer of four hundred and three, Theophilus held a meeting of bishops at a villa called the Oak, just across the water from the capital. They summoned John to answer charges. Some of the charges were silly. One was that he ate alone. John refused to go before a meeting packed with his enemies. And so they deposed him, and the emperor, Arcadius, who was a gentle, tired, rather weak man, and whose wife was the empress Eudoxia, signed the order to send him away.

John left quietly, to stop the people from rioting.

And then, the very next night, something happened in the palace. The old writers do not agree on what it was. One of them says there was an earthquake. Another says only that there was some disaster in the imperial bedchamber. Whatever it was, it frightened the empress very badly, and she sent at once to call John back. The crowds carried him into the city with torches, singing.

For a few months, it was peace.

And then it was not. There was a silver statue of the empress, put up on a tall column right outside the great church, and the celebrations around it were noisy, with music and dancing that could be heard inside during the services, and John complained about it from the pulpit. And it all began again.

At Easter in the year four hundred and four, on the night of Holy Saturday, when hundreds of people were being baptised, soldiers were sent into the church to break up the services. People were hurt. The newly baptised ran out into the night still dripping, and the clergy carried on the Easter services wherever they could, first in a great public bath and later in the open fields outside the city.

And in June, the order came again. Exile. And this time it was meant to be for good.

* * *

John said goodbye in the baptistery of the great church.

He said goodbye to the clergy first, and then he went to the deaconesses. Among them was his dearest friend in Constantinople. Her name was Olympias.

Olympias is a saint too, in both traditions, and she deserves a whole night of her own one day. She came from one of the richest families in the empire. She was married very young, and widowed very young, and she refused to marry again, even when the old emperor Theodosius himself tried to arrange it, and confiscated her property to make her change her mind. She would not. In the end she got her property back, and she spent nearly all of it on the poor, and on the Church, and on prisoners, and on exiles. She was made a deaconess, and she lived with a community of women in a house right beside the great church. She looked after John. She made sure he ate something. She sent him food that his stomach could manage. And she was, perhaps, the only person in Constantinople who could tell him to calm down.

And now he took her hand, and the hands of the other women who served the church with her, and he said to them, more or less, this. Come here, my daughters, and listen to me. Things with me are coming to an end, I think. I have finished my course. Perhaps you will not see my face again. But I ask you this one thing. Do not let your love for the Church grow cold. And whoever they make bishop in my place, if he comes honestly, without plotting for it, then obey him as you would obey me. And remember me in your prayers.

That is the old account, in our own words. And the women wept, and fell at his feet, and he had one of the priests lead them away so that the crowd would not see them and riot.

And then he slipped out by a side door, on to a small boat, and was gone.

That same night, a fire started in the great church. Nobody knows how. It burned the church to the ground, and the wind carried it across the square to the great senate house, which burned too. John's enemies said his friends had done it. His friends said his enemies had done it. Probably it was neither. But it meant that for years afterwards his friends were arrested and questioned and fined, Olympias among them, and she bore it all with great courage, and paid the fines, and would not take communion from the man they had put in John's place.

And John, far away, did not hear about most of it for weeks.

[pause]

* * *

Now we come to the part of the story I most wanted to tell you. The roads.

John was sent to a little town called Cucusus. It was about as far from Constantinople as you could be sent while still being in the empire. It was high up in the mountains of eastern Asia Minor, in what was called Armenia then, and is the middle of Turkey now. It took him seventy days to get there.

He went with a guard of soldiers. He was ill most of the way, with fever. He wrote letters to Olympias from the road, and some of those letters survive, and so we can follow him across the map.

Here is where I want to bring in someone who is not in the old books. The old books say that soldiers guarded John on his journeys. And one of the old writers, Palladius, says that on the very last journey, one of the two guards was rough, and the other was kinder, in secret, when the rough one was not looking. We do not know his name. We do not know anything else about him. So tonight, for the length of this story, we will give him a name, and we will call him Theodore, and everything we say about him from here on is our own invention.

Imagine him, then. Nineteen or twenty. A soldier from a village somewhere in Thrace, who had joined the army because it meant a pair of boots and regular bread. He had never been inside the great church. He had never heard John preach. He had been told that the prisoner was a troublemaking bishop who had insulted the empress, and that the job was to get him to the mountains and come back.

And on the first evening, when they stopped at an inn, the troublemaking bishop asked Theodore how his feet were.

Theodore did not know what to say. Nobody had ever asked him how his feet were.

* * *

They crossed into Asia. They went through Nicaea, where the great council had been held eighty years before, and on across the high, bare plains of Galatia, in the heat of July. John was feverish. Some days he could hardly sit on his mule.

They came to Caesarea in Cappadocia. And here, if you have been listening to this show for a while, you may feel a little shiver. Because Caesarea was Basil's city. Basil, who had built the soup kitchen and the hospital outside the walls, the place they called the Basiliad. Basil had been dead for twenty-five years by now. But the hospital was still there.

And at Caesarea, John was very ill indeed. And some people were kind to him. Doctors came, and looked after him for nothing. Ordinary people came, crowding to see him, and brought him things. John wrote to Olympias that the people of Caesarea treated him wonderfully.

The bishop of Caesarea, I am sorry to say, was not one of them. He did not want trouble. And one morning a crowd of rough monks turned up outside the house where John was lying, shouting that they would burn it down if he did not leave the city at once. The soldiers themselves were frightened.

And then a lady called Seleucia, a wealthy woman of the city, sent word that John should come to her country house, outside the town, where he would be safe. And he went, and she looked after him there. For a little while.

But even she was frightened, in the end. Word came to her house, in the middle of the night, that the monks were coming. And the steward woke John and told him that he had to leave. Now. In the dark.

John wrote about that night afterwards, to Olympias. It was the middle of the night. There was no moon. The road went up into the mountains. And there were raiders in those mountains, a fierce hill people called the Isaurians, who robbed travellers, and so they did not dare to light torches, in case the raiders saw them. And somewhere on that dark mountain road, John's mule stumbled, and John fell off, onto the stones, in the dark, with fever.

He told Olympias he thought he would die. And then he got up, and was helped back on, and they went on.

In our story, it is Theodore who helps him up. He finds John by feel, in the dark, lying on the stones. He gets an arm under his shoulders. John is very light. Theodore is surprised how light he is. Like lifting a bundle of sticks.

And John, sitting in the road, gets his breath back, and Theodore hears him whisper something. Theodore leans down to hear. And what John is whispering, in the pitch dark, on a mountain road, after falling off a mule, with a fever, in exile, is this.

Glory to God for all things.

* * *

That was his phrase. His friends knew it well. He said it all the time. When things were good, glory to God for all things. When things were bad, glory to God for all things. It was not something he said because he was cheerful by nature. He was not especially cheerful by nature. It was something he decided, again and again, to say.

They came to Cucusus at last, at the end of the summer. And Cucusus was, to everyone's surprise, kind.

The bishop of the little town came out to meet him and welcomed him like a brother. A local gentleman called Dioscorus, who had heard that John was coming, had sent servants along the road ahead to meet him, and gave him his own house to live in, and fussed over him, and made sure he had everything. There were no crowds of angry monks. There were mountains, and clean air, and quiet.

It was very cold. Cucusus is high up, and the winters were fierce. John, with his poor stomach and his thin blood, felt the cold terribly. He wrote to Olympias that he spent the winter shut up in one room, with a fire going all the time, wrapped in blankets, and he still could not get warm. And the raiders came down from the hills, and once or twice everybody had to flee to a fortress. But he was alive, and he was among kind people.

And he wrote. Oh, how he wrote.

In our story, Theodore stays on at Cucusus for the winter, with the guard. He brings in firewood. He sits by the door. And every evening he watches the thin man at the table, with a lamp, and a pen, and a stack of letters, writing and writing and writing.

* * *

More than two hundred of the letters John wrote in exile survive. Two hundred! He wrote to bishops in Italy and in Gaul, asking them to help. The bishop of Rome, Innocent, and other bishops of the West took his side and wrote and sent messengers to the emperor on his behalf. He wrote to his friends in Antioch, and in Constantinople. He wrote to missionaries he had sent to the Goths, and to the people of Phoenicia, and organised them, by letter, from the top of a mountain. He wrote to people who had been arrested for being his friends, to comfort them.

And he wrote, above all, to Olympias.

Seventeen of his letters to her survive. And they are some of the most beautiful letters that have come down to us from the ancient world. Because Olympias, back in Constantinople, was not well. She had been arrested, and fined, and questioned, and her friends had been taken away, and she had fallen into what John calls despondency. A heaviness. A sadness that would not lift. We might call it something else today.

And John, who was the one in exile, the one freezing in a mountain town, the one with the fever and the ruined stomach, spent his letters comforting her.

He told her, in one of them, to imagine a great storm at sea. The waves as high as mountains. Ships sinking. Sailors drowning. The whole sea turned to darkness. And then he said, more or less, this is how things look now, I know. I know. But do not lose heart. God is the pilot. And he will calm the storm, not always when we want, but when it is best.

He told her, in another, that he was doing well, he was well looked after, the people were kind, the air was good. Partly that was true. Partly, I think, he was making it sound better than it was, because he did not want her to worry.

He sent her, too, a little book, a short treatise, with a title that is a whole sermon in itself. No one can be harmed except by himself. His point was that no exile, no illness, no loss of money or reputation, can touch the part of a person that really matters. Only our own choices can do that. The soldiers could take him to Cucusus. They could not take him away from God.

In our story, one night, Theodore asks him what he is writing.

A letter to a friend, John says. She is sad.

You are the one in exile, Theodore says.

I know, says John. Funny, isn't it?

* * *

John was at Cucusus for about three years, with a stretch at a nearby fortress called Arabissus when the raiders were bad. And here is the thing that, in the end, undid him.

People kept coming to see him.

His friends from Antioch, which was only a week or two away over the mountains, came in a steady stream. They brought him money and food and news. They sat with him. They carried his letters away. The little town of Cucusus, at the edge of the world, became a kind of pilgrimage. And when this news reached Constantinople, John's enemies were alarmed. He was supposed to be forgotten. Instead, he was more famous in exile than he had been in the capital.

And so, in the year four hundred and seven, a new order came. John was to be moved. Further. Much further. To a place called Pityus, on the far eastern shore of the Black Sea, at the very edge of the empire, in what is now the country of Georgia. It was a tiny, lonely garrison post, by the sea, among the mountains. Nobody would visit him there.

And it was a very long way, and he was very ill, and he was nearly sixty, and they made him walk.

[pause]

* * *

From here on, we follow Palladius, John's friend and biographer, who heard the story from people who were there. And from here on, I am going to slow down.

There were two soldiers, Palladius says. And they had been told, it seems, that if the prisoner died on the road, nobody would mind. One of them, the senior one, marched him hard. In the heat of the summer, he would not let John cover his head, though John was nearly bald by then and the sun burned him. When it rained, the soldier would not let him shelter, and the water ran down his back. When they came to a town with a bath, where a tired old man might have washed and rested, the soldier marched straight past it.

The other one, Palladius says, was kinder. Quietly. When the first was not looking.

In our story, that is Theodore. He has been told to be hard. He has been told this man is a troublemaker. And he has been with him for three years now, and he has watched him, by the lamp, writing letters of comfort to other people, and he has heard him, on the dark mountain road, say glory to God. And Theodore has decided, privately, without saying a word to anyone, that he is on the side of the troublemaker.

So when the senior soldier is up ahead, Theodore walks between John and the sun, so that John is in his shadow. When they stop to drink, Theodore gives John the cup first. When the rain comes, Theodore takes off his own cloak and holds it over John's head for as long as he dares. They do not talk much. There is not much breath for talking.

Three months, they walked. Across the hills of Pontus, through the summer, into September.

* * *

They came, in the middle of September, to a town called Comana, in Pontus. It is a ruin now, not far from the modern town of Tokat. They marched through the town without stopping. And about five or six miles beyond it, by the road, they came to a little church. A martyr's shrine. It was the shrine of a bishop called Basiliscus, who had been martyred in that place nearly a hundred years before, in the time of the persecutions.

They stopped there for the night.

And Palladius tells us that in the night, as John slept, the martyr Basiliscus came to him. And said to him, in the old account, something like this.

Take courage, brother John. Tomorrow we shall be together.

That is Palladius's report, and we tell it as his report. He says, too, that the martyr had appeared the same night to the priest who looked after the shrine, and told him to get a place ready, for brother John was coming.

In the morning, John asked the soldiers if they could stay. Just for the morning. Just until about eleven o'clock. He was not well.

They would not. They marched on.

And they had gone about three or four miles, Palladius says, when it was clear to everyone that he could not go on. He was too ill. Even the hard soldier could see it. And they turned round, and brought him back, slowly, to the little church of Basiliscus.

* * *

And this is where we came in.

He asked for clean clothes. White ones. He was still fasting; he had eaten nothing that morning. And he took off his old travelling clothes, one piece at a time, and put on the white. And he gave away everything he had been wearing, to the people who were there. Even, Palladius says, his shoes.

The priest of the shrine brought him Holy Communion. He received it.

And then he prayed. In front of everyone, Palladius says, he prayed, the prayer that was always on his lips, and at the end of it he said the words his friends knew so well. The words he had said in Antioch, and in Constantinople, and in Caesarea, and on the dark mountain road, and in the cold room at Cucusus with the letters piled on the table.

Glory to God for all things.

And then he said, Amen. And he stretched out his feet. And he went home to God.

It was the fourteenth of September, in the year four hundred and seven. The day, by our calendar, that both East and West keep as the feast of the Holy Cross.

In our story, Theodore is standing at the back of the little church. He does not understand everything that has happened. He has never understood bishops. But he takes off his helmet. And he finds that he is crying. And he finds, also, that the words that come into his mouth, without his deciding, are the same ones.

Glory to God for all things.

They buried John there, beside the martyr Basiliscus, in the little church by the road. Just as the martyr had promised. Together.

[pause]

* * *

And that might have been the end. A troublesome bishop, dead on a road in the middle of nowhere, buried in a village church.

But it was not the end.

The people who loved John did not forget him. Olympias did not forget him; she lived on for a few more years, in exile herself in the end, and she died, as far as we know, still faithful, still generous, still praying for him. His friends in Antioch did not forget him. And the Church, slowly, everywhere, came round. Within about ten years, his name was put back on the lists of bishops prayed for at the altar, in Antioch and then in Constantinople. The quarrel faded. The sermons stayed.

And then, thirty-one years after he died, in the year four hundred and thirty-eight, the emperor of Constantinople sent to Comana for John's body.

The emperor was Theodosius the Second. He was the son of Arcadius, and of Eudoxia. The son of the empress who had sent John away. He had been a small child of three when it all happened. His mother had died young, the same autumn that John went into exile.

They carried John's body in procession, all the way back across Asia Minor, the way he had come. And when the coffin reached the city, it was met with crowds and boats and torches, the whole city out on the water, just like the night of his first return. And the emperor, the historian Theodoret tells us, came to the coffin, and knelt, and laid his face against it. And asked John to forgive his father and his mother, who had sinned against him without knowing what they did.

I think that is one of the most moving things in the whole story. A son, kneeling, asking forgiveness for his parents. And a saint who had spent his whole life preaching forgiveness, who had stood in front of an altar to protect the man who had kidnapped him, being asked for it at last.

John was buried in the church of the Holy Apostles, where the emperors were buried. And there he stayed for nearly eight hundred years.

* * *

It was only after he died that people began to call him by the name we use. Chrysostom. It is Greek, and it means golden mouth. Because of the preaching. And it stuck so well that today, if you say John Chrysostom, everyone knows who you mean, and if you say just John of Constantinople, almost nobody does.

His sermons, hundreds of them, were copied and copied and copied, in Greek and in Latin and in Syriac and in Armenian and in Slavonic, and he became one of the most read writers of the whole Christian world. In the Catholic Church he is one of the Doctors of the Church, the great teachers, and the patron saint of preachers.

And in the Orthodox Church, almost every Sunday, in almost every church, the main service, the Divine Liturgy, is the one that bears his name. The Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom. Tradition has always credited it to him. Scholars think it grew over time, as all liturgies do, around a core that may go back to him. Either way, his name is said, and his prayers are prayed, by millions of people every week.

And on Easter night, in Orthodox churches all over the world, at the end of the long, joyful midnight service, the priest reads out a short sermon that tradition also credits to John. It is about the parable of the workers in the vineyard, who all get the same wage, whether they came at the first hour or the eleventh. And it says, more or less, come in, all of you, everyone, the ones who fasted and the ones who did not, the ones who came early and the ones who came at the very last minute. The table is full. Nobody goes away hungry. Christ is risen, and life reigns.

Which is not a bad thing to be remembered for. Not bad at all, for a thin man with a bad stomach who was never quite allowed to finish his dinner.

* * *

In the thirteenth century, his relics were carried west, to Rome, and they rested in Saint Peter's for a very long time.

And then, in November of the year two thousand and four, Pope John Paul the Second gave a large part of them back to the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople, Bartholomew, together with the relics of Saint Gregory the Theologian, whom we met in the last episode. They were carried back into the city on the Bosphorus, and laid in the Patriarch's church. The Three Holy Hierarchs, or two of them, at any rate, home again.

So if you have had a long day. If things have not gone the way you wanted. If you have been misunderstood, or blamed for things you did not do, or sent somewhere you did not want to go. If you are tired, and cold, and a long way from home. You could think of John. Not the golden mouth in the great church, with the crowds clapping. The thin man on the dark road, sitting on the stones after falling off his mule, getting his breath back. And the words he chose to say, when nobody was listening but a young soldier and God.

Glory to God for all things.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

[pause]

* * *

Now the notes for the curious.

The main source for John's exile and death is the Dialogue written by his friend Palladius, a bishop who was himself exiled for supporting him. It is a passionate book, written to defend John, and it is not always fair to his enemies. The church historians Socrates and Sozomen, writing about forty years after John's death, give fuller accounts of his time in Constantinople, and Socrates is rather more critical of him. Theodoret tells of the return of his body. John's own letters from exile, especially the seventeen to Olympias, give the journey in his own words. J. N. D. Kelly's book Golden Mouth is the best long modern account in English.

What is history. His birth in Antioch, his widowed mother, Libanius, his years in the hills, his ordinations, the riot of the statues, the carriage to Constantinople, the hospitals, Eutropius at the altar, the Synod of the Oak, the two exiles, the fire, Olympias, the journey to Cucusus, the night on the mountain road, the letters, the forced march, his death at Comana and the return of his body in four hundred and thirty-eight, and in two thousand and four.

What is told as someone's report. His mother's plea at the bedside is John's own telling. Libanius's remark about his mother is John's report, and the remark about the Christians stealing him is Sozomen's. The martyr Basiliscus appearing to him, and his last words, are Palladius's report. The story of the three hierarchs in the dream is a later tradition. The Liturgy and the Easter sermon are traditionally credited to him.

What we invented. Theodore, his name, and everything he does, says and thinks. Palladius says only that one guard was kinder than the other. We also invented the weather on particular days, the lamp, and the wording of almost everything, including John's farewell to the deaconesses and his letters, which are given in our own plain paraphrase. Some sharp things John may or may not have said about the empress have been left out; the old writers disagree, and one sermon credited to him on the subject is thought not to be his.

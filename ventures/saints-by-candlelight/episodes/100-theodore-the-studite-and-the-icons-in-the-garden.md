Imagine a garden, a long time ago, inside the walls of a very great city.

It is spring. It is early morning, and cool. There are fruit trees, and rows of beans, and a well with a wooden bucket, and a path of beaten earth that goes all the way round, between the garden and the old stone walls of a monastery.

And along that path, very slowly, comes a line of monks.

There are a great many of them. More than you could count before the front of the line had gone round the corner. They are singing. And each of them, or nearly each, is carrying something in his arms, held up against his chest the way you might carry a sleeping baby. Small painted boards. Faces. The face of Christ. The face of his Mother. The faces of saints, with gold behind them.

Near the back of the line is a boy, not quite a man, with ink on his fingers. His arms are aching. The icon he is carrying is heavier than it looks. And he is thinking, not for the first time that morning, that the emperor has said nobody is to do exactly this.

He looks to the front of the line, where an old man with a grey beard is walking, holding up his own icon, and singing as if he had not a care in the world.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

* * *

The city is Constantinople. Today we call it Istanbul. It sits on a point of land between two seas, where Europe and Asia are so close that you could shout across, if you had a very loud voice and did not mind the ships.

In the seven hundreds it was the biggest and richest city in the Christian world. It had walls that no army had ever got through. It had the great church of Holy Wisdom, Hagia Sophia, with a dome so wide that people said it hung from heaven on a chain. It had palaces, and markets, and harbours full of ships, and hundreds of churches, and dozens of monasteries. It also had a great many people who argued about theology in the street, the way people now argue about football. Saint Gregory of Nyssa, the brother of Saint Macrina, whom we met some nights ago, grumbled about this in a sermon, four hundred years before our story. Ask a man in that city to change a coin, he said, more or less, and he will lecture you on the Father and the Son instead. That is my own rough wording, not his. But you get the idea.

It was the capital of what we now call the Byzantine Empire. They did not call it that. They called themselves Romans, because as far as they were concerned, they were simply the Roman Empire, still going. The emperor was Roman, the laws were Roman, and the language was Greek, which did not seem to them at all strange.

Into this city, in the year seven hundred and fifty-nine, a boy was born to a good family. His father was called Photeinos, and worked in the emperor's treasury, counting the empire's money. His mother was called Theoktiste, and she was by every account a very serious and very devout woman, who ran her household like a small and well-organised army. The boy was called Theodore, which means "gift of God". He had two younger brothers, Joseph and Euthymios, and a little sister.

Now. Remember what I said last time. When Theodore was born, the emperors were against icons. It was a crime, or very nearly, to keep a painted picture of Christ or of the saints and to honour it. And Theodore's family, quietly, at home, did not agree with the emperors. That was the kind of house he grew up in. A house that kept its head down in public and said its prayers in front of the things it was not supposed to have.

He went to school, and he was good at it. Very good. He learned grammar and rhetoric and philosophy, which in those days meant mostly Aristotle, and he learned to write a Greek sentence so well balanced that you could stand a cup of water on it. He would need that later. He was going to write an enormous number of sentences.

And then there was his uncle.

* * *

His uncle was his mother's brother, and his name was Plato. Not that Plato. A different Plato. Byzantine families liked the old names. This Plato had also worked in the treasury, as a young man, and had done well, and then one day he gave it all up, freed his servants, gave away his money and went off to be a monk on Mount Olympus in Bithynia. That is not the Mount Olympus with the Greek gods on it. That is a different Mount Olympus too. It is across the water from Constantinople, on the Asian side, a mountain covered in forests, and in those days it was covered in monasteries as well.

After some years, Plato came back to visit his family in the city. And when he came, he talked. Not loudly. Not to persuade anyone. He just told them about the life. About getting up in the dark to sing the psalms. About working with your hands. About living with brothers who had nothing of their own and wanted nothing, and what that did to a man after a while.

And the family listened. And then, in the year seven hundred and eighty-one, a remarkable thing happened. The whole family decided to go.

Not just Theodore. All of them. His father Photeinos. His two brothers. Several of his cousins. His mother Theoktiste and his little sister went too, to a house for women nearby. They sold or gave away the house in the city. They freed their servants. And they went across the water to a piece of land the family owned in Bithynia, at a place called Sakkoudion, and there, with Uncle Plato in charge, they started a monastery of their own.

Theodore was twenty-two.

Imagine it, for a moment. Imagine your family deciding, all together, at supper one evening, that you were all going to become monks and nuns. Somebody would have to tell the neighbours. Somebody would have to deal with the furniture. Somebody would certainly have to tell the younger brother, the one who had been rather looking forward to a career, that he was now going to be getting up at two in the morning to sing for three hours. We are not told how Euthymios took it. Joseph, the middle brother, turned out to love it, and grew up to be a hymn-writer and a bishop and a saint. So there is hope for younger brothers.

Sakkoudion was not grand. It was a farm on a hillside, with a stream and some trees and a church, and a lot of very hard work. The brothers dug and planted and built. They copied books. They sang the services. Plato was the abbot, and he was gentle and wise and very strict, all at once, which is a difficult combination and a good one. And Theodore, who had come with his whole head full of Aristotle, found that what he really wanted to learn now was obedience, and prayer, and how to love the brother who snored.

He did well at all of it, except perhaps the snoring. Plato made him a priest, and the patriarch of Constantinople ordained him, a good man called Tarasios. And when Plato grew older and wanted to step back, in the year seven hundred and ninety-four, the brothers chose Theodore as their abbot. He was thirty-five.

That same patriarch, Tarasios, had been the one who presided over the council at Nicaea, the one that set John of Damascus's teaching right, and gave the icons back. And Plato had been at that council. So by the time Theodore was an abbot, the icons were back in the churches, the arguments seemed to be over, and life at Sakkoudion was quiet and good.

It did not stay quiet. It almost never does, in these stories.

* * *

Here I need to tell you about an emperor's wedding. It is the reason Theodore got into trouble the first time, and it tells you a great deal about him.

The emperor was a young man called Constantine, the sixth emperor of that name. He had been married, by his mother's arrangement, to a good and pious young woman called Maria. And after some years, Constantine decided he did not want to be married to Maria any more. He wanted to marry somebody else. A lady of the court called Theodote, who happened, as it turned out, to be a relative of Theodore's own family.

So the emperor sent his wife Maria away to a convent, and announced that he would marry Theodote instead. And he found a priest, a man called Joseph, who was willing to perform the wedding.

Now, the Church's teaching on marriage was clear, then and now. You cannot just send your wife away because you would like a different one, even if you are the emperor. Especially if you are the emperor, some people might say, since everybody is watching. But a lot of churchmen in Constantinople looked at their shoes. The emperor was the emperor. It was awkward. Perhaps it would blow over.

Theodore did not look at his shoes.

He and his uncle Plato said, quietly and plainly, that this was wrong. They would not pretend it was not wrong. And they would not share communion with the priest who had performed the wedding, or with anyone who treated it as all right. They did not shout. They did not stand in the street with banners. They simply would not say that a wrong thing was right.

The emperor came, in person, to the hot springs near Sakkoudion, which was not very far from the monastery, partly in the hope that Theodore would come down and greet him and so make everything look friendly. Theodore did not come. Everyone understood what that meant.

So the emperor's soldiers came instead. Some of the monks were beaten, which we will only say once. Plato was shut up in a cell in Constantinople. And Theodore, with a few of his brothers, was sent away into exile, to Thessalonica, a city a long way to the west.

He was not there long. The young emperor's reign came to an end soon afterwards, in a way that is too unhappy to tell at bedtime, and his mother Irene ruled in his place. Irene had her faults, and I will not pretend otherwise, but she had been the one who called the council that gave the icons back, and she liked monks. She called Theodore home.

And then, because the farms of Bithynia were being raided by armies from the east, and because she wanted good monks in her city, she gave Theodore something. She gave him a monastery in Constantinople itself.

It was called Stoudios.

* * *

The monastery of Stoudios was old. Very old, even then. It had been founded more than three hundred years before, by a rich man called Stoudios, who had been a consul, which was a grand Roman title, and who built a beautiful church dedicated to Saint John the Baptist. It stood in the south-west corner of the city, near the great land walls, close to the sea, in a quiet neighbourhood with gardens.

But by the time Theodore arrived, in about the year seven hundred and ninety-eight, it was nearly empty. The emperors who had been against icons had not been kind to monasteries. There were only about a dozen monks left. The buildings were too big for them. The garden had gone wild.

Theodore moved in with his brothers from Sakkoudion. And the house began to fill.

It filled very fast. Young men came from all over the city, and from far beyond it. Within some years, his Life tells us, there were close to a thousand monks at Stoudios, which would have made it one of the biggest monasteries anywhere in the world. Monks who wrote his Lives were proud of their house and may have rounded up a little. But even if it was half that number, it was a great many monks. Imagine feeding them. Imagine the washing.

Theodore had to organise all of it. And it turned out that the boy who could balance a sentence could also balance a monastery.

He wrote a rule, so that everybody knew what was expected. He gave jobs to everyone. There were cooks and bakers, and gardeners, and cellarers who kept the stores, and infirmarians who looked after the sick. There were shoemakers and tailors and carpenters and builders. There were men who looked after guests, and men who gave out food to the poor at the gate. The monastery of Stoudios made nearly everything it needed, and it was very proud of not needing to buy much.

He even wrote little poems for the jobs. Short verses, a few lines each, one for the cook, one for the cellarer, one for the man at the gate, one for the copyist, and so on, each telling the brother what his work was for and how to do it well. They have come down to us. I will not try to quote them, because I would get the wording wrong. But the idea of every one is the same. This job is holy. Do it gladly. Do it well. Even the cook. Especially the cook, some of the brothers might have said, about two hours before lunch.

And three times a week or so, early in the morning, after the night prayers, Theodore would stand up and talk to them all. Short talks. Plain talks. About being patient with each other, about not grumbling, about prayer, about the feast that was coming, about the brother who had died that week and where he had gone. Somebody wrote them down. There are hundreds of them. They are still read in monasteries today.

But the part of Stoudios that matters most for our story, and for the young man with ink on his fingers, is the room where they copied books.

* * *

Let me introduce Petros. He is our own invention, so you will not find him in any history book. He is about sixteen. He came to Stoudios from a family of fishermen on the far side of the Golden Horn, which is the long harbour that runs into the city, and he has big hands that are better at nets than at pens, and he has been put in the copying room anyway.

The copying room at Stoudios was a serious place. Long tables. Good light from the windows. Pots of ink, which they made themselves, and pens, which were cut from reeds, and knives for cutting them, and great sheets of parchment, which were made from the skins of animals, scraped and stretched and dried and very expensive. One mistake on a sheet of parchment was a mistake you had to scrape out by hand. Several mistakes and the sheet was spoiled, and somebody would want to know why.

And Stoudios had rules about copying. There is a list of penances that comes down to us with the Studite rules, and part of it is just for the copyists. It is very specific. It has penances for a copyist who does not keep his book clean. For one who does not follow the original carefully, down to the little marks over the letters. And, my favourite, for one who breaks his pen in a temper.

Somebody, somewhere, at some point, must have broken his pen in a temper, or nobody would have thought to write that down.

On the morning in our story, that somebody is Petros.

He has been working on the same page of the same Gospel book for what feels like a week. It is the part of Saint John's Gospel about the vine and the branches. He has spoiled the first line twice already. And now, on the third try, his hand slips, and a long tail of ink goes right across the word for "vine", and Petros, without quite meaning to, squeezes his reed pen until it goes snap.

The whole room goes quiet. Twenty heads lift from twenty desks.

And at that exact moment, of course, the abbot walks in.

Theodore is not young any more. He is in his forties, with a beard going grey and a thin, tired, kind face. He walks over and looks at the page. He looks at the two halves of the pen. He looks at Petros, who is red to the ears.

"Ah," he says. "The vine."

"I'm sorry, Father," says Petros. "I'll do the penance."

"You will," says Theodore, not unkindly. "It's in the rule. I wrote the rule. I can hardly pretend I've never read it." He picks up the broken pen and turns it over. "Do you know why it's in there, Petros? It isn't because pens are expensive. Reeds grow by the river. We have thousands."

Petros shakes his head.

"It's because a man who breaks his pen in a temper," says Theodore, "has decided, for a moment, that the page is about him. About how well he is doing, and how annoyed he is. And the page is not about him. It is about the vine. It is about the words of the Lord, which you are carrying from one book into another, so that somebody, in a hundred years, in a place you will never see, can read them. You are a bridge, brother. Bridges do not lose their temper."

Petros thinks about this. "Bridges don't have to do John chapter fifteen three times," he says.

And the abbot of Stoudios, in front of the whole copying room, laughs.

"No," he says. "That's true. Cut yourself another pen. Go slowly. And when you get to the end of the page, read the words. Not to copy them. Just to read them. Then you'll remember what you're carrying."

* * *

Something happened in copying rooms like that one, in those years, that you would never guess, and that matters to every one of us who has ever read a book.

Up until then, Greek books had mostly been written in big separate capital letters, each one formed slowly and carefully, like letters cut in stone. It looked very grand. It took a very long time. And it used up a great deal of parchment.

But around this time, in Constantinople, copyists began to use a new kind of writing. Small letters, joined up, flowing, quicker to write and taking up much less room on the page. We call it minuscule, which just means "smallish". And the oldest Greek book we have, written in that new hand with a date in it, is a book of the Gospels now kept in a library in Saint Petersburg. It is dated to the year eight hundred and thirty-five. And most scholars think the monk who copied it was a man called Nicholas, who had been trained at Stoudios as a boy under Theodore, and who later became abbot there, and a saint.

Nobody can prove that Stoudios invented the new writing. It was probably in the air. But the Studite copyists made it their own, and spread it, and the small letters you are reading in a Greek book today are, in a sense, its grandchildren. So the next time you see a page of small, tidy, joined-up letters, you might spare a thought for some tired monks with reed pens, and the rule about not breaking them.

* * *

Now. The icons.

You remember that the council at Nicaea, in seven hundred and eighty-seven, had brought the icons back. For about a quarter of a century, all was well. Then, in the year eight hundred and thirteen, a new emperor came to the throne. He was a soldier, from Armenia, a capable general, and he was called Leo, the fifth emperor of that name.

The empire had been having a bad time. It had lost battles. Things had gone wrong. And Leo looked back over the history books and noticed something. The emperors who had been against icons, years before, had won a lot of battles. The emperors since had lost a lot of them. And he drew a conclusion. Perhaps, he thought, God was displeased with the icons. Perhaps, if they were taken down again, the battles would start going right again.

You may think that was not very good theology. You would be in excellent company. But Leo was the emperor.

So in the year eight hundred and fifteen, he called the bishops together, and they did what he wanted. The icons were to be taken down again. The good patriarch of Constantinople, Nikephoros, who would not go along with it, was removed from his office and sent away. Nikephoros and Theodore had quarrelled once, years before, over that unhappy wedding business. But they had long since made friends, and now they stood together, and they would stay together.

And then came Palm Sunday.

Palm Sunday is the Sunday before Easter, when the Church remembers the Lord riding into Jerusalem on a donkey, with the crowds waving branches and singing. It is a day for processions. And on Palm Sunday, in the year eight hundred and fifteen, the Lives of Theodore tell us, he did a very simple thing.

He called his monks together. He told them to take the icons down from the walls, and the little ones from their cells. And he led them out, all of them, carrying the icons in their arms, round the garden of Stoudios, singing. Not to the palace. Not into the streets. Just round their own garden, inside their own walls, in the morning, so that anybody who was watching, and in that city somebody was always watching, would know.

* * *

And that is where we came in. A long line of monks on a path of beaten earth. Fruit trees. Beans. A well with a wooden bucket. And near the back of the line, with aching arms and ink on his fingers, Petros.

He has been given a small icon of Saint John the Baptist, because the church at Stoudios is the Baptist's church. The Baptist looks out of the painted board at him with his wild hair and his serious eyes. Petros is not sure how a picture so small can be so heavy.

The line comes round the corner by the well, and slows, and stops for a moment, the way long lines do. And Petros finds that he is standing next to the gardener. The gardener is an old monk called Brother Gregory, who is also our own invention, and who smells, as always, faintly of onions.

"Brother," whispers Petros. "Are we going to get into trouble?"

"Almost certainly," says Brother Gregory. "Mind the beans."

"Then why are we doing it?"

Brother Gregory thinks about it. He is not a theologian. He grows vegetables.

"Do you know what Father Theodore said in his talk on Thursday?" he says. "He said, when the emperor's people say a picture of the Lord is just wood and paint, ask them this. Did the Lord really become a man? With a real face? That his Mother could look at, and his friends could see across the room? Because if he did, then you can paint that face. And if you say you can't paint it, you are saying, without noticing, that he never really had one."

Petros looks down at the Baptist.

"And the honour goes through," says Brother Gregory. "Like light through a window. Saint Basil said that, Father says, hundreds of years ago. The honour you give to the picture goes through it to the one in the picture. Nobody here is praying to a board. We're bowing to the board the way you'd kiss a letter from your mother. It's not the paper you love."

Then the line starts moving again, and Brother Gregory says, rather louder than he means to, "Mind the beans!"

And they go on round, singing.

* * *

The trouble came, as Brother Gregory had said it would.

The emperor's officials came to Stoudios and told Theodore he must stop. He would not stop. He said, quite courteously, that the emperor was in charge of the empire, and he would pay the emperor's taxes and pray for the emperor's armies. But the faith of the Church was not the emperor's to change. He had said much the same about a wedding, twenty years before. He was not going to start saying something different now.

So he was sent into exile again. First to a small fortress called Metopa, near a lake in Bithynia. Then, when it turned out he was still causing trouble there, much further away, to a fortress called Bonita, deep in Asia Minor. And later to the city of Smyrna, on the coast. He was treated harshly in those years. He was beaten, and he was often cold, and often ill, and for a while he was kept in a cell with very little light. That is all we will say about that.

But here is the thing about Theodore. You could send him away. You could not stop him writing.

He wrote letters. Hundreds of them. More than five hundred of his letters have come down to us, and there must have been many more. He wrote to his monks, who had been scattered all over the empire, some of them in prison, some of them hiding with friendly families. He wrote to bishops. He wrote to abbots. He wrote to frightened lay people who had given in and taken their icons down and then felt terrible about it, and he told them how to come back. He wrote to mothers, and to widows, and to the brother who looked after the monastery's donkeys. He wrote to his own monks who had been made to give in under pressure, and he was firm with them, and then kind.

His letters were carried by friends, by monks in disguise, by sympathetic merchants and sailors. They went in bags of grain and in the folds of cloaks. A whole network of quiet people passed them on, hand to hand, from one end of the empire to the other. It was, if you like, the ancient equivalent of a group chat, except that getting caught with your phone could get you sent to a fortress.

And in his letters, again and again, he said the same few things. Stand firm. Do not hate the people who are hurting you. Pray for the emperor. Keep the rule as well as you can wherever you are. Keep the night prayers, even if you have to say them alone in a stable. Do not lose your temper. You are a bridge.

Somewhere in a little town on the coast, let us imagine, a young monk with big fisherman's hands is living in hiding in a friendly family's back room. And one evening a man comes to the door with a bag of dried figs, and at the bottom of the bag, folded very small, is a letter in a hand he knows. He goes out to the back of the house and reads it by the last of the light.

It is not long. It says he is well, as well as can be expected. It asks after the brothers by name, one at a time, as if there were nothing in the world more important. It tells him to keep his pen sharp, and his temper blunt.

Petros laughs out loud, alone in the dark yard, and then cries a little, and then goes in and finds a reed and a knife.

* * *

The emperor Leo's reign ended, at Christmas in the year eight hundred and twenty, in a way that is not for bedtime either. Byzantine emperors did not often get to retire. The next emperor was called Michael, the second of that name. Michael did not much like icons himself, but he did not like arguments about them either, and he let the exiles go home.

Theodore was free. He was sixty-one years old, and very tired, and not well.

He did not go back to Stoudios. The emperor still would not allow the icons in the churches, and Theodore would not pretend that was all right, even to get his own monastery back. So he lived quietly with some of his brothers in small houses across the water, on the Asian side, near the sea, and he went on writing his talks and his letters, and he went on praying.

He never saw the icons come back. He lived for six more years, and the emperors were still against them when he died.

* * *

[pause]

It was autumn when he died. The eleventh of November, in the year eight hundred and twenty-six. He was sixty-seven. He was staying at a little monastery dedicated to the martyr Tryphon, on a headland by the sea called Akritas, not far across the water from the great city.

He had been ill for some time. His brothers were around him. The Lives of him say that he asked them to light the candles, and to sing. And they sang the long psalm, the one that begins "Blessed are those whose way is blameless, who walk in the law of the Lord". It is the longest psalm in the whole Bible. It is a hundred and seventy-six verses long, and almost every verse is about loving God's word and keeping it, verse after verse, quiet and steady, like walking.

And somewhere in the middle of that psalm, his Lives say, while his brothers were still singing, Theodore went home to God.

It is a good psalm to go to sleep to, if you ever want one. It does not hurry.

[pause]

He did not see the icons come back. But they did come back.

Seventeen years after he died, in the year eight hundred and forty-three, an empress called Theodora, ruling for her young son, called the bishops together once more. And they brought the icons back into the churches, for good this time. It happened on the first Sunday of Lent. And to this day, every year, on the first Sunday of Lent, the Orthodox Church keeps a feast it calls the Triumph of Orthodoxy, when people carry icons in procession round the church, singing. If you have ever seen it, you have seen something very like what the monks of Stoudios did in their garden, on that Palm Sunday long ago, when it was not allowed.

The year after that, in eight hundred and forty-four, the monks brought Theodore's body home. They carried it across the water and into the city and back to Stoudios, and laid it in the church of Saint John the Baptist, beside his uncle Plato. His brother Joseph, who had been a bishop and had written many hymns, and who had also suffered in the exiles, came home with him and was laid there too. Many of the hymns of Lent, sung in Orthodox churches to this day, go under the names of those two brothers.

And Stoudios went on. It went on for centuries. Its rule was copied by monasteries all over the Christian East. When monks went north, to Kiev, to start the first great monasteries in the lands of the Rus, it was the Studite rule they took with them, more or less, and it shaped monastic life in that whole part of the world. The copying room went on too. The small joined-up letters went on.

The church of Saint John the Baptist at Stoudios is still there, in Istanbul. Or rather, its walls are. It has had a long and complicated life since, and the roof has come and gone. But the old stones still stand, near the land walls, in a quiet neighbourhood, where once there was a garden with beans and a well and a path of beaten earth.

[pause]

His feast is on the eleventh of November, in both the Orthodox and the Catholic Church. In the West it shares the day with Saint Martin of Tours, the soldier who cut his cloak in half for a beggar, whom we met many nights ago. I think the two of them would have got on. Neither of them was much good at pretending.

Theodore is called the Studite, after his monastery. And he is called a confessor, which is an old and very particular word. It does not mean somebody who hears confessions. It means somebody who confessed the faith out loud when it was dangerous, and suffered for it, and did not die for it. Somebody who simply kept saying the true thing, year after year, in exile and in prison and in a cold cell with no light, until it was true again for everyone.

[pause]

And I think there is something you can take from him to bed tonight.

Theodore was not a wizard and not a warrior. Most of what he did was small. He kept a rule. He gave the brothers their jobs. He wrote short poems for the cook. He talked to his monks three times a week. He wrote letters, one after another, five hundred and more, and every one of them asked after somebody by name.

And when the big moment came, the thing he did was small too. He walked round his own garden, singing, holding a picture of a face he loved.

So if today you lost your temper, or broke something, or made a mess of a page you had tried three times. You can put it down now. Cut yourself another pen tomorrow. Go slowly. You are only carrying something from one place to another, for somebody you will never meet. That is a good thing to be. A bridge.

And bridges, as everybody knows, do not have to be finished tonight.

Rest now. That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

What we know about Theodore comes mostly from his own hand. His letters, more than five hundred of them, edited by Georgios Fatouros. His talks to his monks, the Small and the Great Catecheses. His writings in defence of the icons, which you can read in English as On the Holy Icons, in Catharine Roth's translation. His Testament and the documents of the Stoudios rule, translated in the Dumbarton Oaks collection of monastic foundation documents. His funeral speeches for his mother and his uncle. And Lives of him written by monks of his house, the best known by a monk called Michael, a generation or so after he died. For a modern guide, Roman Cholij's book on him, Thomas Pratsch's study and Peter Hatlie's history of the monasteries of Constantinople are a good place to start.

What is history. That he was born in Constantinople in seven hundred and fifty-nine. That his family went into monastic life together at Sakkoudion under his uncle Plato, in seven hundred and eighty-one. That he became abbot there, refused to accept the emperor Constantine's second marriage, and was exiled to Thessalonica. That he moved his community to Stoudios, wrote its rule, its poems for the offices and his talks. That he opposed the return of iconoclasm under Leo the Fifth in eight hundred and fifteen, was exiled and treated harshly, wrote his many letters, and was released under Michael the Second. That he died on the eleventh of November, eight hundred and twenty-six, and was brought home to Stoudios in eight hundred and forty-four.

What is the report of his Lives. The Palm Sunday procession round the garden. The number of close to a thousand monks. The candles and the psalm at his death. We have told these as the Lives tell them. That Nicholas the Studite copied the Gospel book of eight hundred and thirty-five is most scholars' judgement, not certain. The penances for copyists come down with the Studite rules; we have not given their exact wording.

What we invented. Petros, Brother Gregory, the broken pen, the letter in the figs, the weather, and almost all the words anybody says. Theodore's teaching is given in our own plain paraphrase. We only touched the second quarrel over the wedding priest, under the patriarch Nikephoros, which ended in friendship, and we left out Theodore's letters to bishops abroad.

[pause]

That's the story of Saint Theodore the Studite for tonight. His feast is on the eleventh of November, in the Orthodox Church and in the Catholic Church. Blessed are those whose way is blameless, who walk in the law of the Lord. Holy Theodore, confessor of the holy icons, pray to God for us. Goodnight, and God bless.

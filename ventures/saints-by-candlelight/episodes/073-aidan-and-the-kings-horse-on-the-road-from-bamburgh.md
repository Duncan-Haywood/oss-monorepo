We begin, as so many stories do, with a lost battle and a young man with nowhere to go.

Before there was a bishop of Lindisfarne, there was a prince of Northumbria, and his name was Oswald. Northumbria, you should know, was the biggest and most northerly of the English kingdoms. It ran from the river Humber, a long way south, up past what is now Newcastle and on into the lowlands of Scotland, and it was ruled from a fortress on a rock beside the sea called Bamburgh. If you have ever seen photographs of a castle on a cliff over a beach in Northumberland, that is the place. The castle you see today is much newer than the story, but the rock is the same rock, and so is the sea.

Now, Oswald's father had been a king, and then there had been a war, and the king had died, and the family had run. And so the boy Oswald grew up in exile. He did not grow up in a palace. He grew up in the west, a long way over the sea, among the Irish, in a monastery on a small island called Iona.

I would like to tell you a bit about Iona, because it matters a great deal tonight.

Iona is a little island, about three miles long, off the far end of a larger island, off the west coast of Scotland. It has very green grass and very white sand and a great deal of wind. About seventy years before the time of our story, an Irish monk called Columba had landed there with a dozen friends and a boat, and they had built a monastery. Some of you will remember Columba, from an episode about a monster in a river. By the time young Oswald arrived, that monastery had become a famous school. Monks from Iona copied books, and prayed the psalms, and taught people who came from all over, and sent out other monks to start other monasteries, rather the way a dandelion sends out seeds, except with more Latin.

And Oswald lived there, as a refugee, for many years, something like seventeen by the usual reckoning. He learned to speak Irish. He learned the psalms. He was baptised there, and he listened to the monks, and what he took from them, I think, was this: that a person could be strong and gentle at once. That was not obvious in the seventh century. Most people thought that you had to choose.

[pause]

Then, in the year six hundred and thirty-four, things turned. Oswald came back to Northumbria with a small army, a very small one, and the story goes that on the night before the battle he set up a wooden cross, and held it upright himself while his soldiers shovelled earth around the foot, and asked everyone to kneel and pray to the living God to help them, because they were so few. The place is still called Heavenfield. And he won.

Which is how a young man who had lived on a sea-island with some monks came to be king of the whole of Northumbria, with a fortress, and a hall, and a good many cousins, and a kingdom full of people who had mostly never heard the Gospel. Some of them had been baptised. Some had been baptised and then gone back to the old gods when the last king fell. Most of them were somewhere in between, which is where most of us are on a Monday.

And Oswald knew exactly what he wanted. He wanted a bishop. He did not want a visiting priest, or a small chapel, or a letter of encouragement. He wanted a teacher who would stay. And there was only one place he trusted to send one. So he sent a message to his old friends on Iona, a very long way away, to ask them for a bishop for the English.

They sent one. The first one did not work out.

[pause]

I am sorry to say that the first monk they sent was, by the account that came down to Bede, a rather severe man. Bede does not give his name. He went to the Northumbrians, and he preached to them, and they did not listen, and he found them stubborn, and uncivilised, and, as Bede puts it, a people of stubborn and untamed temper. After a short time he gave up. He went home, and stood up in front of the other monks at Iona at a meeting, and he said, in effect, that it was no use. These people can't be taught. They are too hard.

Now, here is the part that I love.

There was a monk in that meeting named Aidan. He was a quiet man. He is not recorded as having said anything at all in all the earlier part of the story. But when Corman had finished his complaint, Aidan spoke up, and what he said, according to Bede, was something like this. Brother, I think the trouble was that you were too hard on them. You gave these simple people the strong food, the perfect teaching, all at once, when you should have started with the milk. You should have given them a little at a time, the way you feed a child, until they were ready for more, and then more, and then the meat.

And there was a silence in the room. Because everyone knew what that meant. The man who had spoken so wisely was the man who ought to go.

So they chose Aidan. They laid hands on him. They made him a bishop, and they sent him to the English. And I find it hard not to smile at that, because he had volunteered for the job by the simple method of being right out loud in a meeting. Which is the most dangerous thing you can do in a meeting, and I say that as a narrator who has been in some.

[pause]

Aidan went east. He crossed Scotland, which in those days took a while, and he came to Bamburgh, and the king came to meet him.

And now Oswald had a problem, which was a pleasant sort of problem. He had a bishop, a very good one. But Aidan did not speak English.

Or rather, Aidan was learning it, and Bede says he was not yet fluent, and a bishop who speaks about one word in three is not much good to a crowd of farmers. So what did the king do? He stood up beside Aidan, at the front of the crowd, and when Aidan spoke in Irish, King Oswald turned and said it again in English. Out loud. In front of everyone.

Pause for a moment and picture that. The king of Northumbria, the winner of the great battle, the man with the fortress and the army, standing in a field next to a thin monk in a plain brown cloak and acting as his interpreter. Saying, as it were, the bishop says, and then repeating whatever the bishop said, as faithfully as he could, while the farmers stared. It must have been a very odd sight. It must also have done a great deal of good. A king who is willing to be somebody's interpreter has already given you the first half of the sermon.

[pause]

Now, where would Aidan make his home? Oswald offered him a place at Bamburgh, in the royal city, which was the sensible thing, in the middle of everything, with the king's table and the king's guard. And Aidan said, politely, no. He asked for an island.

It was a small island off the coast, a few miles from Bamburgh, which is joined to the mainland by a strip of sand. Twice a day the tide comes in and covers the sand, and it is an island, and twice a day the tide goes out and it is not. The English called it Lindisfarne. The monks who came to live there found that it reminded them of Iona. A small place, with the sea on all sides, close to the king but not inside his house. A place from which a man could go out to the people, and also come back to pray.

It is worth saying that this is exactly how a bishop of the seventh century could have chosen to live, and exactly the opposite of how some of them did. Aidan did not build a palace. He built a small wooden church, and a few huts, and he lived there with a handful of monks, and they prayed and studied Scripture, and every morning, as soon as it was light, somebody put on a cloak and went walking.

[pause]

And that is the part I want to tell you about next, because it is the heart of the matter. Aidan walked.

Bede says it in a way that I think he found a little astonishing. Aidan did not ride on journeys, he says, unless some great need forced him to. He went about on foot, in towns and in the country, through the villages and the farms. And whenever he met people, rich or poor, he stopped, and he said hello, and he talked. If they were not Christians, he asked them, gently, whether they might like to be, and told them about it. If they were Christians, he encouraged them, and urged them to give to the poor, and to do good works.

I think the walking is the whole secret. If a bishop rides past you on a horse, he is above you, and you look up at him. If a bishop walks next to you on the road, he is level with you, and he sees the weather you are in. And here is a thing Bede mentions that I like very much. Everyone who went about with Aidan, monk or layman, had to use the time. They were expected to think about Scripture, or to learn the psalms by heart, as they walked. Aidan's road was a school. If you were going to be seen with the bishop, you were going to be learning something by the time you got to the next village. His companions must have complained at times, though Bede does not record it. I like to think one of them once said, in a whisper, to another, that it would be nice just for once to walk somewhere in peace and quiet without having to learn the whole hundred and nineteenth psalm.

Which is a long psalm, as it happens. It has a hundred and seventy-six verses.

[pause]

And he did something else, which I think is just as important. Whatever he was given, he gave away.

Kings and rich people sent him presents. They were glad to. Here was a holy man, and one likes to give presents to a holy man. And when the gifts arrived, Bede says, Aidan did not keep them. If a rich man gave him money, he gave it to the poor, the very first poor person he met. If someone gave him a meal, he shared it. And a great deal of what was given to him went to buy the freedom of people who had been sold into slavery unjustly, and then Aidan would have them taught, and some of them, Bede says, grew up to become priests. That is a thing I think is worth pausing over. A man who had been bought and sold, became a priest because a bishop on foot had spent the king's silver on him.

So there was a pattern. A gift arrives. A bishop, who does not need it, finds someone who does. The gift goes on. The bishop's own pockets stay empty. This was not a rule. It was just how Aidan was made, the way some people are tall.

And so it should not surprise us that when, one day, a king gave him a horse, the horse did not stay long in the stable.

The king in this part of the story is not Oswald. Oswald had been killed in battle some years before, by the pagan king Penda of Mercia, who was very much the villain of the age, and who has an unfortunate habit of turning up in the stories as the weather. Oswald's brother Oswiu took over the north, the part with Bamburgh. But the southern half of the old kingdom, which was called Deira, had a king of its own, and his name was Oswin.

Oswin was, if Bede is to be trusted, one of the most likeable people in the whole of the Venerable Bede's history, and Bede did not give out praise at random. He says that Oswin was tall and handsome, and pleasant in speech, and generous to everyone, high and low, and that he was famous for his humility. Which is a thing a king can have quite a lot of trouble with, because other people are always trying to help him have less of it.

And Oswin was very fond of Aidan. He had a feeling, which was a perfectly good one, that the bishop was spending himself on the roads of the north and that something ought to be done. So he thought about it. And what he thought was this. The bishop's feet must be sore. The bishop's roads include rivers, and rivers in Northumbria are cold, and in winter they are very high. And a bishop who is old, or who is merely tired, cannot always wade. So Oswin had the best horse in his stable brought out, a fine, royal horse, with a king's saddle and a king's bridle, and he sent it to Aidan as a gift, so that the bishop could ride whenever the road was hard, and cross the rivers dry.

Now that is the kind of present you give with a happy heart. And Aidan, I'm told, thanked him. He accepted it. And a day or two later, Bede says, he was riding it. That is the first thing to notice, which gets lost in the retelling. He did not refuse the gift. He did not argue with the king. He took it, as the kindness it was, and he got up on this very large horse and went off down the road.

Let me tell you what I think that morning was like. I will tell you plainly that I am making it up.

I think it was a grey morning, with a bit of wind off the sea, and a thin drizzle that did not quite know whether to be rain. The bishop, I think, sat on this horse a bit stiffly. He had not ridden much for years. A horse of that quality has opinions about its rider, and I picture the horse deciding, within a few strides, that this was a person of very little interest in the field of speed. Behind him there were his companions, on foot, and one of them, the youngest, said something like, my lord, you look very well up there.

And Aidan said, I feel as though I am sitting on a wall that is moving.

And they all laughed. But they went on, because the day was before them, and the horse went at the pace of the walkers, with a kind of patient disdain. And they came, after a while, to the place where the road bends, and runs beside a stream, and there was a figure sitting there, at the edge of the track, with an open hand.

It was a beggar.

And Aidan stopped.

And he got off the horse, which I like to think took a little while.

The beggar, I should think, stood there with his mouth open. I do not think he had been asked to look after a horse before. Now, it is a feature of life in the seventh century that when a bishop was in the neighbourhood, a king invited him to dinner. This was not something one could avoid, and Aidan, who was courteous, did not try. So that very day, or very soon after, Aidan was a guest at the king's table, and the king was there, and the hall was full, and there was a fire.

It had been a hunting day. The king had been out, and had come in cold, and he stood by the fire, warming his hands, as people do. And somebody told him, because in a great hall somebody always does, what had happened to the horse.

You can picture the scene. The king, by the fire. A pause in the conversation. And a person, it may be the servant who looks after the horses, or one of the lords at the table, leaning in and saying, my lord, you gave the bishop the royal horse. And the bishop gave it to a beggar on the road.

And now I think about what a less happy-natured man might have done. A king who is told that his best horse, which was a gift worth a good deal, has been handed to a stranger at the roadside, in the rain. He might have been furious. Kings in that age had been furious for less. And Oswin was not quite furious, but he was a little hurt. I suspect he was more than a little hurt. And so when the meal had started, and the food was on the table, he turned to Aidan and asked him straight out.

Bede gives us the question, nearly word for word. The king asked, why had the bishop given away the royal horse, which was meant for his own use? Had we not other, lesser horses, or other things, that would have done as well for the poor, without your giving away the one that I chose specially for you?

There was a silence at the table. Everyone in that hall knew the king had a right to be asked. Everyone also knew that a bishop was not a man you argued with at dinner. And a bishop who is not a good man, in a case like that, might have said something clever.

Aidan said something very short.

He said, and this is Bede's, as close as I can give it, what are you saying, your majesty? Is that son of a mare more precious to you than that son of God?

I would like to leave that sentence in the air for a moment. Is the foal of a mare dearer to you than a child of God?

That is all he said. He did not shout. He did not quote anyone. He did not give a speech about the duties of kings. He just put the two things side by side on the table, a horse on one side and a poor, coughing man on the other, and let the king look at them.

And the king was standing by the fire, with his back to it, warming himself. And Bede says he took off his sword belt. He unfastened his sword, and handed it to one of his servants, and came across the hall, and knelt down at the bishop's feet. He asked to be forgiven. He said that he would never again say a word about what the bishop gave away, or how he chose to give it.

Now, picture that if you can. A tall, handsome king, in his hunting clothes, on his knees on the floor of the hall, in front of all his lords, because he had been rebuked and the rebuke was right. And Aidan, in return, did what a gentle man does when someone kneels to him. He got up. He raised the king up with both hands, and he told him not to be distressed, and said, in effect, that he was not angry, and that all was well. He said that all he asked was that the king should not be sorrowful. And then, Bede says, he took the king by the hand, and sat him down at the table, and said that now they should be merry, and eat.

And everybody did. The king, relieved, laughed. The lords laughed. The meal, which had been a little stiff, became a good one. The wine went round, in the way that it did. The king was cheerful, the hall was warm, and the whole company felt, I think, the kind of happiness that comes after a quarrel that did not happen.

And Aidan, who had begun the meal in a good mood, was not.

[pause]

Here is what Bede says, and it is the strangest and most tender part of the whole story. As the king grew merrier, the bishop grew more thoughtful, and then he grew sad. He sat there at the table with the others laughing around him, and his face fell, and then, to the surprise of the companions sitting near him, the bishop began to weep.

One of his priests, who was sitting beside him, leaned over and asked him in Irish what was the matter. They spoke in Irish deliberately, because Oswin, who was a Deiran, did not understand it, and the bishop could say what he liked.

And Aidan said, quietly, I know that the king will not live long. I have never before seen a king so humble. I think he will be taken from this life soon, because this people is not worthy of such a ruler.

That is what Bede says he said. I will not tell you more about that than Bede does, which is that it was a thing he said in grief, and a sorrowful thing, and a loving one. Now I want to tell you about the oil, because it is a good story, and a funny one in places, and because it shows us something about how Aidan's friends saw him.

This is another of the stories Bede was told, and he is careful to say that he was told it by a very reliable priest named Cynemund, who knew Utta. I will tell it in the way the old sources tell it, and I will say plainly that it is a story and not a record, and that you may take it as you find it.

There was, in those years, a marriage to be arranged. King Oswiu, the king of the north after Oswald, wanted to marry a princess named Eanfled, who was the daughter of the old king Edwin, and who lived far to the south, in Kent. Edwin was dead. Eanfled had grown up in the safety of the Kentish court, and in the Christian faith, and she was the right person to be queen. So messengers had to go and fetch her.

And the man appointed to do the fetching was a priest called Utta. He was the abbot of a monastery at Gateshead, which is on the south bank of the river Tyne, and he was, Bede says, a man of great seriousness and truthfulness. Which is just the sort of person you pick to travel a very long way to meet a bride.

But there was a difficulty. They would have to go part of the way by sea. And Utta, who was an honest man, was frightened of the sea.

[pause]

Now here is where I will introduce you to what Utta said, and I am going to say plainly that I am guessing at the wording, but I would be surprised if it was far off. Utta went to Aidan before the journey, and I imagine he came in rather quickly, and rather pale, and said something like this. My lord, I am to go to Kent, and I am to bring the princess back by ship, and I have never in my life been on a ship in a storm, and I should like you to pray for us.

And Aidan said that of course he would.

And then, Bede says, Aidan, who knew exactly what sort of nervous person he was dealing with, took out a small flask of blessed oil, which was a kind of oil used in the church for prayer. He gave it to Utta and he said, I know that when you go aboard, you will meet with storms and a contrary wind. But remember, when the sea rises and the ship begins to fill, to pour this oil upon the water, and the sea will be calm, and you will have a good voyage.

Now the first thing I want to say is that Aidan did not say, this will work. He did not say, I have power. He said, in Bede's account, that the sea would be calm, and he had asked God for it. That is the whole point of the oil. It is not a charm. It is a sign. It is a way of saying that this ship, and this nervous priest, and this princess, were in the care of God, and that the bishop on the shore had been praying for all of them.

[pause]

So they set out. Utta went to Kent. They met the princess, and they brought her to the ship, and they put out to sea. And it went well for a good while. And then, as Aidan had said, a storm blew up.

And the ship was thrown about. The waves were high. The sailors did what sailors do, which is to shout, and bail, and shout some more. And I'm told that Utta, in the middle of all this, sat there with his hands over his face, very green, thinking that he would never see the Tyne again.

And then he remembered. He put his hand into his cloak, and there was the flask.

I would like to think, and I am inventing here, that he took the cork out with shaking fingers, and tried to remember the exact words, and then thought, no, I will just do what he said. And he poured the oil onto the sea. Over the side of the ship.

And Bede says that at once the wind dropped, and the waves went down, and the sea went flat, and the ship sailed on in a calm that nobody could account for until they came safely to land. The princess was brought to the north. The marriage took place. And the flask of oil, which was said to be quite small, was kept as a kind of remembrance.

I want to be very careful here. Bede tells this, and tells it with some pride in his old bishop. We will leave the explaining of it where Bede leaves it, with God, to whom the winds and the water belong. A bishop prays. God acts. A priest, who is very frightened, pours oil in the sea and says thank you afterwards, and the thank you is the part that really counts.

And I would just add that, in the course of the story, an honest man who was afraid of storms found out, in the middle of one, that he had been prayed for. I think many of us would like to know that.

Let me tell you one more story, a short one, and then it will be time to slow down.

By the time Aidan was an older man, the enemy king Penda of Mercia, the one who had killed Oswald, was making war on the north again. And one year Penda came with a great army to Bamburgh itself, the royal city, and found that he could not take it, because the rock was too strong. And so he did what an angry king without a siege engine does. He pulled down the cottages and the barns of the nearby villages, and piled the planks and thatch and wattle against the walls of the fortress, in a great heap, and set it on fire, hoping that the wind would carry the flames over the wall and burn the town.

Now Aidan was not at Bamburgh at that moment. He was on Inner Farne, a small rocky island a little way out to sea, where he used to go for a few days of quiet prayer. And Bede says that he looked out from the island and saw the smoke, and the flames, rising from the royal city, and he lifted up his eyes, and said, Lord, see how much evil Penda is doing.

That is all he said. Bede's whole account of it is only that.

And at that, Bede says, the wind changed. It turned right around, and blew the flames back from the city, onto the men who had lit them. And Penda and his army gave up, and went away, much shaken.

[pause]

Now, I want to be honest about that one. It is a story. It was told in the north for a long time, and written down by Bede about eighty years later, and it has no way to be checked. The fire at Bamburgh, I should think, was real. The prayer on the island, perhaps. And the wind, well, winds do change on that coast. Whether it was a wonder is for God to say. What I would like you to hear is the thing Bede wanted you to hear, which is how short the prayer was. Lord, see what is being done. That is all. And when you are small, and the fire is a long way off, and nothing you can do will get there in time, that is a very good prayer to say.

And that, I think, is the last of the stories. Now let us go slowly.

I said that Aidan wept at a table, and that he knew a king would not live long. And what happened next was this.

Oswin was king of Deira for seven years. He was a good king, and he was a loved one, and in the year six hundred and fifty-one he and Oswiu, the king of the north, came to the edge of a war. Oswiu raised an army. Oswin, who did not wish to fight his own cousin, and who knew that more men would die if he did, disbanded his forces and went into hiding at the house of a man he trusted, called Hunwald, at a place called Gilling. And Hunwald betrayed him.

Oswin was killed on the twentieth of August. Bede says that he died as a Christian, humbly, and that later Oswiu was very sorry. He built a monastery on the very spot, in his memory, with monks to pray every day for both of them, the dead king and the one who had killed him. That is a thing I find moving. It is the sort of thing that tends to happen near a good person's grave.

And Aidan heard.

He was at Bamburgh. He was an old man by then, or an old man by the standards of the north in that century. He had been bishop for about sixteen years. He had walked, I would think, thousands of miles. And he heard what had happened to his friend, the humble king, and he was already ill. He must have known, as he had known at the table. His body was worn out.

And so a tent was put up for him, outside the church at Bamburgh, against its west wall. He had always loved to be near a church. A beam leaned against the wall, a buttress of wood to hold the wall up, and the tent was fastened to it. And in that tent, with his head against that beam, on the thirty-first of August, less than two weeks after the king he had mourned, Aidan died. Bede says he was bishop for seventeen years.

He did not die in a bed. He died with his back against the church, and his face to the sea.

[pause]

And I would like to tell you one last small thing from Bede, which is about the beam.

Bede says that years afterward the church at Bamburgh was burned, twice, in the wars. And each time, he is told, the fire burned everything else around it, and left the wooden beam that Aidan had leaned against standing, untouched. Whether the beam is a wonder or a coincidence or a bit of kind exaggeration by people who loved the bishop, I do not know. But I like that Bede thought it was worth recording. It was just a piece of timber, and it stayed upright. That is the sort of thing people say about the people they loved.

Aidan's body was carried to Lindisfarne, and buried among the brothers there, in the churchyard of the little church on the island. A little later, a new bishop, Finan, built a bigger church there. And much later, after the island had known all sorts of things, including a great monastery, and the Lindisfarne Gospels, and Saint Cuthbert, who as a shepherd boy is said to have seen a vision on the night Aidan died, and then the Vikings, and then centuries of quiet, the pilgrims began to come. They come still. At low tide, you can walk across the sand, as they did, over a causeway that vanishes behind you, and find a ruined priory, and a small parish church, and the sea. There is a statue of Aidan there now.

And I would say that it is exactly right, that it is a statue of a man who is standing up. He is not on a horse.

[pause]

His feast day is the thirty-first of August, in the Catholic Church and in the Orthodox, and he is remembered as the apostle of the north. That is not, I think, because he was a great scholar, though he knew his Scripture. It is not because he was a great organiser, although he founded a school and several monasteries and sent out a number of young men who became bishops themselves. It is because of the way he did the work. He went among the people, on his own feet, and spoke to them at their own level, and gave away what he was given. Bede, who disagreed with the Irish about some matters, including the date of Easter, nonetheless wrote of him, and I will paraphrase, that he loved him very much, and that everything he taught was what he lived.

And I think we can finish with that, because it is a good thing to be said of anyone. That he lived what he taught. That if you gave him a horse, he found someone who needed it. That if you asked him why, he told you the truth, once, kindly, and then sat you down to dinner.

Let me give you the notes, for those who are curious.

Everything about Aidan that we have comes from one man, the Venerable Bede, a monk of Jarrow, who finished his Ecclesiastical History about the year seven hundred and thirty-one, eighty years after Aidan died. Bede never met him. But he knew the monasteries Aidan had started, and he talked to men who remembered, and he names some of his informants. Bede is, as historians go, a very careful one, and he says what he has heard and what he has not. The facts of Aidan's life, that he was an Irish monk of Iona, that King Oswald asked for him, that he settled on Lindisfarne, that he went about on foot, and that he died at Bamburgh on the thirty-first of August in the year six hundred and fifty-one, are as sure as anything from that century. The conversation at the table, the horse given to the beggar, and the king's kneeling, are told by Bede as things reported, and I have kept them as close to his words as I could. The oil and the storm, the wind that turned the fire at Bamburgh, and the beam that stood through the flames are legends in the sense that they are reports of wonders, handed down, and I have told them as such. What I invented was everything else that is small: the grey morning, the joke about the moving wall, the beggar's cough, the mutterings of the young monks about the long psalm, and Utta's trembling fingers. If any of those were wrong, nothing important would change.

There is an old man sitting in a courtyard, in the late afternoon, and he is laughing.

It is not a big laugh. It is the kind of laugh that you hear from someone who has not laughed for a long time, and is a little surprised to find that it still works. It comes out of him like a creak from a door that has been shut all winter.

In front of him, on a mat, there is a baby. A very small, very new baby girl, with her fists up beside her ears, as if she is about to make an important point and has forgotten what it was. She is looking at him with great seriousness. And he is looking back at her.

"Well," says the old woman who is standing behind him, with a cloth over her shoulder. "Have you thought of a name yet? Because she has been here three days and I am running out of things to call her that are not 'little one.'"

The old man does not take his eyes off the baby.

"Yes," he says. "I have thought of a name."

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Tonight's story begins at the end of a very long book.

The Book of Job is one of the great books of the world. People who do not believe a word of the Bible still read it, and argue about it, and write plays and poems about it. It has been called the most beautiful poem in the Hebrew language, which is a big thing to say, and I am not going to argue. Most of it is in poetry, long speeches, one after another, the way a play is mostly speeches. And most of those speeches are about the hardest question there is, which is: why do good people suffer?

That is not a bedtime question. So tonight we are not going to try to answer it. Wiser heads than mine have spent their whole lives on that book. Saint Gregory the Great, the Pope who sent missionaries to England, wrote a commentary on Job so long that it fills thirty-five books, and he started it as a series of talks for a group of monks, in Constantinople. I like to picture the monks settling down after supper, and Gregory saying, "Now, where were we? Ah, yes. Chapter three." And the monks looking at each other.

No. Tonight we are only going to visit the last page. The part after the storm, when the wind has died down and the sun comes out and the story, very quietly, turns into a story about a family. About bread on the table, and gold rings, and lambs, and three little girls with three of the most beautiful names in the Bible.

But to get to the last page, we need to know, very briefly, what was on the pages before. So let me tell you, in a few minutes, a story that takes the Bible forty-two chapters.

* * *

There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job.

That is how the book begins, like a fairy tale. Once upon a time. And we are not told when. We are not told much about where, either. The land of Uz was somewhere to the east or the south-east of Israel, out towards the desert, perhaps near Edom, the red hill country south of the Dead Sea. Job was not an Israelite. He lived, it seems, in the old days, the days of the patriarchs, when a man's wealth was counted in animals and a father offered sacrifices for his own family, without any temple or any priest.

People have long wondered whether Job was a man who really lived, whose story was handed down and then told as a great poem, or whether he was the hero of the poem from the start, the way a parable has a hero. The prophet Ezekiel mentions him by name, next to Noah and Daniel, as one of the three most righteous men anyone could think of. Saint James mentions him in his letter, and says, you have heard of the steadfastness of Job. The Church has never needed to settle the question, and tonight we will not try. We will tell the story as the book tells it.

And the book tells us that Job was blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil. He had seven sons and three daughters. He had seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred yoke of oxen and five hundred donkeys, and a very great household. He was, the book says, the greatest of all the people of the east. Which I think means that if you had lived in the land of Uz and someone had said "the big house," you would have known which house they meant.

His children loved each other. His sons took turns throwing feasts, each on his own day, and they would send for their three sisters to come and eat and drink with them. And Job, every time a round of feasts was over, would get up early in the morning and offer a sacrifice for each of them, just in case. "It may be," he said, "that my children have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts." He was that kind of father. The kind who checks the locks twice.

And then everything was taken away from him.

I am going to tell this part quickly and quietly, because it is very sad, and it is not our story tonight. In one day, the book says, messengers came running, one after another, each one arriving before the last had finished speaking. The oxen and the donkeys were gone. The sheep were gone. The camels were gone. And a great wind had come across the wilderness and struck the house where his sons and daughters were feasting, and they had all gone home to God.

And Job stood up, and tore his robe, and shaved his head, and fell to the ground, and worshipped. And he said, The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord.

After that he became very ill, with sores all over him, and he went and sat down among the ashes. The old Greek translation says it was outside the town, on the heap where the rubbish was burned. And three friends heard about it and came from their own homes to comfort him. Eliphaz the Temanite. Bildad the Shuhite. Zophar the Naamathite. And when they saw him from a distance, they did not recognise him. And they wept, and tore their robes, and threw dust in the air over their heads, and then they sat down on the ground beside him for seven days and seven nights, and no one said a word to him, because they saw that his suffering was very great.

And honestly, if they had kept that up, they would have been the best friends in the whole Bible.

But then they started talking.

* * *

That is the middle of the book. Round after round of speeches. Job cries out that he does not understand why this has happened to him, and he wishes he could put his case before God, the way you would put a case before a judge. And his friends, each in turn, tell him, more and more crossly, that God is just, and so if Job is suffering, Job must have done something to deserve it, and he had better own up.

They say a great many true things about God, those friends. That is the strange part. They say beautiful things. But they say them at the wrong time, to the wrong person, for the wrong reason. They use what they know about God like a stick to hit a man who is already lying in the ashes.

And Job will not take it. He says, I have not done this. I do not know why it has happened, but I know it is not that. And in the middle of all his anger and grief, he says one of the most famous things in the whole of the Old Testament, the words that are sung at funerals to this day. I know that my Redeemer lives, and that at the last he will stand upon the earth. And after my skin has been destroyed, yet in my flesh I shall see God, whom I shall see for myself, and my eyes shall behold, and not another.

Then a fourth, younger man called Elihu, who has been waiting politely for the old men to finish, which takes a very long time, makes a long speech of his own. And then, at last, the Lord answers Job out of the whirlwind.

And what the Lord says is not an explanation at all. It is a tour.

Where were you, the Lord says, when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding. When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy? Have you entered the storehouses of the snow? Can you send out the lightning, so that it goes, and says to you, here we are? Do you know when the mountain goats give birth? Do you give the horse his strength? Is it by your wisdom that the hawk soars, and spreads her wings towards the south?

On and on it goes, the sea and the stars and the rain and the wild donkey and the ostrich, who leaves her eggs on the ground and forgets about them, because God did not give her much sense, the book says, and yet when she runs, she laughs at the horse and its rider. Which is one of my favourite lines in the Bible. Even the silly ostrich is in God's tour of the world. Even the ostrich is his.

And at the end of it, Job answers, very simply. I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear. But now my eye sees you. And he lays down his complaint, and repents in dust and ashes.

He never learns why. The book never tells him about the first two chapters, the scene in the heavenly court that we, the readers, are allowed to see. He is not given an explanation. He is given God.

And that is where our story begins.

[pause]

* * *

The storm has passed. The wind has dropped. Job is still sitting in the ashes, outside the town, and his three friends are still sitting there too, I suppose a little stunned, with their beards full of dust.

And then the Lord speaks to Eliphaz.

Eliphaz is the eldest of the three, it seems, the one who always spoke first. And the Lord says to him, My anger is kindled against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has.

Now, that is a very surprising sentence. Job has said some wild things over the last forty chapters. He has shouted. He has argued. He has said he wishes he had never been born. And the friends have said a great many respectable, pious, tidy things. And yet it is Job, God says, who has spoken rightly. Job, who was honest. Job, who spoke to God, and not just about him.

Then the Lord tells Eliphaz what to do. Take seven bulls and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up a burnt offering for yourselves. And my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept his prayer, and not deal with you according to your folly.

My servant Job. Four times in this short passage, God calls him that. My servant Job. As if to make sure that nobody, least of all those three, misses the point.

Let us imagine them going off to find the animals. The book does not tell us where three foreign gentlemen sitting on a rubbish heap found seven bulls and seven rams at short notice. Perhaps they had brought their own flocks with them, the way wealthy travellers did. Perhaps they had to go and buy them, and haggle, and drive them back up the road. I like to think of Bildad, who was a little stout, in my mind, trotting along behind seven bulls with a stick, saying "This way, please. No. This way," and the bulls taking absolutely no notice.

And then they come back. And here is the picture I want you to hold on to. The three friends, standing beside the altar, with their animals. And Job, still covered in sores, still sitting in the ashes, with nothing at all, getting slowly to his feet, and raising his hands, and praying for them.

For the men who had told him, for days on end, that it was all his own fault.

He could have said no. He could have said, "Oh, now you need me, do you?" He could have said something very clever and very cutting, and I think most of us would have understood. But he did not. He prayed for them. And the Lord accepted Job's prayer.

And then comes the sentence that the whole ending turns on. And the Lord restored the fortunes of Job, when he had prayed for his friends.

When he had prayed for his friends. Not before. Not when he was proved right, or when the argument was won. When he had prayed for the people who had hurt him.

* * *

Now we come to the part of the story that I love best. Because after the whirlwind, and after the sacrifice, the very next thing the book tells us is about a dinner party.

Then came to him all his brothers and all his sisters, and all who had known him before, and they ate bread with him in his house.

Isn't that lovely? It is such an ordinary sentence, after all those chapters about the foundations of the earth and the storehouses of the snow. They ate bread with him in his house. That is how you know the worst is over. Not when there is thunder, but when there is bread.

And it is a little pointed, too, if you read it carefully. All his brothers and all his sisters, and all who had known him before. Where had they been? In chapter nineteen, Job says, My brothers are far from me, and my acquaintances are wholly estranged from me. My relatives and my close friends have failed me. The guests in my house have forgotten me, and my servants count me as a stranger. My breath is strange to my wife. Even young children despise me. They had all stayed away. Illness and bad luck can be like that. People do not know what to say, and so they say nothing, and then it has been so long that they are embarrassed to come, and so they stay away a little longer.

But now they come. All of them. And Job lets them in.

Let us imagine that dinner. The house, in my mind, is not yet very grand. It is a smaller house than the old one, with a courtyard and a fig tree and an oven in the corner, and the household is small too. There is an old woman there, and since the book does not mention her, we are going to invent her, and call her Adinah. She has been in Job's household since before any of the children were born. She nursed them all, every one of the ten, and she has stayed, all through the bad years, when nearly everyone else went away. She is not afraid of Job, or of anyone. She is the kind of old woman who has opinions about how bread should be baked and does not keep them to herself.

And on this evening, she is baking a great deal of bread.

"How many are coming?" she says, as the first of the relations appear at the gate.

"All of them," says Job.

Adinah looks at the gate. Then at the oven. Then at Job.

"All of them," she says. "After all this time. And they will all want to eat."

"That is generally the idea of a meal, Adinah."

She sniffs, and goes back to the oven, and bakes a great deal more bread than she said she would, which is the way of old women who sniff.

And they come in, the brothers and the sisters and the cousins and the old neighbours, a little shy, a little ashamed, I think, not quite looking him in the eye. And they sit down. And they eat bread with him. And the book says that they showed him sympathy and comforted him for all the evil that the Lord had brought upon him.

That is the book's own way of putting it, and it does not soften it. The family do not pretend nothing happened. They do not say, "Well, it all worked out in the end." They sit with him and they talk about it, about all of it, the children, the sickness, the long years in the ashes, and they comfort him. That is what they should have done the first time. Better late than never. Much better.

And then, as they leave, each one of them gives him a gift. Every one of them, the book says, gave him a piece of money, and a ring of gold.

That piece of money is a strange and interesting word in Hebrew. It is a kesitah. It only turns up three times in the whole Bible. Once here. Once when Jacob buys a field outside the town of Shechem for a hundred kesitahs. And once when Joseph's bones are buried in that very same field, many years later. Nobody is quite sure what a kesitah was. A weight of silver, probably, or a piece of silver of a certain weight. But some of the very old translators thought it was something else. In the Latin Bible, Saint Jerome's translation, it says that each of them gave Job one sheep, and one golden earring. And the old English Catholic Bible, the Douay, which was made from the Latin, says it beautifully: And they gave him every man one ewe, and one earring of gold.

I rather like the idea of the sheep. Picture it. All those relations going home down the road, and behind them, in the courtyard, a growing crowd of sheep. One from each guest. Baa. And Adinah standing in the doorway with her hands on her hips, looking at them.

"Well," she says. "We will not need to buy wool for a while."

It would not have been enough to make anyone rich, a sheep and a ring from each guest. That is not the point. The point is that this is how a family says, we are sorry we were not here. We are here now. Here is something to start again with.

And Job did start again.

* * *

And the Lord blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning.

Here the book gives us a list of numbers, the same kind of list as at the very start of the story, and I am going to read them to you, because whoever wrote the book clearly enjoyed this part, and I think we should too.

At the beginning, you remember, Job had seven thousand sheep. Now he had fourteen thousand sheep. He had had three thousand camels. Now he had six thousand camels. He had had five hundred yoke of oxen. Now he had a thousand yoke of oxen. He had had five hundred donkeys. Now he had a thousand donkeys.

Double. Every single one. Exactly double. The book does not say how long it took. It does not say whether the first sheep came from the guests at the dinner party, though I like to think so. It just tells us, with great satisfaction, the way you would tell the end of a fairy tale, that the Lord gave Job twice as much as he had had before.

Now, I want to say something carefully here, because this part of the story is very easy to get wrong. The book is not saying that if you are good, you will always get rich. The whole of the rest of the book has just spent forty chapters saying that it is not as simple as that. That was the mistake the three friends made. Good people do suffer. Good people sometimes stay poor all their lives. The ending is not a rule. It is a gift. God was not paying Job back, as if he owed him, and he was not paying him for being patient. He was being generous, the way God is generous. Saint James says it well, in his letter: You have heard of the steadfastness of Job, and have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful.

Compassionate and merciful. That is what the double sheep are about.

But there is one thing on the list that is not doubled. And it is the thing that matters most.

He had also seven sons and three daughters.

Seven sons and three daughters. The same number as before. Not fourteen and six. Seven and three.

People who read the Bible carefully have noticed this for a very long time, and many of them have thought about why. And the answer that many Christian readers have given, and which I find very beautiful, goes like this. The sheep and the camels and the donkeys that were lost were lost for good. So to give Job twice as much, God had to give him twice as many. But the children who had gone home to God were not lost for good. They were waiting. And so seven more sons and three more daughters were already double. Ten with him in the land of Uz, and ten waiting for him in the house of God.

The book itself does not say that. It is a reading, a way of seeing it, not a line of Scripture. But it is a very old and a very kind one, and it is the one I would like to give you to take to bed tonight.

[pause]

* * *

And now, at last, we come to the three girls.

The book does something here that it does not do anywhere else. All through the story, nobody in Job's family has a name. Not his wife. Not his first ten children. Not the seven new sons, either. Not one of them is named. But when it comes to the three new daughters, the book stops, and tells us, one by one, what they were called.

And he called the name of the first Jemimah, and the name of the second Keziah, and the name of the third Keren-happuch.

Let me tell you what those names mean. Because they are wonderful.

Jemimah is the first. Nobody is completely sure what it means, but most people think it means dove. A little dove. The bird of peace, the bird that came back to Noah with an olive leaf in its beak, to say that the waters had gone down. Which is not a bad name for a child born after a flood of trouble. The old Greek translators of the Bible, who sometimes heard things differently, thought it meant day. Daylight. And that is not a bad name either, for a child born after a long night.

Keziah is the second. Keziah means cassia. Cassia is a spice, the bark of a tree, very like cinnamon, sweet and warm. It was precious, and it came from far away. In the Psalms, when the poet describes a king dressed for his wedding, he says, your robes are all fragrant with myrrh and aloes and cassia. So Keziah is a name that smells good. It is a name like Cinnamon. A name for a girl who makes the whole house smell of something warm.

And then there is the third one. Keren-happuch. Which I think is the best of all.

Keren-happuch means a horn of eye paint.

Now, you need to know that in the ancient world, all across that part of the world, women, and quite often men too, painted their eyes. They used a fine black powder, made from a mineral called antimony, and they put it round their eyes with a little stick, to make them look bigger and darker and more beautiful. The Egyptians did it. Queen Jezebel did it, in the Book of Kings, before she looked out of her window. And they kept the powder in a little container, and often that container was made from the tip of an animal's horn, polished and stoppered. A horn of eye paint. The ancient equivalent, more or less, of the little pot of make-up you might find on a shelf in the bathroom.

So imagine. You are a girl in the land of Uz, and your big sister is called Dove, and your other big sister is called Cinnamon, and you are called Make-up Box.

I think it is marvellous. It is a name that says, you are so beautiful that you are the very thing that other people use to make themselves beautiful. It is a joke and a compliment at the same time, the sort of name a father who has started laughing again might give.

Let me take you back, now, to the scene at the beginning. The courtyard, the late afternoon, the fig tree. Job, sitting on a bench. The baby on the mat. And Adinah, with the cloth over her shoulder.

"Have you thought of a name yet?" she says.

This is the third girl. Jemimah is there already, a serious little girl of five or six, who has very firm views about everything, and who is sitting on the step, guarding her new sister from an imaginary danger. Keziah is three, and she is in the corner of the courtyard, investigating a beetle, and has jam, or whatever they had instead of jam, on her chin.

"Yes," says Job. "I have thought of a name."

"Well?"

"Keren-happuch."

There is a pause.

Adinah looks at the baby. The baby looks at Adinah.

"A horn of eye paint," says Adinah, very slowly.

"Look at her eyes," says Job.

And Adinah looks. And the baby's eyes are very dark, and very large, and very solemn, as if they have been painted round with antimony by the most careful hand in the world. And Adinah, who has been in the household forty years, and who has seen everything, and who has been through all of it, and who has not cried in front of anyone since the day of the great wind, finds that she has to turn round very suddenly and see to something in the oven.

"It is a ridiculous name," she says, with her back to him. "She will be teased."

"No one will tease her," says Job. "Not in this house."

And from the step, Jemimah says, in a very firm voice, "I will not let anyone tease her."

And that was settled.

* * *

The book says one more thing about the three girls before it lets them go. And in all the land there were no women so beautiful as Job's daughters.

The book does not usually bother with that sort of thing. It does not tell us whether Job was handsome, or whether his friends had nice beards. But it tells us that the three girls grew up to be the most beautiful women in the whole land of Uz. I think the book is smiling when it says it. It is the kind of thing a happy father says, and everyone around him nods and agrees, because it is true, and because it would be very rude not to.

Let us imagine them, growing up. Jemimah, serious and kind, the one who keeps everyone in order, who knows where everything is, and who her younger brothers go to when they have scraped a knee. Keziah, warm and quick, always laughing, always in the kitchen with Adinah, who pretends to find her a nuisance and secretly saves her the best bits. And Keren-happuch, the youngest, quiet, watchful, with those great dark eyes, who sits beside her father in the evening and asks him questions. Questions about the stars and the snow and the hawk and the mountain goats.

"Father," she says one evening, when she is perhaps eight or nine, "is it true that you talked to God, out of the wind?"

Job is quiet for a while.

"It is true that he talked to me," he says. "I mostly listened. I had done enough talking."

"What did he say?"

"He asked me where I was when he laid the foundations of the earth."

Keren-happuch thinks about this.

"Where were you?"

"Nowhere at all," says Job. "That was rather the point."

"And then what did he say?"

"Then he told me about the ostrich."

"The ostrich?"

"The ostrich," says Job, "who leaves her eggs in the sand and forgets about them, because God did not give her much sense. But when she gets up to run, she laughs at the horse and its rider."

Keren-happuch laughs, the way children laugh at ostriches. And Job laughs too. That creaky laugh again, but it is less creaky now. He has had some practice.

"Was that all?"

"No," says Job. "But that is the part I think about most, at night. That even the ostrich is his. Even the silly bird who forgets her eggs. He knows when she runs. He made her laugh. He did not forget her, even if she forgets everything else." He puts his hand on her head. "So he does not forget anyone."

That conversation, I must remind you, is our own invention. The book does not tell us what Job said to his children. But the words about the ostrich and the foundations of the earth are the book's own, from chapters thirty-eight and thirty-nine, and I think any father who had heard them would have told them to his children, at bedtime, more than once.

* * *

And now we come to the very last surprise of the book, and it is a quiet one, and it is about money.

And their father gave them an inheritance among their brothers.

You may need me to explain why that is surprising. In those days, and for a very long time afterwards, in most of the world, when a father died, everything he owned went to his sons. The land, the flocks, the house. His daughters would be given a dowry when they married, and they would be looked after, but they did not usually inherit the land. That was simply how it was done.

There is a story about this in the Book of Numbers, which we told on this show not long ago. Five sisters, the daughters of a man called Zelophehad, whose father had died with no sons. They came to the door of the tent of meeting, in front of Moses and the priest and all the leaders, and they said, why should our father's name disappear from his family, just because he had no son? Give us a possession among our father's brothers. And Moses took their case to the Lord, and the Lord said, the daughters of Zelophehad are right. And it became the law in Israel that when a man died with no sons, his inheritance would go to his daughters.

But Job had sons. Seven of them. He did not need to give anything to his daughters at all. Nobody would have expected it. And he did it anyway. He gave them a share among their brothers, alongside them, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

Why? The book does not say. But I think we can guess a little. Here was a man who had lost everything, and been given it all back, as a gift, not as a payment. Here was a man who had been told by God himself that the world is full of things that nobody earned. The snow, the rain on land where nobody lives, the wild donkey, the ostrich, the morning stars. And so perhaps it seemed to him that the best thing to do with gifts was to give them away. Generously. More than anyone expected. To the ones nobody thought had any claim.

Let us imagine the day he tells them.

It is a spring morning. The three girls are grown women now, and Jemimah is about to be married. The whole family is gathered in the big room, the seven brothers along one wall, the three sisters along the other, and Adinah, very old now, very small, sitting in the best seat by the door, because she has earned it, and because nobody would dare to give it to anyone else.

And Job tells them. The fields by the stream, and these flocks, and that house, will go to his sons, as they would expect. And these fields, and these flocks, and that well, will go to Jemimah, and Keziah, and Keren-happuch.

There is a silence.

The eldest brother speaks first. In my imagining, his name is not given, because the book does not give it, and so I will not give it either.

"Father," he says. "That is not how it is done."

"No," says Job. "It is not."

There is another silence. And then the second brother, who has always been the funny one, says, "Well, I suppose it means that Keziah will be able to buy her own cinnamon now, instead of stealing ours."

And everyone laughs. And Keziah throws a cushion at him. And it is settled. And Adinah, by the door, nods, very slowly, as if this were something she had suggested years ago, which, for all we know, she had.

* * *

There is a book, a very old book, written in Greek, about two thousand years ago, called the Testament of Job. It is not in the Bible. Neither the Catholic Church nor the Orthodox Church reads it as Scripture. It is one of those many old writings that people made up, around the edges of the Bible stories, to fill in the parts the Bible left out. But it has a lovely idea about Job's three daughters, and I want to tell it to you, as a story, which is all it is.

In the Testament of Job, when the old man is about to die, he divides his property among his sons. And his three daughters say, what about us? Are we not your children too? And Job says, I have something for you that is better than anything I have given your brothers. And he gives each of them a shining sash, a many-coloured cord, to tie around her waist. And when they put them on, the old story says, their hearts were changed, and they no longer thought about the things of the earth, and they began to sing hymns to God in the language of the angels.

It is a strange and beautiful idea, and it was clearly written by somebody who had read that last chapter of Job, and noticed the daughters, and loved them, and wanted to give them something more. But it is not the Bible. In the Bible, Job gives them an inheritance among their brothers, fields and flocks and good, solid, earthly things. And I think I like the Bible's version better. It is not that the daughters were too heavenly to need an inheritance. It is that their father thought they deserved one, just like their brothers. That is a gift you can live on.

[pause]

* * *

And after this, Job lived a hundred and forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons' sons, four generations.

Four generations. Think of it. His children, and their children, and their children's children. All those small feet running through the courtyard. All those small faces at the table. A house that had been completely empty, filled up again until it was spilling over at the gate.

Let us imagine one more evening, a long way down the years.

Adinah has long since gone home to God, at a very great age, with all ten of the second children round her, and a good many grandchildren too. She is buried under the fig tree, because she said she wanted to be able to keep an eye on the courtyard.

And Job is very old now. Older than anybody in the land of Uz can remember anyone being. He sits on his bench in the evening, by the fig tree, with a blanket over his knees, and the stars come out, one by one, over the desert. The morning stars, and the evening stars, and all the others.

And round him there are children. Great-grandchildren, and great-great-grandchildren, too many to count, and he has given up trying to remember all their names, so he calls them all "little one," which is what Adinah used to do. And one of them, a little girl, the granddaughter of a granddaughter of Keren-happuch, perhaps, with her great-great-grandmother's dark eyes, climbs up on to the bench next to him and says, "Tell us about the ostrich."

And he does. He always does.

He tells them about the storehouses of the snow. And the hawk spreading her wings towards the south. And the mountain goats. And the wild donkey who scorns the noise of the city. And the morning stars singing together when the foundations of the earth were laid. And the silly ostrich, who forgets everything, and is not forgotten.

And one by one, the children fall asleep. On the bench, and on the mat, and against his knees. And Job stops talking, and sits very still, so as not to wake them, and looks up at the stars, and says, very quietly, to someone nobody else can see, the thing he said at the very beginning of his story, all those years ago, on the worst day of his life.

The Lord gave. And the Lord has taken away. And the Lord has given again. Blessed be the name of the Lord.

That last part, I should say, the words about giving again, are our own. But I do not think Job would mind.

* * *

And Job died, the book says, an old man, and full of days.

That is the very last sentence. Old, and full of days. It is the same thing the Bible says about Abraham, and Isaac, and King David. It means a long life, and a full one, a life that had everything in it that a life can hold. The worst sorrow, and the greatest joy. Ashes, and bread. The whirlwind, and the fig tree in the evening.

The old Greek translation of the Bible, the one the first Christians mostly read, adds a few lines at the end of the book that are not in the Hebrew. It says that Job will rise again, with those whom the Lord raises up. And it says that some people believed Job was the same man as a king of Edom called Jobab, who is mentioned in the Book of Genesis, in a long list of kings. Whether that is right, nobody knows. It is a note someone added to the end of the book, a long time ago, the way you might write a note in the margin of a book you love. But that first line, that Job will rise again, is something Christians have always believed about all the righteous, and it is what Job himself said, in the middle of his sorrow. I know that my Redeemer lives. And in my flesh I shall see God.

The Church has always loved Job. In the Orthodox Church, during the first days of Holy Week, the week before Easter, the Book of Job is read in the evening services, so that every year the people hear about the man who suffered without knowing why, and was given back more than he lost, just before they keep the days when the Lord himself suffered, and rose. And in the Catholic Church, for many centuries, the prayers that were said for those who had died were full of the words of Job, so that when people mourned, they had Job's own words to say. In the Orthodox Church he is called the holy and righteous Job, the Long-suffering, and his feast is on the sixth of May. In the Roman Martyrology, the Catholic Church's great calendar of the saints, he is remembered as Saint Job on the tenth of May.

A saint who was not a Christian, and not even an Israelite. Just a man in the land of Uz, who feared God, and was honest with him, and prayed for his friends, and named his daughters Dove, and Cinnamon, and Make-up Box, and gave them a share of everything he had.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Book of Job, chapter forty-two, verses seven to seventeen, told straight, in a plain rendering. The story of Job's first troubles is in chapters one and two, and the words I know that my Redeemer lives are in chapter nineteen. The Lord's speech from the whirlwind, with the foundations of the earth and the ostrich, is in chapters thirty-eight to forty-one, and Job's answer is at the start of chapter forty-two. I have summed all of that up very briefly, and left out a great deal.

The prophet Ezekiel names Job with Noah and Daniel in chapter fourteen of his book, and Saint James speaks of the steadfastness of Job in chapter five of his letter. The daughters of Zelophehad are in the Book of Numbers, chapter twenty-seven. The kesitah turns up in Genesis thirty-three and Joshua twenty-four. The sheep and the golden earring are how Saint Jerome's Latin Bible, and the Douay translation made from it, render Job forty-two, verse eleven. What the names of the three daughters mean is how most scholars read them, though Jemimah is not certain, and the old Greek translation gives the names differently. The idea that the children were not doubled because the first ones were not lost for good is a reading many Christian teachers have given, not a line of Scripture. Saint Gregory the Great's Morals on the Book of Job is a real and very long book. The Testament of Job is a real old writing, but it is not Scripture, and I have told it as a story.

Adinah the old nurse is our own invention. So are the courtyard and the fig tree, the dinner party in detail, the conversations about the names and the ostrich, the day of the inheritance, and the evenings at the end. The book does not tell us how Job named his daughters, only what he named them, and it does not tell us what he said to them. Nothing has been added to what God, or Job, or his friends, or his family say or do in the Book of Job.

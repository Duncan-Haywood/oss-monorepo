There is a small room behind the palace in Jerusalem, with one window, and a long table, and on the table there are a great many pieces of broken pottery with numbers scratched on them. Silver brought in. Silver paid out. So much to the carpenters. So much for cedar beams.

At the end of the table an old man is reading out loud. He is not reading the numbers. He has put the numbers down. He is reading from a scroll, and his voice, which is usually very dry and very calm, the voice of a man who adds things up for a living, has gone quiet and slow.

Across the table from him there is a young man, hardly more than a boy, holding a pen that he has forgotten to use. A drop of ink is gathering on the end of it. It falls on the table. Nobody notices.

And in the doorway, where he has been standing for some time without anyone seeing him, is the king. He is twenty-six years old. And as the old man reads, the king puts his hand to the neck of his robe, takes hold of it, and tears it, right down the front.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

We are in Jerusalem, about six hundred and twenty-two years before Christ, as most people reckon it, and Jerusalem is not at its best.

It is a city on a ridge, in the hills of Judah, with steep valleys on three sides. At the top of the ridge is the temple that Solomon built, three hundred years before, and next to it the palace of the kings. Below them, running down the narrow spur of hill to the south, is the old city of David, which by now is very old indeed, and crowded, and smells, on a warm day, of goats and woodsmoke and people. And across the valley to the west, on a second, broader hill, there is a newer part of the city, where people have been building houses for the last hundred years or so, ever since the northern kingdom fell and refugees came south with whatever they could carry. The Bible calls this part of the city the Second Quarter. In Hebrew it is the Mishneh, which means something like the second one, or the copy. If you have ever lived in a place called New Town that is now about four hundred years old, you will understand the feeling.

The people of Jerusalem have had a difficult century. Their kings, on the whole, have not helped. The worst of them was Manasseh, who reigned for fifty-five years, which is a very long time to be the worst of anything. He put up altars to other gods in the courts of the temple itself. He brought in the worship of the sun and the moon and the stars. He listened to fortune-tellers. The Book of Kings has a long list, and it is not bedtime reading, so we will leave it there. By the time Manasseh died, the temple of the Lord was cluttered and half-forgotten, a sort of religious junk shop, with the God of Israel pushed into a corner of his own house.

Manasseh's son Amon reigned for two years, did much the same, and was killed by his own servants in his own house. And the people of the land, the ordinary landowners of Judah, who had had quite enough, made his little son king instead.

His name was Josiah. He was eight years old.

Now, an eight-year-old king does not do very much ruling. He has advisers. He has a mother, whose name the Bible gives us, which it does not always do: she was Jedidah, the daughter of Adaiah, from a village called Bozkath, out in the low hills towards the coast. Her name means beloved. I like to think she was the reason for what happened next, but the Bible does not say so, so I will only think it quietly.

What the Book of Chronicles does tell us is that in the eighth year of his reign, when he was sixteen, and still, as the book says, a boy, Josiah began to seek the God of David his father. And four years later, at twenty, he began to clear out the junk. The altars on the hills. The carved poles. The images. It took years. And then, in the eighteenth year of his reign, when he was twenty-six, he turned to the temple itself, and decided that it was time for the builders.

[pause]

* * *

The building works began, as building works do, with money.

People who came up to the temple had been dropping silver into a chest by the door for some time, and the keepers of the threshold, the priests who stood at the temple doors, had been collecting it. Nobody had spent it. So the king sent his secretary, a man called Shaphan son of Azaliah, up to Hilkiah the high priest, with instructions.

Count the silver, said the king. Give it to the men in charge of the work. Let them pay the carpenters, and the builders, and the masons, and buy timber and cut stone to repair the house.

And then the king said something that I want you to notice, because it is one of the most surprising sentences in the whole of the Old Testament. He said: and do not ask them for an account of the money that is given to them. Because they deal honestly.

[pause]

I will say that again, slowly, for anyone listening who has ever run a building project. The king said: do not ask the builders for receipts. Because they are honest.

This sentence has made accountants nervous for about two thousand six hundred years. I am told that there are people who work in church finance offices who cannot read it without needing to sit down. But there it is, in the Second Book of Kings, chapter twenty-two, verse seven, and it was apparently true. The Book of Chronicles adds that the men in charge of the work were Levites, and that the Levites who supervised the builders were the ones who were skilled with musical instruments. So the picture is of a building site in Jerusalem, with honest masons cutting stone, and carpenters fitting cedar beams, and the overseers walking about among them, who were, in their spare time, the temple musicians. I have no idea whether that made the work go faster. But I suspect it made it go more cheerfully.

It was while they were bringing out the silver, says Chronicles, that Hilkiah the high priest found something.

* * *

In the scribes' room at the palace, where we began, there was a young man called Ira.

He is our invention, so do not look for him in the Bible. He was fifteen, the son of a scribe, and the grandson of a scribe, and he had been in Shaphan's office for two years, which meant that he had graduated from sharpening the pens to actually holding one. His job, on the day our story really begins, was to sit at the long table with the potsherds, the broken bits of pottery that the office used for scrap paper, and to write down numbers as Shaphan read them out.

So much silver weighed out of the chest. So much given to the overseers. So much for cedar. So much for the quarrymen. Ira had very neat handwriting, and an excellent memory for numbers, and he was, if we are honest, a little bored.

Shaphan came back from the temple in the middle of the morning, carrying a leather bag. He put the bag down on the end of the table without saying anything about it, and went through the accounts with Ira, line by line, as he always did. Then he gathered up the potsherds, straightened his robe, and went to report to the king.

Ira, who was not supposed to, followed him as far as the door of the king's room, and stood just outside it, where he could hear.

And this is the bit of the story that I love most, because the Bible tells it exactly as a civil servant would. Shaphan went in to the king and gave his report. Your servants have emptied out the silver that was found in the house, he said, and have delivered it into the hands of the workmen who have the oversight of the house of the Lord.

Very good, presumably said the king.

And then Shaphan said, in the same tone of voice, as though it were the next item on the agenda, which I suppose it was: also, Hilkiah the priest has given me a book.

[pause]

That is what he says. In the Hebrew it is just as flat as that. A report on the money, and then, by the way, a book. You can almost see him tucking it at the bottom of the pile. Any other business.

And then Shaphan read it to the king.

* * *

We are not told what the book looked like. It would have been a scroll, of leather or papyrus, rolled up and perhaps tied. The text calls it the book of the law, and a little later the book of the covenant. It does not tell us how long it was, or how old, or how it came to be lying in the temple, in a corner, or a storeroom, or the bottom of a chest, through all the long years of Manasseh, when nobody was looking for it.

What was it? People have wondered about that for a very long time. Many readers, ancient and modern, have thought that it was the Book of Deuteronomy, or a large part of it, the long last speech of Moses on the edge of the promised land. There are good reasons for thinking so. Much of what Josiah does next sounds like Deuteronomy. But the text itself does not say, so neither will I. It is enough to know that it was the law of the Lord, and that it had been lost, and now it was found.

And here is something that Deuteronomy does say, if it was Deuteronomy. It says that every king of Israel should have a copy of this law written out for himself, and keep it with him, and read it all the days of his life, so that his heart would not be lifted up above his brothers. It says that every seven years the whole law should be read aloud to all the people, men and women and children and the strangers in the towns, so that they could hear and learn. And it says, in the most famous passage of all, hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one; and you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your might. And these words shall be on your heart, and you shall teach them to your children, and talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise.

When you lie down. So you are, in a way, doing something very old this evening.

Whatever exactly was in the book, the young king had never heard it. Nobody in Jerusalem, it seems, had heard it for a very long time. And as Shaphan read, in his dry, careful voice, about the covenant, and the blessings for keeping it, and the curses for breaking it, and the people who would be scattered if they went after other gods, the king began to understand that this was not an old document. It was a description. It was a description of his grandfather's reign, and his father's, and of the city outside the window.

And Ira, standing outside the door where he was not supposed to be, heard a sound he had never heard before, which was the sound of a king tearing his clothes.

* * *

When a person in the Bible tears their clothes, it is not a tantrum. It is grief, and it is repentance, both at once. It is what you do when you hear that someone has died, or when you realise that you have done a terrible wrong. Jacob tore his clothes when he was shown Joseph's coat. Job tore his robe when the messengers came. And now Josiah, who had been trying to put things right since he was a boy, heard what the Lord had actually asked of his people, and realised how far they had all come from it, and he tore his robe right down the front.

Then he did something else that I think is very much to his credit. He did not decide, on his own, what it meant. He did not call his advisers together and ask them what the politically sensible response would be. He sent people to ask the Lord.

He called in five men. Hilkiah the high priest. Shaphan the scribe. Shaphan's son Ahikam. A man called Achbor, the son of Micaiah. And Asaiah, the king's servant, which was a title, rather like a senior minister.

Go, said the king, and ask the Lord for me, and for the people, and for all Judah, about the words of this book that has been found. Because great is the wrath of the Lord that is kindled against us, because our fathers did not listen to the words of this book, to do all that is written in it about us.

That is a remarkable thing for a king to say. Our fathers did not listen. He is talking about his own father and his own grandfather. He does not make excuses for them. He also does not say, well, that was them, it is nothing to do with me. He says, us.

So the five men went out. And Shaphan, who was not a young man, and had a leather bag with a precious scroll in it, and had had a very long morning, looked round the scribes' room, saw Ira trying to look as though he had been there all along, and said: you. Carry this. And come with us.

[pause]

* * *

Now you would think that if the king's high priest and the king's secretary and a senior minister were sent to ask the Lord about something, they would go to the temple. Or to the most famous prophet in the city.

And there were prophets in the city. That is what makes this so interesting. We know from the Book of Jeremiah that Jeremiah had begun to prophesy five years before, in the thirteenth year of Josiah, though he was still young, and was perhaps not in Jerusalem that day. We know from the Book of Zephaniah that Zephaniah son of Cushi prophesied in the days of Josiah too. There were priests and prophets in the temple courts.

But the five men did not go to the temple. They went down from the palace, and out across the city, and over to the western hill, to the newer neighbourhood, the Second Quarter. And they went to the house of a woman.

The Bible introduces her with a short list, the way it often introduces people, by their family. Huldah the prophetess, it says, the wife of Shallum, the son of Tikvah, the son of Harhas, the keeper of the wardrobe. And then, as though anticipating the question of how to find her, it adds: she was living in Jerusalem, in the Second Quarter.

That is all we are told about her. We do not know how old she was. We do not know how she came to be a prophetess, or how long people had been coming to her, or whether this was the first time a king had sent to her or the tenth. But the way the five men went straight to her door, without any discussion, suggests that it was not the first time anyone had asked. Everybody, apparently, knew where to find Huldah.

Her husband Shallum was the keeper of the wardrobe. We are not told whose wardrobe. It may have been the king's robes, or it may have been the vestments of the priests in the temple. Either way, I find it very pleasing to think of Huldah's husband, a man who looked after clothes for a living, on the very morning that the king tore his. Some people have wondered whether this Shallum is the same as the Shallum who was Jeremiah's uncle, whom the Book of Jeremiah mentions much later. It is possible. It is a common name. Nobody knows.

As for Huldah's own name, it is very probably the Hebrew word for a weasel, or a mole, some small quick creature that lives in the ground. We do not know why her parents chose it. Perhaps she was a small quick baby. I like to think it was meant fondly, and that she grew into it, a quick, bright woman who noticed things, and whom it was very hard to fool.

* * *

So here is the procession, as I imagine it. And this part is ours, not the Bible's.

Five men in good robes and one boy with a leather bag, going down the steep streets from the palace in the middle of the day. Hilkiah the high priest in front, because he was the high priest. Shaphan, a little out of breath. Ahikam his son, who was about forty and kept offering to take his father's arm, and kept being refused. Achbor and Asaiah, talking quietly to each other. And Ira at the back, holding the bag with both hands, as though it might jump.

They went down into the valley, past the women at the pool with their water jars, who stopped talking to watch them go by. They went up the other side, into the Second Quarter, where the streets were straighter and the houses newer, and there was a wall that people called the Broad Wall, very thick, built in a hurry a hundred years before when the Assyrians were coming. They went past a man selling fish, and a man mending a roof, and a dog that came out of a doorway to look at them and decided they were not worth barking at.

Ira, who had never in his life walked anywhere with the high priest, was trying very hard not to be noticed, and at the same time very much hoping that someone he knew would see him.

Achbor, after a while, said what several of them had been thinking. You know, he said, there are prophets at the temple.

There are, said Asaiah.

We could have gone to one of them, said Achbor. It would have been closer.

Hilkiah the high priest, who had not said very much all morning, said without turning round: the king said to ask the Lord. Not to ask whoever was closest.

And that was the end of that conversation.

[pause]

* * *

Huldah's house was a house like the others in the street. Two storeys, with a flat roof, and a courtyard in the middle, and a loom standing in the corner of the courtyard with a half-finished piece of cloth on it. There may have been a fig tree. There usually was.

We are told nothing about what happened at the door. The Bible says only that they went to Huldah, and they spoke with her. So I will tell you what I imagine, and you can hold it lightly.

I imagine that she was in the courtyard when they came. I imagine that she looked at the five of them, with their good robes, and the dust on their feet from the walk, and the boy at the back holding the bag, and that she understood at once that this was not a social call. I imagine that she offered them water, because it was a hot day and they had come a long way, and that Shaphan, who was a polite man, drank it, and that Ira drank his in one go and then wished he had not.

And I imagine that Hilkiah explained. The repairs. The silver. The book, found in the house of the Lord. The king, who had heard it read and torn his robe. The question: what does the Lord say about the words of this book, to the king, and to the people, and to Judah.

And then I imagine that Huldah said, read it to me.

That line is ours. The Bible does not say whether she heard the book read, or whether she needed to. But I like to think of Shaphan, for the second time that day, unrolling the scroll in a courtyard in the Second Quarter, and reading it in his careful voice, while the loom stood in the corner and the dog barked somewhere down the street. And Huldah listening, the way a person listens when they are listening to more than one voice at once.

Ira, sitting on the edge of a step with the empty bag across his knees, watched her face. He said afterwards, in my version of the story, that he had never seen anyone listen like that. As though every word was being weighed on very fine scales. As though she could hear the Lord in it.

And when Shaphan had finished, there was a long quiet.

[pause]

* * *

Then Huldah spoke. And from this point on, I am not inventing anything. This is what the Bible says she said, in a plain rendering.

Thus says the Lord, the God of Israel: tell the man who sent you to me.

[pause]

I want to stop there for a moment, because that is a wonderful beginning. The king of Judah, the son of David, has sent his high priest and his secretary and his ministers across the city to her door. And she says: tell the man who sent you to me. Not his majesty. Not the king. The man. Before the Lord, the king of Judah is a man, like any other man, who has asked a question and is waiting for an answer.

Thus says the Lord, she went on. Behold, I will bring evil upon this place, and upon its people, all the words of the book that the king of Judah has read. Because they have forsaken me, and burned incense to other gods, to provoke me with all the work of their hands. My wrath will be kindled against this place, and it will not be quenched.

That is a hard word. She did not soften it. She did not say, well, perhaps it will not be so bad. The book was true, and what it said would happen, would happen. Nobody in that courtyard could have mistaken it.

But she was not finished. And the next part is spoken to one person.

But to the king of Judah, she said, who sent you to ask the Lord, this is what you shall say to him. Thus says the Lord, the God of Israel. As for the words that you have heard: because your heart was tender, and you humbled yourself before the Lord when you heard what I spoke against this place and against its people, that they should become a desolation and a curse, and because you tore your clothes and wept before me, I also have heard you, says the Lord.

I also have heard you.

Therefore, behold, I will gather you to your fathers, and you shall be gathered to your grave in peace, and your eyes shall not see all the evil that I will bring upon this place.

[pause]

And the five men brought back word to the king.

* * *

That is all. That is the whole of Huldah, in the Bible. One visit, one answer, a handful of sentences. She is not mentioned again.

And yet I think it is one of the most important visits in the whole history of Israel. Because of what the king did next.

He did not sulk. He did not say, well, if the disaster is coming anyway, there is not much point. He did not say, well, it will not happen in my lifetime, so it is not my problem. He did exactly the opposite. He had been told that the Lord had heard him. And so he decided that everyone else should hear too.

He sent for all the elders of Judah and Jerusalem. And he went up to the house of the Lord, and with him, the Bible says, went all the men of Judah, and all the people of Jerusalem, and the priests, and the prophets, and all the people, both small and great. And he read in their hearing all the words of the book of the covenant that had been found in the house of the Lord.

The king read it himself. Not Shaphan this time. Not a priest. The king stood up in front of the whole people, small and great, and read them the book.

And then he stood by the pillar, the place where the kings of Judah stood on solemn days, and he made a covenant before the Lord, to walk after the Lord, and to keep his commandments and his testimonies and his statutes with all his heart and all his soul, to perform the words of the covenant that were written in that book. And all the people stood to the covenant.

I imagine Ira was there, somewhere in the crowd, among the small. I imagine Huldah was there too, though nobody would have pointed her out. Just a woman from the Second Quarter, standing with her husband, the keeper of the wardrobe, listening to the king read.

* * *

Then the king went through the city, and the temple, and the whole country, and took away everything that did not belong to the Lord. The altars of the sun and the moon and the stars in the courts of the temple. The images and the carved poles. The horses that earlier kings had dedicated to the sun, at the entrance to the house of the Lord. The high places on the hills. The Book of Kings gives a long and careful list, and he did not leave anything out. He carried the broken pieces down to the Kidron valley and burned them there, and threw the dust into the stream.

It was the biggest clear-out in the history of Jerusalem. And then, when it was done, the king said: now let us keep the Passover.

Keep the Passover to the Lord your God, he said, as it is written in this book of the covenant.

And they did. Chronicles tells us about it in loving detail, the way you would describe a wedding. The priests in their places. The Levites, who were told to put the holy ark back in the house that Solomon had built, and stop carrying it about on their shoulders. The singers in their places, the sons of Asaph, singing. The gatekeepers at every gate, who did not have to leave their posts even for the meal, because their brothers the Levites brought it to them. And the king gave from his own flocks thirty thousand lambs and young goats, and three thousand bulls, for the people to eat, so that nobody would be left out because they were poor.

No Passover like it had been kept, says the Book of Kings, since the days of the judges who judged Israel, nor in all the days of the kings of Israel and the kings of Judah. In the eighteenth year of King Josiah, this Passover was kept to the Lord in Jerusalem.

All of that, in one year. It began with a builder's site and a chest of silver. And it went through a woman's courtyard in the Second Quarter.

[pause]

* * *

Before Josiah, says the Book of Kings, there was no king like him, who turned to the Lord with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses. Nor did any like him arise after him.

With all his heart, and all his soul, and all his might. If you were listening earlier, you will have heard those words before. They are the words from the book. The words about loving the Lord your God. The writer of Kings is telling us, very quietly, that Josiah became the thing he read.

But the Book of Kings is honest, and it does not pretend that one good king could undo everything. It says, plainly, that even so, the Lord did not turn from the great anger that the reign of Manasseh had stirred up against Judah. What Huldah said would happen did happen, a generation later. The Babylonians came. The temple was burned. The people were carried away. That is another story, for another night, and it is a sad one, though it does not end sadly.

And Josiah himself was gathered to his fathers sooner than anyone would have wished. When he was thirty-nine, Pharaoh Neco of Egypt marched north through the land, and Josiah went out to meet him at Megiddo, and he was killed there. His servants carried him home to Jerusalem in a chariot, and buried him in his own tomb. And all Judah mourned for him. The Book of Chronicles says that Jeremiah the prophet wrote a lament for Josiah, and that the singing men and singing women spoke of Josiah in their laments for a very long time afterwards.

People sometimes ask how that fits with what Huldah said: that he would be gathered to his grave in peace. And the plainest answer is the one she gave herself, in the very next breath: your eyes shall not see all the evil that I will bring upon this place. He did not see the city burn. He did not see the temple he had cleaned fall, or his people led away. He went down to his fathers with the covenant still kept, the Passover still remembered, and the book still being read. That is the peace he was promised. It is not the same as a long life. But it is not nothing.

[pause]

* * *

Now it is time to go a little slower. The candles are low.

What happened to Huldah? We do not know. The Bible does not say another word about her. She goes back into her house in the Second Quarter, and the door closes, and that is that.

But she was not quite forgotten. Several hundred years later, when the temple had been rebuilt, and rebuilt again, and the great platform of the Temple Mount had been built around it, the rabbis who wrote the Mishnah described its gates. There were five gates to the Temple Mount, they said, and two of them, on the south side, were called the Huldah Gates. They do not explain the name. Many people think that the two old gateways that you can still see today, blocked up with stone, in the southern wall of the Temple Mount, are those same Huldah Gates, and that pilgrims climbing up from the city of David went in through them. So, perhaps, for hundreds of years, everyone who came up to pray at the temple from that side walked in through a door with a prophetess's name on it.

There is also an old tradition, kept for a long time in Jerusalem, that Huldah's tomb is on the Mount of Olives. Christians in the city have their own traditions about the same place. It is told as tradition, not as history, and I tell it to you the same way.

And the rabbis of the Talmud wondered, as we did, why the king's men went to Huldah, when Jeremiah was a prophet in those days. They gave more than one answer. One of them was that the men knew she was a woman, and thought that a woman might ask for mercy more tenderly. Which is a kind answer, and a very human one, and tells you more about the rabbis than about Huldah, perhaps. The Bible itself does not say why they went to her. They went because she was a prophetess. That was reason enough.

As for Ira, our invented scribe, I like to think that he grew old in the scribes' room, and became a scribe of the king himself, with a room of his own and a boy of his own to sharpen his pens. And I like to think that every seven years, when the law was read aloud to all the people, as the book said it should be, he stood somewhere near the front, and listened, and remembered a courtyard with a loom in the corner, and a woman's face, listening to every word as though it were being weighed on very fine scales.

And we do know about some real people from that morning. Ahikam son of Shaphan, who kept offering his father his arm, at least in my version, appears again in the Book of Jeremiah, years later, when Jeremiah was in danger of being put to death for prophesying. It was Ahikam son of Shaphan, it says, whose hand was with Jeremiah, so that he was not given over to the people to be killed. And later still, when Jeremiah's friend Baruch read Jeremiah's scroll aloud in the temple, he read it in the room of Gemariah, another son of Shaphan. The family that read the book to the king went on, it seems, being a family that listened when the word of the Lord was read.

* * *

There is a book in the Bible, the Book of Sirach, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, that has a long chapter near the end in praise of the famous men and women of Israel. And when it comes to Josiah, it says this. The memory of Josiah is like a blending of incense, prepared by the art of the perfumer. It is sweet as honey to every mouth, and like music at a banquet of wine.

That is not a bad thing to have said of you. Like music at a feast.

And then, a very long time afterwards, in a small town in Galilee, a young man stood up in the synagogue on the Sabbath, the way people did, and they handed him a scroll. It was the scroll of the prophet Isaiah. And he unrolled it, and found the place, and read: the Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor. And then he rolled it up again, and gave it back, and sat down. And every eye in the place was on him. And he said: today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.

That young man was descended from Josiah. The Gospel of Matthew says so, in the long list of names at the very beginning. Josiah the father of Jechoniah and his brothers, at the time of the deportation to Babylon. And then, many generations on, Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born.

So that is a family, too, that read the book aloud.

* * *

It is a good thing to think about, just before you go to sleep. That the words of the Lord can be lost, and found again. Not only in a temple storeroom. In a house. In a life. In a drawer you have not opened for a long time.

And it is a good thing to notice how they were found. Not by one person alone. A priest found the book. A scribe read it. A king listened. Five men walked across the city together, and a woman in a courtyard heard it and told them what the Lord was saying. And then a whole people, small and great, stood together and listened, and promised. The book was not meant for any one of them alone.

When the Ethiopian official in the Acts of the Apostles was reading the prophet Isaiah in his chariot on the road to Gaza, and Philip the deacon came running alongside and asked him whether he understood what he was reading, he said: how can I, unless someone guides me? And he invited Philip to come up and sit with him. That is how it usually works. We read the Scriptures together. Somebody reads, and somebody listens, and somebody explains, and somebody asks the question that everyone else was too shy to ask.

Saint Jerome, who spent most of his long life with the Scriptures, said that to be ignorant of the Scriptures is to be ignorant of Christ. The Church has quoted him on that ever since. But there is a gentle side to it, too, which is that you do not have to know them all by yourself, tonight. You only have to listen. Someone has been reading them aloud, in one room or another, for a very long time, and they are still reading.

And if your heart is heavy tonight with something you heard, or something you found out, or something you wish you had done differently, remember what the Lord said to the young king through Huldah. Because your heart was tender. Because you humbled yourself. I also have heard you.

The book is found. The lamps are lit. The people are listening.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Kings, chapters twenty-two and twenty-three, and the Second Book of Chronicles, chapters thirty-four and thirty-five, which tell it side by side. Both are told straight, in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. Manasseh and Amon are in Second Kings, chapter twenty-one. Josiah seeking the Lord at sixteen, the clearing out at twenty, the musician Levites over the builders, the finding of the book while the silver was brought out, the Passover in detail and Jeremiah's lament are from Chronicles. The two books spell the names of Huldah's husband's family a little differently, and give Josiah's reforms in a different order; we have followed Kings for the order. What the book was is not said in the text; that it was Deuteronomy, or a form of it, is a common view, ancient and modern, not a settled fact. The meaning of Huldah's name, whose wardrobe Shallum kept, and whether he was Jeremiah's uncle, are not known. That the Second Quarter was on the western hill is what many people think. The two Huldah Gates are in the Mishnah, tractate Middot; which of today's blocked gates they were is what many people think. The tomb on the Mount of Olives is a tradition. The rabbis' answer about mercy is in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Megillah, folio fourteen. Ahikam and Jeremiah are in Jeremiah, chapter twenty-six; Gemariah's room is in chapter thirty-six. The praise of Josiah is in Sirach, chapter forty-nine. Saint Jerome's saying is quoted by the Second Vatican Council in Dei Verbum.

Ira, the scribes' room, the walk across the city, the dog, the loom, the talk on the way, and Huldah's words, read it to me, are our own invention. Nothing has been added to the word of the Lord that Huldah gave, or to what the king, the priest or the scribe say or do in the book.

There is a beach, very early in the morning, and it is crowded.

It is crowded with people who are wet to the knees and sandy to the elbows. There are donkeys with their ears flat back. There are goats who have decided, as goats do, that whatever just happened was nothing to do with them. There are children asleep on their fathers' shoulders, and children wide awake and asking questions that nobody is answering. And behind them all there is the sea, grey and flat and very, very quiet, as if it had never done anything unusual in its life.

Nobody is saying much. They are all just standing there.

And then, near the front of the crowd, a very old woman reaches out a hand. A girl beside her, about fifteen, is holding a round drum made of a hoop of bent wood with a skin stretched over it. The old woman takes it from her. She weighs it in her hand, as if she is remembering how. She lifts it up.

And she hits it.

Let me tell you where we are.

We are in Egypt, in the north of it, in the green, flat country where the river Nile stops being one river and spreads out into a great fan of channels on its way to the sea. The Bible calls part of it the land of Goshen. It is good land. It floods every year, gently, and when the water goes down it leaves behind black mud so rich that you could more or less drop a seed on it and step back. There are palm trees, and fields of barley and flax, and everywhere along the water there are tall green reeds and papyrus, whispering in the wind, taller than a man.

Living in this country, and they have been living there a long time, are the people of Israel. They came down to Egypt as one big family, in the days of Joseph, when there was a famine, and they were welcome. Joseph was the most important man in the country after Pharaoh. His brothers were given good land. Their children had children. And the book of Exodus says that they were fruitful, and increased greatly, and the land was filled with them.

And then, the book says, there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.

That is one of the saddest sentences in the Bible, and one of the shortest. It means that the people who remembered were gone. The kindness was forgotten. And the new king looked at all these foreigners in his country, and he did what frightened rulers have done ever since. He decided they were a danger. He put taskmasters over them. He made them build store cities for him, called Pithom and Raamses. And the book says that the Egyptians made their lives bitter with hard service, in mortar and in brick, and in all kinds of work in the field.

If you have ever made a brick, you will know it is a muddy, heavy, endless sort of job. You dig the clay. You tread it with your feet, with straw and water. You press it into a wooden mould, and turn it out on the ground in rows to dry in the sun, and then you do it again. And again. A good worker might make many hundreds in a day. The Egyptians painted pictures of exactly this on the walls of at least one great tomb, men stooping over the moulds, and a man with a stick standing over them, which tells you something about the job.

And then the king did something worse. And I will say it plainly and quickly, because it is a hard thing, and then we will move on, because that is what the book does too. He gave an order to all his people that every baby boy born to the Hebrews was to be thrown into the river Nile. The girls could live. The boys could not.

The book tells us, before it tells us anything else, that there were brave women who would not do it. Two midwives, called Shiphrah and Puah, who feared God more than they feared the king, and let the babies live, and that is a story for another night. But the order stood.

And it is into this country, and this year, and this order, that tonight's story begins. In one small mud-brick house, belonging to a family from the tribe of Levi. A father called Amram, and a mother called Jochebed, and two children. A little boy of three, called Aaron. And an older girl.

The book, in this first part of the story, does not tell us her name. It just calls her his sister. But later, in the Book of Numbers, we are told that Amram and Jochebed had three children, Aaron, Moses, and Miriam their sister. Only one sister. So nearly everybody, Jewish and Christian alike, has always taken the girl by the river to be Miriam. And so shall we.

We are not told how old she was. Old enough to be sent out on her own, and to stand very still for a very long time, and to think very quickly. So let us say she was a girl of about ten. And she was about to have a baby brother.

* * *

The baby was born. And his mother, the book says, saw that he was a fine child.

The Hebrew word there is a very simple one. It is the word for good. It is the same word that is used right at the beginning of the Bible, when God looks at the light, and the land, and the sea, and all the living things, and sees that they are good. Some old readers liked that very much. A mother looks at her new baby and sees what God saw on the first days of the world. I like it too. I think most mothers would recognise it.

So she hid him.

The book says she hid him for three months, and I would like you to think for a moment about what that means. Three months. With a baby. In a small house with thin walls, in a street full of neighbours, in a country where the king's men might come by. Anybody who has had a baby in the house will know that babies are many wonderful things, but quiet is not usually one of them. They cry when they are hungry. They cry when they are tired. They cry, as far as anyone can tell, when they have simply remembered that crying is an option.

I imagine the whole family became very good at certain things in those three months. At rocking. At humming. At having a coughing fit, very loudly, at exactly the moment the baby started up. And I imagine a girl of ten, sitting by the door, keeping watch on the street, and learning to tell, from the sound of footsteps, who was a neighbour and who was not.

But a baby grows. He gets louder. He gets harder to explain. And the book says, simply, when she could hide him no longer.

What did she do?

The book tells us, and it tells us carefully, step by step, like a recipe. She took a basket made of papyrus. She daubed it with bitumen and with pitch, which are two kinds of the black, sticky stuff that comes out of the ground and that people used for waterproofing, the way you might tar the bottom of a boat. She put the child in it. And she put it among the reeds, by the bank of the river.

Now here is a small thing that I love. The Hebrew word for this basket is a rare word. In the whole of the Bible it is used for only two things. One of them is this little papyrus basket. And the other one is the ark of Noah.

The same word. Once for a great wooden ship, big enough for a family and two of every kind of creature, and once for a basket you could carry under one arm. Both of them daubed with pitch. Both of them floating on dangerous water. And both of them with somebody inside whom God was going to keep safe, so that through him he could save a great many others. The book does not explain the echo. It just uses the word, and lets you notice.

Let us imagine the night before. The lamp lit. The mother sitting on the floor with an armful of papyrus stems, splitting them and weaving them, over and under, round and round, very tight, the way you weave a basket that must not leak. The girl beside her, holding the pot of pitch near the fire to keep it soft. Aaron asleep in the corner. The baby asleep too, for once, as if he knew.

And nobody says very much. What is there to say? The mother is doing the only thing she can think of, which is to obey the king's order to the letter. Put the boy in the Nile. Well, she is putting him in the Nile. In a boat.

That last part is only a guess, by the way. The book does not tell us what Jochebed was thinking. But many readers have noticed it.

* * *

In the morning, the mother carried the basket down to the river and set it among the reeds at the water's edge, where the current was slow and the papyrus grew thick. And then she did the hardest thing she ever did. She walked away.

And the girl stayed.

The book says, his sister stood at a distance, to know what would be done to him.

Stood at a distance. That is all she did. She did not hold the basket. She did not have a plan, as far as we know. She just took her stand, a little way off, among the reeds, where she could see the basket and nobody could see her, and she watched. To know what would be done to him. To see what would happen.

There is an old Jewish tradition about this moment, written down by the rabbis many centuries later, and I will tell it to you, as long as you remember that it is a tradition, not Scripture. They said that Miriam was a prophetess even as a child, and that before her brother was born she had told her parents, my mother is going to have a son, and he is going to save Israel. And when the baby was put in the river, they said, her father turned to her and said, well, daughter, where is your prophecy now? And so, the rabbis said, she stood at a distance to know what would become of it.

That is not in the book. But I can see why they liked it. A girl among the reeds, with her heart in her mouth, waiting to see whether God meant what she thought he meant.

So. The sun comes up higher. Flies buzz. A heron stalks past on long legs, looks at the basket, decides it is not a fish, and stalks on. And the girl stands there, very still, with mud between her toes.

And then there are voices. Women's voices. Laughing. Coming down the bank.

It is the daughter of Pharaoh. She has come down to the river to bathe, with her young women walking along the bank beside her. We do not know which Pharaoh, and the book does not give her a name. Later traditions did, more than one, and they do not agree. The Jewish historian Josephus called her Thermuthis. The rabbis thought she was the Bithiah, daughter of Pharaoh, who is mentioned once, in passing, in the First Book of Chronicles. Neither is certain. For tonight, she is simply the princess.

And the princess sees the basket among the reeds.

And she sends her servant to fetch it. And the servant wades in, and pulls it out, and brings it to her, dripping. And the princess opens the lid.

And the book says, she saw the child, and behold, the baby was crying.

I am very glad the book tells us that. Not smiling. Not cooing up at her like a baby in a painting. Crying. Red in the face, furious, hungry and wet and very much alive, the way real babies are. And it says, she took pity on him.

And she said, this is one of the Hebrews' children.

She knew. Of course she knew. She knew what her father had ordered, and she knew exactly why a baby boy would be floating in a basket in the reeds. And there was a moment there, the book does not linger on it but it is there, where she could have done what she was supposed to do. And she did not. She held him.

And this is the moment. This is the moment when the girl among the reeds, who has been standing so still for so long, steps out.

I always imagine her walking up to the princess very politely, with her hands behind her back, as if she just happened to be passing. As if girls often just happened to be passing through the papyrus with mud up to their knees. And the book gives us her words exactly.

Shall I go and call you a nurse from the Hebrew women, to nurse the child for you?

What a question. It is the cleverest question in the whole chapter. She does not say, please don't hurt him. She does not say, that's my brother. She offers the princess a practical solution to a practical problem, which is that she is now holding a hungry baby and has no idea what to do with it. And she offers it in such a way that the princess only has to say one word.

And the princess says it. Go.

And the girl goes. The book calls her a young girl here. And she runs. And I think she ran faster than she had ever run in her life. Up the bank, through the fields, down the street, in at the door. And the book says, she went and called the child's mother.

Then the princess said to the mother, take this child away, and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages.

And I have to say, if you are looking for a happy ending to a very frightening morning, I do not think you can do much better than that. A mother who had walked away from her son that morning, not knowing if she would ever see him again, walked home with him in her arms that afternoon. Under the protection of the king's own daughter. And she was paid for it.

The child grew. And when he was older, the book says, the mother brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. And she named him Moses, because, she said, I drew him out of the water.

In Hebrew, the name sounds like the word for drawing out. It also happens that in the Egyptian language, a word very like it simply means son, or born of, and you can hear it at the end of the names of several Pharaohs. Which of those was in her mind, or both, nobody can now say. Either way, it is a good name for a boy who came out of the river.

And then the story moves on, and leaves the girl behind. The book does not tell us what Miriam did for the next eighty years.

We know a little of what happened to Moses. He grew up in the palace. Saint Stephen, in the Acts of the Apostles, says he was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians. When he was grown, he went out to see his own people at their work, and saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, and struck him down, and fled for his life to the land of Midian, which is a story we have told already. He married there, and kept sheep, for forty years. And then, at a bush that burned and was not burnt up, God sent him back.

And all that time, Miriam was in Egypt. Making bricks, perhaps, or baking bread, or raising children, or doing whatever the women of a family of Levi did in those long hard years. She saw her parents grow old. She saw her little brother Aaron grow up and marry. She grew old herself. And we know from what happens next that the people of Israel never forgot her. She was not just somebody's sister. When we meet her again, the book calls her Miriam the prophetess.

And then, one day, Aaron went out into the wilderness, to the mountain of God, and met an old man with a shepherd's staff in his hand, and kissed him. And when the two of them came back into Egypt together, Miriam would have looked up from whatever she was doing and seen her baby brother. Eighty years old now. With a long white beard. And the same face, I expect, that had screamed at a princess out of a wet basket.

The book tells us that Moses was eighty, and Aaron eighty-three, when they stood before Pharaoh. So if Miriam was the girl among the reeds, and older than Aaron, as most readers have always thought, she must have been about ninety. The book does not say. But she was very old, and she still had a great deal to do.

Then came the long hard months, which are their own stories for other nights. Moses and Aaron before Pharaoh. The staff that became a serpent. The river turned to blood, and the frogs, and the gnats and the flies, and the hail and the locusts and the darkness, one after another, and each time Pharaoh's heart hardened, and each time he said no.

Until the last night. The night of the Passover. When the people of Israel ate a lamb, standing up, dressed for a journey, with their sandals on their feet and their staffs in their hands, and in the middle of the night Pharaoh sent for Moses and Aaron and said, go. Get out. Take your flocks and your herds and go.

And they went. All at once, in a great hurry. The book says they took their bread dough before it had risen, still in the kneading bowls, wrapped up in their cloaks on their shoulders. Hundreds of thousands of people, and a mixed crowd of others who went with them, and flocks and herds, and the bones of Joseph, which they had promised long ago to carry home.

Now, at this point, I need to introduce someone. She is not in the book, because we made her up. If you heard our episode about the waters of Meribah, you have already met her as an old woman. Tonight she is young.

Her name is Ziva. That is a modern Hebrew name, and therefore entirely wrong for the period, but I like it, and she is not here to object. She is about fifteen. She lives a few streets away from Miriam's family, and she has known Miriam all her life, the way you know the old lady at the end of the road who always has something to say and is usually right about it.

On the night they left Egypt, there was no time to pack. Ziva's mother was shouting. Her little brother was crying. Her father was trying to get the goats out of the yard, and the goats, being goats, had other ideas. And in all that noise, Ziva stood in the middle of the room and looked around for what to bring.

And what she grabbed was her tambourine.

Her mother had made it for her. A hoop of bent wood, with a goatskin stretched over it and laced tight. It was not useful. You could not eat it. You could not wear it or carry water in it. Her mother looked at it and said, Ziva, really? And Ziva said, I don't know, I just want it, and stuffed it in the bundle with the bread dough.

She did not know why. Later, she would be very glad.

Here is the funny thing. There is an old Jewish tradition, which the great medieval commentator Rashi wrote down from older sources, that the women of Israel did exactly this. That they packed their tambourines on their way out of Egypt, on purpose. Because, Rashi says, they trusted that God was going to do wonderful things for them, and they wanted to be ready to sing. Again, that is a tradition, not Scripture. But I think it is a lovely one. Imagine leaving home in the middle of the night, with nothing, and packing the drum.

And so they walked. God did not lead them by the short road, along the coast to the land of the Philistines, the book says, in case they met a war and changed their minds and went back. He led them round, by the way of the wilderness, toward the sea. And he went in front of them in a pillar of cloud by day, to show them the way, and a pillar of fire by night, to give them light, so they could travel by day and by night.

Then God told Moses to turn back, and camp by the sea, at a place called Pi-hahiroth, between a place called Migdol and the water.

Now, I am going to be honest with you. Nobody knows for certain where that was. The Hebrew name of the sea in this story is the sea of reeds, or the sea of rushes. It is the same word for reeds as in the story of the basket. The old Greek translation of the Bible called it the Red Sea, and so do most English Bibles. Whether it was the Red Sea we know, or one of its long northern arms, or one of the great marshy lakes that used to lie between Egypt and the desert, scholars have argued about for a very long time, and are still arguing. We will leave them to it. For tonight it is simply the sea.

And they camped by it. With the water in front of them, and the desert on either side.

And then, behind them, someone saw dust.

Pharaoh had changed his mind. Of course he had. The book says that when he was told the people had gone, he and his servants said, what is this we have done, that we have let Israel go from serving us? And he got his chariot ready, and took six hundred of the best chariots of Egypt, and all the other chariots, and went after them.

And the people of Israel looked up, and saw the Egyptians coming. And they were terrified.

And then they said something to Moses that I think is one of the most human things anybody says in the whole Bible. They said, were there no graves in Egypt, that you have brought us out here to die in the wilderness? Didn't we tell you, back in Egypt, to leave us alone and let us serve the Egyptians?

Were there no graves in Egypt. Egypt, the country of the pyramids. The country that was more or less famous for graves. I do not think they meant it as a joke. But fear makes people say very sharp things, and very silly ones, often at the same time.

And Moses answered them. And these are his words, as the book gives them.

Do not be afraid. Stand firm, and see the salvation of the Lord, which he will work for you today. The Egyptians whom you see today, you shall never see again. The Lord will fight for you. And you have only to be still.

Stand firm and see. You have only to be still.

And here is another small thing hidden in the Hebrew, which I promise is the last bit of Hebrew for a while. The word Moses uses there, stand firm, take your stand, is the very same word that the book used eighty years before, for the girl among the reeds. His sister took her stand at a distance, to know what would be done to him. Stand firm, and see the salvation of the Lord. The same word. The book does not point it out. But I think there was at least one person in that crowd who knew exactly what it felt like.

Let us go and find her.

It is evening. The pillar of cloud has moved. The book says the angel of God, who had been going in front of the camp, moved round and went behind them, and the pillar of cloud moved from in front of them and stood behind them, between the camp of Egypt and the camp of Israel. And it was cloud and darkness to one side, and it lit up the night on the other, so that the two camps did not come near each other all night long.

Ziva is sitting on the sand, on her bundle, with her arms round her knees. She has been crying, and is trying not to let anybody see. Her little brother is asleep against her. Down the beach, she can see the great dark wall of the cloud, and beyond it, very faintly, the noise of horses.

And somebody sits down beside her. Slowly, the way very old people sit down, with a hand on the ground first.

It is Miriam.

Can't sleep, says Miriam.

No, says Ziva.

Neither can I, says Miriam. Too old. I've done enough sleeping. And they sit for a while, looking at the water.

Then Ziva says, are you frightened?

And Miriam thinks about it, honestly, the way old people do when a young person asks them a real question.

Yes, she says. Of course. Who wouldn't be. And then she says, but I have done this before, you know.

Done what? says Ziva.

Stood by the water, says Miriam, waiting to see what the Lord would do.

And she tells her. About the basket, and the pitch, and the papyrus. About the reeds, and the heron, and the mud between her toes. About the princess, and the crying, and the run home. Ziva has heard the story before, everyone has, but never from Miriam herself, and never like this, in the dark, with the sea in front of them.

I stood there all morning, says Miriam. I didn't know what was going to happen. I didn't have a plan. I just knew I wasn't supposed to go away. And then the moment came, and I knew what to say.

And what if it hadn't come? says Ziva.

Miriam is quiet for a little while.

Well, she says, then I would still have been the one standing there. That's the part we get to do. The rest is his.

And she pats Ziva on the knee, and gets up, slowly, and goes off to find Aaron and tell him that he doesn't know which way east is. And Ziva lies down next to her brother, and to her own surprise, she sleeps.

And in the night, the book says, Moses stretched out his hand over the sea.

And the Lord drove the sea back with a strong east wind, all night long, and made the sea into dry land, and the waters were divided.

All night. I want you to notice that. The book does not say it happened in a flash. It says a wind blew all night. A great hot wind out of the east, out of the desert, roaring over the camp, flapping every tent, blowing sand in everybody's eyes. And in the dark, under that wind, the water was going back.

And then it was time.

The people of Israel went into the middle of the sea on dry ground, the book says, and the waters were a wall to them, on their right hand and on their left.

I have tried for years to imagine what that was like, and I am not sure I can. Walking in the dark, in the middle of the night, by the light of the pillar of fire behind you. The sand wet and firm under your feet, rippled where the water had been, with shells in it, and stranded weed, and perhaps the odd surprised fish. And on either side, close enough to touch if you were brave, the sea. Standing up. Dark and shining and breathing in the wind.

Ziva walked with her little brother on her back, and her tambourine bumping against her in the bundle, and her mother's hand gripping her elbow. Nobody talked. There was just the wind, and the shuffle of thousands of feet, and the goats complaining, and somewhere, far ahead, an old woman's voice saying, keep going, keep going, don't look at it, look at your feet.

It was a long way. It took the rest of the night.

And behind them, the Egyptians came after them, into the middle of the sea, horses and chariots and horsemen.

And in the morning watch, the book says, which is the last part of the night, just before dawn, the Lord looked down on the Egyptian army from the pillar of fire and of cloud, and threw them into confusion. Their chariot wheels stuck, and clogged, so that they drove heavily. And the Egyptians said to each other, let us flee from Israel, for the Lord is fighting for them.

And Moses stretched out his hand over the sea again. And as morning came, the sea went back to its place.

And the army that had come after them into the sea did not come out again.

That is all I am going to say about that, and it is nearly all the book says. It is a hard thing, and the book does not make a song and dance about it. Not yet, anyway. It says, simply, thus the Lord saved Israel that day from the hand of the Egyptians.

And then it says something I like very much. It says, and the people feared the Lord, and they believed in the Lord, and in his servant Moses.

They believed. After all those months. After were there no graves in Egypt. They stood on that beach in the grey morning, wet and shaking, with the sea flat and quiet behind them, and they believed.

Then, the book says, Moses and the people of Israel sang this song to the Lord.

And there follows one of the oldest and greatest songs in the whole Bible. Eighteen verses of it. Many scholars think its words are among the very oldest in the book. I will not give you all of it tonight, but I will give you some, because it is too good to leave out.

I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea.

The Lord is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation. This is my God, and I will praise him. My father's God, and I will exalt him.

Who is like you, O Lord, among the gods? Who is like you, majestic in holiness, awesome in glorious deeds, doing wonders?

You have led in your steadfast love the people whom you have redeemed. You have guided them by your strength to your holy dwelling.

The Lord will reign for ever and ever.

Can you hear it? It is a big song. A men's song, probably, at least to start with. Thousands of voices on a beach, rough and loud and not very well in tune, the way crowds sing.

And then the book tells us one more thing. Two verses. Almost as if it were an afterthought. But I think it is the best part.

Then Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a tambourine in her hand.

And all the women went out after her, with tambourines and with dancing.

And Miriam sang to them, Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea.

Let us go back to the beach. To where our story started.

The men are singing. And near the front of the crowd, an old woman, ninety years old perhaps, is standing very still and listening. And beside her is a girl of fifteen, who has just found something in her bundle, squashed in next to the dough. A hoop of bent wood with a goatskin stretched over it.

And Miriam looks at it. And she holds out her hand.

And Ziva gives it to her.

And Miriam the prophetess, the girl who once stood among the reeds, takes the tambourine in her hand, and weighs it, as if she is remembering how. And lifts it up. And hits it. Once. Twice. And then she starts to dance.

Not a careful little old lady's dance, either. I imagine it was the sort of dance you do when you have waited eighty years for something. With her hair coming loose, and her feet in the wet sand, laughing, singing at the top of her voice. Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously!

And Ziva follows her. She cannot help it. Nobody can. And then Ziva's mother. And the women from their street. And other women, who have their own drums, it turns out, because they had packed them too. And the women who have no drums at all clap their hands. And soon there is a long, swaying, stamping line of women all down the beach, with an old woman at the front of it, and the sound of the drums rolling out across the water.

The book says Miriam answered them. It is the word for answering, for one voice and then another. It may mean that the men sang a verse, and the women sang back. Or that Miriam sang a line, and the women sang it after her. Either way, she was the one leading. And the line she sang was the first line of the big song. The one everybody could remember.

Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea.

Over and over. Until they were hoarse. Until the sun was well up and the children were all awake and the goats had given up complaining and were just staring. Until everyone was laughing and crying at once, the way people do on the morning after the worst night of their lives, when they find that they are, against all odds, still alive.

This, by the way, was not the last time the women of Israel would do this. It became a custom. Long afterwards, when Jephthah came home from a battle, his daughter came out to meet him with tambourines and dancing. When David came home from killing the giant, the women came out of all the towns of Israel, singing and dancing, with tambourines. Psalm sixty-eight remembers the young women playing tambourines in the procession. And the very last psalm of all, the hundred and fiftieth, says praise him with tambourine and dance.

But Miriam was the first. And it was Ziva's drum.

That night, they camped on the far shore. And it was very quiet.

Ziva sat on the sand with her tambourine in her lap. The skin had a small tear in it now, from all that hitting, and her mother said she would fix it. Her little brother was asleep. The cloud was in front of them again, glowing faintly, ready for the morning.

And Miriam came and sat down beside her, slowly, with a hand on the ground first.

Your drum, says Miriam. Sorry. I didn't ask.

That's all right, says Ziva.

I'll tell you something, says Miriam. That's the first time I've done that since I was about your age. And she laughs. My knees are going to tell me all about it in the morning.

And they sit for a while, looking at the water. It is quiet and flat and silver under the stars, the way water is when it has nothing at all on its mind.

Then Ziva says, Miriam, what did you mean, last night? About standing there.

And Miriam thinks about it.

I mean, she says, that most of the time, the Lord doesn't need us to do very much. He drew the water back. He clogged the wheels. I didn't do that. Moses didn't really do that, though don't tell him I said so. Mostly we just have to stand there. Not run away. Keep watching. And then, every now and then, there is a moment when he wants us to step out of the reeds and say something. Or pick up a drum.

And how do you know which is which? says Ziva.

And Miriam smiles in the dark.

You don't, she says. Not always. You just stay close enough to see.

And Ziva holds her tambourine in her lap, with her thumb on the torn skin, and decides that she will keep it for ever.

And she did. But that, as you may know, is another story.

So what happened to them all?

The people of Israel set out the very next morning, into the wilderness of Shur, and walked for three days and found no water, and when they did find water it was bitter. And that, too, is a story for another night.

Miriam went with them. All the way. For forty years, she walked with the people through the wilderness, with her two brothers, one the prophet and one the priest. The book does not tell us very much about those years. There is one hard story about her, at a place called Hazeroth, when she and Aaron spoke against Moses, and she was shut out of the camp for seven days. We will tell that one properly another night, in daylight. But I will tell you how it ends. It ends with the whole people of Israel, all those hundreds of thousands, refusing to move on. The book says the people did not set out on the march until Miriam was brought back in. They would not go without her.

And at the very end of the forty years, at a place called Kadesh, in the wilderness of Zin, Miriam died, and was buried there. The book gives it one sentence. There was a later Jewish tradition that, all through the wilderness years, a well of water followed the people for Miriam's sake, and that when she died, it dried up. That is a tradition, not Scripture. But it is true that in the very next verse of the book, there was no water for the congregation. We told that story too.

And she was not forgotten. Hundreds of years later, the prophet Micah has God remind his people of everything he had done for them. I brought you up from the land of Egypt, he says. I redeemed you from the house of slavery. And I sent before you Moses, and Aaron, and Miriam.

All three of them. Not just the famous one.

And there is one more thing, which I have kept until the end. When the Bible was translated into Greek, in the centuries before Christ, the name Miriam was written Mariam. And it became one of the commonest names for Jewish girls in the time of Jesus, as far as old books and old tombs can tell us. Which is why there are so many Marys in the Gospels. Mary Magdalene. Mary the wife of Clopas. Mary of Bethany. And one young woman in Nazareth, who also sang a song one day, about the Lord who had done great things, and cast down the mighty from their thrones.

They all carried Miriam's name.

And the song on the beach is still being sung. Saint Paul says that our fathers all passed through the sea, and were all baptised into Moses in the cloud and in the sea, and ever since, Christians have seen in the crossing a picture of baptism, of going down into the water and coming up free. On the night before Easter, at the great Vigil, the Catholic Church reads the story of the crossing of the sea, and after it, every year, the people sing the song of Moses. In the Orthodox Church, the first of the nine biblical songs on which the hymns of the morning service are built is this one, in the words of the old Greek translation. Let us sing to the Lord, for gloriously has he been glorified. Horse and rider he has hurled into the sea. And the Book of Revelation, at the very end of the Bible, has the saints standing beside a sea of glass, holding harps of God, singing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb.

So somewhere, every single day, somebody is still singing Miriam's song.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Exodus, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The king who did not know Joseph, the bricks and the cruel order are in chapter one. The baby, the basket of papyrus with bitumen and pitch, the sister standing at a distance, Pharaoh's daughter, the crying child, the offer of a nurse, the wages and the name are in chapter two, verses one to ten. The camp by the sea, the chariots, were there no graves in Egypt, stand firm and see, the cloud between the camps, the east wind all night, the walls of water, the wheels and the morning, are in chapter fourteen. The song of Moses, and the two verses about Miriam and the women, are in chapter fifteen. The words of Moses, of Pharaoh's daughter, of the sister and of the people are kept as the book gives them, in a plain rendering, and the song is given only in part.

The book does not name the sister in chapter two; that she was Miriam comes from Numbers, chapter twenty-six. It does not give her age. Where the sea was is an open question. Miriam at Hazeroth is in Numbers, chapter twelve, and her death in chapter twenty. Micah's words are in Micah, chapter six. Saint Paul's are in First Corinthians, chapter ten.

Miriam's prophecy as a girl, and the name Bithiah, come from the rabbis in the Talmud. The tambourines packed on purpose come from Rashi. The name Thermuthis comes from Josephus. They are told as traditions.

And Ziva, her mother, her little brother, her tambourine, the weaving of the basket, and every word between Ziva and Miriam, are our own invention.

The tent is hot, and crowded, and it smells of goats.

In the middle of it, on a low table, someone has put down a bag. It is an old bag. It is a very old bag. It has been mended so many times that it is now more mending than bag. And out of it, very carefully, one of the elders of Israel lifts a loaf of bread.

Or what used to be a loaf of bread.

It is grey. It is as hard as a roof tile. And on one side of it there is a small, furry patch of green.

The elder turns it over. He sniffs it. He taps it on the table, and it goes tock, like a piece of wood. Then he looks up at the four tired, dusty strangers standing in front of him, in their cracked sandals and their threadbare cloaks, and he says, "You baked this at home?"

And the youngest of the strangers, who is about sixteen, and whose sandals are tied on with string, looks the elder straight in the eye and says, "Yes, sir. Hot from the oven. On the day we left."

Which is true. Every word of it.

It is just not the whole truth.

If you have been listening on recent nights, you will know where we are in the story of Israel.

Moses has died on the top of Mount Nebo, looking out over the land he would not enter. Joshua, his helper since he was a young man, is now the leader of the people. They have crossed the river Jordan, which stopped flowing to let them over, and they have made their camp on the western side, at a place called Gilgal, not far from the city of Jericho. And Jericho itself has fallen, with its walls, and its trumpets, and a red cord hanging in one window, which is a story we told a long time ago, in episode forty-seven.

Now, Gilgal is in the bottom of the Jordan valley. And the Jordan valley is low. Not low the way your garden is low after it has rained. Low the way almost nowhere else on earth is low. It is hundreds of feet below the level of the sea. It is warm there even in winter, and in summer it is like living inside a loaf of bread while it is still in the oven, which is a comparison I will come back to.

And if you stand at Gilgal and look west, you see a wall. Not a city wall. A wall of mountains. The hill country of Canaan rises up out of the valley, steep and brown and bare, ridge after ridge, and at the top, something like three thousand feet above you, there is a high, cool, green plateau, with olive trees and vineyards and little towns of stone.

One of those towns is called Gibeon.

[pause]

Gibeon is not a big city, as cities go. But it is a good one. It sits on a rounded hill, and under the hill there are springs, which in that country is the same as saying it is rich. The people of Gibeon have water when other people do not. They grow grapes, and make wine, and keep it cool in cellars cut into the rock. They have three or four smaller towns that belong with them, scattered across the hills nearby, with names that I will tell you later, because I want you to remember one of them.

The people of Gibeon are not Israelites. The Book of Joshua calls them Hivites. They are one of the peoples who already live in the land when Israel arrives. They have their own elders, and their own ways, and, as far as we can tell, no king. And they have been listening to the news.

Because the news, that year, was extraordinary.

Forty years before, a whole nation of slaves had walked out of Egypt. The sea had opened in front of them. They had lived in the desert ever since, fed every morning with bread from heaven. On their way north, two great kings of the Amorites, on the far side of the Jordan, Sihon of Heshbon and Og of Bashan, had come out to fight them, and both had been beaten. Then the river had stopped, in flood, for them to cross. Then Jericho had fallen. And then, just recently, a town called Ai, which is not very far from Gibeon at all.

Now, the Bible tells us what the other kings of the hill country decided to do about all this. They decided to fight. The book says that when the kings on the hills, and in the lowlands, and along the coast of the great sea, heard this news, they gathered together with one accord to make war on Joshua and on Israel.

And Gibeon decided something different.

* * *

I want to tell you about a council.

The Bible does not describe it. It only says that the people of Gibeon acted with cunning. But somebody had to think of the plan, and somebody had to agree to it, and somebody had to bake the bread. So this part is ours.

Imagine, then, the elders of Gibeon, sitting in the gate of their town, in the evening, with the shadows long and the swallows going round. They are worried men. One of them says, we could join the other kings. Another says, Jericho had walls, and look what happened to Jericho. Another says, we could run. And another says, run where?

And then the oldest of them says something like this. "Everything we have heard says the same thing. Their God gave them this land. He is the one who opened the sea and stopped the river. You do not fight that. You make peace with it, if it will have you."

"They will not make peace with us," says someone else. "We are in the land. They make peace with people far away. With people who are not in their way."

There is a silence.

"Then," says the oldest elder, slowly, "we shall have to be far away."

* * *

The baker of Gibeon, in our story, was a woman called Ama.

She was old, and short, and strong in the arms the way bakers are, and she had been making bread in the same clay oven for so long that she said she could tell how hot it was by talking to it. And one morning, two of the elders came to her door, looking embarrassed, and asked her to do something she had never been asked to do in her life.

"We need some bread," said the first elder.

"Everyone needs bread," said Ama. "That is why I get up so early."

"We need," said the second elder, "bad bread."

Ama put her hands on her hips.

"Old bread," the first elder explained. "Bread that looks as if it has been carried for weeks. Dry. Hard. A bit mouldy, if possible."

"In this house," said Ama, "the bread is never mouldy. It does not last long enough."

But they explained, and she listened, and her face changed. Because she had grandchildren too. And she had heard about Jericho.

So she did it. She baked the loaves, good loaves, and then she did all the things a baker spends her whole life trying to avoid. She left them out in the sun on the roof until they were hard. She wrapped them damp, in a corner where the air did not move, and she waited, and every morning she went and looked at them, and frowned, and said, "Not yet." And on about the fifth or sixth day she came down the stairs holding one up between her finger and thumb, with a little green patch on the side of it, and said, in a voice of great sorrow, "This is the worst bread I have ever made."

It was perfect.

Meanwhile, the rest of the town was busy too. Because the Book of Joshua tells us exactly what the envoys took with them, and it is one of my favourite lists in the whole Bible. Old sacks on their donkeys. Old wineskins, cracked and split and tied up where they had burst. Old sandals, patched, on their feet. Old clothes on their backs. And provisions of bread that was all dry, and crumbly, and mouldy.

I like to imagine the whole town turning out its cupboards. Somebody's grandfather's sandals, which had been mended so many times that nobody could remember the original. The wineskin that leaked, that nobody had thrown away. The cloak with the hole in the elbow that the dog had been sleeping on. It must have been, for a few days, the only town in history having a competition to see who owned the shabbiest things.

And the envoys were chosen. Grown men, mostly, sensible men, good talkers. And one of them, in our story, was Ama's grandson Pazi, who was sixteen, and had been chosen because, as the elders said, he had an honest face.

"That," said Ama to him, the night before they left, "is exactly the problem."

* * *

The road from Gibeon down to Gilgal is not long, as the crow flies. A day's walk, more or less. But it is all downhill, and it is very steep, and it goes from the cool green top of the hills to the hot brown bottom of the world, and by the time the envoys came out onto the floor of the valley, they did not have to pretend to be tired and dusty. They were tired and dusty.

Pazi's sandals had been his great-uncle's. One of them had come apart on the way down, and he had tied it back on with string.

"Good," said the leader of the envoys, looking at it. "Very good. Very far country, that."

And there in front of them was the camp of Israel.

I do not think the people of Gibeon had ever seen anything like it. Tents, and more tents, and more tents again, spread out across the valley as far as you could see. Smoke from cooking fires. Children running about. Goats. Donkeys. Long lines of people going down to the river and coming back with water jars. And in the middle, the Tabernacle, the tent of the Lord, with the ark of the covenant inside it.

The envoys were brought, with their donkeys and their sacks, to Joshua. And to the men of Israel. And they said the first line of their speech, the line they had practised all the way down the mountain.

"We have come from a far country. Now make a covenant with us."

And the men of Israel looked at them. And here is where I think the story gets interesting, because the men of Israel were not fools. They said, straight away, the obvious thing. They said, "Perhaps you live among us. Then how can we make a covenant with you?"

You can imagine the envoys' hearts going thump.

But they had an answer ready, and it was a very clever one, because it was not really an answer at all. They said to Joshua, "We are your servants."

And Joshua, who was also not a fool, said, "Who are you? And where do you come from?"

* * *

And then the leader of the envoys made his speech. I am going to give it to you nearly as the book has it, because it is a small masterpiece.

"Your servants have come from a very far country," he said, "because of the name of the Lord your God. For we have heard of his fame, and of all that he did in Egypt, and of all that he did to the two kings of the Amorites who were beyond the Jordan, Sihon king of Heshbon, and Og king of Bashan, who lived at Ashtaroth.

"And our elders, and all the people of our country, said to us, take provisions in your hand for the journey, and go to meet them, and say to them, we are your servants. So now, make a covenant with us.

"This bread of ours, we took it hot from our houses for our provisions on the day we set out to come to you. And look at it now. It is dry, and it is mouldy. And these wineskins, which we filled, were new. And look, they have burst. And these clothes of ours, and our sandals, are worn out, because of the very long journey."

Now listen to what he did not say.

He talked about Egypt, which was forty years ago. He talked about Sihon and Og, on the far side of the river, which was a while ago. He did not say one word about the river stopping. He did not say a word about Jericho. And he certainly did not say a word about Ai, which was just over the hill from his own front door.

And here is a small thing you may remember. Back in Jericho, in episode forty-seven, when Rahab hid the spies under the flax on her roof, she said almost the same words. We have heard how the Lord dried up the water of the sea when you came out of Egypt, and what you did to the two kings of the Amorites, Sihon and Og. It seems that was the story everybody in Canaan was telling. Rahab heard it and hung a red cord in her window. The Gibeonites heard it and packed their oldest sandals.

But Rahab said it before the river stopped and before Jericho fell. The Gibeonites said it afterwards, and still stopped at the same place, as if nothing newer had reached them yet. Because if you really lived very, very far away, that is how the news would reach you. Late. Old news first. The newest news not yet. That is how clever these people were. They even got the gossip to look far away.

And while he was speaking, the men of Israel did something very natural. They took some of the envoys' provisions. They looked at the bread, and turned it over, and broke it, and perhaps they tasted it, or tried to. They felt the wineskins. They looked at the sandals, and at Pazi's bit of string.

And then the Bible says one short sentence, and it is the sentence that the whole story turns on. It says that the men took some of their provisions, and did not ask counsel from the mouth of the Lord.

They checked the bread. They did not pray.

I want to be fair to them. It is exactly what any of us would have done. The evidence was right there on the table. You could smell it. Why would you need to ask God whether a mouldy loaf of bread is mouldy?

And so Joshua made peace with them, and made a covenant with them, to let them live. And the leaders of the congregation of Israel swore an oath to them.

Pazi, standing at the back, with his sandal tied on with string, could hardly breathe.

* * *

Three days.

That is how long it took. The Bible says that at the end of three days after they had made the covenant with them, the people of Israel heard that they were their neighbours, and that they lived among them.

I do not know how they found out. Perhaps someone from Israel went up into the hills and saw a familiar face. Perhaps somebody's donkey wandered home on its own and they followed it. Perhaps one of the envoys, at the bottom of the hill, said to another one, a bit too loudly, "Well, home by supper." The Bible does not say. It just says that they heard.

And the people of Israel set out, and on the third day they came to the towns. And here are their names, as I promised. Gibeon. And Chephirah. And Beeroth. And Kiriath-jearim. Remember that last one. Kiriath-jearim. It means something like the town of the woods. We will come back to it at the very end.

Now, imagine the scene. The army of Israel comes up over the ridge, and there is Gibeon, on its hill, with its springs and its vineyards. And standing in the gate, there are four men they recognise. And one of them, a boy of about sixteen, still has string on his sandal.

And the people of Israel did not attack them.

The Bible says it plainly. They did not strike them, because the leaders of the congregation had sworn to them by the Lord, the God of Israel.

But the people grumbled. Oh, they grumbled. The Bible says that the whole congregation murmured against the leaders. You can understand why. They had been tricked. They had been made to look silly, by a bag of bad bread. And now they were stuck with it.

And the leaders stood up in front of all the people, and they said this. "We have sworn to them by the Lord, the God of Israel. So now we may not touch them. This is what we will do to them. We will let them live, so that wrath will not come upon us, because of the oath which we swore to them."

I want you to notice something about that. The oath was made because of a lie. The Gibeonites had tricked them. Any lawyer in any country in the world would tell you that a promise you were tricked into is not a promise. And the leaders of Israel did not argue about that. They did not say, well, technically. They said, we swore it in the name of the Lord. The name of the Lord is not something you take back because you got the facts wrong.

* * *

Then Joshua sent for the people of Gibeon. And he said to them, "Why did you trick us? Why did you say, we are very far away from you, when you live right here among us?"

And then he told them what would happen. They would live. But from now on they would serve. They would be, in the famous phrase, hewers of wood and drawers of water. They would cut the firewood and carry the water. And not just for anyone. The book says, for the house of my God.

And the people of Gibeon answered him. And I think their answer is the most honest moment in the whole story, so I am going to give it to you nearly as it stands.

"It was told to your servants," they said, "for certain, that the Lord your God commanded his servant Moses to give you all the land. And we were very much afraid for our lives because of you. And so we did this thing. And now, look. We are in your hand. Do to us whatever seems good and right in your eyes."

No more speeches. No more mouldy bread. Just the truth. We were frightened. We did a sly thing. We are in your hands.

And Joshua did as he had said. He saved them out of the hand of the people of Israel, and did not let them be killed. And he made them, that very day, hewers of wood and drawers of water, for the congregation and for the altar of the Lord, in the place which the Lord would choose.

Now, I want to say something here, gently, because this is a bedtime show and not a lecture. I am not going to tell you that lying is a good idea. The Bible does not tell you that either. Joshua asks them straight out, why did you trick us, and they have no answer except that they were afraid.

But I do notice that out of all the cities in that whole hill country, there was only one that came to Israel asking for peace. The Book of Joshua says so, two chapters later. There was not a single city, it says, that made peace with the people of Israel, except the Hivites who lived in Gibeon. All the others came out to fight.

And I also notice that, in the end, they were given a place near the altar of God. It was not a grand place. Chopping wood and carrying water is hard and humble work. But it was a place in the house of the Lord. They had come down the mountain hoping only to stay alive. They went back up it with a job in God's house.

Pazi, walking home up the long hill that evening, with his grandmother's terrible bread still in his sack, did not feel he had done very well. But he was alive. And so was everyone he loved.

When he got home, Ama was waiting at the door.

"Well?" she said.

"They knew," said Pazi. "They found out. In three days."

Ama nodded.

"And they kept their word anyway," said Pazi. "Even though it was a trick. They said they had sworn by their God, and that was that."

Ama was quiet for a long time.

"Then it is a good thing," she said at last, "that we went to them, and not to the other kings."

And she took the last of the mouldy bread out of his sack, and looked at it, and threw it to the chickens. Who, being chickens, ate it anyway.

[pause]

There is one more part to the story, and it is the part I like best.

Not long afterwards, the king of Jerusalem heard what had happened. His name was Adoni-zedek. And he did not like it at all. Because Gibeon, the Bible says, was a great city, like one of the royal cities, bigger than Ai, and all its men were strong. And now Gibeon had gone over to Israel.

So Adoni-zedek sent to four other kings, the kings of Hebron, and Jarmuth, and Lachish, and Eglon, and he said, come up and help me, and let us attack Gibeon, because it has made peace with Joshua and with the people of Israel.

And they came. Five kings, and all their armies. And they camped around Gibeon, and they made war against it.

And the men of Gibeon, standing on their walls, looking down at five armies, did the only thing they could think of. They sent someone running down the mountain, to Gilgal, to the people they had tricked.

I like to think it was Pazi. The Bible does not say who it was. But somebody went down that long steep road, as fast as he could, to the camp at the bottom of the valley, and stood in front of Joshua, and said, in the words of the book, "Do not take your hand away from your servants. Come up to us quickly, and save us, and help us. Because all the kings of the Amorites who live in the hill country have gathered together against us."

Now think about that from Joshua's side.

These were the people who had lied to him. Who had made him look a fool in front of the whole of Israel. Who had put a mouldy loaf of bread on his table and looked him in the eye. And now they were asking him to march his whole army up three thousand feet of mountain, in the dark, to fight five kings at once, for their sake.

He could have said, you made your bed. He could have said, our oath was to let you live, not to fight your battles. He could have said, we will come in the morning.

He went. That night.

The Lord said to Joshua, do not be afraid of them, for I have given them into your hand. And Joshua went up from Gilgal, he and all the fighting men with him. And the Bible says, in words that I think are the most beautiful in the chapter, that he came upon them suddenly, having marched up all night from Gilgal.

All night. Up that road. For the people who had fooled him.

* * *

What happened next is a battle, and I am not going to tell it to you in detail, because it is bedtime. The book says the Lord threw the five armies into confusion before Israel, and they fled, down the long steep pass to the west of Gibeon, the pass of Beth-horon. And as they went down it, the Lord sent great hailstones out of the sky on them.

And then there is a moment that people have wondered about for three thousand years.

Joshua, on that day, spoke to the Lord, in the sight of Israel, and said:

Sun, stand still over Gibeon,
and moon, over the valley of Aijalon.

And the sun stood still, and the moon stopped, until the people had won their battle. And the book says, is this not written in the Book of Jashar? Which was an older book of songs and poems, that the Bible quotes more than once, and which is now lost. And it says that the sun stayed in the middle of the sky, and did not hurry to go down, for about a whole day. And that there has been no day like it, before or since, when the Lord listened to the voice of a man. Because the Lord fought for Israel.

People have argued for a very long time about how to understand that, and I am not going to settle it at bedtime. The Book of Sirach, many centuries later, remembering Joshua, simply asks: was not the sun held back by his hand, and did not one day become as long as two?

But I want you to notice where the sun stood. Not over Jericho. Not over Israel's own camp. Over Gibeon. The longest day in the Bible was spent keeping a promise to the people who had tricked Israel with mouldy bread.

[pause]

So what became of the Gibeonites?

They stayed. That is the main thing. They lived on in their four towns, on the green top of the hill country, cutting wood and drawing water for the house of God, just as Joshua had said.

And here is a strange and lovely thing. Long before any of this, Moses, at the very end of his life, on the plains of Moab, had gathered all the people of Israel together to renew their covenant with God. And in the Book of Deuteronomy he lists who was standing there. The heads of the tribes, and the elders, and the officers, and all the men of Israel, and the little children, and the wives. And then he says, and the stranger who is in your camp, from the one who cuts your wood to the one who draws your water.

The woodcutter and the water-carrier were in the covenant already. Before the Gibeonites had ever thought of their mouldy bread, there was room for them.

[pause]

And now I want you to remember Kiriath-jearim. The town of the woods. One of the four towns that belonged to Gibeon.

Many years later, in the days of the prophet Samuel, the ark of the covenant, the holiest thing in Israel, the golden chest that had been carried through the desert and across the Jordan, had nowhere to go. It had been captured by the Philistines, and sent back, and the town where it first arrived did not feel able to keep it. So they sent messengers to Kiriath-jearim, and said, come down and take it up to you.

And the men of Kiriath-jearim came, and took it, and carried it to the house of a man called Abinadab, on the hill. And there the ark of the Lord stayed, the Bible says, for twenty years.

Twenty years. In the town of the woods. In one of the four little towns that had once sent its envoys down the mountain in old sandals, hoping only to be allowed to live.

And later still, in King David's day, the old tent of meeting from the wilderness, and its bronze altar, stood at Gibeon itself, on the high place there, with priests serving before it morning and evening. That is why young King Solomon went up to Gibeon to pray, at the start of his reign, and why the Lord appeared to him there in a dream and said, ask, what shall I give you. We told that story a few nights ago, in episode one hundred and thirty-nine. I did not tell you then whose town it was. Now you know. It was the town of the people with the mouldy bread.

[pause]

The oath went on being remembered, too. Hundreds of years later, the Second Book of Samuel tells us, King Saul in his zeal broke it, and tried to get rid of the Gibeonites, and the Bible counts that as a serious wrong that had to be put right in David's day. That is a hard story, and not one for bedtime. But it makes the same point as tonight's. A promise made in the name of the Lord does not wear out, even after centuries.

And when David himself was an outlaw, hiding at Ziklag, which you may remember from last night, the First Book of Chronicles lists the brave men who came to join him there. And one of them was a man called Ishmaiah, the Gibeonite, a mighty man among the thirty, and a leader of the thirty. A Gibeonite, among David's heroes.

And much later still, when the people came home from exile in Babylon and rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem, the Book of Nehemiah lists who rebuilt which bit of the wall. And there, in chapter three, are the men of Gibeon, repairing their stretch, stone by stone. And in the list of the families who came home, there are the sons of Gibeon, ninety-five of them.

Still there. Still part of the story. The better part of a thousand years after their envoys came down the mountain.

[pause]

And the town itself is still there, in a way.

There is a village north-west of Jerusalem called el-Jib, and most scholars think it stands on the site of old Gibeon. In the nineteen fifties and early sixties, an American archaeologist called James Pritchard dug there. And he found, cut down into the rock of the hill, a great round shaft, nearly forty feet across, with a staircase spiralling down its side, and then steps going on down further still, through the rock, to water. A way to reach water from inside the town walls. And he found dozens of cellars cut in the rock, where wine could be kept cool. And on the handles of old wine jars, he found the name of the town, Gibeon, scratched into the clay.

Scholars argue about the dates, and about how the ruins fit the story of Joshua, and I am not going to try to settle that tonight. But it is nice to know that the people of Gibeon really did have water, and really did make wine. And that somebody, a very long time ago, wrote the name of their town on a jar handle, so that nobody would forget whose wine it was.

[pause]

And there is a psalm, Psalm fifteen, which asks a question. Lord, who may live in your tent? Who may dwell on your holy hill? And it gives a list of answers. The one who walks blamelessly. The one who speaks the truth from his heart. And then this. The one who swears an oath, even to his own hurt, and does not change.

The one who keeps his promise, even when it costs him.

I think of Joshua, going up that road in the dark.

So let us leave them at the top of the hill, on an evening some years later.

The battles are over. The camp has moved on. In Gibeon, the swallows are going round the gate again, and the shadows are long, and somebody is baking bread, good bread, the ordinary kind, which never lasts long enough to go mouldy.

And a young man, not quite so young now, is walking down the path from the spring with a water jar on his shoulder. He is taking it to the altar of the Lord. It is his job. It is a humble job, and it is heavy, and he will do it again tomorrow, and the day after.

His sandals are new. He has not needed string for years.

And he is not afraid any more.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Joshua, chapter nine, and chapter ten, verses one to fourteen, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. We have left out most of the battle in chapter ten, and the rest of that chapter, which is war and not for bedtime.

The line that only Gibeon made peace is from Joshua, chapter eleven. The woodcutter and the water-carrier in the covenant are from Deuteronomy, chapter twenty-nine. The ark at Kiriath-jearim is from First Samuel, chapters six and seven. The tent of meeting at Gibeon is from First Chronicles, chapter sixteen, and Second Chronicles, chapter one. Saul and the Gibeonites are from Second Samuel, chapter twenty-one; we have only mentioned it. Ishmaiah the Gibeonite is from First Chronicles, chapter twelve, and the men of Gibeon on the wall are from Nehemiah, chapters three and seven. Sirach's lines on the sun are from chapter forty-six, and the oath kept to one's own hurt is from Psalm fifteen.

What the sun standing still means has been argued about for centuries; we have told it as the book tells it and not explained it. That the house of Abinadab was in Kiriath-jearim is Scripture; whether his family were Gibeonites, the Bible does not say, and neither do we.

The identification of Gibeon with el-Jib, the pool and the inscribed jar handles are from James Pritchard's excavations, described in his book Gibeon, Where the Sun Stood Still. Most of what he found is from centuries after Joshua.

Ama, Pazi, the council, how the bread was made old, the number of envoys, the string, the chickens, and the messenger being Pazi, are our own invention. The Bible names none of the envoys and does not say how many there were.

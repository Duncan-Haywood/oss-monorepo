Egeria and the View That Moses Saw

The pilgrim Egeria crosses the Jordan to the plains of Moab, where Moses said goodbye to his people, drinks from a spring the monks call the water of Moses, and climbs Mount Nebo to stand at the door of a small church and look at the land Moses saw and never entered. Told from chapters ten to twelve of her own travel letter, with the last chapter of Deuteronomy told straight.

Full script

It is the middle of the day, on the top of a mountain, and a woman in a dusty travelling cloak is standing in the doorway of a very small church, with her hand shading her eyes.

Below her, the world falls away. It does not slope. It falls. Down and down, past brown ridges and grey ridges and a thin green thread of river, to a long flat sheet of water that shines like a dropped knife.

Beside her stands an old priest. He has seen this view every day for thirty years and he is still not tired of it. He is pointing.

"That is the Jordan," he says. "That is Jericho, the green patch, there, beyond the river. And that, down there, is the Dead Sea. And that is where Moses stood."

"Here?" says the woman. "In this doorway?"

The old priest smiles. "Somewhere very near here," he says. "God did not tell us which stone. But he looked at that. The same river. The same sea. The same land. He looked at all of it, and he was not allowed to go in."

The woman is quiet for a long moment. Then she says, very softly, "And was he sad?"

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story comes from the travel letter of Egeria, a pilgrim who journeyed to the holy places about sixteen hundred and forty years ago, together with the last chapter of the Book of Deuteronomy, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and a boy, some donkeys and all the small conversations are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

If you were with us last time, you will remember Egeria.

If you were not, here is all you need to know. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, probably from the north-west corner of Spain, or possibly from the south of France, and we don't know which, because the beginning of her letter is lost. Somewhere around the year three hundred and eighty, she set off to see the places in the Bible with her own eyes. And she wrote it all down in a long letter to a group of women at home, whom she calls her sisters, and sometimes, very grandly, her ladies, her venerable ladies, her light.

Part of that letter has survived. Only part. It turned up in a single old copy in a library in Italy in eighteen eighty-four, with the front and the back missing, like a book a dog has been at. But what is left is wonderful. It is one of the oldest travel diaries we have written by a woman, and it is one of the oldest descriptions we have of how Christians prayed in Jerusalem. And all the way through it, there is Egeria herself: curious, brave, enormously polite, never tired, or never admitting it, and always, always asking questions.

Last time, she climbed Mount Sinai, straight up, like a wall, on a Sunday morning, and saw the bush still growing in a garden at the bottom. Tonight, she is back in Jerusalem, where she has been living for some time. And she has had another idea.

This is how she puts it. "Some time later," she writes, "I wanted to go to Arabia, to Mount Nebo, the mountain God told Moses to climb."

Now, a word about geography, so we all know where we are.

If you stand in Jerusalem and walk east, you go downhill. Then you keep going downhill. Then, just when you think you have run out of downhill, there is more. Jerusalem stands high in the hills, and the valley of the River Jordan, to the east, is so low that it is below the level of the sea. Far below. The Dead Sea, at the bottom of that valley, is the lowest place on the dry face of the whole earth. If you walked from Jerusalem down to the Dead Sea, you would drop more than a kilometre in height in a single day, which is the kind of thing that makes your knees write you letters of complaint.

Then you cross the Jordan. And on the far side, the ground goes up again, steeply, into a long line of hills and high flat country. In Egeria's day, the Romans called that country Arabia, which is confusing, because it is not what we would call Arabia. Today it is part of the Kingdom of Jordan. In the Bible it is the land of Moab. And at the edge of those hills, looking back west over the valley towards Jericho and Jerusalem, there is a mountain with a long flat shoulder. That is Mount Nebo.

And why did Egeria want to go there? Because of the very last chapter of the five books of Moses.

You may know the story. Moses had led the people of Israel out of Egypt. He had stood before Pharaoh. He had led them through the sea. He had climbed Sinai and come down with the Law. And then, for forty years, he had walked with them through the wilderness, and listened to them complain, and prayed for them, and argued with God on their behalf, and argued with them on God's behalf, which is the kind of job that would turn anyone's hair grey, and probably did.

And at the end of those forty years, they came to the plains of Moab, at the edge of the Jordan, opposite Jericho. And the promised land was right there. Just across the river. You could see it.

But Moses would not cross. God had told him so, years before. Once, in the wilderness, at a place called Meribah, when the people were thirsty and angry, God had told Moses to speak to a rock so that water would come out. And Moses, who was tired and furious with the people, struck the rock with his staff instead, twice, and said some sharp words. And the water came. But God told Moses and his brother Aaron that, because they had not trusted him and had not shown his holiness to the people, they would not lead them into the land.

Moses asked, later, if he might be allowed to go over all the same. Let me go over, he prayed, and see the good land beyond the Jordan. And God said, Speak no more to me of this. But he said, Go up to the top of the mountain, and look. Look west and north and south and east. See it with your eyes. Because you shall not go over this Jordan.

And so, at the end of his life, Moses climbed Mount Nebo. And God showed him the whole land. And there Moses died.

That is the place Egeria wanted to see. Not a place where something big and loud happened, like the sea parting. A place where an old man stood on a hill and looked at something he had waited his whole life for, and then lay down.

So she packed. Again.

They left Jerusalem early.

Egeria never travelled alone. She tells us she went with holy men, which in her letter usually means a mixture of priests and monks who knew the way and knew the Scriptures, and there would have been servants, and baggage, and almost certainly donkeys, because Egeria used donkeys whenever she could and walked whenever she couldn't.

Let us give one of those donkeys a boy. The letter does not mention him, so he is entirely ours. His name is Malchus. He is about twelve. He comes from a village near Jericho, and he has been leading donkeys up and down the road from Jerusalem since he was small enough to ride in a basket. He has very strong opinions about donkeys.

"This one is called Patience," he told Egeria, on the first morning, slapping a grey flank. "Because she has none."

"And that one?"

"That one is called Thunder. Because he is very quiet."

Egeria considered this. "Do you name all your donkeys the opposite of what they are?"

"Only the ones I like," said Malchus.

Egeria, who had been all over the eastern half of the Roman Empire by this time and had met bishops and hermits and soldiers and at least one very anxious official, decided she was going to like Malchus.

The road went down. It went down through the hills, past places where the rock was folded like cloth, past little caves where hermits lived and watched the travellers go by, past a cistern, past another cistern. The air got warmer. The air got heavier. Egeria's ears did a thing ears do when you drive down a mountain, except that she was on a donkey called Patience and nobody had told her about ears.

"It is very warm," she said, at one point.

"It is winter," said Malchus. "You should come in summer. In summer, the birds walk."

By evening, they were in the green country around Jericho, with its palm trees and its springs. And the next day, they came to the Jordan.

Now, the Jordan is not a large river. People who grow up on the stories expect something enormous, something like the sea parting, and then they arrive and find a brown, winding, cheerful river that you could throw a stone across, with reeds and willows on its banks and frogs having opinions in the shallows.

But it is still the Jordan. It is where Joshua led the people across into the land with the waters held back. It is where Elijah and Elisha crossed on dry ground. It is where John baptised, and where the Lord himself came down into the water. Egeria, who knew her Bible extremely well, stood on the bank for a while and did not say anything.

Then they crossed. In Egeria's day, there were places where you could ford it, and ferries, and Malchus knew which was which, and which ferryman would try to charge a foreign lady double, and how to look at him until he stopped.

On the far side, the land opened out into a great plain, flat and wide, running along the foot of the hills of Moab. And in that plain there was a little town, with a bishop and a church. In Egeria's day it was called Livias. A long time before, it had another name, and long before that, the people of Israel had camped there.

Here is something lovely. Egeria tells her sisters that you could still see, in that plain, the traces of the camp. "The foundations of the encampment of the children of Israel," she says, "and of the places where they lived, still appear there to this day."

Now, would the tents of Israel have left marks in the ground that lasted fifteen hundred years? Almost certainly not. Tents are not that kind of thing. More likely there were old walls or field banks or the ruins of some later village, and the local people, who loved the story, pointed to them and said, there, that was where they camped. And Egeria, who loved the story too, believed them. She was very trusting. But she was also very honest, as we shall see, and she only ever told her sisters what she was shown and what she was told. So we shall do the same, and simply say: that is what they showed her.

What is not in doubt is that this was the right sort of place. The Bible says the people camped in the plains of Moab, by the Jordan, opposite Jericho. And here was the plain. And there was the Jordan. And over the river, among its palm trees, was Jericho.

And here, in this plain, the Book of Deuteronomy happens.

Deuteronomy is Moses's long farewell. It is a series of speeches he gave to the people, all together, before he died. He reminds them of everything that has happened. He gives them the Law again, so the young ones, who were children or not yet born when they left Egypt, can hear it from his own mouth. He tells them to love the Lord their God with all their heart and soul and strength. He tells them to write it on their doorposts and teach it to their children and talk about it when they sit in the house and when they walk on the road and when they lie down and when they rise up.

And then he writes it all down. And then he lays his hands on Joshua, who will lead them after him. And then he sings them a song, a great long song about God's faithfulness, which begins, "Give ear, O heavens, and I will speak; and hear, O earth, the words of my mouth." And then, last of all, he blesses them. Tribe by tribe. One by one.

Egeria tells us that the people of Livias showed her where each of these things was done. Here, they said, is where Joshua was filled with the spirit of wisdom, because Moses laid his hands on him. Here is where Moses wrote the book. Here is where he spoke the song. And here is where he blessed the children of Israel, each in turn, before he died.

And at each place, Egeria did what she always did.

You remember her custom. She tells us about it so often that her sisters must have been able to say it with her. Whenever they came to a holy place, they would stop. First, a prayer. Then, a reading from the Scriptures, the passage that belonged to that place. Then a psalm, suitable to the place. Then another prayer.

So in the plain of Moab, by the Jordan, they read Deuteronomy. Egeria says they read the song, and they read the blessings. Out loud. In the place where they were first spoken.

I like to imagine Malchus, standing at the back with the donkeys, listening. He has heard some of these words in his own village, on the Sabbath, or in his own church, read by a deacon who mumbles. But here is this foreign lady with a strange accent, standing in a field, with her eyes shut, while an old priest reads Moses blessing the tribes. "Let Reuben live, and not die." "The beloved of the Lord shall dwell in safety by him." "The eternal God is thy refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms."

"Why does she shut her eyes?" he whispers to one of the monks.

"So she can see it better," says the monk.

Malchus thinks about this for a long time. Then he tries it. With his eyes shut, he can hear the river, and the wind in the dry grass, and Thunder chewing. And the old voice saying, Underneath are the everlasting arms. And it is true, he decides. You can see it better.

The next morning, they set out for the mountain itself.

Egeria tells us they took with them some of the holy people of that place, who knew the way. Nebo is not far from Livias as the crow flies, but crows don't have to climb. The road went east, across the plain, and then up, into the hills.

And some miles along, they turned aside a little off the road, because there was something Egeria's guides wanted her to see.

It was a spring.

Not a little trickle. A real spring, coming out of the rock at the foot of the hills, fresh and clear and cold, enough of it to fill a stream. In that hot dry country, it was like finding a song in the middle of a silence. And there were trees round it, and green, and the sound of water.

Egeria describes it, and you can hear how much she liked it. She says the water was very clear and very good to drink, and that there was a lot of it. And she says that some holy monks lived there beside the spring. She calls them, in a word she uses for the very serious ones, ascetics. Men who had given up nearly everything for God, and kept, it seems, a spring.

Of course, Egeria asked about the water. Egeria asked about everything.

And the monks told her: this is the water that Moses gave the children of Israel, in this wilderness.

Now, I have to be honest with you, the way Egeria was honest with her sisters. The Bible tells of two times when Moses brought water out of a rock: once early in the journey, near Sinai, and once at Meribah, near Kadesh, which is the time that cost him the promised land. And the Bible places both of those far to the south of here. Not in the hills of Moab.

But the people of Israel travelled a long way, and camped in many places, and must have been thirsty in all of them. And people who live by a beautiful spring, in a place full of the story of Moses, will naturally come to think that Moses must have known it. Egeria tells us only what the monks told her. And so shall we. It is a tradition. A very old one. Whether Moses drank there or not, the monks did, and Egeria did, and so, I am quite sure, did the donkeys.

"Is it true?" asked Malchus, wiping his mouth. "That this is Moses's water?"

One of the old monks looked at him, very kindly, with water dripping off his beard.

"All water is Moses's water," he said. "And all water is God's. He gave it to them in the desert. He is giving it to you now. Drink."

Malchus decided that was not quite an answer, but that it was a good enough one, and drank.

They prayed there. They read the passage from Moses about the water from the rock. They sang a psalm. And the monks, who did not get many visitors from the far side of the world, gave them what monks always give, which is a little bread and a lot of blessing. And then they went on up.

Mount Nebo is not like Sinai.

Sinai, you may remember, is a great raw fist of granite that goes straight up into the sky. Nebo is gentler. It is a long ridge at the edge of the high country, with a rounded top. But it is still a long way up from the Jordan valley, which you will remember is below sea level, and the path went up and up through brown stony slopes with a little scrubby green clinging on here and there.

Egeria tells us, quite cheerfully, that a good part of the way they could ride on the donkeys. But that there was a part that was so steep that they had to get down and walk, and it was hard going.

"Patience does not like this part," said Malchus, hauling on a rope.

"Neither does Egeria," said Egeria, who was hauling on herself.

"You climbed Sinai," said one of the monks. "I heard you climbed Sinai straight up."

"I did," said Egeria. "And I have been reminding my knees of it ever since, and they have not yet forgiven me."

But she kept going. She always kept going. A monk in Spain, three hundred years later, would write about her that she never let the weakness of her body hold her back, and you can see it here. A woman, no longer young, in a long dress and a cloak, walking up a steep path in the heat of the morning, because she wanted to stand where Moses stood.

As they went higher, the air changed. It got cooler, and cleaner, and it smelled of dry herbs and stone. And behind them, as they climbed, every time they stopped to catch their breath and turned round, the valley below had got wider and wider. As if the world were slowly being unrolled.

"Don't look yet," said the old priest, at one point. "Wait till the top."

"I can't help it," said Egeria.

"No," he agreed. "Nobody can."

On the very top of Mount Nebo, there was a church.

Egeria tells us it was not large. That will not surprise you, if you remember Sinai. The holy mountains of Egeria's world seem to have had small churches on their summits, which makes sense, if you have ever tried to carry roof-beams up a mountain.

And there were people living there. Priests and holy monks, who looked after the church and prayed in it and welcomed visitors like Egeria.

They went inside. And Egeria noticed something.

In the church, near the place where the pulpit stood, there was a raised place in the floor. Not very high. About the size, she says, of a grave.

So of course, she asked what it was.

And the holy men told her. This is what they said, and I'll give it to you as near as I can to the way she wrote it down.

"Here holy Moses was laid by the angels. Because, as it is written, no one knows where he was buried. So it is certain that he was buried by angels. His actual tomb, where he was laid, is not shown to this day. But as our elders who lived here showed this place to us, so we show it to you. And our elders said that it had been handed down to them by their own elders."

Now, isn't that a careful thing to say? Listen to how they put it. They don't say, here is the tomb of Moses, step this way, mind the step. They say: Scripture tells us nobody knows where he is buried. We don't know either. But this is the place our elders showed us, and their elders showed them, and so we show it to you.

I think that is about as honest as a tradition can be.

Let me tell you what the Bible says. It is the very end of Deuteronomy, and it is very short, and very beautiful, so I am going to tell it to you straight.

Moses went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of the peak called Pisgah, opposite Jericho. And the Lord showed him all the land. The hill country of Gilead in the north, as far as Dan. All of Naphtali. The land of Ephraim and Manasseh. All the land of Judah, as far as the western sea. The south country. And the plain of the valley of Jericho, the city of palm trees, as far as Zoar.

And the Lord said to him, "This is the land I promised to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob, saying, I will give it to your children. I have let you see it with your eyes. But you shall not go over there."

So Moses, the servant of the Lord, died there, in the land of Moab, as the Lord had said.

And he buried him in a valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth-peor. But no one knows the place of his grave to this day.

He buried him. Did you hear that? The Bible does not say the people buried him. It says he buried him, and the he is the Lord. That is why the monks on Nebo talked about angels. If God buried Moses, they reasoned, then surely he sent his angels to do it, the way a king sends his servants. And there is a very short and very strange verse in the Letter of Jude, in the New Testament, which mentions the Archangel Michael arguing with the devil about the body of Moses. So the idea of angels at Moses's burial is not something the monks of Nebo made up out of nothing. But the Scripture itself says only this: God buried him. And no one knows where.

And then the Bible says one more thing about Moses, which I have always loved.

Moses was a hundred and twenty years old when he died. His eye was not dim, nor his strength gone.

He could still see. Right to the end. He could still see the whole land, from the top of the mountain, with clear eyes.

And the children of Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab for thirty days.

And there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face.

That is how the five books of Moses end. Not with a battle, not with a crowning, not with the people marching triumphantly into the land. With an old man on a hill who saw it all with clear eyes and was buried by God.

So in that small church on the top of Nebo, by the raised place in the floor, Egeria and her companions did what they always did. They prayed. They read that passage, the last chapter of Deuteronomy. They sang a psalm. They prayed again.

And Malchus, at the back, with his eyes shut, heard about the eye that was not dim, and thought about his grandfather, who was not a hundred and twenty but who could still see a hawk on a hilltop before anyone else in the village. And he decided that he would like to be like that when he was old. Clear-eyed, right to the end.

Then they came out of the church. And the priests said a wonderful thing.

Egeria remembers it like this. "If you want to see the places that are written about in the books of Moses," they said, "come out of the door of the church, and from the summit, as far as it can be seen from here, look. And we will show you each place."

And she adds, about herself and her friends: "We were very glad of this. And we went straight out."

I love that. You can almost hear her, sixteen hundred years ago, nearly tripping over her own cloak to get through the door.

And from the door of that little church, they looked.

Egeria tells her sisters what they saw, and she tells it like a person standing on a high place with a friend pointing, there, and there, and that one there. They saw almost the whole valley of the Jordan. They saw where the river runs down into the Dead Sea. On their own side of the river, below them, they saw Livias, where they had stayed. On the far side of the river, they saw Jericho. And beyond, she says, a great part of Palestine, which is the land of promise. As far as the eye could see.

It would have been, if they were lucky with the air, very far. From the top of Nebo on a clear day, you can see right across the valley to the hills of Judea on the other side, and Jericho in its green patch at their foot, and, so they say, on the very clearest days, the high places around Jerusalem itself, where Egeria had set out a few days before. A whole country, laid out like a map that someone has coloured in with gold and brown and blue.

"Moses saw this," said Egeria quietly.

"He saw more, I think," said the old priest. "Scripture says the Lord showed him the whole land, from Dan in the far north to the western sea. Some say God gave him eyes to see it all. Some say it is just a way of telling it. Either way, he saw what God wanted him to see."

"I keep thinking," said Egeria, "how hard it must have been. To come all that way. Forty years. And then to stop here. Just at the edge."

And this is where we came in. The old priest pointing, and Egeria asking, "And was he sad?"

The priest thought about it for quite a long time.

"I think," he said, at last, "it is hard to be sad when God is standing next to you showing you something beautiful. Scripture says God talked with Moses face to face, the way a man talks with his friend. And here, at the end, the Lord came and stood with his friend on a mountain and showed him the land. I think Moses knew that the land was never really the point. The people were going to the land. Moses was going to God."

Egeria did not say anything for a while after that. Then she said, "I should like to write that down."

"Then write it down," said the priest. "Your ladies at home will want to know."

I should tell you, in fairness, that the priest's answer is our own invention. Egeria did not record what anyone said about whether Moses was sad. But the Church has thought about this mountain for a very long time. And many Christians, over the centuries, have pointed out that Moses did, in the end, stand in the promised land. Hundreds of years later, on a high mountain, when the Lord Jesus was transfigured and his face shone like the sun, there were two people talking with him. One was Elijah. The other was Moses.

So the old man did get in, in the end. Just not by the road he expected.

But Egeria was not finished with the view. Because the priests had more to show her. They pointed out, to the left and to the right, the places from the books of Moses.

There, they said, was Heshbon, the city of Sihon, king of the Amorites, whom the children of Israel defeated on their way, when he would not let them pass through his land in peace. Egeria tells us it was still called by nearly the same name in her day, and it still is: there is a town there today called Hesban.

And then they showed her something else. Down to the south, at the edge of the Dead Sea, they pointed to a city called Zoar. You may remember Zoar from the Book of Genesis. It was the little town that Lot ran to, with his daughters, when the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah were destroyed. And as they ran, the angels told them not to look back. And Lot's wife looked back, and became a pillar of salt.

And the priests pointed to the place, below them, near the sea, where that pillar had stood.

And here, my friends, is my favourite moment in the whole of Egeria's letter. Because she turns to her sisters, and she says, in effect: now listen to me, because I am going to tell you the truth.

"But believe me, my venerable ladies," she writes, "the pillar itself cannot be seen. Only the place is shown. The pillar itself is said to have been covered over by the Dead Sea. Certainly we saw the place, but we saw no pillar. I cannot deceive you about this."

I cannot deceive you about this.

Isn't that marvellous? Here is a woman who has believed every lovely thing that every monk has told her. She saw the bush of Moses. She saw the foundations of the camp of Israel. She has just drunk from the spring that Moses gave. And here, when everyone around her has been pointing at the place of the pillar of salt, she turns to her friends at home and says: I didn't see it. I am not going to tell you I did. I will tell you what I saw, and I will tell you what I was told, and I will not mix the two up.

And she adds a detail, like a good reporter. The bishop of Zoar, the bishop of that very town, was there, or she met him somewhere along the way, and he told her that it was now some years since the pillar could be seen. The place where it had stood, about six miles from Zoar, was now entirely covered by the water.

"I am sorry about the pillar," said Malchus, who had been rather looking forward to it.

"So am I," said Egeria. "But it is better to be sorry and truthful than to be pleased and wrong."

Malchus thought about that, too. He was doing a lot of thinking on this trip. It was not what he had expected when he set out with the donkeys.

The sun was getting lower when they started down.

They did not stay long on Nebo. Egeria does not tell us exactly how long, but she says they went back down the way they had come, past the spring, where the ascetics waved, and down to the plain, and to Livias, and from there back across the Jordan, and up through the hills to Jerusalem.

Going down was easier. It always is, for the lungs, and always worse, for the knees.

Patience the donkey, who had no patience, wanted very badly to get home, and kept trying to trot. Thunder, who was very quiet, said nothing about anything, and plodded. Malchus walked in front, singing a song from his village under his breath, and every so often looking back over his shoulder.

"Don't look back," said one of the monks, with a twinkle. "Remember Lot's wife."

"I'm only looking at the mountain," said Malchus.

"That's all right, then," said the monk. "You may look back at a mountain. Mountains are for looking at."

And the mountain sat behind them, in the evening light, turning gold and then pink and then grey, with its little church on the top and the old priest, very probably, standing in the doorway, watching the sun go down over the land that Moses saw.

That night, back in Livias or wherever they lay down, Egeria will have done what she always did at the end of a long day. Said her prayers. And then, I like to think, by the light of a small oil lamp, she took out her writing things, and started to make notes, for her sisters. The church. The raised place. The spring. The view. Heshbon. Zoar.

And the pillar, which she did not see.

I cannot deceive you about this.

That was, as far as we know, about sixteen hundred and forty years ago.

Egeria went back to Jerusalem. She was not finished travelling, not by a long way. Soon after Nebo, she tells us, she had another idea, and set off again, north this time, to see the land of Job, which is another story, for another night. And after that, she went on to the far north, to Edessa and to the city of Abraham, and in the end to Constantinople, where her letter breaks off, with her planning yet another journey.

We don't know what happened to her after that. She has never been made a saint. She has no feast day. But a monk in Spain called Valerius, three hundred years later, read her letter and wrote to his brothers about her, and called her the blessed Egeria, and held her up to them as an example. If a woman could cross the whole world for love of God, was the gist of it, then a monastery full of men could surely manage the walk to morning prayers.

And the mountain?

The little church on the top of Nebo was made bigger, over the years. The monks stayed. More people came. Other churches were built around it, with beautiful floors of tiny coloured stones, mosaics, full of shepherds and hunters and animals of every kind, and trees heavy with fruit. Then, in time, the monks went away, and the churches fell, and the wind blew sand over the floors.

And then, in the nineteen thirties, a group of Franciscan friars, who look after many of the holy places in that part of the world, bought the top of the mountain, and began to dig. And under the sand and stone, they found the church. The very church Egeria described, or at least its foundations, and the larger churches built over it later, and the mosaic floors, with their shepherds and their animals, nearly as bright as the day they were laid.

The friars are still there. They built a new roof over the old church, to protect the floors. And pilgrims still climb up, from all over the world, to stand at the edge of the summit, and look west. In the spring of the year two thousand, Pope John Paul the Second, an old man by then, stood on that mountain and looked out over the land too, at the start of his pilgrimage to the holy places.

And on a clear day, you can still see it all. The Jordan, winding down to the sea. Jericho, in its green patch. The hills of the far side, rising up. And the Dead Sea, shining.

No pillar of salt, though. I'm afraid I cannot deceive you about this.

The view is the same view. The same view the old priest showed Egeria. The same view God showed his friend Moses, when he was a hundred and twenty years old, with his eye not dim.

Some things you are allowed to see, but not to reach. Not yet. Most of us have something like that, if we are honest. A place, or a person, or a thing we hoped for, that we can see from the top of the mountain and cannot walk into. Not in this life, anyway.

Moses's story says that is not the end. That God stands next to us on the mountain. That he shows us the land. That underneath are the everlasting arms.

And that, at the very end of the day, when the sun goes down over the hills, he takes care of us himself.

Here are the notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from chapters ten, eleven and twelve of Egeria's Travels, the long Latin letter written by a pilgrim woman to her sisters at home, probably in the early three hundred and eighties, and found in a single incomplete manuscript in Arezzo in eighteen eighty-four. The journey from Jerusalem with holy men, the crossing of the Jordan, the town of Livias in the plain, the traces of the camp of Israel, the places of Joshua's commissioning, the writing of Deuteronomy, the song and the blessings, the readings at each place, the spring off the road with the monks beside it, the climb partly riding and partly on foot, the small church on the summit, the raised place the size of a grave and the priests' words about it, the invitation to look out from the church door, the view, Heshbon, Zoar, the place of Lot's wife with no pillar to be seen, and the bishop of Zoar's report are all in the letter. Her own words are given in our plain rendering, shortened in places.

That the spring is the water Moses gave, and that the raised place marks Moses's resting place, are the local traditions she was told, and we have told them as traditions. Scripture places the water from the rock far to the south, and says that no one knows where Moses is buried. The angels at his burial are the monks' explanation; the verse about Michael is in the Letter of Jude.

The story of Moses on Nebo comes from the Book of Deuteronomy, chapters three and thirty-one to thirty-four, with Meribah from Numbers twenty, Sihon from Numbers twenty-one, and Lot's wife from Genesis nineteen, told straight. The Franciscan excavation is described in Sylvester Saller's study of the Memorial of Moses.

The boy Malchus, the donkeys called Patience and Thunder, the old priest's answer about whether Moses was sad, and every other word anyone says, apart from Egeria's own reported words, are our own invention.

That's the story of Egeria, and the view that Moses saw, for tonight.

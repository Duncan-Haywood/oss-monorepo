The Good Samaritan and the Road Down to Jericho

A lawyer asks Jesus a tidy question, who is my neighbour, and gets back an untidy story: a man beaten and left half dead on the steep road to Jericho, two respectable travellers who hurry past, and a Samaritan who stops. A story of a road, a donkey, an inn and a question turned round, from the tenth chapter of Luke.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Jesus, a lawyer of the Law, a traveller on the Jericho road, a priest, a Levite, a Samaritan, an innkeeper
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; Luke's Gospel is Scripture in both churches, and this parable is read in both
Scripture and history
Luke chapter ten, verses twenty-five to thirty-seven, is Scripture; the road from Jerusalem down to Jericho is a real road that falls about a thousand metres in some seventeen miles, and the enmity between Jews and Samaritans is a real part of the world of the Gospel
Legend
none; the parable is a story Jesus tells, and the old custom of calling a ruined inn on that road by the Samaritan's name is much later and is named in the notes only as a custom
What we invented
the road's weather, the donkey's temper, the traveller's thoughts, what the priest and the Levite said to themselves, the Samaritan's journey and the talk at the inn are our own invention, told in the spirit of Luke; the lawyer's question, the two answers, the beating, the three who see the man, the oil and wine, the donkey, the inn, the two denarii, the promise to return and the closing question are kept close to Luke's account
Feast day
The parable has no feast day of its own; the Catholic Church reads it at Mass on the Fifteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time in Year C, and the Orthodox Church reads it in its own cycle of Luke

Full script

Picture a road, in the heat of the day, with nobody on it.

It is a stony road, and it runs downhill between bare brown hills, the kind of hills that have more rocks in them than anything else. There are a great many places beside it where a person could stand without being seen. And in a dry gully a little way off the road, lying on his side with his cheek against the dust, is a man who a short while ago was walking along thinking about his supper.

He cannot get up. He is not sure he wants to, because everything hurts. But he can hear something. Far up the road, there are footsteps, and they are coming closer. And he lies quite still, and he listens, and he tries to decide whether to call out.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is a parable from the Gospel of Luke, chapter ten, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is the one we call the Good Samaritan. A few of the small things around the edges, the weather on the road, the donkey's opinions, what people were thinking as they walked, and the talk at the inn, are our own invention, told in the spirit of Luke, and the notes at the end say which. The question, the answers, the beating, the three travellers, the oil and wine and the two silver coins we have kept as close to Luke as we can. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let me tell you first where we are, and why this story was told at all.

Jesus is on the move. In Luke's Gospel, from about the middle of the ninth chapter, he has set his face towards Jerusalem, and everything after that happens, one way and another, on the road. He sends out seventy-two disciples ahead of him in pairs, to the towns he means to visit, and they come back in a very good mood and tell him that even the demons obey in his name. He is delighted with them. He thanks his Father, out loud, for hiding things from the clever and showing them to little children. It is a happy scene. And it is into this scene, with the crowd still glowing, that a man stands up.

Luke calls him a lawyer. He does not mean a person in a suit with a briefcase. He means an expert in the Law of Moses, a scholar, someone who had spent years learning what the Scriptures said and arguing about what they meant. Think of the sort of person who, at a dinner party, knows exactly which fork you ought to be using, and why, and can tell you the history of the fork. He is not a villain. Luke tells us only that he stood up to put Jesus to the test, and a test, in those days, was not necessarily an insult. Teachers tested each other all the time, the way two chess players might begin a game.

And he asks a very good question. He asks, "Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?"

I should like you to notice that. Not what must I believe, or what must I know. What must I do? He is a man of action, in his way, and he wants a list.

Jesus does not give him a list. Jesus does what a good teacher does, which is to hand the question back. "What is written in the Law?" he says. "How do you read it?"

Now the lawyer is on home ground, and I think he is rather pleased. He knows this. He has known it since he was a boy. And he gives the answer that any scholar of his day would have been proud of. He puts two commandments together, one from the book of Deuteronomy and one from Leviticus. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength, and with all your mind. And you shall love your neighbour as yourself.

It is a beautiful answer. It is, if you think about it, the whole thing in a sentence. And Jesus says so. "You have answered rightly," he says. "Do this, and you will live."

Here the story could have ended. The lawyer has asked, and been answered, and been told that he was right. Many of us would have said thank you and sat down, and gone home with a warm feeling.

But Luke tells us something very small and very human. He says that the lawyer, wanting to justify himself, said to Jesus, "And who is my neighbour?"

I like that Luke tells us why. He wanted to justify himself. He wanted, I think, to be sure that he was already doing well. Because if the neighbour is only the person next door, and the people one knows, and the people of one's own village and one's own faith, then it is a manageable thing, and a person can say that he has done it. It is a kind of tidy question. It has edges. It has a boundary that can be drawn on a map, with a nice neat line, and the lawyer would like to know where the line goes, so that he can check that he is inside it.

And Jesus does not draw him a line. Jesus tells him a story.

Before the story, I must tell you about the road, because everyone in the crowd that day would have known it, and we are not from there.

The road goes from Jerusalem to Jericho. Jerusalem is a city on a high ridge, about seven hundred and fifty metres above the level of the sea, which is two and a half thousand feet, give or take. Jericho is in the valley of the Jordan, and the Jordan valley is the lowest dry land on the face of the earth. Jericho lies well below sea level, something like two hundred and fifty metres below it. If you do the sum, the road drops by about a thousand metres, or three thousand three hundred feet, over a distance of some seventeen miles. That is steep. It is steeper than any road you are likely to have driven on to get to the seaside. And so when Jesus begins his story with the words "A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho," every person listening knew that he meant down, really down, a long hard downhill walk, in a country where the sun beats on the rocks.

It was also a road with a reputation. The hills on either side are full of cracks and caves and gullies, and the road winds so much that a person cannot see far ahead. It was the kind of road where, when you set out, someone in the family says, "Do be careful, and don't go on your own." Highwaymen were a known trouble there. People said so, in the old days, and they said it for a long time afterwards, and I do not think Jesus is asking his listeners to imagine anything unusual. This is simply a bad road. It is the kind of road where bad things happen to ordinary people.

And Jericho, at the bottom, was a town that mattered. It was a green town with palm trees and springs and a great deal of money, and it was close enough to Jerusalem that many people who worked in the Temple are said to have lived there. We will come back to that.

So that is our road. And now we will take it, with the man in the story. Jesus does not give him a name, and nor shall we.

He sets out early, I think. That is our guess, and not Luke's. He is a man with business in Jericho, and a day's walk to do, and he has had a good breakfast and said his prayers and is in a decent mood. He has a bag with a little money in it and a cloak against the cold of the morning, and a spare tunic, and some bread wrapped in a cloth. He is not rich. He is not poor either. He is an ordinary person, going down an ordinary road, to do an ordinary thing.

The morning is lovely. The air is clear, and he can see for miles, which on this road is not something you can say very often. He walks quickly at first, because the road is going his way, and the stones are loose underfoot and make a pleasant rattle. He sings a little, under his breath. He thinks about the person he is going to see. He thinks about the price of olive oil.

I like to imagine that he has a donkey, and that the donkey has been left behind with a cousin, because the cousin needed it. But that is only a notion of mine. Luke says nothing of it, and later in the story there will be a donkey, but it will belong to somebody else. So let us say that our traveller goes on foot.

By midmorning the sun is high and the road has become what it always becomes at that hour, a long white strip between two walls of baking rock. He stops once to drink from a flask. He stops again to take a stone out of his sandal. He does not notice, because there is no reason to, that the road ahead of him has no one on it, and that the road behind him has no one on it either.

It is a bend in the road that does it. There is a place where the path goes round a great fold of rock, and for a moment he cannot see in front of him, and for a moment he cannot be seen from behind. And as he comes round it, there are men there.

Luke says they are robbers. He says they stripped him and beat him and went away, leaving him half dead. It is all told in about a dozen words. I think Luke, or the Lord whose words he is writing down, knew that nobody wants the details, and nobody needs them. Everyone listening has some picture of it, and every one of those pictures is bad enough. We shall not add to them. It happens quickly. And then it is over, and the men have gone off up the hill with his cloak and his bag and his flask and his spare tunic, and even his sandals, and the road is silent, and he is lying in the dust.

Half dead, says Luke. It is a careful phrase, and I think it matters, so remember it. He is not dead. But he is not far from it, and anyone looking at him from the road might not be sure.

I said that someone was coming. Let us go back to our opening, to the heat and the quiet. He has been lying there for some time. The sun has moved. He cannot say how long it has been, because the first hour after such a thing is not like an ordinary hour. There is a ringing in his ears. His mouth is full of dust. And somewhere in the middle of all this, quite slowly, there comes the sound of someone walking.

It is the steady sound of a person who is not in a hurry but is not dawdling either. Sandals on stone. He hears it coming round the same bend. And the sound slows, and then it stops, and he knows that whoever it is has seen him.

Luke's way of telling this is wonderfully plain. He says, "Now by chance a priest was going down that road."

By chance. The Greek word means that it simply happened so, that it was a coincidence, and Luke is telling us that nobody planned this. It was not a test set up by God, or a trap laid by someone clever. It was just a priest on a road. These things happen. A person comes round a corner at a particular moment, and what he does next is entirely up to him.

Now a priest in those days was a man of the family of Aaron, who took his turn at serving in the Temple at Jerusalem. The priests came up to the city for their weeks of duty, offered the sacrifices, burned the incense, and then went home again, and many of them, as I mentioned, are said to have lived in Jericho. So a priest going down the road from Jerusalem to Jericho is very likely a priest who has just finished his week at the Temple. He has been in the holiest place in the country. He is tired, and he is, I expect, rather moved by it all, and he is going home.

And he sees the man by the roadside. Luke says, "when he saw him, he passed by on the opposite side."

That is all. He does not say anything unkind. He does not mock, or kick, or scold. He crosses over and walks on, keeping as far as the width of the road allows.

Now, why? Jesus does not say. That is worth noticing, because it would have been so easy to say. He could have said that the priest was heartless, or greedy, or in a hurry for his dinner, and he says none of those things. He just says what the priest did. And so the people who have listened to this parable for two thousand years have had to guess.

One guess is very old, and it is the one that scholars usually give. The Law of Moses, in the book of Leviticus, says that a priest was not to make himself unclean by touching a dead body, except for his closest family. If you were a priest returning from, or going to, your duties, that was a serious matter, because it would stop you serving until you had been made clean again, and there was a whole process for that. And the man in the ditch was half dead. He might, for all the priest could tell, be wholly dead. A careful priest might say to himself, "I cannot take the risk."

I want to be fair to the priest, and I think Jesus is fair to him as well. He does not make him a monster. The rule he is keeping is a real rule, and a good one, from the Law of God. It is not unreasonable to think that he walked on with a heavy heart, and told himself that someone else would come, and that there was nothing he could do. We have all said things like that to ourselves. But I will also say, because I think it is true, that a rule about touching the dead is a very odd thing to be thinking about when a living man is lying by the road. And Jesus, who knew the Law better than any of them, does not tell us that this was a good reason. He tells us only what the man did, and he lets us feel the weight of it.

Notice, too, that this is only a guess, and Luke does not give it. Perhaps the priest was afraid. Perhaps the robbers were still about, and he thought that the man by the road might be a decoy, to draw a traveller close. That, too, has been suggested, and it is a sensible fear on a road like that one. We do not know. What we know is that he looked, and he crossed, and he went on.

And the sound of the sandals fades, going down the road.

The man in the gully hears them go. I do not know what he thinks. Jesus does not tell us, and I would rather not put words in his mouth, but I think we can imagine that he does not think anything for a good while. He lies there. A fly lands on his arm and he cannot lift it away.

The sun moves further. The shadows of the rocks, which were short, begin to lean across the road. And then, after a long time, there is another sound.

Another walker. Another pair of sandals. And a second figure comes round the bend.

Luke says, "So likewise a Levite, when he came to the place and saw him, passed by on the opposite side."

A Levite was a member of the tribe of Levi, which was the tribe that helped with the Temple: the singers, the gatekeepers, the people who looked after the building and its furniture and its music. They were not priests, who came only from the family of Aaron, but they were close to the holy things, and they had their own duties and their own turns. So the Levite is, in a way, a smaller version of the priest. He, too, is a religious person. He, too, has quite possibly come from the Temple. He, too, is respectable.

But I notice one difference in the telling, and it is very small. The priest saw the man and passed by. The Levite, Luke says, came to the place, and then saw him. The Levite comes closer. He actually walks up to the spot, and he looks, and then he too passes by on the other side. It is almost worse. The priest might not have seen how bad it was. The Levite came and looked, and went on.

I do not say that to be hard on him. I say it because I think Jesus is building a story the way a person builds a staircase, one step at a time, so that each step is a little higher than the last, and so that by the time we reach the top we are looking at something we did not expect. And I think everyone in that crowd, who had been listening so carefully, was now thinking the same thing. They were thinking, "Well, we know what comes next."

Because there was a way these stories went. When a storyteller in that country told you about a priest, and a Levite, there was a third person coming, and the third person was a layman, an ordinary Israelite, one of the people. The priest and the Levite are the professional religious men, and they have failed. So surely now comes a good, plain, ordinary Jew, one of ourselves, to set things right and to show up the hypocrisy of the grand people in Jerusalem. The crowd was ready for that. I think many of them were looking forward to it. They would have liked a story in which the common man was better than the priest.

And Jesus does the other thing.

"But a Samaritan," says Jesus, "while travelling, came to where he was."

And I think there must have been a little stir in the crowd. Because to them, a Samaritan was the last person you would expect, and the last person you would want.

I must explain this carefully, and kindly, because it is the heart of the story. The Samaritans were the people of the old northern country, around the hills of Samaria, between Judea in the south and Galilee in the north. They were cousins, in a way. They read the first five books of Moses and held them sacred, and they worshipped the God of Israel. But they had their own holy mountain, Mount Gerizim, where they offered worship, and they did not accept the Temple at Jerusalem or the rest of the Jewish Scriptures as the Jews did. There had been centuries of quarrelling over it, with burnt temples and bad feeling on both sides. The Gospel of John, in the story of the woman at the well, puts it in a single dry sentence: Jews do not share things in common with Samaritans. It was not a polite disagreement over the teacups. It was a real, old, bitter division, the kind that is passed from grandparents to children, and neither side forgot it.

So when Jesus says "a Samaritan," he is not saying, "and then a nice foreign gentleman." He is saying something closer to this: and then the very person that every man in this crowd has been taught to avoid. The one they do not trust. The one they would not eat with. The enemy, if you like, or near enough.

I do not think we can feel exactly how it landed. But if you have ever been surprised by who turned out to help you, you will have a little of it. If someone you had been told to dislike was the one who stopped, and everyone else with a good reputation went on, then you will understand what a story it was.

So the Samaritan comes round the bend. In our telling, he has a donkey, and the donkey is the first thing the man in the gully hears: a small clatter of hooves and the creak of a pack. I have decided that the donkey is not in a good mood. It has been walking for hours, and it does not see why it should stop. But it stops, because the man leading it has stopped.

Now, here is a thing I want you to hear. Luke tells us what the Samaritan did, and what he felt, and he puts the feeling first. Luke says, "when he saw him, he had compassion."

It is a strong word, the one that Luke uses. It comes from the Greek word for the inward parts, the stomach and the heart and the gut, the place where people feel things so hard that it hurts. It is the same word that Luke uses when Jesus meets the widow of Nain, burying her only son, and when the father sees his lost son coming home along the road. It is the word for a feeling that cannot sit still. It is not "he felt a little sorry." It is, if you like, "his insides turned over." And then, and only then, he acts.

And what he does, and I want you to listen to this slowly, is a very long list of small and practical things.

He goes to him. He does not cross to the other side. He goes up to him, and I imagine him crouching down in the dust, as you do when you want to be at the level of someone who has been hurt. He looks at the man's face, and sees that he is alive.

He takes out his own oil and wine. Travellers did carry both, in those days, in the same way that we might carry a little first-aid kit. The wine was a sort of antiseptic. It stung, but it cleaned. The oil was to soothe. In the book of the prophet Isaiah there is a line about wounds that have not been closed, or bandaged, or softened with oil, and so it seems that this was the usual way of looking after an injury, the best that people had. So the Samaritan pours on the wine, and the oil, and the man in the ditch gives a small groan, which is the best sound that he has made all day.

Then he bandages the wounds. And what does he use? Luke does not say. I have often wondered. A traveller cannot have had a great many bandages. I like to think that he tore up something of his own, a piece of his cloak or his spare tunic. That is only a guess, and it is mine, not Luke's. But it is the sort of thing people do.

Then he lifts him onto his own animal.

Think what that means. The man is a dead weight, a big man, perhaps, or a small one, in any case badly hurt and unable to help. The Samaritan is on a lonely road, in a hostile country, with robbers somewhere in the hills who might at any moment come back. He has already stopped for longer than a wise traveller would. And now he gets the man up onto the donkey, which does not want to be loaded, and the man cries out, and the Samaritan says something soothing, and the donkey says something rude in donkey. And then he walks. He has given up his seat, you see. He leads the donkey down the road himself, step by step, with his hand on the injured man to keep him from sliding off.

It is not a quick walk. The road still has some way to go. And I like to imagine the two of them going along it, the one on the donkey and the one beside it, not saying very much, because the man on the donkey cannot speak, and the man walking has nothing to say that is better than silence. There is the clop of hooves. There is the sound of breathing. The sun goes lower and the hills turn the colour of a lion, and a little wind comes up the valley from the Jordan, bringing the smell of water.

Luke says nothing about whether the Samaritan was afraid. I think he must have been. He would have known exactly how it looked. A Samaritan, alone, with a wounded Jew on his donkey, arriving at a Jewish inn on the road to a Jewish town. Anyone might ask what he had done to him. But he goes on.

The inn, when they reach it, is a long, low building around a yard. Travellers on that road would stop at such a place for the night: a courtyard where the animals were tied, a roof along one side where people could sleep on mats, some sort of kitchen, and a man in charge. It was not a hotel, as we would think of one. It was rougher. But it had a door, and a lamp, and a floor, and it was the best that the road had to offer.

Luke says, "he brought him to an inn, and took care of him."

I want you to take in that last phrase, because it is easy to hurry over. He took care of him. The Samaritan does not drop the man at the door and leave. He stays. He sits up with him through the night, I think, and he gives him water, a little at a time, and keeps him warm, and listens to him breathe. We are not told whether the man spoke, or when he woke, or what he said when he saw who had been looking after him. Luke leaves all that out. I wish he had told us, I have to admit. But the parable is not about the man in the bed. It is about the one who stopped.

Let us imagine, since we are allowed small inventions, a quiet scene in the grey of the early morning. The innkeeper comes in, a heavy, practical man who has seen a good many guests and a good many odd things. He looks at the man on the mat, now with bandaged wounds and a bit of colour in his face, and at the Samaritan beside him, who has not slept, and who is washing his hands in a bowl.

"He'll live?" says the innkeeper.

"I think so," says the Samaritan. "He'll need a good many days."

"Days cost money," says the innkeeper. He does not say it unkindly. It is a plain statement of fact, like the weather.

"I know," says the Samaritan.

All of that is our invention, you understand. Luke has no such talk. But it explains what comes next, which is Luke's.

The next day, Luke says, the Samaritan took out two denarii, gave them to the innkeeper, and said, "Take care of him; and whatever more you spend, I will repay you when I come back."

Let us look at that.

A denarius was a silver coin, and it was about a day's pay for a working man. We know that from another of Jesus' parables, in Matthew's Gospel, where the labourers in the vineyard agree with the owner on a denarius for the day. So two denarii is two days' wages. That is not an enormous sum, but it is not nothing either. In those days, two days' keep at an inn for a man who is being nursed is a fair share, and what's more it is the kind of sum that a person pays in advance when he intends to do the job properly.

And then there is the promise. "Whatever more you spend, I will repay you when I come back." He is leaving an open account. He does not know how much it will be. He does not ask the man in the bed for anything, or for his name, or what he can pay. He simply says that he will be back, and he will settle the bill, whatever it is.

I find that rather wonderful. He gives the innkeeper money for now, and his word for later. And the innkeeper, who has seen a good many travellers, must have thought very hard about whether to believe him. But I think he did. There was something about the man who had sat up all night.

And then the Samaritan picks up the rope of his donkey, and he goes on his way. The donkey, I imagine, is greatly relieved. And that is the last we hear of him. We do not know his name, or where he came from, or whether he ever came back to settle the bill. The parable simply stops, as a parable should. It has done what it came for.

Now we are back in the crowd. Jesus has finished. There is a pause, the kind of silence in which a lot of people are rearranging their thoughts. And the lawyer is standing there with his question still in his hand.

You will remember what the question was. "Who is my neighbour?" And remember what he wanted: a boundary, a nice neat line, so that he could be sure of being inside it.

Jesus has not answered him. Or not in those words. Look at what he does. He asks a question of his own, and it is a different question. He says: "Which of these three, do you think, was a neighbour to the man who fell among the robbers?"

Do you see what has happened? The lawyer asked, "Who is my neighbour?" meaning, who counts, who do I have to care about, where does the line go. And Jesus has turned it right round. He asks, "Who was a neighbour?" He has made the neighbour not the one who is helped, but the one who helps. The question is no longer about who qualifies for kindness. It is about who becomes kind.

And the lawyer, to his great credit, answers honestly. He says, "The one who showed him mercy."

I notice that he cannot quite bring himself to say the word Samaritan. It is a small thing. He says "the one who showed mercy," as if the word were still a little hard to get out. But he says it. He gives the true answer, and it costs him something to do so. I rather admire him for that. A man who had wanted to justify himself has been brought, gently, to say the very thing he did not expect to say.

And Jesus says to him, "Go and do likewise."

That is the end. Luke does not say what the lawyer did. He does not say whether he went away thoughtful, or cross, or changed. He leaves the man standing there, and the story ends with an instruction. Go, and do the same. And that, if you think about it, is what the parable has been aiming at all along. It was never meant to be a story we simply enjoy, and put back on the shelf. It is meant to send us out of the door.

And now let us let the evening come down.

The crowd goes home. The lawyer goes home, too, to wherever he lives, and I hope he slept. Somewhere on the road the light is going. The hills have turned from gold to grey to blue, and the first lamps are lit in the houses of Jericho. And in the inn on the road, the man on the mat is sleeping, with a clean bandage on his head and a cup of water within reach.

It is a story, of course. Nobody ever claimed that these people lived, or that this exact thing happened on that exact day. Jesus told it as a parable, as a story that is true in the way that stories are true. But it has been on the road ever since. It has been told in churches and schools and kitchens for two thousand years. People have named hospitals after the Samaritan. They have named those who stop to help a stranger after him, whether or not they know where the name comes from. And there is an old custom of pointing out, on that same steep road, a ruined inn, and calling it by his name. It is a much later building, and nobody pretends otherwise. But I think it says something, that people have wanted to find the inn, and to stand in it.

Very early on, the teachers of the Church began to hear another voice in the story. Origen, in the third century, passes on a reading he had from an older teacher: the man on the road is Adam, and every one of us, who has fallen from Jerusalem, the place of peace, into a hard and dangerous world. The robbers are the powers that harm us. The priest and the Levite are the Law and the prophets, which could point the way but could not heal. And the Samaritan is Christ himself, who came down to us, and bound our wounds, and carried us to the inn, which is the Church, and promised to return. Saint Augustine loved this reading, and preached it. It is a beautiful one, and the Fathers meant it with their whole hearts. But I should say clearly that it is a reading, a way of meditating on the story. It is not what Jesus says to the lawyer. To the lawyer he says, plainly, go and do likewise. And I think the two go together. If the Samaritan is Christ, then those who have been picked up and carried to the inn have been shown exactly what to do for the next person on the road.

That is a good thing to think about, as you lie in the dark. It is a thing I think we can all do in small ways. Not many of us will meet a man in a ditch. But most of us, tomorrow, will pass someone who needs a little help, and be able to choose between the near side of the road and the far side.

Let the lamps go down now. The donkey is asleep in the yard, with one ear twitching. The innkeeper is checking the door. Far up the road the stars are coming out over the hills, as they did that night and the night before, and the road is quiet, and it is safe, for the moment, and for tonight it belongs to nobody but you.

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the Gospel according to Luke, chapter ten, verses twenty-five to thirty-seven. It is Scripture in the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and in Protestant Bibles too. It appears only in Luke. It is a parable, which is to say a story Jesus made up in order to teach, and not a report of something that happened to a particular man, and so there are no names in it, and we gave it none.

What we have kept from the text: the lawyer standing up to test Jesus; his question about eternal life; the answer handed back, "What is written in the Law? How do you read it?"; the two commandments from Deuteronomy and Leviticus; "You have answered rightly; do this, and you will live"; the lawyer's wish to justify himself and his second question, "And who is my neighbour?"; the man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho; the robbers, who strip him, beat him and leave him half dead; the priest, who sees him and passes by on the other side; the Levite, who comes to the place, sees him and does the same; the Samaritan, who is moved with compassion; the oil and wine, the bandages, the animal, the inn and the care; the two denarii and the promise of repayment; Jesus's question, "Which of these three was a neighbour?"; the answer, "The one who showed him mercy"; and "Go and do likewise."

What we flagged as uncertain or as a reading. The reason the priest and the Levite pass by is not given by Luke. The reading that they feared to be made unclean by touching what might be a corpse rests on the rules in Leviticus chapter twenty-one, and many commentators offer it; others suggest fear of robbers, and some say that neither reason excuses them. We gave both and decided nothing. That many priests lived in Jericho is commonly said, and it is probable, but we have no list, and we gave it as a likelihood. The figures for the road, about seventeen miles and a fall of around a thousand metres, are approximate, and our own rounding. The denarius as a day's wage is taken from Matthew chapter twenty. The wine and oil as dressing for wounds, and the line in Isaiah chapter one, are background. The long Christian reading of the Samaritan as Christ is real and old, found in Origen and Augustine and many others, but it is a way of reading and not Luke's own explanation. And the old ruined inn shown on the Jericho road is a custom from long after the Gospel; we did not rely on it.

What we invented: the weather, the donkey and its opinions, the traveller's breakfast and his song, the priest's tiredness, what the Samaritan tore for bandages, the night he sat up, the talk with the innkeeper, and the evening at the end. None of it is in Luke. None of it changes what happens, or what it means. And we have told the beating as briefly as Luke does, because a bedtime story should not frighten anyone, and because this one is not about the beating at all. It is about who stops.

For further reading, Luke chapter ten is short. Read it slowly, in a quiet room, and notice that the last thing Jesus says is not an explanation but a sending.

That's the story of the Samaritan on the road to Jericho for tonight. It has no feast day of its own, but the Catholic Church reads it at Mass in the middle of the summer in one year of its three, and the Orthodox Church reads it in the weeks of Luke. Thank you for listening to Saints by Candlelight. If you enjoyed it, share it with someone who could use a gentle story at the end of a long day. Lord Jesus, who came down the long road to find us, give us eyes to see the one who is lying by the way, and the courage to cross over to their side. Bind up what is broken in us, and teach us to bind up what is broken in others. May the God who is merciful watch over you tonight, and keep you till the morning. Goodnight, and God bless.

Timothy and the Cloak Left at Troas

In what tradition holds is his last letter, Saint Paul, in a harder prison in Rome, writes to Timothy at Ephesus that only Luke is with him, that Mark is to be brought along, and that Timothy should come before winter and bring the cloak he left with Carpus at Troas, and the books, and above all the parchments. Told from the Second Letter to Timothy, with the journey itself imagined and labelled as imagined.

What's true in this story

Who
Saint Timothy, Saint Paul the Apostle, Saint Luke, Saint Mark, Saint Tychicus, Carpus of Troas, Demas, Lois and Eunice, and the people Paul greets and sends greetings from
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; the letter is Scripture for both; Timothy, Paul, Luke, Mark and Tychicus are honoured as saints in both, and Carpus is counted among the seventy disciples in Eastern tradition; Demas is not honoured as a saint, and we judge him no further than the letter does
Scripture and history
the letter is the Second Letter to Timothy, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture and the Church has always received as Saint Paul's last letter; Timothy's family, his joining Paul at Lystra and his waiting at Troas are in the Acts of the Apostles, chapters sixteen and twenty; the round travelling cloak is the Roman paenula; the closing of the sailing season is in Vegetius; parchment books as gifts are in Martial
Legend
that Timothy was the first bishop of Ephesus is an early tradition, recorded by Eusebius; that he later died at Ephesus trying to stop a pagan festival comes from a much later account and is labelled as such; whether Timothy reached Rome before Paul died is not known, and the episode says so

Feast day

It is late afternoon, in a house near the harbour of a town called Troas, on the coast of what is now Turkey.

A young-ish man, not quite as young as people keep telling him he is, is kneeling on the floor beside an old wooden chest. The owner of the house is holding up a lamp. And out of the chest, carefully, the young-ish man lifts a cloak.

It is a big, round, heavy thing, made of thick wool, with a hole in the middle for your head, so that when you put it on it falls all the way round you like a small tent. It smells of woodsmoke, and of the sea, and very faintly of sheep. There is a patch on one side, sewn by someone who was better at preaching than at sewing.

Under the cloak there are scrolls. And under the scrolls, wrapped in a cloth, there is a little stack of parchment books.

The man holding the lamp says, he left all of this here. Years ago. He said he'd be back for it.

Tonight is what comes after that. Or at least, what the Church has always believed comes after that. Because there is one more letter.

It is called the Second Letter to Timothy. It is short. You could read it aloud in about a quarter of an hour. And it is written from a different sort of prison. Not a rented flat with a wine shop downstairs. Somewhere harder, and darker, and harder to find. Paul says, in this letter, that he is chained like a criminal. He says a friend of his came to Rome and had to search for him, and search hard, before he found him. And he says, very simply, that he does not expect to come out.

But before we get to the letter, let me tell you about the man it was written to. Because Timothy is one of those people who is in the background of nearly everything, and almost never in the middle of it, and I think that is a shame.

Timothy came from a little town called Lystra, up on the high dry plateau in the middle of what is now Turkey. His mother was a Jewish woman called Eunice, and his grandmother was called Lois. We know their names because Paul mentions them, in tonight's letter, which tells you something about Paul. He had met the grandmother. He remembered.

His father was a Greek. We don't know his name, and we don't know whether he was still alive when the story begins. But the faith in that house came down through the women. Paul says that Timothy had known the holy Scriptures from childhood. The Greek word really means from when he was a baby. So you have to picture a little boy in a dusty town, a long way from Jerusalem, sitting on his grandmother's knee, hearing about Abraham, and Joseph, and David and the giant, from the old scrolls, or from the old woman's memory, which in some houses was the same thing.

And then one day, when Timothy was probably still quite young, two strangers came to Lystra. One of them was Paul, and the other was Barnabas. And it did not go well. First the people of the town decided they were gods, and tried to offer them a sacrifice, with garlands and an ox, which Paul and Barnabas found deeply embarrassing. And then, a little while later, after some people came from other towns and stirred up the crowd, the same people stoned Paul, and dragged him out of the town, and left him for dead.

He was not dead. His friends stood round him, and he got up, and walked back into the town. And the next day he left.

Timothy may well have seen that. He would have been a boy, or a youth. And in tonight's letter, Paul reminds him of it. You followed it all, he says, what happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, at Lystra. You know.

Then, a few years later, Paul came back. And by then Timothy had become a Christian, and so had his mother, and the believers in Lystra and the next town all said good things about him. And Paul asked him to come along.

And he did. For the rest of Paul's life.

He is there in Acts, being left behind in one city to look after things, and sent on ahead to another, and catching up again. He is named as the co-author at the top of six of Paul's letters. Paul and Timothy, servants of Christ Jesus. He was sent to Corinth when the Corinthians were being difficult, and to Thessalonica when the Thessalonians were being shaken by trouble, and to Philippi with news. Paul calls him my beloved and faithful child in the Lord. And to the Philippians he writes something I find very touching. I have no one else like him, he says. Like a son with his father, he has served with me in the gospel.

From the letters, we can also gather a few things about Timothy himself. He was probably a bit shy. Paul has to tell people to make him welcome and not to look down on him. He seems to have been younger than the people he was put in charge of, and to have felt it. Let no one despise your youth, Paul tells him. And he had trouble with his stomach. We know this because in the first letter Paul wrote to him, in the middle of some quite serious instructions about how to run a church, he suddenly says: stop drinking only water, and take a little wine, for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments.

I love that line. It is the most fatherly thing in the whole New Testament. Here is a great apostle, writing a letter that will be read in churches for two thousand years, and in the middle of it he is nagging his friend about his digestion.

At the time of tonight's story, Timothy was at Ephesus. That is the great city on the west coast of Turkey, with the huge theatre and the famous temple of Artemis, where the silversmiths once rioted because Paul was bad for the idol business. Paul had left Timothy there to look after the Christians, and to keep them from getting tangled up in strange ideas and endless arguments. Which, if you have ever been in charge of anything, you will know is a full-time job.

And one autumn, a letter came.

We don't know who brought it. But in the letter Paul says, almost in passing, I have sent Tychicus to Ephesus. Tychicus was an old friend, a man from that part of the world, who had carried letters for Paul before. So it is a good guess, and it is only a guess, that Tychicus brought this one too.

So let us imagine him arriving. Dusty from the road. Tired from the sea. Knocking at the door of the house where Timothy was staying. And Timothy opening it, and seeing who it is, and his face lighting up. And then seeing Tychicus's face, and the look on it, and going still.

Tychicus hands him the letter. It is a sheet of papyrus, folded and tied. And Timothy takes it over to the window, where the light is, and unrolls it, and reads.

Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God. To Timothy, my beloved child. Grace, mercy and peace.

And then: I thank God, whom I serve with a clear conscience, as my ancestors did, when I remember you constantly in my prayers, night and day. As I remember your tears, I long to see you, so that I may be filled with joy.

Your tears. Paul remembered that Timothy had cried, the last time they said goodbye. And he did not say it to embarrass him. He said it the way you say it to someone you love. I remember. I haven't forgotten.

And then: I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that lived first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice, and now, I am sure, lives in you.

Timothy, by the window, has to stop for a moment there.

And then he reads on. And here is a line which has been a comfort to a great many nervous people over the centuries, so if you are a nervous person, this one is for you.

Paul says: I remind you to stir up, to fan into flame, the gift of God that is in you through the laying on of my hands. For God did not give us a spirit of fear, but of power, and love, and self-control.

Fan into flame. Like a fire that has burned down low in the night, and you kneel down in the morning and blow on the embers, gently, until they catch again. Paul knew Timothy. He knew he was the kind of man whose fire burned low sometimes, out of shyness, and tiredness, and his stomach, and everyone telling him he was too young. And he did not tell him off for it. He told him to blow on the embers.

Do not be ashamed, Paul says, of the testimony about our Lord, or of me, his prisoner. Share in suffering for the gospel, by the power of God.

And he says that everyone in the province of Asia has turned away from him. He names two of them, Phygelus and Hermogenes, which is not much of a way to be remembered, if you think about it. Two thousand years, and the only thing anybody knows about you is that you weren't there.

But then he names someone who was. A man called Onesiphorus. He often refreshed me, Paul says, and he was not ashamed of my chain. When he arrived in Rome, he searched for me eagerly, and he found me. And you know very well, Paul adds, how much he did for us at Ephesus.

So Timothy knew Onesiphorus. Knew his house, probably, and his family. And now he learns that this friend from Ephesus went all the way to Rome and walked round the city, asking at prisons, asking soldiers, being told no, not here, try over there, until at last someone said, yes, that one, he's down here. Then Paul starts giving Timothy advice. Some of it is about how to run a church, and some of it is about how to be a person, and they turn out to be quite a lot of the same advice.

Be strong, he says. What you heard from me, pass on to faithful people who will be able to teach others. Be like a soldier, who doesn't get tangled up in other business. Be like an athlete, who wins by keeping the rules. Be like a farmer, who works hard and gets the first share of the crops.

Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead. That is my gospel, Paul says. That is the thing I am suffering for, wearing chains like a criminal.

And then he says: but the word of God is not chained.

I think that is one of the bravest sentences anybody has ever written. A man sitting in the dark, with iron on his wrists, and he says, well, they've chained me. But they can't chain that.

And he gives Timothy a little saying, which may have been a hymn, or something the Christians said together. Here is a trustworthy saying, he writes.

If we have died with him, we will also live with him.

If we endure, we will also reign with him.

If we deny him, he will also deny us.

If we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself.

That last line is the one to hold on to, at night. If we are faithless, he remains faithful. Not because we are good at being faithful. Because he is.

There is more. There are warnings about people who will only want to hear what they like, people with itching ears, Paul calls them, which is a very good description. I imagine Timothy putting the letter down on the window ledge, at that point. And looking out of the window for quite a while, at the street, and the people going up and down it, and the light changing on the walls.

And then picking it up again. Because it wasn't finished.

Do your best to come to me soon, he says.

The young man who once ran home, and the old man who once said never again. That is how it ends between them. Bring Mark.

When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas. And the books. And above all, the parchments.

Now, people have been thinking about that one sentence for a very long time.

Saint John Chrysostom, the great preacher, preaching on this letter about three hundred years later, stopped on it. He tells his congregation that the word may mean a garment, or, as some people said, a sort of bag that the books were carried in. Then he asks a very good question. What did a man who was about to go to God want with books? And his answer is that Paul wanted to put them into the hands of the faithful, so that they would have them after he was gone, in place of his teaching. And the cloak, Chrysostom says, he sent for so that he would not have to take one from somebody else. Paul had always been very particular about not being a burden to anyone. Even now, it seems, he would rather have his own old coat brought two seas than be given a new one.

The word Paul uses is phailonēs, which is the Greek way of saying the Latin word paenula. And a paenula was the ordinary travelling cloak of the Roman world. Round, heavy, usually of thick wool, with a hole for the head, and sometimes a hood, so that it kept off the rain and the wind and doubled as a blanket at night. Travellers wore them. Soldiers wore them. Cart drivers wore them. It was not a fine thing. It was the sort of thing you would leave behind in somebody's house in the summer, because it was too hot and too heavy to carry, and say, I'll pick it up next time I'm passing.

And now it was going to be winter, in a stone prison, in Rome. And he wanted his coat.

The books are probably scrolls. Papyrus scrolls, rolled up. We don't know what they were. Some people guess they were scrolls of the Scriptures, the Law and the Prophets and the Psalms. That seems very likely to me, from a man who had just written that all Scripture is breathed out by God, but it is a guess.

And the parchments. Paul uses a Latin word there too, membranae, borrowed straight into Greek. In those days people had started making little books out of parchment, which is very thin, very carefully prepared animal skin. You folded the sheets and stitched them together, rather like a modern notebook. People used them for notes and drafts, and sometimes for whole books. A Roman poet of a few years later, Martial, wrote little verses to go with presents, and some of the presents are copies of Homer and Virgil in parchment, small enough to carry on a journey.

What was in Paul's parchments? Nobody knows. Perhaps Scripture. Perhaps his own notes. Perhaps copies of his letters. Perhaps something he was still working on. He doesn't say. He just says, above all, the parchments.

And he adds, a few lines later, as if he had just thought of it: Do your best to come before winter.

There was a reason for that, beyond the cold. In the Roman world, the sea closed in winter. Not officially, quite, but nobody sensible sailed. A Roman writer called Vegetius, writing a few hundred years later about military matters, gives the dates the old sailors kept. From about the eleventh of November until about the tenth of March, he says, the sea is shut. If Timothy didn't get across before then, he would be stuck on the wrong side of it until spring.

And spring, both of them knew, might be too late.

Somewhere along the way he had to find Mark. Paul just says, get Mark. He doesn't say where. And so, in our telling, and this is purely ours, Timothy has sent a message ahead, and Mark is waiting for him at Troas.

Troas was a port. It sat on the coast a little way south of where the old city of Troy had been, looking out across the water towards the islands and, beyond them, Europe. If you wanted to get from Asia to Greece, this was where you got on a boat.

And Timothy knew it well. This was where it had all started, for him, and for a great many other people. They pray, and they cry, and they give the two travellers far too much food, and a purse of money for the apostle, and a pair of thick socks.

And then, in the morning, the long road.

The Romans had built a great highway straight across the top of Greece, from the Aegean Sea on one side to the Adriatic on the other. It was called the Via Egnatia. It ran for hundreds of miles, over mountains and across plains and round the edges of lakes, paved in stone, with a milestone every Roman mile, so you always knew exactly how far you had come and exactly how far you still had to go, which is either encouraging or not, depending on the day.

The road went through Thessalonica. Which is where Demas had gone.

I don't know what Timothy thought, walking through Thessalonica. In our telling he does not go looking for Demas. That isn't what he has been asked to do. But I think he would have prayed for him, walking through those streets. Paul had said of the people who deserted him: may it not be held against them. Timothy would have known what Paul meant by that.

And on, west. Through the autumn hills. The leaves turning. The mornings misty. Shepherds bringing their flocks down off the high pastures. The two of them walking, mile after mile, with the big cloak rolled on top of Timothy's bag by day, and spread over the pair of them by night, in barns and inns and once, it may be, under a hedge.

Every few days, at an inn, there would be someone coming the other way, from the coast. And Timothy would ask, are the ships still sailing? And they would say, a few. Not for long.

On the far side of the mountains, the road comes down to the Adriatic, to the port of Dyrrachium. And from there, the ships went across to Italy, to the port of Brundisium, on the heel of the boot. About a hundred miles of water.

We are, in our telling, well into November now. The wind has changed. The sea is darker. The harbour at Dyrrachium is full of ships that have been pulled up and are not going anywhere until March. And for one long day, Timothy and Mark go up and down the quays, asking every captain they can find. Are you crossing? No. Are you crossing? Not likely, in this. Are you crossing? Maybe. Tomorrow. If the wind drops. And it'll cost you.

And they pay it. With the Philippians' money, I expect, which Timothy will apologise to the apostle about later.

The crossing is rough. I won't make it frightening. It is bedtime. Let us just say that it is grey and loud and very wet, and that Timothy is sick over the side twice, and that Mark holds onto the back of his belt both times. And that the big round cloak, pulled over both their heads, keeps most of the sea off. And that in the evening, the coast of Italy comes up out of the dark, and the little lights of the town of Brundisium.

And then the last part. The Appian Way. The great stone road to Rome. More than three hundred miles of it, from the heel of Italy up to the city.

This is the road Paul walked, the first time, with a soldier beside him. You heard about it last time. When the Christians of Rome heard he was coming, some of them walked out as far as a little market town called the Forum of Appius to meet him, and some came as far as a place called the Three Taverns. And Luke says that when Paul saw them, he thanked God, and took courage.

So let us leave Timothy and Mark on that road.

It is evening, and it is cold, and they have been walking all day. They are not far from the Three Taverns. There are tombs along the side of the road, the way there always were outside Roman cities, and cypress trees, and in the distance, the low hills where Rome is. The light is going. The stones of the road are pale in the dusk.

Timothy has the cloak on. Mark has the books. And the parchments are in Timothy's bag, wrapped in their oiled leather, held against his side.

And they are both thinking the same thing, and neither of them says it, which is: are we in time?

And here, I am going to stop. Because this is where our imagining ends, and this is where the Scriptures stop too.

We don't know whether Timothy reached Rome. We don't know whether he saw Paul again, or brought him his cloak, or read to him from the parchments in the dark. Nothing in the Bible tells us. The last thing we hear from Paul is that line. Grace be with you.

There is one more small thing, though. At the very end of the Letter to the Hebrews, which was written by someone whose name we do not know for certain, there is a sentence that says: you should know that our brother Timothy has been set free. If he comes soon, I will see you with him.

So at some point, Timothy was in prison himself. And came out. We don't know when, or where, or why. Some people have wondered whether he was arrested when he came to Rome, for being the friend of a man in chains. It would fit. But nobody knows. It is a wondering, not a fact.

What the Church remembers is this. The historian Eusebius, writing in the time of the Emperor Constantine, records the tradition that Timothy was the first bishop of Ephesus. That he went back, in other words. That he went home to the church Paul had left in his care, and looked after it, for the rest of his life. Which seems very like him.

And there is a much later story, from long after, which says that when he was an old man, there was a rowdy pagan festival in the streets of Ephesus, with masks and clubs and a great deal of drinking, and Timothy went out into the street to plead with the crowd to stop. And they turned on him. And so he went home to God. It is a late story, and we cannot lean on it very hard. But it would not be out of character for a shy man who had been told, a long time ago, that God had not given him a spirit of fear.

Saint Paul, the Church remembers, died in Rome under the Emperor Nero, outside the city, near the road that runs down to the sea at Ostia. And he lies there still, under the great church called Saint Paul Outside the Walls.

And the cloak?

Well. Here is a thing I find very lovely. That round Roman travelling cloak, the paenula, the one with the hole in the middle for your head, did not go out of fashion in the Church. As the centuries went by, ordinary Roman clothes slowly became the clothes that clergy wore at the altar, long after everybody else had stopped wearing them. And the historians of church vestments say that the big round outer garment that a priest wears to celebrate the liturgy grew out of that same ordinary cloak. In the West it became the chasuble. In the East it is still called the phelonion, from the very word that Paul used.

So every Sunday, all over the world, in East and West, priests go up to the altar wrapped in a thing that began as a traveller's coat. Not Paul's coat. Nobody is saying that. Just the same kind of coat. The kind you leave with a friend in the summer, and send for in the autumn, when it starts to get cold.

And the letters are still here. Paul's, and the one to Timothy among them. And the line about the cloak, which Saint John Chrysostom wondered about, is still in it. I think he was right, that it is there to show us how little Paul had. But I think it is also there for another reason. To show us that the man who fought the good fight and finished the race also got cold, and wanted his books, and missed his friends. And asked for them. And was not ashamed to ask.

Do your best to come before winter.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Letter to Timothy, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and which the Church has always received as Saint Paul's last letter, written from his final imprisonment in Rome. Many modern scholars think the letter may have been written by a follower of Paul, in his name, after his death. The Church has always read it as Paul's, and we have told it that way. Everything the letter says is kept, in our own plain rendering, shortened in places, with nothing added.

Timothy's family, his joining Paul at Lystra, the stoning at Lystra, the vision at Troas, and the night with Eutychus are from the Acts of the Apostles. The little wine for his stomach and let no one despise your youth are from the First Letter to Timothy. Our brother Timothy set free is from the Letter to the Hebrews.

That Tychicus carried the letter is a guess. The round cloak is the Roman paenula. Chrysostom's thoughts on the cloak and the books are from his tenth homily on this letter.

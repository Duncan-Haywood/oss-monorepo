Aaron and Hur and the Hands Held Up at Rephidim

Amalek attacks Israel in the desert at Rephidim; young Joshua leads the fight in the valley while old Moses stands on the hill with the staff of God, and when his arms grow too heavy, his brother Aaron and his friend Hur sit him on a stone and hold his hands up, one on each side, until the sun goes down. Told from the Book of Exodus, chapter seventeen, verses eight to sixteen.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Moses, Aaron, Hur, Joshua son of Nun, the people of Israel, and Amalek
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Book of Exodus as Scripture; Moses is honoured as a saint and prophet in both traditions, Aaron the priest among the holy forefathers, and Joshua as a righteous man and prophet; Hur has no feast day that we know of
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Book of Exodus, chapter seventeen, verses eight to sixteen, told straight in a plain rendering; the book does not say how big either side was, how long the fighting lasted beyond one day until sunset, which hill it was, or where exactly Rephidim lay, so none of that is guessed
Legend
that Hur was the husband of Miriam, the sister of Moses, is a Jewish tradition reported by the historian Josephus, not something the Book of Exodus says, and the episode labels it so; the early Christian reading of the raised arms as a picture of the cross is told as how the Church Fathers read the passage, not as something Moses knew
What we invented
the conversations between Moses, Aaron, Hur and Joshua beyond what the book reports, the choosing of the stone, the shade, the water skin, the position of the sun through the day and what the three men said and thought on the hill are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; nothing is added to what happens in the battle or to what the Lord says
Feast day
Moses the prophet is remembered on the fourth of September in the Orthodox Church and in the Roman Martyrology, and Joshua son of Nun on the first of September in the Orthodox Church; we give no calendar date for Aaron or Hur that we cannot vouch for

The staff was not a magic wand. Moses was not a wizard, casting a spell from a safe distance. All through the Bible, when people pray, they lift up their hands. The psalms say, I lift up my hands towards your holy place. Let the lifting up of my hands be an evening sacrifice. Many Christian readers, from very early on, have read Amalek in another way too. As a picture of the things that come for us when we are tired. The things that attack the stragglers inside us, the weak and worn out parts at the back, when we have had a long day and our prayers are heavy and our hands keep drooping. And Moses had stood on the hill all day long, with his arm raised and a pole in his hand, so that everyone in the valley could see it. And now he says, it was never me that was the banner. It was the Lord. The Lord is my banner.

The last verse of the passage is a hard one to translate. The Hebrew is unusual, and the people who know it best are not sure what it means. Some read it as, a hand upon the banner of the Lord. After that, Hur slips quietly out of the story. The book does not tell us how his life ended. We meet his name once or twice more, as the grandfather of Bezalel, the craftsman, if that is the same Hur. And then nothing. A man who held up a hand, on one afternoon, and then went home.

Aaron went on to become the first high priest of Israel, and to lift up his own hands over the people, many times, to bless them. He did not always get things right. But he died an old man on a mountain called Hor, at the edge of the promised land, and the whole people mourned him for thirty days.

Moses, as you probably know, never crossed the Jordan himself. He saw the promised land from the top of another mountain, Mount Nebo, at the very end of his life, and went home to God there.

And Joshua, who looked up the hill all that long day at Rephidim, was the one who led the people across. He lived to be very old, and at the end of his life he gathered the whole people together and said, choose this day whom you will serve. As for me and my house, we will serve the Lord.

The Church Fathers, the great teachers of the first Christian centuries, loved this story. Saint Justin Martyr, who lived about eighteen hundred and fifty years ago, noticed two things about it.

He noticed that the young man fighting in the valley had a name which, in Greek, is exactly the same as the name of Jesus.

And he noticed the shape that Moses made, on that hill, with his arms held out on either side. And he said that it was the shape of the cross. That the people won, not because Moses prayed, only, but because Moses prayed in that shape, with his arms outstretched. Another very early Christian writing, the Epistle of Barnabas, says much the same thing.

Moses could not have known that, of course. He just knew that his arms were heavy, and that his brother and his friend were holding them up. But Christians reading the story many centuries later looked at that picture, of a man on a hill, with his arms stretched out, held there until the going down of the sun, while the people below were saved, and they could not help thinking of another hill, and another afternoon.

So the next time you see a priest standing at the altar with his arms held out, or the next time you lift up your own hands to pray, you might think of three old men on a hilltop in the desert.

And the next time you see someone who is tired, someone whose hands are heavy, someone who has been holding something up all by themselves for much too long, you might think of Aaron and Hur. Who did not fight in the battle. Who did not make any speeches. Who are mostly remembered for one thing.

They found a stone. They sat him down. And they held his hands up, one on one side, and one on the other, until the sun went down.

The meaning of the last verse is uncertain in the Hebrew, and we have said so.

The conversations on the hill, the swapping of hands, the choosing of the stone, the grips and the jokes, and Joshua's night of choosing men are our own invention.

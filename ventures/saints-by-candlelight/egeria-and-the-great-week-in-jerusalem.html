Egeria and the Great Week in Jerusalem

Back in Jerusalem, the pilgrim Egeria writes down for her sisters at home how the whole city keeps the week before Easter, which they call the Great Week, from the walk down the Mount of Olives with palm and olive branches to the long night of candles in Gethsemane and the Easter vigil. Told from chapters twenty-nine to thirty-nine of her own travel letter, with the Gospels told straight.

It is the middle of the night, on a steep hillside just outside a city, and the hillside is moving.

Or rather, it is covered with people, thousands of them, and every one of them is walking downhill, very slowly, in the dark. Here and there among them are lights. Tall church candles, two hundred of them and more, held up high so that the old and the tired can see where to put their feet. The candles make a river of little flames, winding down between the olive trees towards a garden at the bottom of the hill.

Nobody is talking. Some people are singing, quietly. Some are crying. A small boy is asleep on his grandmother's shoulder, with an olive branch from last Sunday still clutched in his fist.

And near the middle of the river of lights, a woman from the far west of the world is trying very hard to remember every single thing she sees, because her sisters at home will want to know exactly how it was done.

If you have been with us for the last few weeks, you know Egeria very well by now.

If you haven't, here is all you need. Egeria was a woman from the far west of the Roman Empire, very probably from the north-west corner of Spain, though we cannot be quite sure, because the beginning of her letter is lost. Some time around the year three hundred and eighty, she travelled east to see the places in the Bible with her own eyes. She lived in Jerusalem for about three years, and from there she went on journey after journey. Up Mount Sinai. Up Mount Nebo. North to Edessa and Harran. And she wrote it all down, in a long, chatty, endlessly polite letter to a group of women at home, whom she calls her sisters, and her ladies, and sometimes, when she is very fond of them, her souls.

Only part of the letter survives, in a single old copy found in a library in Arezzo, in Italy, in eighteen eighty-four. And the letter has two halves.

The first half is the travel diary. Mountains, mules, bishops, wells. That is the half we have been following on the last few nights.

The second half is something different. It is a description, the most detailed one that has come down to us from that whole century, of how the church in Jerusalem prayed. What they did every morning and every evening. What they did on Sundays. What they did at Epiphany, and in Lent, and above all in the week before Easter. She wrote it, she says, because she knew her sisters would want to know. And she was right. Her sisters wanted to know. And so, it turns out, have liturgists, historians and curious Christians of every kind, ever since that old copy came to light.

So tonight we are not going anywhere. Tonight we are staying in Jerusalem, for one week. The week, Egeria says, that they call there the Great Week.

First, let me show you round, because Egeria assumes her sisters know nothing about the place, and she is quite right to.

Jerusalem in the three hundred and eighties was a city of about the size of a large market town today. It sat on its hills, with its walls round it, and on the east side, across a deep valley, rose the Mount of Olives. The emperor Constantine had become a Christian some sixty years before, and he had built churches at the holy places, very grand ones, and pilgrims had been coming ever since. From Egypt and Syria, from Greece and Italy, from Gaul and Spain. Egeria was one of thousands.

Inside the city, near the western edge, was the great group of buildings round the place where Christ was crucified and buried. And I want you to picture it, because we are going to spend a great deal of the week there.

At one end, the western end, was a great round church, built over the tomb itself. They called it the Anastasis, which is the Greek word for resurrection. In the middle of it, behind a rail, was the cave of the tomb.

Then, going east, you came out into a big open courtyard, paved, with a roof of sky. At one corner of the courtyard was a rock, sticking up, with a cross on top of it. That was Golgotha, the place of the Crucifixion. Egeria simply calls it the Cross. When she says the people went "before the Cross", she means into the courtyard in front of it. When she says they went "behind the Cross", she means a little chapel tucked in on the far side of the rock.

And then, east again, was the biggest building of all, a great basilica with columns and marble and gold, which they called the Martyrium, the place of witness, because it stood beside the place where the Lord had suffered. Egeria also calls it simply the great church.

So: the tomb, the courtyard with the rock, and the great church. Remember those three, and you will never get lost.

Then, across the valley, on the Mount of Olives, there were two more holy places. Halfway up was a church built over a cave, where, they said, the Lord used to sit and teach his disciples. It was called the Eleona, from the Greek word for olives. And at the very top was the place they called the Imbomon, the place on the summit from which, they said, the Lord had ascended into heaven.

And down at the bottom of the mountain, by the valley, was the garden. Gethsemane.

And that is the map. You will need it.

The other thing you need to know is that the church in Jerusalem had a great many services, and Egeria went to all of them. All of them. There was a service before dawn, at cockcrow, and a service at dawn, and services at the third hour and the sixth hour and the ninth hour, which is to say at about nine, at noon and at three in the afternoon, and a service at the lighting of the lamps in the evening, which they called the lucernare. The monks and the nuns went to every one. The ordinary people went to as many as they could. And the bishop came to most of them, and at the end of each one he said a prayer, and blessed everybody, and then every single person came up, one by one, to have the bishop's hand laid on them as they went out.

I mention this because I want you to understand how much walking and standing there was in this city. And that was in an ordinary week. The Great Week was not an ordinary week.

Now, for our story tonight, let us give Egeria some company.

Egeria does not tell us who she stood next to at the services, and so I am going to make it up, and I am telling you now that I am making it up. Let us say that there was an old woman called Maro. Maro lived in a village a little way out from the city, and she had walked in for Holy Week every year since she was a girl, which was a very long time ago. She spoke Syriac, the language of the countryside, and almost no Greek. And this year she had brought her grandson, a small boy called Yohannan, who was five, and who had never been to the city before, and who thought it was the most wonderful place in the whole world.

Egeria, of course, spoke Latin. And Greek a little, by now. And Syriac not at all.

You might think this would be a problem. But this is one of my favourite things in the whole letter. Egeria tells her sisters that in the Jerusalem church the bishop always spoke in Greek, even though he knew Syriac. And so beside him, always, there stood a priest, who put everything the bishop said into Syriac, so that everyone could understand. And for the people like Egeria, who knew only Latin, there were brothers and sisters, she says, who knew both Greek and Latin, and who explained it all to them in Latin. So that nobody, she says, was left out.

Picture it. A crowd of a thousand people, and the bishop speaking, and a priest repeating it in another language, and here and there in the crowd, little knots of people, with a helpful neighbour murmuring a third language into their ears. It must have sounded like a beehive. I think it is lovely.

So let us say that Maro and Egeria met in the crowd, one evening in Lent, and could not say a single word to each other, and got on wonderfully all the same. Maro would point at things. Egeria would nod. Yohannan would tug at Egeria's sleeve and tell her very long stories in Syriac, and Egeria would say "really?" in Latin, in all the right places, and he seemed perfectly satisfied with that.

The Great Week began, as it still does in the Orthodox Church today, with the Saturday before. Lazarus Saturday.

On that Saturday, Egeria tells us, everyone went out to Bethany, about two miles from the city. On the way, about half a mile before the village, there was a little church at the place where Mary, the sister of Lazarus, ran out to meet the Lord. And there they stopped, and a hymn was sung, and the Gospel was read.

Here is that Gospel, told straight, from the eleventh chapter of John.

Lazarus of Bethany was ill. His sisters, Martha and Mary, sent word to Jesus, "Lord, the one you love is ill." And Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. But when he heard, he stayed where he was two more days. And when he came, Lazarus had already been in the tomb four days. Martha went out to meet him, and Mary stayed in the house, until Martha came back and said to her quietly, "The Teacher is here, and is calling for you." And Mary got up quickly and went out to him, and fell at his feet, and said, "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." And when Jesus saw her weeping, and the friends who had come with her weeping too, he was deeply moved. And he asked, "Where have you laid him?" And they said, "Lord, come and see." And Jesus wept.

Then he went to the tomb, which was a cave with a stone across it, and he said, "Take away the stone." And he lifted up his eyes and thanked his Father. And then he cried out in a loud voice, "Lazarus, come out!" And the dead man came out, his hands and feet bound with strips of cloth, and his face wrapped. And Jesus said, "Unbind him, and let him go."

They read that, at the place where Mary ran out. And then they went on into the village, to the church at the tomb of Lazarus, which they called the Lazarium, and it was so full, Egeria says, that the whole field round it was full of people too.

And there, at the end, a priest stood up and announced Easter. That is, he told everyone, from the Gospel, that six days before the Passover, Jesus came to Bethany, where Lazarus was, whom he had raised from the dead. And so, from that day, everyone knew the Great Week had begun.

Let us imagine Yohannan, on that Saturday, standing very close to the cave of the tomb, and looking into the dark, and asking his grandmother something in a very small voice. And then, at the eleventh hour, about five o'clock, as the sun began to sink behind the city across the valley, the deacon read the Gospel. And this is the one everybody was waiting for.

Here it is, told straight, from the twenty-first chapter of Matthew.

Jesus and his disciples were coming up to Jerusalem, and when they came to the Mount of Olives, he sent two of the disciples into a village to bring a donkey and her colt. And as soon as that Gospel was read, Egeria tells us, the bishop got up, and everybody got up, and they all set off, on foot, down the Mount of Olives, towards the city. And all the children of the place, she says, every last one of them, even the ones too small to walk, who had to be carried on their parents' shoulders, all of them carried branches. Some had palm branches, and some had olive branches. And so the bishop was brought down the mountain, she says, in the same way that the Lord had been brought down it.

Yohannan, of course, had been waiting for this all afternoon. Let us imagine that somebody had handed him an olive branch at the top of the hill, a branch nearly as tall as he was, and that he refused, absolutely refused, to let his grandmother carry it, and that he walked the first part of the way down holding it straight up in front of him like a soldier with a spear, and singing the only bit of the hymn he knew, which was "Blessed is he," over and over again, very loudly and slightly behind everybody else.

And let us imagine that, about a third of the way down, he got very quiet, and his grandmother picked him up and put him on her shoulders, and he went on waving the branch from up there, more slowly, like a little flag.

And now here is the detail in this part of the letter that I love best of all.

Egeria says that they went down, from the top of the mountain to the city, and right through the city to the Anastasis, all on foot, the whole way. Even the great ladies, she says, and the great gentlemen, who would normally never be seen walking anywhere. And they went, she says, slowly. Slowly, slowly. So that the people would not get tired. And so it was already evening when they came to the Anastasis.

Slowly, slowly. In Latin it is lente et lente. I have read a lot of descriptions of grand processions, and they nearly always tell you how splendid they were. Egeria tells you how slow it was. And why. So that nobody would be left behind. A procession that goes at the pace of the oldest grandmother and the smallest child. I think that may be the most Christian thing in the whole letter.

And when they came to the Anastasis, even though it was late, they still had the evening service, the lucernare, with all the lamps lit, and then a prayer at the Cross, and then the people were sent home.

Yohannan, let us imagine, was asleep on his grandmother's shoulder long before they reached the gate. But he did not let go of the branch.

Monday and Tuesday, Egeria says, were much like any other day, except that the afternoon service in the great church went on from three o'clock until well into the evening.

But on Tuesday night, when all that was done, everyone went out of the city again, across the valley and up the Mount of Olives, to the church at the cave, the Eleona. And the bishop went right into the cave, the cave where, they said, the Lord used to teach his disciples. That is the long chapter where the disciples sat with Jesus on the Mount of Olives and asked him about the end of all things. And he began by saying to them, "Take care that no one leads you astray." And he told them of wars and rumours of wars, and that they should not be alarmed. And he told them that heaven and earth would pass away, but his words would not pass away. And that no one knows the day or the hour. And that they should keep awake, therefore, and be ready.

Read on the Mount of Olives, at night, in the cave, by the bishop, standing. And then, Egeria says, a prayer, and a blessing, and everybody went home. Very late.

On Wednesday, it was the same as Monday. But at the end, at night, at the Anastasis, a priest went in behind the rail, and read the passage of the Gospel in which Judas Iscariot went to the chief priests and agreed with them what they would pay him to hand the Lord over to them.

Matthew tells it in three sentences. One of the twelve, who was called Judas Iscariot, went to the chief priests and said, "What will you give me if I hand him over to you?" And they paid him thirty pieces of silver. And from that moment he began to look for an opportunity.

And when that passage was being read, Egeria says, there was such moaning and groaning from all the people, that no one could hear it without being moved to tears.

I find that very striking. They were not hearing anything new. They had heard it every year of their lives. And still, every year, when it came to the moment when a friend sold his friend, the whole church groaned out loud. As if they were hearing it for the first time. As if it were happening to someone they knew. Which, of course, they believed it was.

Let us imagine that Yohannan, who did not understand the Greek, or the Syriac very well either, turned round and looked up at all the grown-ups groaning, and was very worried, and that Egeria, who could not explain anything to him in any language he spoke, simply knelt down and gave him a piece of honey cake from her bag. And that he ate it, and felt better, and that this was, for the rest of the week, a sort of arrangement between them.

And then came Thursday.

On Thursday the people gathered in the great church earlier than usual, at the eighth hour, about two o'clock, because, Egeria says, the dismissal had to be done sooner that day. And there the Eucharist was offered. And then, before everyone left, the archdeacon stood up again, and said: at the first hour of the night, let us meet at the church on the Eleona. Because great toil lies ahead of us tonight.

Great toil. He was not exaggerating.

But first, Egeria says, they all went round to the little chapel behind the Cross, behind the rock of Golgotha, and there the bishop offered the Eucharist again, and everyone received communion. And there they stayed until about eleven o'clock at night, singing hymns and antiphons suitable to the place and the day, Egeria says, with readings in between, and prayers. And they read the Gospel passages in which the Lord spoke to his disciples on that night, sitting in that very cave.

And at about midnight they went up, with hymns, to the very top of the mountain, to the Imbomon. And there they stayed, with more hymns and readings and prayers, until the cocks began to crow.

Here, I think, we must pause for a moment and think about what this meant. These people had worked all day, or prayed all day, which in Jerusalem in Holy Week was very nearly the same thing. They had walked across the valley and up a mountain. And now they were going to stay up all night. All of them. Not just the monks. Egeria is quite clear about this. Old and young, she says, rich and poor, everyone was there. And on this one night, she says, nobody left before the morning.

And let us imagine that Egeria sat down next to her, on the cold ground, because she was not, any more, quite as young as she had been when she climbed Mount Sinai either.

And above them, in the dark, were the stars. The same stars, they must have thought, that had been above this hill on that night.

At cockcrow, they came down from the summit, singing, to a place a little way down the mountain. And here there was a church, which Egeria calls an elegant church, very fine, built at the place where the Lord prayed.

You remember. It is in the twenty-second chapter of Luke. After the supper, Jesus went out, as was his custom, to the Mount of Olives, and the disciples followed him. And while they lay on the table, the bishop, sitting, held the ends of the holy wood firmly in his hands. And the deacons who stood round kept watch.

Now. Why, you may be asking, the guard? Why did the bishop hold on so tightly, and why did the deacons stand round so watchfully, if everybody was only coming to kiss it?

Somebody bit it.

I think we can all agree that this is a very bad idea, and also that we can all, if we are honest, picture exactly the kind of person who would do it. Someone who had come a very long way. Someone who wanted, more than anything, to take a little piece of this home. Someone who leaned down to kiss it and then, at the very last moment, had a terrible thought. It is not the sort of thing that happens, I think, to people who do not care. It happens to people who care far too much, in completely the wrong way. Their bishop, if it was Cyril, already knew how much people longed for even a splinter. Years earlier, in those same lectures to the people preparing for baptism, he had said that the whole world had by now been filled with pieces of the wood of the Cross, carried off from that very place. He says it as a sign of how far the faith had spread.

And here I must add, as Egeria does not, one note about this wood. In later years a story was told, and you may well know it, that the empress Helena, Constantine's mother, had found the Cross herself, buried in Jerusalem. That story is first written down a few years after Egeria was there, by Saint Ambrose and others, and it grew in the telling. Egeria does not tell it. It was kissed, with tears, because of whose it was. And everyone at that table knew that the Lord who had hung on it was not in the box. He was risen.

There is no one, she says, old or young, who does not weep, on that day, for those three hours, more than you could imagine. Because the Lord suffered all this for us.

Let us imagine that Maro was one of the older ones. And Egeria, I think, after the night she had just had, may have been one of them too.

Every year, in churches all over the world, in the East and in the West, people carry palms and branches on the Sunday before Easter, and children wave them, and somebody sings "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord." Tonight's story comes from the second half of Egeria's Travels, the long Latin letter written by a pilgrim woman to her sisters at home, probably in the early three hundred and eighties, and found in a single incomplete copy in Arezzo in eighteen eighty-four. The Saturday at Bethany is in chapter twenty-nine, the Great Week in chapters thirty to thirty-eight, and the Easter octave in chapter thirty-nine. The bishop's Greek, with a priest putting it into Syriac and helpers explaining in Latin, is in chapter forty-seven, and so is the cheering in Easter week. The archdeacon's announcements, the slow walk down the Mount of Olives with the children carrying palm and olive, the cave on Tuesday night, the groaning at the reading about Judas, the offering behind the Cross, the night on the mountain, the two hundred candles, reaching the gate when people could just recognise one another, the bishop sending everyone home to sit down for a while, the chair and the table on Golgotha, the deacons on guard against another bite, the ring and the horn, the three hours of readings and the newly baptised taken first to the tomb, are all in her letter. That the wood was the true Cross, the ring Solomon's and the horn the kings', and that each holy place was the very spot, are the traditions of the Jerusalem church in her day, told as traditions.

Patrick and the Ship That Was Ready

A sixteen-year-old farm boy is carried off across the sea to mind sheep in Ireland. Years later he walks two hundred miles to a ship, loses his lunch to a lack of pigs, and comes back to the island that took him. Saint Patrick, in his own words, with no snakes and no shamrocks.

Notes for the curious.

Saint Patrick is a real person, and unusually well documented for his age, because he wrote about himself. Two short Latin works of his survive: the Confession, a defence and account of his life, and a letter to the soldiers of a British chieftain called Coroticus, who had raided Ireland and carried off newly baptised Christians. Almost everything in tonight's story that is about his capture, his six years as a herdsman, the voice and the ship, the twenty-eight days, the pigs, and the dream of Victoricus is in the Confession, and where we could we have kept his own words. The dates are uncertain. He lived in the fifth century, and scholars still argue about whether he died closer to the year four hundred and sixty or four hundred and ninety.

He was a Christian before the division between East and West, and he is honoured as a saint by both the Catholic and Orthodox Churches. His feast is the seventeenth of March.

The story of the shamrock, the story of the snakes, the fire on the hill, and the contest with the druids are legends. Several are from Muirchu's Life of Patrick, written some two hundred years after his death, and the shamrock does not appear in connection with him until a good deal later. The hill in Antrim where he is said to have kept sheep is a later tradition; the Confession does not name the place of his slavery, and we have not named it either.

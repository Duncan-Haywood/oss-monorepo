The Woman of Shunem and the Child on the Prophet's Bed

A boy cries out in his father's harvest field, "My head, my head," and by noon he is gone. His mother lays him on the prophet's bed, saddles a donkey, and says to everyone who asks only one word, "Shalom." Second Kings, chapter four, verses eight to thirty-seven, Scripture told straight for bedtime, about a long road, a held pair of feet and a door shut on a room.

Audio is being recorded. The full script is below.

What's true in this story

Who
Elisha, the woman of Shunem and her husband, her son, Gehazi
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; the second book of Kings (the fourth book of Kingdoms in the Greek numbering the Orthodox Church uses) is Scripture in both traditions and in Protestant Bibles
Scripture and history
the book tells this as an event in the life of Elisha in the northern kingdom of Israel; the Church receives it as Scripture, and the notes say where we leave the chapter alone
Legend
none; later teaching reads the story as a sign of the resurrection, and the notes say that is a reading the Church has made and not something the chapter states
What we invented
the narrator's jokes, the sounds and smells of the house and the field, the thoughts of the woman on the road and the small talk of the servant who goes with her are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; what the woman, her husband, Elisha and Gehazi say, and what is done, are told as the chapter gives them, and neither the woman, her husband, her son nor the servant is given a name because the book gives none
Feast day
the Orthodox Church remembers the holy prophet Elisha on the fourteenth of June, and the Carmelite family in the Catholic Church honours him as well; the woman of Shunem is given no name in the book and no feast day is claimed for her here

Full script

Picture a boy running across a field in the heat of a summer morning, and he is quite happy, and he does not know that he has very little time left to be happy in.

The field is full of reapers. They are bent over, in a long line, and the blades of their sickles flash and the barley falls, and behind them the women and the children are tying it up in bundles, and somewhere there is a skin of water going from hand to hand. It is loud, in the way that work is loud. Men are calling to each other. Somebody is singing a song with a rude second verse. And across the stubble, in a little white tunic that is already no longer white, comes the only son of the family who owns the field, to find his father.

He is not sure what he wants. To be where his father is. That is all. He is a small boy, and that is a reason, and it is quite enough.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is from the second book of Kings, chapter four, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, and it is about the prophet Elisha, and a woman in a town called Shunem. A few of the small things around the edges, the sounds of the field, the thoughts of the woman on the road, and the talk of the servant who goes with her, are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original, and the notes at the end say which. What the people in the chapter say, and what is done, we have kept as close to the book as we can. It is, I should warn you, a story with a hard night in the middle of it. But it is a gentle story in the way it is told, and it is a good one to fall asleep to, because of how it ends. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

Let me tell you first where we are, and when.

We are in the northern kingdom of Israel, the half of the old country that broke away from the house of David, somewhere in the ninth century before Christ. The kings are mostly bad ones. The land is divided, and often at war with its neighbours, and now and then it is hungry. And in the middle of all of this there are the prophets, the people who speak for God, and in this part of the Bible the most important of them are two men, who come one after the other. The first is Elijah, who goes up to heaven in a chariot of fire. The second is the man he leaves his cloak to. That is Elisha.

If you have heard the last story about Elisha, the one with the widow and the jars of oil, then you have already met the woman we are talking about tonight, because that story ends in her house. We left her there very briefly. Tonight we are going to stay.

Elisha is a farmer's son. He was ploughing with twelve yoke of oxen when Elijah threw his cloak over his shoulders, and he said goodbye to his parents, and he killed the oxen and cooked them over the wood of the plough, and he followed. Since then he has been walking up and down the country as a sort of travelling servant of God. He tells kings what the Lord says. He helps widows. He is quite often seen with a small group of disciples, the sons of the prophets, who live together in little communities, and he is always on the road.

And a man on the road needs a place to eat.

The town of Shunem is in the north, on the southern side of a ridge called the hill of Moreh, looking out across a wide, green, flat valley called Jezreel. It is good farming country. It is, in fact, some of the best in the whole land, with deep soil, and plenty of grain, and a great many roads running through it, because the valley is the way that armies and traders go between the coast and the Jordan. You could sit on a wall in Shunem and watch the whole country go by.

And it is in Shunem that the book tells us, quite simply, that there lived a great woman.

Now, that is a funny phrase, and it is worth stopping on. The Hebrew word is the one you would use for a woman of substance, or standing, or wealth. She is not a queen, and she is not a prophet. She is simply a person who matters in her town, with a house of her own, and a husband, and land. Her husband seems to be older than she is, and quietly in the background. And she is, you will find, the sort of woman who sees what needs doing, and does it without making any fuss.

One day Elisha comes through, on his way to somewhere else. And the woman sees him, and she does a very ordinary and kind thing. She presses him to stop and eat.

There is a sort of person who does that. You will have met them. They are standing at the door before you have properly arrived, and there is a chair, and there is a plate, and you have not even said you were hungry. And Elisha, who has walked a long way and is not a man to say no to bread, goes in and sits down. And after that, the book says, whenever he came that way he turned in there to eat.

It became a habit. Which is how most good things start.

And one day, perhaps sitting at the table after he has gone, she says something to her husband. She says: "Look. I know that this is a holy man of God, who is continually passing our way. Let us make a little room on the roof, with walls, and put a bed there for him, and a table, and a chair, and a lamp, so that whenever he comes to us, he can go in there."

I like that very much. You will notice the list. A bed, because he is tired. A table, because he will want to write, or to pray, or to put down a cup. A chair. And a lamp. It is a whole little life, in one sentence. It is the sort of list that someone makes who has thought about what it would be like to be a guest with nowhere of your own. She is not building him a shrine. She is building him somewhere to put his feet up.

And her husband, who has nothing to say against it, says nothing. And the room is built.

Imagine it, if you like. A small square room on the flat roof, open to the evening air on the stairs side, with a window looking out over the valley. A mat on the bed. A clay lamp on a stand, with a little flame that does not move when there is no wind. And a man coming up the outside stairs at the end of a long dusty day, who has been in the houses of the great and of the poor, and who has not, for a very long time, been given a room that was simply for him.

Some days later he comes back, and he is lying on the bed in his little room, and he is thinking. He is a prophet, which means that he thinks a good deal about what God wants, but he is also a man who has been given a gift, and he does not like to leave a gift without a thank-you.

So he says to his servant, whose name is Gehazi, "Call this Shunammite." Which means, simply, "the woman from Shunem." And Gehazi goes and calls her, and she comes and stands in the doorway. She does not come in. She stands, the book says, before him.

And Elisha says to Gehazi: "Say to her, 'See, you have taken all this trouble for us. What is to be done for you? Would you have a word spoken on your behalf to the king, or to the commander of the army?'"

That is a real offer. Elisha knows people. He can speak to the king. If she had a quarrel over land, or a grievance, or a favour to ask, here is a man who could do something about it. A smaller person might have said yes. Many would.

And she says: "I dwell among my own people."

Think about what that means. She is saying: I do not need anything. I have my place. I have people who know me, and a household, and the ordinary life of the town, and I do not need to be lifted out of it, or to be noticed by the great. I am at home. It is a very good answer, and a very serene one, and it was not given by someone who has not thought about it. If you want to know what contentment looks like, I should say it looks like that sentence.

But Elisha is not satisfied. He is a man who owes a debt and wants to pay it. And he says to Gehazi, "What then is to be done for her?"

And Gehazi, who is, I think, a rather observant servant, and who is going to turn out to be a more complicated person than he seems, says what he has noticed. He says: "Well, she has no son, and her husband is old."

Nobody had said it. She would certainly never have said it. It is the thing that sits at the back of that house like a closed door. And perhaps, in the way of these things, it is exactly because she does not say it that someone has to.

"Call her," says Elisha.

And she is called, and she stands again in the doorway. And Elisha says: "At this season, when the time comes round, you shall embrace a son."

Well. We have heard this sort of promise before, in the Bible, a few times. Abraham and Sarah heard it, under a tree at Mamre, and Sarah laughed. Hannah heard something like it, in the house of the Lord at Shiloh, and she wept. But this woman does neither. She says something very different, and very honest, and it tells you what the years have cost her.

She says: "No, my lord, O man of God. Do not lie to your maidservant."

You see what she is saying. I have lived a long time with this. I have stopped hoping. Please, do not say it unless it is true. It would be kinder to say nothing at all than to say something beautiful and let me hope. It is one of the bravest things said in the whole of the Bible, I think, because she is telling the prophet of the Lord not to toy with her heart.

And the book does not make a speech about it. It simply says: the woman conceived, and she bore a son, at that season, the next year, as Elisha had said to her.

So the child grows. The book is brief here, and so shall we be. We might imagine a house that, for the first time in a great many years, is full of noise. Small feet on the floor. A voice that wants to know why. A father, who is old, and who has not expected any of this, walking about with a rather silly look on his face. And a mother who has learned not to take anything for granted, and who watches her child sleep sometimes for longer than there is any reason to.

And the child grows, as children do. And then comes the day of the harvest.

It is the day we began with. The field is full of reapers, the barley is falling, and the little boy goes out across the stubble to find his father. And I do not know what he said to him when he got there. The book does not tell us. But in a minute, quite suddenly, he cries out. He says: "My head, my head!"

That is all. Two words, said twice. Whatever it was, a sunstroke, a fever, something in his head that no one then could have named, it is not for us to guess, and the book does not say. And his father, who is a man of few words, says to a servant: "Carry him to his mother."

Notice that. Not "go for a doctor," because there are no doctors to go for. Not "run to the prophet," not yet. Just: take him to his mother. It is, when you think about it, the oldest instinct there is. When a child is hurt, you take him to his mother.

And the servant picks him up and carries him across the fields and through the town and up to the house, and puts him in her arms.

And she takes him on her lap. And she holds him there, the book says, until noon. And then he dies.

I am not going to say anything about that. There is nothing to say about it, and the book does not say anything. It says it in a single line, the way that it happens: and he died. It is the plainest sentence in the chapter. And I think the plainness is a kind of respect.

So we will sit quietly for a moment, in a room in Shunem, at noon, with the sun on the floor.

And then the woman does something that I find, every time I read it, quite extraordinary.

She stands up, with her son in her arms. She climbs the outside stairs to the little room on the roof. And she lays him on the bed of the man of God, the bed she made for him, with the table and the chair and the lamp. And she shuts the door on him, and goes out.

She does not scream. She does not tear her clothes, which is what people did, and what no one would have blamed her for. She does not call the neighbours. She does not even tell her husband what has happened.

She has a plan. And it is not a plan that anyone would call reasonable. It is simply that she knows where the answer is, and she is going to go and get it.

She sends a message out to her husband in the field. And she says: "Send me one of the servants and one of the donkeys, so that I may quickly go to the man of God and come back."

And her husband, who has not been told, is a bit puzzled, and he says: "Why will you go to him today? It is neither new moon nor Sabbath."

That is a lovely detail, if you listen to it. It tells you that people in her day went to visit the prophet on holy days, on the new moon and on the Sabbath, when you would naturally go to hear a man of God. It was a day for going. So her husband thinks: it is an odd day to be going. What is the hurry?

And she says: "All will be well."

Or rather, because this is the Hebrew, she says a single word. She says: "Shalom."

It is the word for peace. It is also the word you say at the door when you come in, and the word you say when you go out, and the word you say when someone asks how you are. It can mean "hello," and it can mean "goodbye," and it can mean "all is well," and it can mean something very deep indeed, about wholeness, and rightness, and things being as they should be. And I do not think she is lying. I think she is saying it in the way you say a word to hold something steady. It is what you say when you cannot afford to say anything else.

And her husband, who has the sense to know that his wife has a reason, says no more.

The servant saddles the donkey. And the woman says something to him, as she gets on, which tells you everything about her mood. She says: "Drive, and go forward. Do not slacken the pace for me unless I tell you."

Now, Shunem is a long way from Mount Carmel. It is, by our reckoning, perhaps twenty-five miles, something like that. That is a guess of ours, from the shape of the country, and not a number in the book. It is across the whole width of the valley of Jezreel, and then up into the hills on the far side, and on a donkey, with a servant walking or riding beside, it is a very long day's journey indeed.

Imagine it. The road goes west and north across the wide flat valley, in the heat. The barley is being cut on every side. There are other people on the road, merchants and soldiers and farmers, and they all go by, and say good day, and she says good day back. There are fig trees, and olive trees, and every now and then a village. The servant trots along beside her, and I think that he has a great deal of curiosity, and not nearly enough answers, and has the good sense to keep his mouth shut. And the woman rides, with her hands on the donkey's neck, and her face set, and she is saying something to herself all the way. I do not know what. I think it might be, quite simply, his name.

And I think that she does not cry. Not on that road. Not yet. She is saving it. She is saving everything she has for the moment she gets to where she is going.

And on the horizon, getting bigger, is the long, green, forested shoulder of Mount Carmel.

Now, the book tells us what happens at the other end, in a way I think is lovely. Because Elisha is on the mountain, as it happens, and he is sitting there, doing whatever it is prophets do, and he looks up, and he sees someone coming from a long way off.

He says to Gehazi: "Look, there is the Shunammite. Run at once to meet her, and say to her: 'Are you well? Is your husband well? Is the child well?'"

He does not know. I want you to notice that, because it is going to come up in a minute. He sees her on the road, a long way off, riding hard, and he can tell from the way she rides that something is the matter, and he sends his servant to ask.

And Gehazi runs. And he comes up to her, and he asks her the three questions. Are you well? Is your husband well? Is the child well?

And to every one of them, she says: "Shalom."

It is the same word. She says it to Gehazi, and he, I suppose, takes it at face value, and goes back. And she keeps on riding, up the mountain. She is not going to tell it twice. She is not going to tell it to a servant. There is one person she is going to say it to, and she is going to say it to him, and nobody else will have it first.

And she gets to the top, and she sees Elisha, and she does not stop, and she does not bow, and she does not speak. She gets down from the donkey, and she falls on the ground in front of him, and she takes hold of his feet.

She takes hold of his feet. It is a gesture of begging, and of honour, and of something more desperate than either. It is what you do when you have come as far as you can come, and you will not let go.

And Gehazi, who has been following behind, sees this, and he comes forward to push her away. He thinks she is being improper. He thinks she is making a scene in front of the master.

But Elisha says: "Let her alone, for she is in bitter distress; and the Lord has hidden it from me, and has not told me."

I find that a very moving thing for the book to say. Elisha is a prophet. He has told kings what they were going to do before they did it. He knows things. And here is something he does not know, and he says so, openly, in front of his servant. The Lord has hidden it from me. He does not pretend. He does not say, "I have been aware of this for some time." He says: I do not know. God has not told me. Let her speak.

It is a lesson in humility, I think, from the man himself. A prophet does not know everything. He only knows what he is told. And the rest of the time, like the rest of us, he has to wait and listen and ask. And in this case he has one person in the world who can tell him, and she is lying on the ground holding on to his feet.

And she does speak. And what she says is not a prayer, and it is not a request. It is the one thing, I think, that has been going round in her head for twenty-five miles. She says:

"Did I ask my lord for a son? Did I not say, 'Do not deceive me'?"

That is all of it. She does not say that her son has died. She does not need to. She says only what is underneath it, which is: you gave him to me. I did not ask. I told you not to. And now.

I do not know how you hear that. I hear it as a sort of terrible faith. Not an accusation, exactly, though it sounds like one. It is more like a person standing at a door that they know is the only one, and saying: this is where I have come. She is not angry at God. She has come, after all, to the man of God. It is simply that she is telling the truth, and the truth is that this has hurt more than she knew a thing could.

And Elisha understands. He does not make a speech. He does not tell her it will be all right, or give her a reason, or say that these things are sent to test us. He does not say anything of the kind. He turns to Gehazi, and he gives him a job.

He says: "Gird up your loins, and take my staff in your hand, and go. If you meet anyone, do not greet him, and if anyone greets you, do not answer. And lay my staff upon the face of the child."

Gird up your loins means, tuck up your robe into your belt, so you can run. And do not greet anyone, and do not answer, means: do not stop. Not for a conversation, not for a blessing, not for the ordinary courtesies of the road, which in that country could take half an hour. Go straight there.

That is what we would call an emergency. It is a very practical set of instructions. And the staff, the prophet's own staff, is meant to be a sign of his authority, and, I think, of his concern.

But the woman is not satisfied. And she says to Elisha, quite plainly, and quite sharply: "As the Lord lives, and as you yourself live, I will not leave you."

It is an oath. She is swearing by God, and by Elisha himself, that she will not go home without him. Not the servant, not the staff. You. And you can hear what she is saying. I have not come all this way for a stick. I have come for you.

And Elisha, who is a wise man and does not argue with a woman in that frame of mind, gets up and follows her. That is how the book says it: so he arose and followed her. The prophet, following the woman, down the mountain. It is one of my favourite lines in the chapter.

And Gehazi goes on ahead.

He goes quickly, as he has been told. He does not greet anyone. He crosses the valley as fast as a man can who is carrying a stick, and he comes at last to the house in Shunem, and goes up the outside stairs, and opens the door of the little room.

And there is the child, on the bed. The bed that was made for a guest.

And Gehazi lays the staff on the boy's face, as he was told, and he waits.

And nothing happens.

The book says: there was neither sound nor sign of life. There was no voice, and no attention. It says it with the same plainness as before. The staff is a good staff. It is the prophet's own. And nothing happens.

I want to say something about that. There is a temptation, when we tell this sort of story, to think that the holy thing works in the same way every time, like a key in a lock. You say the words, you use the staff, and out comes the miracle. But the book does not tell it that way. It lets Gehazi do exactly what he was told to do, and nothing comes of it. It is not that the staff was a trick. It is that God is not a machine, and the prophet is not a wizard, and the staff is only a staff. The life of a child is in the hand of God, and God will give it when God chooses, and not because a servant has laid a piece of wood on a face.

And Gehazi, who is not a stupid man, understands that. He turns round, and he goes back along the road to meet his master. And when he meets him, he says: "The child has not awakened."

That is a gentle way of saying it, isn't it? The child has not awakened. It is what you would say to a mother, perhaps. Or what you would say to yourself, on a day when you were not ready to say anything else.

And Elisha says nothing. He goes on.

He comes to the house. And he goes in. And the book says, with great care: the child was lying dead on his bed.

Notice the care. Not "had died." Not "was apparently dead." It says, flatly, that he was dead. It does not leave a gap for us to wonder whether it was a faint, or a sleep, or an error. The book wants us to know what we are looking at.

And Elisha goes up. He goes into the little room, the room that she built for him, the room with the table and the chair and the lamp, and he finds the child on the bed. And he does something very particular. He shuts the door.

He went in therefore, and shut the door upon the two of them, and prayed to the Lord.

The two of them. Not Gehazi. Not the mother. Not the father, or the neighbours. The prophet, and the child, and God.

I do not know what he said. The book does not say. It tells us only that he prayed. That is the key to the whole thing, and I think that is why it is put in so short a form. He does not summon the miracle. He does not perform it. He asks. In the room, behind the shut door, with a dead child, he prays to the Lord. And everything that follows, however strange it looks, follows from that.

And then he does what the book describes, in a very physical way.

He goes up on the bed. And he lies on the child. The book is exact. He put his mouth upon his mouth, and his eyes upon his eyes, and his hands upon his hands, and he stretched himself upon him.

It is a strange image, and a tender one. It is the posture of someone trying to give warmth to a very cold person. Mouth to mouth, eye to eye, hand to hand. He is making himself the same size as the child. He is bringing every part of his own living body to bear on every part of the dead one. It is as close as one person can come to another.

Elijah did something very like it, long before, at Zarephath, in the story of another widow's son, and the book of Kings makes sure we notice. There the prophet stretched himself on the child three times, and cried out to the Lord, and the Lord heard. Here the thing is done twice, but the pattern is the same. It is the sign of a man who is not only praying with his lips but with his whole self.

And a wonderful thing happens. The book says: the flesh of the child became warm.

Not yet alive. Warm. It is the first sign. It is the smallest, the quietest, and so, in a way, the most hopeful. It is the first thing that you would notice, if you had your hand on his, and I do not think that I need to say any more than that.

And Elisha gets up. He does not rush. He does not call for anyone. He gets off the bed, and he walks once to and fro in the house, up and down. I think it is the walk of a man who has got to the end of what he can do, and is waiting, and does not know how to wait standing still.

And then he goes up again, and he stretches himself on the child once more.

And the child sneezes. Seven times.

I like to think that is the funniest and the most wonderful sentence in the whole of the Bible. The child sneezed seven times. And I will tell you, in the notes, about the Hebrew word, which is a little uncertain. But there it is. Seven sneezes, as a small boy does, when he has woken up in a room with a very large man lying on top of him.

And then, the book says, the child opened his eyes.

I think that is the whole of it, really, but there is a little more, and it is the best part.

Elisha calls to Gehazi, who is, presumably, outside the door, waiting. And he says: "Call this Shunammite."

Again. The same words he used years before, in the same room, in the same little house, when he first called her to promise her a son. Call this Shunammite. And Gehazi calls her, and she comes up the stairs, for the second time in a single day.

And Elisha says only: "Take up your son."

That is the whole of his speech. Three words in English. He does not say "your child has been restored," and he does not say "praise the Lord." He does not make a thing of it, or ask for thanks. Take up your son. It is what you would say to someone at the door of a nursery. Come and get him.

And she comes in. And the book says: she fell at his feet, and bowed to the ground. It is the second time in the story that she has been at the prophet's feet. The first time she held them in a grief that would not let go. This time she bows to them, in thanks. It is a perfect circle, if you think about it. She went to him with nothing but a single word, and she goes back with everything.

And then, the book says, she took up her son, and went out.

That is the end. She carries him down the stairs. And that is where the chapter leaves us. It does not tell us what she said, or what the boy said, or what the husband made of it all when he got home from the field. We might imagine a kitchen, and a warm evening, and the barley being brought in, and a bowl of something hot, and a small boy who is very hungry, and has a bit of a headache, and does not understand why his mother keeps picking him up. But that is only us. The book itself ends on the little room, the staircase, and the woman going down with her child.

And I think it is worth saying that she does not say "Shalom" this time. She has no need. The word has come true.

Now. Let us slow down a little, and think about it, because there is a good deal in this story, and it is the sort of story that rewards being turned over in the dark.

I want to say three things.

The first is about the room. The woman of Shunem made a room for a prophet, with a bed and a table and a chair and a lamp, because she wanted to be kind. It was an act of simple hospitality, with no reason behind it and no reward in view. And the whole of the rest of the story takes place in that room. It is the room where she is promised a son. It is the room where she lays him down. It is the room where he is given back. Nothing she did for Elisha was a bargain, and she never treated it as one. When he offered her a favour, she said she was at home among her own people. But God has a way of making a place for those who make a place for others, and it is not always the place they expected.

The second is about her one word. Shalom. She says it to her husband, and she says it to Gehazi, and in a way she says it to herself, all across the valley. It is, I think, a kind of prayer. It is not a denial of what has happened. She knows perfectly well. It is more like a hand held up against something enormous, saying: not yet. Not till I have got to the one who can help. And there are times in anyone's life when that is exactly what faith is. It is not the absence of fear, or of grief. It is holding on, with whatever you have, to the one thing that you believe to be true, which is that there is someone to take hold of.

And the third is about her hands, which hold the prophet's feet. I do not think the book wants us to miss that. Everything else in her life has been quiet, and polite, and in its proper place. A little room. A modest answer. A word that means everything and nothing. But when she gets to the top of that mountain, she throws all of that aside, and she takes hold of him, and she does not let go. And the prophet, who knows how little he knows, says: let her alone. The Lord has not told me. Let her speak. And I think that is a good thing to learn, for anyone who is ever sitting beside a person in trouble. Do not push them away. Do not tell them to calm down. Let them speak, and listen, because they know something you do not.

And that is the story. Now the wind-down, and I will go slowly.

Elisha lives for many years after this. He goes on walking up and down the country, and he goes on being a prophet, and he does a great many things that you may hear about on other nights. There is a famine, and a pot of stew that turns bitter and is made sweet. There is a general with a skin disease, who learns to wash in the Jordan. There are chariots of fire on a hillside, and a lost axe-head, and a great many kings.

And the woman of Shunem appears once more, a little later in the book, in a famine. Elisha warns her to leave the country for seven years, and she takes her household and goes to live among the Philistines. When she comes back, her house and her land have been taken by someone else, and she goes to the king to ask for them. And at the very moment that she comes in, the king happens to be listening to Gehazi telling him all the wonderful things Elisha has done, and Gehazi is just at the part about the dead child being brought back to life. And the woman walks in, and Gehazi says: "My lord the king, here is the woman, and here is her son, whom Elisha restored to life."

So there they are, the two of them, mother and boy, standing in front of the king, and the story is being told about them while they stand there. And the king asks her, and she tells him. And he gives her back everything, her house, her fields, and all the income of them from the day she left.

I think that is a good way to meet her again. Not in a crisis, but in a courtroom, with her son beside her, and the whole of the story carried by someone else's lips. And the king is simply kind. It is a quiet ending, for someone who has already had so much.

Her husband we never hear of again. Her son we do not hear of again, after that scene. The book does not say what he became. I like to think that he became a farmer, as his father was, and that every year at harvest time he went out into the field and looked at the sky, and did not take it for granted. But that is only a thought, and the book does not tell us.

What it tells us is enough. That on a hot day in a barley field a child cried out, and his mother carried him up a flight of stairs, and shut a door, and rode twenty-five miles on a donkey with a single word in her mouth. And that the one she went to did not know, and asked, and prayed, and that the God who had given the child once gave him again.

Take your time now. The lamp is low. The room is quiet. Somewhere, in a house on a hill, there is a small boy asleep, who is going to be perfectly all right.

Now, the notes, for the curious.

The story you have heard is from the second book of Kings, chapter four, verses eight to thirty-seven. In the Greek numbering that the Orthodox Church uses, this is the fourth book of Kingdoms. It is Scripture in the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and in Protestant Bibles too. Verses one to seventeen of the same chapter, the widow's jars and the promise of a son, we told in the story of the jars of oil, and tonight we took up where it left off. The rest of the chapter, from verse thirty-eight on, the stew that turned bitter and the twenty loaves, we left for another night.

What we have kept from the text: Elisha passing through Shunem; the woman pressing him to eat; her words to her husband about a holy man of God; the little room on the roof, with the bed, the table, the chair and the lamp; Elisha's offer to speak to the king or the commander; her answer, "I dwell among my own people"; Gehazi's remark that she has no son and her husband is old; the promise; her words, "Do not lie to your maidservant"; the birth of the child; the cry in the field, "My head, my head"; the father's instruction to carry him to his mother; the death at noon; the laying of the child on the prophet's bed and the shutting of the door; the request for a servant and a donkey; the husband's question about the new moon and the Sabbath; her answer, "Shalom"; her instruction to drive on and not to slacken the pace; the journey to Mount Carmel; Elisha seeing her from afar and sending Gehazi with three questions; her answer to each, "Shalom"; her taking hold of his feet; Gehazi's attempt to push her away; Elisha's words, "the Lord has hidden it from me"; her words, "Did I ask my lord for a son?"; the staff and its failure; her oath, "I will not leave you"; Elisha's following her; Gehazi's report, "The child has not awakened"; the shutting of the door on the two of them; the prayer; the stretching on the child twice; the warmth; the walk to and fro; the sneezing seven times; the opening of the eyes; the call, "Call this Shunammite," and the words, "Take up your son"; her bowing at his feet; and her carrying her son out.

What we flagged as uncertain or as a reading. The distance from Shunem to Mount Carmel is our own estimate, of about twenty-five miles, from the lie of the land, and not a figure in the book. The Hebrew word for "sneezed" is rare, and some scholars have wondered whether it means something a little different, such as "snorted" or "sniffed." Most translations say "sneezed," and so did we, and we said we would tell you. The word that we have rendered "great woman," in the Hebrew, is the same word that the Bible uses of a person of wealth or standing, and we have taken it that way. Gehazi, who we meet here as a rather sharp and helpful servant, is a more troubled figure later in the book; we have not told that part, and have only hinted that he is more complicated than he seems. And the idea that Elisha's stretching himself on the child is a gesture of sharing warmth, of making himself the size of the child, is an observation that many readers have made, and not something that the chapter states.

The Church has also read this story, from very early on, as a sign of the resurrection of the dead, the child on the bed being given back to his mother as the dead will be given back to those who love them. That is a reading the Church has made, in its teaching and its liturgy, and it is a good one. But the chapter itself does not say it, and we have not put it into the mouth of any of the people in the story. Elisha's prayer is also a reminder, in the usual way of the Bible, that miracles are God's. The staff did nothing, and the prophet prayed. And one small observation about the map: the village of Nain, where Jesus raised a widow's son in the Gospel of Luke, lies on the same hill of Moreh as Shunem, a few miles away. That is geography, not a claim, but it is a thing that many readers have noticed.

What we invented: the sounds and smells of the barley field, the song with the rude second verse, the people on the road, the donkey, the thoughts of the woman as she rides, and the little puzzled silence of the servant who goes with her. None of it is in the chapter. None of it changes what happens, or what it means. And we have kept the death of the child as plain as the book does, and told it as gently as we could, because a bedtime story should not frighten anyone, and because this one, in the end, is a story about being held.

For further reading, the fourth chapter of the second book of Kings is not long. Read it slowly, in a quiet room, and notice how little the book says about what she felt, and how much it shows.

Gerasimus and the Lion Who Lost the Donkey

A fifth-century abbot by the river Jordan pulls a thorn from a lion's paw, gives him a job guarding the monastery donkey, and learns something about blame when the donkey goes missing and the lion is left carrying the water.

What's true in this story

Who
Saint Gerasimus of the Jordan, the lion Jordanes, Abba Sabbatius, the monks of the lavra by the Jordan
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox
Scripture and history
Gerasimus, his lavra near Jericho by the Jordan, its way of life, and his death in about the year four hundred and seventy-five are historical, attested by Cyril of Scythopolis
Legend
The lion, the thorn, the lost donkey, the camel caravan and the lion's death at the grave come from John Moschus's Spiritual Meadow, written down well over a century after Gerasimus died
What we invented
The lion's and the donkey's personalities, most dialogue, the young monk Brother Theodore, the soldier's words, the camel drivers coming back to apologise, and small details of daily life are our own invention
Feast day
Orthodox churches keep Saint Gerasimus on the fourth of March; the Roman Martyrology names him on the fifth of March

But it is the river where Joshua led the people of Israel into the promised land, and where Elijah was taken up, and above all where John baptised the Lord. So for Christians it has never been a small river at all. It is a small river with a very large memory.

And in the fifth century, which is roughly fifteen hundred years ago, that whole wilderness between Jerusalem and the Jordan was filling up with monks.

This was a little like a gold rush, except that instead of gold, people were looking for silence. Men came from all over the Roman world, from Asia Minor and Armenia and Egypt and Cappadocia, to live in caves and huts in the desert and pray. Some lived alone. Some lived in big communities with walls and bells and a kitchen. And some lived in a kind of arrangement in between, which was called a lavra.

The word lavra means something like a lane, or a path. Picture a scattering of small cells, little huts or caves, strung out along a path, each one far enough from its neighbour that you could not hear the man next door humming. In the middle, somewhere, is a common building with a church, a bakery, a storeroom, and the abbot.

Our saint, Gerasimus, ran one of these, a mile or two from the Jordan, near Jericho.

He was not from there. He came from Lycia, on the southern coast of what is now Turkey, a green, mountainous place by the sea. He had become a monk at home, and then, like so many others, he came to the Holy Land. We know a little about him from a careful, rather wonderful historian-monk called Cyril of Scythopolis, who wrote about the desert fathers of Palestine in the following century. Cyril tells us, quite frankly, that when Gerasimus first arrived he got himself tangled up on the wrong side of one of the great theological arguments of the day, and that an older and very holy monk named Euthymius sat him down and set him straight. Gerasimus listened, changed his mind, and the two became friends. I find this enormously encouraging. Even saints have to be corrected sometimes, and the holy ones are the ones who say "Oh. Yes. So by the time our story begins, Gerasimus is an old man, the abba of a lavra, which means its father. And his lavra had a rule which was, I have to say, not for everyone.

One, that they took their fasting seriously. And two, that you could not, in that lavra, make a cup of tea, which would have been the end of me personally.

They each had one garment, and a mat to sleep on, and a jug for water. Some notes for the curious.

The story of the lion comes from a book called The Spiritual Meadow, by John Moschus, a monk who travelled the monasteries of Palestine, Egypt and beyond in the late sixth and early seventh centuries, collecting stories from the elders, and writing them down. The lion is in chapter one hundred and seven. Moschus says he heard it from the fathers of the lavra of Gerasimus. So it is a legend, a monastery story, told and retold for more than a hundred years before it was written, and we've told it as one.

Gerasimus himself, though, is history. Cyril of Scythopolis, writing in the sixth century, mentions him in his Life of Euthymius, including the story of how Euthymius corrected him, and the way of life of his lavra: the cells, the palm-leaf work, the weekend gatherings, the open doors. A good modern book on all of this is Derwas Chitty's The Desert a City. And if you'd like more stories of saints and animals, Helen Waddell's old book Beasts and Saints is a delight.

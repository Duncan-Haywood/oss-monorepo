Barzillai and the Old Man at the Jordan

When King David flees across the Jordan from his son Absalom, an eighty-year-old farmer of Gilead called Barzillai loads his carts with beds, bowls, wheat, beans, honey and cheese for the hungry people at Mahanaim, and when the king comes home and offers him a place at court, he asks only to go home and be buried near his father and mother. Told from the Second Book of Samuel, chapters seventeen and nineteen, with the First Book of Kings, chapter two.

What's true in this story

Who
Barzillai the Gileadite of Rogelim, King David, Chimham who went over with the king, Machir son of Ammiel of Lo-debar, Shobi son of Nahash of Rabbah; remembered Absalom, Joab, Mephibosheth, King Solomon, and the sons of Barzillai in the books of Ezra and Nehemiah
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Books of Samuel and Kings as Scripture; David is honoured as a holy king and prophet in both traditions; Barzillai and Chimham are not named among the saints with feasts of their own in either tradition, and the episode says so
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Second Book of Samuel, chapter seventeen, verses twenty-two to twenty-nine, and chapter nineteen, verses thirty-one to forty, with the First Book of Kings, chapter two, verse seven, told straight in a plain rendering; David's reign is commonly placed around a thousand years before Christ, and that date is given as approximate
Legend
none told as fact; the meanings of the names Barzillai and Rogelim, the site of Rogelim, and Chimham being Barzillai's son are given as likely, not certain; the link between Chimham and the lodging place near Bethlehem in Jeremiah is given as what many readers think
What we invented
Barzillai's household, his grandchildren and servants, the loading of the carts, the road to Mahanaim, the meeting with Machir and Shobi, the days in the camp, the weather, the thoughts of Barzillai, David and Chimham, and every word not given in the chapters are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original; nothing is added to what Barzillai, David or Chimham do in the books
Feast day
King David is remembered on the twenty-ninth of December in the Roman Martyrology, and in the Orthodox Church on the Sunday after Christmas, with Joseph the Betrothed and James the brother of the Lord; the righteous of the Old Testament are remembered together on the Sunday of the Holy Forefathers before Christmas in the Orthodox Church; Barzillai has no feast day

We are east of the river Jordan, roughly a thousand years before Christ. Historians argue about the exact dates, as historians always do, so let us just say: a long time ago.

Gilead is good country. It gets more rain than the hills around Jerusalem. It has oak woods, and pasture, and springs. In the old days it was famous for two things. One was its sheep and cattle, because there was grass. The other was its balm, a sweet-smelling ointment made from the resin of some tree or shrub that nobody is now completely sure about. What happened afterwards?

We do not know when Barzillai died. The book does not tell us. It is a nice thing to hope that he got his wish, and sat on his bench in the evenings for a few more years, and told his great-grandchildren the story of the time the king came to Mahanaim, more and more grandly each time. And that one day, when he was ready, they laid him to rest in his own town, near the grave of his father and his mother, just as he had asked.

But the king did not forget him.

Years later, when David was very old himself, and lying on his bed, and knew that he was dying, he called his son Solomon to him to tell him the things a king needs to know. And in among the instructions about the kingdom, there is one small sentence that I think is one of the most beautiful things David ever said.

Deal kindly with the sons of Barzillai the Gileadite, he told Solomon. Let them be among those who eat at your table. For with such kindness they came to meet me when I fled from Absalom your brother.

So at the very end of his life, with everything else he had to think about, David remembered the cheese.

And there is something more, very faint and far away, that I want to give you.

In the Book of Jeremiah, four hundred years and more after David, there is a mention, just in passing, of a place near Bethlehem called the lodging place of Chimham. Geruth Chimham, in Hebrew. Many readers think it was named after our Chimham, and that perhaps David gave him land near Bethlehem, David's own home town, and that he settled there, and his family after him. Nobody can be sure. But it is a pleasant thought, the young man from Gilead who went over the river with the king, and whose name was still on the map four centuries later.

And further on still, after the exile in Babylon, when the people came home and made lists of all the families who returned, the Books of Ezra and Nehemiah mention a family of priests called the sons of Barzillai. They were called that, the books say, because one of their ancestors had married one of the daughters of Barzillai the Gileadite, and had taken his name. Think of that. Some long-ago priest, marrying into the old man's family, and deciding that the name worth carrying was not his own, but his father-in-law's. Barzillai. The one who fed the king.

Five hundred years after a string of donkeys came down a hill road with beds and lentils, people were still proud to be called by his name.

Barzillai never did anything that the world calls great. He did not win a battle. He did not write a psalm. He did not see an angel, though he lived near a town named after the night when angels came. He was just an old man who heard that some people were hungry, and got up off his bench.

But I think the angels at Mahanaim would have recognised him. Because that is what angels do, in the old stories. They turn up when you are tired and far from home, and they bring bread. And then, when you try to thank them, they will not take anything, and they go away.

There is a line in the psalm I mentioned at the beginning, the ninetieth psalm, the one that says threescore years and ten, or perhaps fourscore. A little further on, it says this. So teach us to number our days, that we may get a heart of wisdom.

I think Barzillai had numbered his days. He knew exactly how many he probably had left. And it had not made him sad, or frightened, or grasping. It had made him generous with the time he had, and content with the place he was in, and very clear about what mattered. A heart of wisdom. Which is a lovely thing to have, at eighty, or at any age.

And I think, too, of David, a few months before, lying down in a strange place with everything gone wrong, and saying, I lay down and slept. I woke again, for the Lord sustained me. And part of the way the Lord sustained him, those nights at Mahanaim, was an old man from Rogelim, and his beds, and his basins, and his honey.

So the river runs on, brown and quick, between the willows. And on the eastern side the hills of Gilead go blue, and then grey, and then dark, as the evening comes on. And somewhere up there, in a village nobody can find any more, an old man is sitting on a bench against a warm stone wall, with a dog asleep at his feet, watching the road, and not waiting for anything at all.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Samuel, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. David's flight from Absalom is in chapter fifteen. The crossing of the Jordan and the three men who brought food to Mahanaim, with the whole list, beds and basins and cheese, are in chapter seventeen, verses twenty-two to twenty-nine. The battle and David's grief are in chapter eighteen. Barzillai at the Jordan, his answer to the king and Chimham going over are in chapter nineteen, verses thirty-one to forty. David's words to Solomon about the sons of Barzillai are in the First Book of Kings, chapter two, verse seven. The lodging place of Chimham near Bethlehem is in Jeremiah, chapter forty-one, and the priestly sons of Barzillai are in Ezra, chapter two, and Nehemiah, chapter seven. Mahanaim's name comes from Genesis, chapter thirty-two.

Everything Barzillai, David and Chimham do and say at the river is in the book, told in a plain rendering. What I invented is everything around it: the household at Rogelim, the news arriving, the loading of the donkeys, the meeting of the three men, the evenings in the camp, Barzillai's son who once left home, and the goats. The meanings of Barzillai and Rogelim, Chimham being Barzillai's son, Shobi being the son of that King Nahash, and the link between Chimham and the place near Bethlehem are what many readers think, not certain.

Barzillai is not counted among the saints with a feast day of his own, in either tradition, and neither is Chimham. King David is.

That's the story of Barzillai and the old man at the Jordan for tonight.

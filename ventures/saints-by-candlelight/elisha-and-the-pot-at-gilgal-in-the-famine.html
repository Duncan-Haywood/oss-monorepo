Elisha and the Pot at Gilgal in the Famine

In a year of famine the Prophet Elisha comes back to Gilgal, where the company of the prophets sits at his feet with nothing to eat; one of them gathers wild gourds into the great pot and there is death in it, a handful of flour makes it safe, and a man from Baal-shalishah walks in with twenty barley loaves for a hundred men. Told from the Second Book of Kings, chapter four.

What's true in this story

Who

Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the Books of Kings as Scripture and honour Elisha as a holy prophet, as the Jewish tradition from which the story comes honours him too
Scripture and history
the story is Scripture, the Second Book of Kings, chapter four, verses thirty-eight to forty-four, told straight in a plain rendering; every word that Elisha, his servant and the company of the prophets say in the book is kept as the book gives it
Legend
none told as fact; the identification of the wild gourds with the bitter colocynth, which Gilgal this was, where Baal-shalishah lay, and whether this famine is the seven-year famine of chapter eight are given as likely identifications and open questions; the Christian reading of the twenty loaves as a sign pointing to the feeding of the five thousand is told as a reading

And most of us, like Hodiah, stand in front of it some mornings, looking at the bottom of the jar, wondering whether today is the day.

And I would like you to think about the gourds. All the things we gather, very keenly, with the best intentions, that turn out bitter in the pot. And how good it is to have somebody who will shout, when it tastes of death, and somebody to shout to.

And then I would like you to think about the man from Baal-shalishah, coming up the road with a sack on his shoulder, the morning after the last of the flour had gone. Not with a great deal. Just twenty loaves. Just the first of what he had. Given away, in a bad year, to the Lord.

They ate, and had some left.

Somewhere at Gilgal, in the dark, a great clay pot is sitting upside down on a shelf, washed and dry, with a crack down one side that nobody has mentioned for years. A cloak is folded by the wall. An empty flour jar has its lid on. And in the corner of the store room there is a sack, with a few pieces of barley bread at the bottom of it, that nobody needed to eat. Kept, just in case. For tomorrow.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Second Book of Kings, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture, chapter four, verses thirty-eight to forty-four. The famine, Elisha at Gilgal, the sons of the prophets sitting before him, the great pot, the one who went out for herbs and came back with wild gourds in his lap, the shout that there is death in the pot, the flour, the man from Baal-shalishah with twenty barley loaves of the firstfruits and fresh ears in his sack, the servant asking how he could set this before a hundred men, and Elisha's answer, they shall eat and have some left, are all there. The words of Elisha, of his servant and of the company of the prophets are kept as the book gives them, in a plain rendering.

The book does not name the servant in these verses, so we have not either. It does not name the man who gathered the gourds, or the man from Baal-shalishah. That the gourds were the colocynth is a likely identification, not a certain one. Which Gilgal this was, and where Baal-shalishah lay, are open questions.

The firstfruits belonging to the priests are in Numbers, chapter eighteen, and Deuteronomy, chapter eighteen, and the first sheaf in Leviticus, chapter twenty-three. Gilgal and the end of the manna are in Joshua, chapter five. The house that was too small is in Second Kings, chapter six, and the seven years of famine in chapter eight. The five barley loaves are in John, chapter six.

And Ira and Hodiah, the flour jar, the cracked pot, and everything said in the story that the book does not give, are our own invention.

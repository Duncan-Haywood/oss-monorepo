Tonight's trouble is a nation called Midian.

This went on for seven years.

By the end of it, the people of Israel were living in caves and in mountain hideouts, because it was the only place where nobody would look for them. They would plant a field in the spring, and every autumn the Midianites would come and clear it. So the people did the only sensible thing a person can do when they have run out of sensible things. And God heard them, and the first thing he did was to send a man to tell them a few honest words. Not an army, not yet. The tree is an oak, in a village called Ophrah, and it belongs to a man named Joash. Under the oak, quite suddenly, there is a stranger sitting. The book calls him the angel of the Lord. And a little way off, there is a young man named Gideon, and he is doing something very odd.

He is threshing wheat. Now, threshing is what you do with a harvest. You spread the grain out on a flat, open place, a threshing floor, where the wind can blow through it and carry off the chaff. And that is the whole point. It needs space and it needs air.

But Gideon is not doing it on a threshing floor. Gideon is doing it in a winepress.

A winepress, in those days, was a pit cut into the rock, low and narrow and hidden. You trod grapes in it. It was the last place in the world you would choose for threshing wheat, because there was no wind and no space and your elbows kept hitting the wall. But it was also the last place a Midianite would think to look. So Gideon is in the pit, with his little heap of wheat, working very quietly, and glancing up at the rim every few moments to see if anyone is coming.

This is the man. This is the future leader of Israel. He is hiding in a hole in the ground with his lunch.

I am not saying that to laugh at him. Honestly, I would have been in the hole beside him. Seven years is a long time to be afraid.

And then the stranger under the oak calls across, and he says: "The Lord is with you, mighty warrior."

Imagine hearing that, from inside a winepress.

Gideon looks up. The book does not tell us what his face does, but I think it is easy enough to guess. And he answers, very politely and very honestly, the way a person does who has had a hard seven years.

"Please, my lord," he says, "if the Lord is with us, why has all this happened to us? Where are all his wonders that our fathers told us about? They said, did not the Lord bring us up from Egypt? But now the Lord has left us, and given us into the hand of Midian."

Which is, I think, one of the most honest sentences in the whole Bible. He does not pretend. He does not say, oh well, it's all for the best. He says what everyone in the country is thinking. And the Lord does not scold him for it.

What the Lord does is look at him, and say, "Go in this strength of yours, and save Israel from the hand of Midian. Have I not sent you?"

Now Gideon, to give him his due, does not leap up and grab a sword. He says what any sensible person would say.

"Please, Lord, how can I save Israel? My clan is the weakest in the whole tribe, and I am the youngest in my father's house."

And the answer comes: "But I will be with you."

That is the whole of it. That is the reason. Not that Gideon is strong, or clever, or well connected, because he is none of those things. Just: I will be with you. And you will strike Midian as if it were one man.

Gideon takes a moment. He has, after all, just been told something enormous, in a hole, by a stranger, and he would very much like to be sure about it. So he asks for a sign.

"If I have found favour with you," he says, "then give me a sign that it is really you speaking with me. And please do not go away until I come back with my offering."

And the Lord says, "I will stay until you return."

So Gideon hurries off to his house. He kills a young goat and he bakes bread, and he puts the meat in a basket and the broth in a pot. Gideon brings the food out, and the angel tells him what to do. Put the meat and the bread on this rock, he says, and pour the broth over them.

And now we come to the plan. And it is, I think, the strangest plan in the Bible, and that is saying a great deal.

Gideon divides his three hundred men into three companies of a hundred each. And he gives each man three things. A trumpet. An empty jar. And inside the jar, a torch.

That is all. No swords. No spears. Just a trumpet, and a pot, and a flame.

Now think about this properly. In the night, a flame in the open is easy to see. Anyone in the camp can see it, and anyone can shoot at it. But a flame inside a clay jar is something else. A man could walk right up to the edge of the camp, carrying a lit torch, and nobody would see it. Because the jar covers it. It is only a person, in the dark, with a jar.

And then, at the right moment, you break the jar. And the light comes out.

Gideon explains the plan to his men as they stand there. "Look at me," he says, "and do likewise. When I come to the outskirts of the camp, do as I do. I should say, there is nothing very grand about this. It is not a fancy battle plan. It has about three parts. You walk quietly, you smash a jar, you make a very loud noise. But I do think there is something wise in it, because the plan depends on every single man doing the very small thing he has been asked to do. Not a hero among them. Just three hundred men, with a pot, standing in the dark, waiting for a signal.

It is the beginning of the middle watch. That is the middle of the night, when the guards have just changed, and the new ones have not quite woken up.

Gideon and his hundred come to the edge of the camp. Everything is quiet. A camel coughs somewhere, and shifts its weight. The cooking fires are low, and somebody is snoring in a tent, like a saw in a log.

Gideon takes a breath. And he blows his trumpet.

And all three hundred blow theirs.

Three hundred torches blazing in the dark, from every side, all round the camp, with three hundred trumpets blaring over them, and three hundred voices roaring, "A sword for the Lord and for Gideon!"

Now. Notes for the curious.

Everything that happens in tonight's story follows the book of Judges, chapters six and seven, with a glance at the start of chapter eight. The seven years of Midian and the hiding in caves, the stranger under the oak at Ophrah, the winepress and the greeting, the sign of the fire on the rock, the night at the altar of Baal and the second name Jerubbaal, the muster at the spring of Harod, the two fleeces, the sorting of the army from thirty-two thousand to ten thousand to three hundred, the lapping of the water, the visit with Purah, the dream of the barley loaf, the plan with the jars, the trumpets and the torches, the shout, and the fleeing of the camp, are all in the text. The people's offer of the kingship, and Gideon's answer, are in chapter eight. The Letter to the Hebrews, chapter eleven, names Gideon among those who lived by faith.

What we invented is small. Gideon's feelings in the winepress, the talk with Purah on the way down, the neighbour at the doorway, the goat and the broth, the pause at the spring and the quiet of the dawn after, are our own. We have also left out the battle's later hard chapters, which are in the text and are not for bedtime, and the story of the golden ephod in chapter eight, which belongs to another night.

Two things are worth being careful about. First, the dates. Scholars place the age of the Judges at many different times, and we do not try to pick one. Second, the fleece. Some people use it as a pattern for decisions, and as we said, the text does not offer it as a pattern. God has already spoken to Gideon, and the fleece is a mercy to a frightened man.

Gideon is remembered in the Orthodox Church among the righteous of the Old Testament, with a commemoration on the first of October, and he is read in the Catholic Church in the readings at Mass in ordinary time, though without a fixed feast of his own.

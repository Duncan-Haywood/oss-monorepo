Cosmas and the Canon for Christmas Night

An orphan boy in Damascus grows up beside his adopted brother John, learns music from a ransomed monk, follows him into the desert gorge of Mar Saba, and is sent away to be bishop of a little port by the sea, where he writes the hymn that still opens Christmas in the Eastern churches. Saint Cosmas the hymn-writer, a sequel to episode ninety-nine.

Full script

It is the middle of the night, and the church is full of people and candles, and it is warm, the way a church only gets warm when everyone in town has come at once.

Outside, the sea is breathing against the harbour wall. Inside, a boy of about fourteen is standing at the front, by the reading desk, holding a book that is far too heavy for him. His mouth has gone dry. He has never sung this in front of anybody. Behind him, in the bishop's chair, sits a small old man with a white beard and ink on his fingers, and he is smiling as if he knows a secret.

The boy takes a breath. And then he sings, and his voice cracks on the very first word, and somebody at the back laughs, kindly, and the boy goes red to the ears and keeps going anyway.

"Christ is born. Glorify him."

And the whole church sings it back to him, so loudly that the candle flames lean.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Tonight's story is about Saint Cosmas the hymn-writer, a monk and bishop in the Holy Land in the seven hundreds, whose Christmas hymn is still sung every year in the Eastern churches, and his early life comes from an old Life written down a couple of centuries later, so the boy at the reading desk, his grandmother, and nearly everything anybody says out loud are our own invention, told in the spirit of the original. So get comfortable. Dim the lights. And let's begin.

If you heard episode ninety-nine of this show, you met a man called John of Damascus. He was the clever son of a rich Christian family who worked for the caliph in Damascus, gave it all up to become a monk in the desert, sold baskets in his old city as a penance, and wrote the great defence of holy icons.

And in that story, out of the corner of your eye, there was a second boy.

He grew up in the same house. He ate at the same table. He learned his letters from the same teacher, sitting on the same bench. He was not John's brother by birth. He was an orphan, the old Life says, and John's father took him in and raised him as his own. And his name was Cosmas.

Tonight is his night.

Now, I have to tell you at the start that we know much less about Cosmas than about John. John wrote long books about theology, and books have a way of leaving a trail. Cosmas wrote hymns. And hymns are funny things. Everybody knows the words. Everybody sings them, every year, for centuries. And almost nobody remembers who wrote them, or where, or what he had for breakfast that morning. A hymn goes out into the world like a child leaving home, and it does not write back.

So here is what we can say. Cosmas lived in the Holy Land in the seven hundreds. He was a monk. He became the bishop of a small town by the sea called Maiuma, which was the harbour of the old city of Gaza. He was close to John of Damascus all his life. And he wrote some of the greatest hymns in the whole Christian East, hymns that are sung at Christmas, and at Epiphany, and in Holy Week, every single year, in Greek, and Arabic, and Slavonic, and Romanian, and Georgian, and in English too, from Alaska to Australia.

The Byzantines sometimes called him Cosmas Hagiopolites. That means Cosmas of the Holy City, which is to say Jerusalem. So perhaps that is where he was born, before he was an orphan in Damascus. We don't know how he got from one to the other. The old sources do not say, which I think is a pity, because there is a whole story in that gap, and I would very much like to hear it.

So let's go back. Let's go to Damascus, in the early seven hundreds.

Damascus at that time was the capital of a great empire. The caliphs of the Umayyad family ruled from there, over lands stretching from Spain in the west to the edge of India in the east. They had built a magnificent mosque in the middle of the city, the Great Mosque, on the site where a church of Saint John the Baptist had stood, and it glittered with gold mosaics of trees and rivers and palaces. The markets were full of everything. Silk. Spices. Glass.

And a great many of the people in Damascus were still Christians. They spoke Greek, or Aramaic, or more and more, Arabic. Some of them, like John's family, worked for the caliph's government, keeping the accounts, because they were very good at accounts and the caliphs knew it. John's grandfather had done it. John's father, Sergius, did it. It was the kind of family where the children were expected to be clever, and were given every chance to be.

So imagine a big house in Damascus. A courtyard with a fountain in the middle, and a fig tree, and the sound of the street just on the other side of the wall. A household of servants. A father who comes home late from the palace, tired, with ink on his cuffs. And two small boys, one born there, and one who arrived one day holding somebody's hand, not quite sure where he was, or whether he was allowed to touch the fountain.

He was allowed. It was his home now.

The old Life tells a story about how the two boys got their teacher. We told it in episode ninety-nine, but it belongs to Cosmas too, so let me tell it again, briefly.

The Life says that one day Sergius was walking through the slave market in Damascus. In those days, prisoners taken in wars and raids were sold there, which was a cruel and ordinary thing in the world at the time. And among the prisoners was a monk from Italy, from across the sea. His name, by a nice coincidence, was also Cosmas. He was sitting there weeping.

Somebody asked him why. Was he afraid? No, he said. He was weeping because he had spent his whole life learning things, grammar and poetry and music and mathematics and the stars, and he had never had the chance to teach any of it to anyone. And now he would die a slave, and all that learning would be wasted.

Sergius bought him on the spot, the Life says, set him free, took him home, and gave him two pupils. His son John. And his adopted son, Cosmas.

Now, this meant there were two people in the house called Cosmas. Cosmas the teacher, who was old and serious and from Italy, and Cosmas the boy, who was small and had just learned not to fall in the fountain. Mealtimes must have been confusing. "Cosmas, pass the bread." "Which one?" "The one nearest the bread." I imagine the household worked out a system. Big Cosmas and little Cosmas, probably. Children are good at that sort of thing.

We cannot check this story. It was written down a long time later, and stories like this one have a way of getting tidier with age. But whoever taught those two boys taught them very, very well. They learned grammar, the old Life says, and logic, and arithmetic, and geometry, and astronomy. And they learned music.

And this is the part I want you to notice, because it matters for the rest of the story.

Music, in those days, did not mean what it usually means now. It did not mostly mean playing an instrument. It meant understanding how sound works. Why some notes sound well together and some do not. How a melody rises and falls. How the old Greek modes go, the different families of tunes, each with its own feeling, some bright and some sorrowful. And it meant poetry too, because in the Greek world, poetry and music had always gone together. A poem was something you sang.

I like to imagine the two boys at their lessons. John is the quick one. He has his hand up before the question is finished. He argues. He wants to know why. And Cosmas is the listener. He sits by the window, and he does not say much, and when the old teacher sings a line of a hymn to show them how a mode goes, Cosmas sings it back, exactly, the first time, and then sits quietly and hums it to himself for the rest of the afternoon.

That is not in the Life. I made it up. But it is the kind of thing that must have happened in some form, because of what both of them grew up to be. One became the great thinker of his age. The other became its great singer.

The Life says that when the boys had learned everything the old teacher had to give, he came to Sergius and said, more or less, "I've done what I promised. They know more than I do now. Let me go." And he went off to be a monk in the desert, at a monastery near Jerusalem, a monastery called the Lavra of Saint Sabbas.

Remember that name. We'll be going there.

The boys grew up. John, as we heard in episode ninety-nine, followed his father into the caliph's service, and had a good career, and then, at some point, the old Life says, the two of them left Damascus and went to the desert. They went to the same place their teacher had gone. The Lavra of Saint Sabbas, which people today call Mar Saba.

Now, there is a little honesty I owe you here. Historians are not quite sure about the two brothers going to Mar Saba together. The evidence that John lived there is fairly good. The evidence for Cosmas is thinner, and some scholars think the later Life may have put him there to keep the brothers side by side. But the tradition is old and much loved, and it is certainly true that the two of them belonged to the same world, of monks and hymn-writers in Palestine, and that their hymns grew up together. So we will follow the tradition, and say so.

If you heard episode one hundred and four, you have been to Mar Saba already. It is one of the strangest places on earth. A deep, dry gorge in the wilderness between Jerusalem and the Dead Sea, the Kidron valley, with cliffs of pale stone dropping straight down, and in the cliffs, caves. Hundreds of caves. And in the caves, monks. Saint Sabbas had founded it about two and a half centuries before, and he had started out by living in one of those caves himself, climbing in and out on a rope.

By Cosmas's time, there was a proper monastery there, clinging to the side of the gorge, with towers and walls and a church, and a tomb where Sabbas lay. But it was still the desert. The sun beat down on the rocks in the day. At night it got cold, astonishingly cold for somewhere so hot, and the stars came out so thick and bright that you could almost read by them. There was not much water. There was a great deal of silence.

And there was singing.

That is the thing about Mar Saba that matters most for our story. The monks there sang the services, day and night. The long night service in particular, the one that runs from the dark into the dawn, which is called Matins, or Orthros. And at Mar Saba, over the years, a way of praying the services grew up that spread across the whole Eastern Church. The Rule of Saint Sabbas, people called it, though Sabbas himself did not write it all down. It is the ancestor of how the Orthodox churches still pray today.

And in the middle of that long night service, there is a hymn called a canon.

I explained what a canon is in the episode about Saint Andrew of Crete, episode ninety-eight, but let me remind you, because tonight we are going to watch one being made.

A canon is a long hymn in nine parts, called odes. And each of the nine parts is built on a song from the Bible. The first is the song Moses and the people sang after they crossed the Red Sea. The third is the prayer of Hannah, when she gave her little boy Samuel to God. The fourth is from the prophet Habakkuk, who heard the Lord coming and trembled. The sixth is the prayer of Jonah from inside the great fish. The seventh and eighth are the song of the three young men in the fiery furnace in Babylon, who walked about in the flames and sang. And the ninth is the song of Mary, the Magnificat, my soul magnifies the Lord. The second ode, from the end of Deuteronomy, is a stern one, and is usually left out, except in Lent.

So a canon is like a necklace, with nine old beads from the Bible, and the hymn-writer threads new beads between them. Each ode starts with a short verse called the irmos, which means a link, and the irmos always has one foot in the old Bible song and one foot in the feast of the day. Then come the other verses, all to the same tune.

It is a beautiful form. It is also very, very hard to do well. You have to know the whole Bible, and theology, and music, and Greek poetry, all at once, and make it sound simple.

Cosmas was going to do it so well that people are still singing his canons twelve hundred years later.

Now I want to give Cosmas some company. In episode ninety-nine we imagined a young monk at Mar Saba called Elias, a boy from a village near Bethlehem who carried water from the well and had strong opinions about the well. He is invented. But he is ours, and I am fond of him, so let us bring him back. He is a bit older now. He has been helping John by sharpening pens, and he has become very good at it, and rather proud.

So it is late one night, in the late autumn. Winter is coming to the desert. Elias is walking along the narrow path on the side of the gorge with a little clay lamp, on his way back to his own cave, when he sees a light in Cosmas's cell. That is unusual. Cosmas is a quiet monk, who goes to bed when the bell says to. Elias stops, and knocks on the door frame.

"Brother Cosmas? Are you ill?"

"No," says a voice from inside. "Come in. Mind the jug."

Elias comes in, and steps over the jug, mostly. The cell is tiny. A mat on the floor. A shelf. A little window cut in the rock, with the stars in it. And Cosmas, sitting cross-legged with a board across his knees and a sheet of parchment on the board, and ink, and pens, and a book open beside him. He has ink on his fingers and a smudge of it on his nose.

"You've got ink on your nose," says Elias.

"Have I? Then I'm working properly."

Elias looks at the book. "What is that?"

"Gregory," says Cosmas.

Elias knows the name. Every monk knows that name. Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, the great bishop and poet who lived about four hundred years before. Gregory the Theologian, the Eastern Church calls him. There was a whole episode about him on this show, episode ninety-two, about the little church in Constantinople he called Anastasia, where he preached when the Christians of the true faith there were few and poor and had nowhere else to go.

"It's his sermon for Christmas," says Cosmas. "He preached it in Constantinople, in that little church, perhaps. Listen to how it starts."

And he reads it out, in Greek, and I'll give it to you in English. This, at least, is no invention. These really are the first words of Gregory's Christmas sermon, which he preached about the year three hundred and eighty.

"Christ is born. Glorify him. Christ from heaven. Go out to meet him. Christ on earth. Be lifted up. Sing to the Lord, all the earth."

Elias waits. "That's it?"

"That's the start," says Cosmas. "Isn't it wonderful? It's like someone running into a room and shouting the news before he's got his coat off. No introduction. No 'dear brothers'. Just: he's born. Come and see."

"So what are you doing with it?"

Cosmas looks down at the board on his knees. "I've been asked to write a canon for Christmas," he says. "And I think it should start with Gregory."

Now, let me stop the story there for a moment and tell you what we really know, and what we don't.

We do not know where Cosmas wrote his Christmas canon. We don't know when. We don't know if anybody asked him to, or if he just started. That night in the cell is mine. But we do know what he wrote. Because it has never stopped being sung.

Here is the first verse, the irmos of the first ode. Remember that the first ode is always tied to the song of Moses at the Red Sea, which begins, "I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously." Cosmas took Gregory's opening words, almost exactly, and tied them to Moses's song. In plain English, it goes something like this.

Christ is born. Glorify him. Christ from heaven. Go out to meet him. Christ on earth. Be lifted up. Sing to the Lord, all the earth. And sing his praise with gladness, all you peoples, for he has been glorified.

That last line is Moses. The first lines are Gregory. And the thing in between, the stitching, is Cosmas. That is what a great hymn-writer does. He doesn't always make up something new. He takes the old treasures, the Bible and the Fathers, and he sets them side by side so that they shine at each other.

I want you to picture that. A preacher in Constantinople, shouting the good news in a little borrowed church, about the year three hundred and eighty. And four centuries later, a monk in a desert cave picks up those words and sets them to music. And for more than twelve centuries after that, every Christmas, people all over the world sing them, mostly without knowing who either man was.

That is what I mean about hymns not writing home.

Cosmas went on, through all the odes. Let me give you a couple more, in my own plain words.

For the sixth ode, the one built on Jonah's prayer from inside the great fish, he wrote something rather surprising. He says, more or less, that the sea monster spat Jonah out exactly as it had taken him in, like a newborn baby. And in the same way, the Word of God dwelt in the Virgin, and took flesh, and came forth, and left her as she was before. It is a startling picture. A sea monster and a baby. But that is the old way of reading the Bible, looking for Christ in the corners of the Old Testament, and Cosmas does it with a sort of joy.

And for the ninth ode, the one built on Mary's own song, the Magnificat, he wrote what may be the loveliest verse of all. Again in my own words.

I see a strange and wonderful mystery. The cave is heaven. The Virgin is the throne of the cherubim. The manger is the room where Christ our God lies, the one whom nothing can contain. We praise him, and we magnify him.

The cave is heaven. That's the whole of Christmas in four words, really. The God who made the stars, lying in a feeding trough in a cave outside Bethlehem, which is not far at all from Mar Saba. On a clear day, from the hills above the gorge, you could practically walk there before lunch.

There is one more thing about the canon that I love. The scholars tell us that if you take the first letter of each of the verses, they spell out a line of Greek. It means something like, Christ, become a man, stays what he was, God. So the whole hymn is a kind of secret message, hidden down the side of the page, saying the same thing the hymn says out loud. You can only see it if you look at the written book. When you sing it, it just goes by, unnoticed. I think that is rather like a lot of good things.

Back to the cell. Let's say Elias sits down on the edge of the mat, because he has walked a long way and the lamp is warm.

"Brother John's writing one too," he says.

"I know," says Cosmas. "He told me."

This part is true in a way. The service books of the Eastern Church, for Christmas, have two canons, one after the other, to be sung on the same night. The first is by Cosmas. The second, the books say, is by John of Damascus. And John, being John, wrote his in iambic metre. That is an old, strict, classical Greek kind of verse, twelve syllables to a line, counted by long and short sounds in a way that had mostly gone out of ordinary speech centuries before. It is fiendishly difficult. It is the kind of thing a clever person does to show it can be done. People who know Greek well say that John's iambic canons are magnificent, and also that you sometimes need a dictionary and a strong cup of coffee to follow them.

The same thing happened at Epiphany, the feast of Christ's baptism in the Jordan. Two canons. One by Cosmas, one in iambics by John.

So I picture it like this. Two brothers, raised in the same house, taught by the same old man, standing side by side once more. John, the brilliant one, doing something very hard and very grand. And Cosmas, the quieter one, doing something that sounds simple and goes straight in. Christ is born. Glorify him.

"Are you competing?" Elias asks. "You and Brother John?"

Cosmas laughs. "We've been competing since we were six," he says. "He always won at arithmetic. I always won at singing. Our teacher used to say it was a good thing God made two of us, because one would have been insufferable."

"Which one?"

"He never said," says Cosmas. "He was a very wise man."

Then he picks up his pen again, and Elias, without being asked, picks up the knife and starts sharpening the other pens, because that is what he is good at, and the two of them sit there in the lamplight for a long time, while the stars move slowly across the little window, and outside, far down at the bottom of the gorge, a fox goes about its business in the dark.

Cosmas did not stay at Mar Saba forever.

At some point, probably in the seven hundred and forties, though the dates are not certain, he was made a bishop. The tradition says it was the Patriarch of Jerusalem who chose him. And he was sent to be bishop of Maiuma.

Now, Maiuma is not a famous name. But Gaza is. Gaza is one of the oldest cities in the world. It sits near the coast at the southern end of the land, where the road from Egypt comes up out of the desert, and it has been a market and a crossroads for thousands of years. Maiuma was its harbour, a little town on the shore a few miles away, where the ships came in. Fishermen. Traders. Sailors. Nets drying on the beach. Salt in the air.

And the country around Gaza was holy ground in its own way, too. Saint Hilarion, whom Saint Jerome wrote about as the father of the monks of Palestine, was born near Gaza, long before. In the five hundreds there had been famous monks there, wise old men called Barsanuphius and John, who answered hundreds of letters from people asking for advice, and a gentle teacher called Dorotheos, whose little book of instructions monks still read. And Gaza had once had a famous school, where young men learned to speak beautifully. So it was a place that knew the value of good words.

I imagine the morning Cosmas left Mar Saba. Let me give it to you as I picture it, though none of this is written down.

It is early. The sun is not yet over the edge of the gorge, and the rocks are blue and grey. Cosmas has one bag. A monk does not need more than one bag. He has his books in it, wrapped in a cloth, and his pens, and a cloak for the cold. John walks with him to the top of the cliff path, where the track goes off toward Bethlehem and the road west.

They stand there for a while. Neither of them is any good at goodbyes.

"Write," says John at last.

"You're the one who writes," says Cosmas. "I'll sing. You'll hear me from here if the wind's right."

John smiles. "It's a long way to Gaza."

"Then I'll sing loudly."

And John does the thing that brothers do, which is to say something practical when they mean something else. "Take the extra cloak," he says. "The sea's cold in winter. You don't know the sea."

"I've never even seen it," says Cosmas. And he realises it is true. He grew up in Damascus, a city of gardens on the edge of the desert. He has spent years in a gorge where the nearest water is the Dead Sea, which hardly counts. And now he is going to be bishop of a harbour town.

He takes the extra cloak.

Elias, I think, would be there too, a little further back, trying not to show that his eyes were wet, and holding out a small bundle of very well sharpened pens.

Maiuma, when Cosmas got there, would not have looked much like a great Christian city. Things had changed a lot in the Holy Land in the century before. The Arab armies had come, a hundred years earlier, and the land was now ruled by the caliph. Christians were still very many, in towns and villages all across the country. They kept their churches and their bishops and their monasteries. But they were no longer in charge, and they paid a special tax, and they had to be careful.

And then, in Cosmas's time, there was a change of rulers. In the year seven hundred and fifty, the Umayyad caliphs in Damascus were overthrown by a new family, the Abbasids, who would build a new capital far to the east, at Baghdad. Damascus, the city where Cosmas grew up, stopped being the centre of the world. These were uncertain years. But a bishop of a little harbour town does not mostly deal with the fate of empires. He deals with people.

So let me tell you about some people in Maiuma. They are invented, I should say again. But there must have been people very like them.

There is a fishing family who live in a house near the harbour, with a blue door, because blue was supposed to be lucky. The father goes out in the boat every night and comes home every morning smelling of fish. The mother mends nets. And the grandmother sits on a stool outside the blue door, in the sun, and sells dates, and knows everything that happens in Maiuma, generally before it has happened.

And there is a boy. Let us call him Andreas. He is eleven when the new bishop arrives. He is supposed to be learning to fish, but he is bad at it. He gets seasick. He drops things over the side. What he likes is the church, because it is cool inside, and because there is singing.

So it is Andreas who sees the new bishop first. He is sitting on the harbour wall, avoiding the boat, when a thin man in a dusty monk's habit comes down the road from Gaza, with one bag and two cloaks, and stops at the edge of the water. And just stands there. Looking at the sea.

Andreas watches him for a while. The man doesn't move.

"Are you all right?" Andreas says finally.

The man turns. He has a kind face, and there are tears in his eyes, which is embarrassing, because he is clearly a grown-up.

"I've never seen it before," the man says. "The sea. I've read about it all my life. Moses crossing it. Jonah being thrown into it. The Lord walking on it. I've written verses about it. And I've never seen it." He looks back at the water. "It's much bigger than I thought."

Andreas considers this. He has seen the sea every day of his life. He has never thought of it as anything except wet and cold and full of fish that he is not very good at catching.

"It's mostly just water," he offers.

"Yes," says the man. "That's what's so astonishing."

And then he asks the way to the church, and Andreas shows him, and only when they get there and the old priest comes running out and kneels down in the dust, does Andreas realise that the dusty man with two cloaks is the new bishop.

His grandmother, I'm sorry to say, had known since Tuesday.

We don't know what kind of bishop Cosmas was. The old sources say he ruled his church well and lived a holy life, and that is about all they say. So I will tell you what bishops in a place like Maiuma had to do, and you can imagine him doing it.

He had to say the services, every day. He had to preach. He had to baptise babies, and bury the dead, and marry young couples, and settle quarrels between neighbours about whose goat ate whose cabbages. He had to look after the poor, the widows and orphans, which a man who had been an orphan himself would perhaps not have needed to be told twice. He had to visit the sick. He had to keep the church roof from falling in. And he had to train the clergy, the priests and deacons and readers, including teaching them to sing.

That last part, I think, he enjoyed.

So let us say that, quite soon after he arrives, the bishop notices that there is a boy who is always hanging about the church when he is supposed to be on his father's boat. And one day the bishop calls him over.

"You're the boy from the harbour wall."

"Yes, Father. Sorry, Your Grace. Sorry."

"Do you like singing?"

Andreas thinks about lying, and decides there is no point. "Yes."

"Sing me something."

So Andreas sings him the only thing he knows all the way through, which is a psalm the old priest taught him. And he gets the tune slightly wrong in the middle, and goes too high at the end, and stops, and looks at his feet.

The bishop is quiet for a moment. Then he says, "That was terrible."

Andreas's face falls.

"In the middle," says the bishop. "The middle was terrible. The beginning was very good. And the end would have been very good if you'd breathed first. Come back tomorrow, after the morning service. Bring your grandmother's permission. And don't tell your father yet, because I'm going to need to think of a way to explain it to him."

And so, the way these things happen, a fisherman's boy who was bad at fishing became a reader in the church at Maiuma, and learned his letters, and learned the tunes, and learned to breathe first. And his father, once he got used to the idea, was rather proud. Because there was, after all, one fewer person dropping things over the side of the boat.

The years went by. Cosmas kept writing.

Not just for Christmas. Under his name, the service books of the Eastern Church carry canons for some of the greatest feasts of the year. For the Theophany, the baptism of Christ in the Jordan, which the East keeps with great joy in January, blessing the waters. For the Transfiguration, when Christ shone on the mountain before Peter and James and John. For the Exaltation of the Holy Cross in September. For Palm Sunday. For Holy Thursday, the night of the Last Supper. And short canons, of only two or three odes, for the evenings of Holy Week, the quietest and saddest and holiest week of all.

I find it rather moving that a man who had never seen the sea until he was grown up would write so many hymns about water. The Red Sea parting. Jonah. The Jordan, where Christ was baptised. Perhaps he wrote some of them looking out of a window at the harbour of Maiuma. I hope so.

And there is one short verse that the Church has long credited to Cosmas, which you may know even if you have never been to an Orthodox church in your life. It is sung at nearly every service in the Byzantine churches, every day. In tradition it comes from the ninth ode of one of his short canons for Holy Friday. It is a verse in praise of the Mother of God. In my own plain words, it goes like this.

More honourable than the cherubim, and more glorious beyond compare than the seraphim, you who without stain gave birth to God the Word, true Mother of God, we magnify you.

Think about that. A verse written by a quiet bishop in a little harbour town, perhaps, twelve hundred years ago, and it is sung every single day, all over the world, by people who mostly have no idea where it came from. Every day. If you added up all the times it has ever been sung, you would get a number so large that I don't think there is a word for it. There is probably a word for it in Greek. The Greeks had words for everything.

So let's go back to the start. To the night I showed you at the beginning.

It is Christmas, some years after Cosmas came to Maiuma. Andreas is fourteen now. His voice is in the middle of breaking, which is a terrible time for a reader, because you never know if a note is going to come out as a squeak or a croak, and sometimes it is both, one after the other.

And the bishop has asked him to sing the first verse of the canon on Christmas night. In front of everyone.

All day he has been sick with nerves. His grandmother has given him a spoonful of honey for his throat, and then another, and then told him firmly to stop eating the honey. His father has offered to take him out on the boat to calm him down, which did not calm him down at all. And now it is the middle of the night, and the church is full, every fisherman and sailor and trader and grandmother in Maiuma, and the candles are lit, and the bishop is in his chair, and it is time.

Before he starts, Andreas looks over at the bishop. And the old man leans forward, just a little, and says, so quietly that nobody else can hear:

"Breathe first."

So Andreas breathes. And he sings. And his voice cracks on the very first word, and somebody at the back laughs, kindly, and he goes red to the ears.

And he keeps going anyway.

"Christ is born. Glorify him. Christ from heaven. Go out to meet him."

And the whole church sings it back, so loudly that the candle flames lean over.

Afterwards, in the grey of the morning, when everybody has gone home to sleep and eat and sleep again, Andreas finds the bishop sitting on the harbour wall, in the same place where he first saw him. Looking at the sea.

"My voice cracked," says Andreas.

"It did," says Cosmas. "On the first word."

"Everyone heard."

"Everyone did." The bishop pats the wall beside him, and Andreas sits down. "Do you know what Saint Gregory did, the man who first said those words? When he first preached in Constantinople, his church was so small and his people were so few that his enemies laughed at him. Once, he tells us, they even came to his little church with stones. He was not at all what the great city thought a bishop should look like."

"What happened?"

"They're still singing his words," says Cosmas. "Here. Tonight. Four hundred years later, in a fishing town he never heard of, sung by a boy whose voice cracked." He smiles. "The words don't belong to Gregory, Andreas. Or to me. Or to you. They belong to the one who was born. We just get to carry them for a while, and pass them on, and try not to drop them."

Andreas thinks about this. Then he says, "I'm very good at dropping things. Ask my father."

"I know," says the bishop. "That's why God gave you a voice instead of a net."

And they sit there together, the old man and the boy, and watch the light come up over the water, gold and pink and then white, the sea that is mostly just water, and that is, if you look at it properly, astonishing.

We don't know how Cosmas's life ended. The old sources do not tell us the year he died, and they do not tell us how. Some books say he lived on into the second half of the seven hundreds, and that his brother John died before him, at Mar Saba, around the year seven hundred and fifty. If that is right, then there was a time, perhaps a long time, when Cosmas was the last one left. The last of the two boys from the house in Damascus with the fountain and the fig tree.

I hope there was somebody to sing for him at the end. I like to think there was. A young reader, perhaps, who had learned to breathe first.

The Orthodox Church keeps his feast on the fourteenth of October, and calls him Saint Cosmas the hymn-writer, or sometimes Saint Cosmas the Poet, bishop of Maiuma. And his brother John's feast is on the fourth of December. So they are kept apart in the calendar. But they are never apart in the service books. Every Christmas, they stand side by side again. First the canon of Cosmas, then the canon of John. The two brothers, still singing.

And there is one more thing, which I think Cosmas would have liked best of all.

In the Eastern churches, Christmas does not just arrive on the twenty-fifth of December. It is announced. On the twenty-first of November, more than a month before, on the feast of the Entry of the Mother of God into the Temple, the choirs begin to sing Cosmas's verse for the first time that year. Christ is born. Glorify him. It comes in quietly at the end of each ode, like a herald at the gate, a full five weeks early, to tell everybody to get ready.

So if you go into an Orthodox church on a dark evening late in November, as the year is running down and the nights are drawing in, you may well hear it. A choir somewhere at the front, by candlelight, singing the words that Gregory shouted in a little borrowed church, that Cosmas set to music in a cave in the desert, that a boy with a cracking voice sang in a fishing town by the sea.

Christ is born. Glorify him.

It is dark there now, in the Kidron gorge. The monastery of Mar Saba is still there, clinging to the cliff, and there are still monks in it, and they still sing the long night service. And far away to the west, on the coast, the sea is still breathing against the shore, the way it has always done, and the stars are out over the water.

And you have had a long day. Perhaps you have been carrying something heavy. Words you meant to say and didn't. Or words you said and wish you hadn't. You can put them down now. They are not yours to carry through the night. Somebody much bigger than you is holding all the words of the world, the good ones and the broken ones, and none of them are lost.

So rest. Breathe first. And let the old song carry you, the way the sea carries a boat.

That is the end of the story. The rest is only notes.

Now the notes for the curious.

Our sources tonight are fewer than usual, because Cosmas left so little behind except his hymns. The hymns themselves are in the service books. For English readers, the Festal Menaion and the Lenten Triodion, translated by Mother Mary and Archimandrite Kallistos Ware, have his great canons. Andrew Louth's book on John of Damascus discusses Cosmas and the honest doubts about his life. Egon Wellesz's history of Byzantine music and hymnography explains how canons work. And Gregory of Nazianzus's sermon on the birth of Christ, his thirty-eighth oration, really does begin, Christ is born, glorify him.

What is history. That Cosmas was a monk and hymn-writer of Palestine in the eighth century, close to John of Damascus, and bishop of Maiuma, the harbour of Gaza. That the canons we named are sung under his name, and that his Christmas canon opens with Gregory's words, and that the Christmas and Theophany canons stand in the books beside iambic canons credited to John. That the Christmas verses begin to be sung on the twenty-first of November.

What is tradition. That Cosmas was an orphan adopted by John's father, the ransomed teacher from Italy, and the two brothers going to Mar Saba together, come from the later Life of John of Damascus, and some historians doubt parts of it. The hymn More honourable than the cherubim is credited to him by long tradition. The acrostic hidden in the Christmas canon is reported by the scholars who have studied the Greek text. His dates of birth and death are not known.

What we invented. Elias, Andreas and his grandmother, the night in the cell, the farewell on the cliff, the harbour, the Christmas night, and nearly every word anybody says. The hymns are given in our own plain paraphrase.

Most of us will never stand in front of a king. Nobody is going to send a man with a sharp-pleated kilt to fetch us to a palace. But every one of us, at some time or other, will be asked to go along with something we know is wrong. It might be very small. Leaving someone out. Laughing when we should not. Keeping quiet. And we will be frightened, or embarrassed, or tired, and we will not have the right words.

And on those days I hope we remember Shiphrah and Puah, walking home together on a dusty path in the evening. They did not have an army. They did not even have a very good speech. They just knew, quite simply, who was really in charge. And so they went on doing what they had always done. They knelt on the floor in the lamplight, and they caught the babies, and they wiped their faces, and they put them into their mothers' arms.

And God noticed. He always does.

Some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the Book of Exodus, chapter one, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians read as Scripture. The new king who did not know Joseph, the store cities of Pithom and Raamses, the more they afflicted them the more they grew, the king's order to the midwives, the two stones, the fear of God, the king's question, the midwives' answer about the lively Hebrew women, God dealing well with them and making them houses, and the king's last order about the river, are all in verses eight to twenty-two. The king's words, his question and the midwives' answer are kept as the book gives them, in a plain rendering, and I have only added a few words of Puah's around the edges.

The book does not say whether the midwives were Hebrews or Egyptians; Josephus thought they were Egyptians, and the rabbis of the Talmud thought they were Hebrews. It does not say how many midwives there were, or which king this was. The meanings of the two names are guesses. The tradition that Shiphrah and Puah were Jochebed and Miriam is in the Talmud, tractate Sotah, and Rashi's readings of the names and the houses are in his commentary on these verses. Saint Augustine wrote about the midwives in Against Lying, and Saint Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae, where he says they were rewarded for their fear of God and their kindness, not for any untruth. The old Egyptian tale of the three royal births is in the Westcar Papyrus.

And Zibiah, Leah and her family, the walks to and from the palace, the porridge, and every word the midwives say to each other, are our own invention.

That's the story of Shiphrah and Puah for tonight. They have no feast day of their own that we can vouch for, but the Orthodox Church remembers all the righteous of the Old Testament together on the Sunday of the Holy Forefathers, in the weeks before Christmas, and that seems a good day to remember two midwives too.

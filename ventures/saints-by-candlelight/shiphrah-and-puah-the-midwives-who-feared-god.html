It is the grey part of the morning, before the birds have properly started.

In a small mud-brick house, in a narrow street, somebody has just been born. You can tell, because the room has gone from very loud to very quiet, and then, after a long, long breath, to loud again in a completely different way. A thin, cross, wonderful little wail.

A woman is kneeling on the floor with the baby in her two hands. She is not young. Her sleeves are rolled to the elbow. It is a boy.

Hello, and welcome to Saints by Candlelight, stories of saints, angels and patriarchs from the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, told for bedtime. These episodes are written by an AI and read by a synthetic voice, and they try very hard to be faithful. Let me tell you where we are.

We are in Egypt, in the north, in the flat green country where the Nile spreads out into many channels on its way to the sea. If you have been listening to the last few nights, you know this place already. The land of Goshen. Palm trees and barley fields, reeds taller than a man, and black mud left behind by the river every year, so rich you could almost watch things grow.

Living here are the people of Israel, the children and grandchildren and great-great-grandchildren of Jacob, who came down to Egypt long ago in a time of famine, seventy people in all, the book says, when Joseph his son was the second man in the kingdom. They were welcome then. And the book of Exodus begins by telling us, in one of its first sentences, that the people of Israel were fruitful, and increased greatly, and multiplied, and grew exceedingly strong, so that the land was filled with them.

It is a happy sentence. It sounds a lot like the very first blessing in the Bible, on the first page of Genesis, when God tells every living thing, be fruitful and multiply. Families. Children. Houses full of noise.

And then comes the sad sentence. There arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.

We are never told this king's name. I want you to notice that, because it is going to matter at the end. The book calls him the king of Egypt, or Pharaoh, which is not really a name at all, more like a title. It means something like the great house, the way people today might say the palace when they mean whoever lives in it. People have spent a great deal of ink trying to work out which Pharaoh it was. The book simply does not care to tell us.

What it does tell us is what he did. He looked at all these foreigners, so many of them, and he was afraid. He said to his people, look, they are too many, and too strong for us. If there is ever a war, they might join our enemies. Come, let us deal shrewdly with them.

And dealing shrewdly, it turned out, meant bricks. Taskmasters, and long days in the mud, building store cities for the king called Pithom and Raamses. Hard service, the book says, in mortar and brick and in every kind of work in the field.

But here is the strange thing, and the book says it with something like a smile. The more the Egyptians oppressed them, the more they multiplied, and the more they spread abroad. The plan was not working. You cannot really frighten a people into having fewer children. Babies, as anyone who has ever had any will tell you, do not consult the government.

So the king, who was clearly not the kind of man to give up on a bad idea, had another one. And to carry it out, he needed help. He needed the people who were there, in the room, when the babies came.

He needed the midwives.

Now, let me tell you a little about midwives, because they are some of the quiet heroes of the whole Bible, and they do not get many episodes.

A midwife is a woman who helps other women have their babies. The word in English is very old, and it simply means with-woman, the woman who is with the mother. In the ancient world there were no hospitals, and no doctors standing by in a white coat. When a baby was coming, somebody ran for the midwife. Day or night. Rain or shine. In the middle of supper, or in the middle of the night, which, as every midwife in history will tell you, is when babies prefer to arrive.

The Bible mentions them a few times, always at the big moments. There is a midwife with Rachel, when her youngest son Benjamin is born, telling her not to be afraid. There is a midwife with Tamar, when she has twins, who ties a scarlet thread on the wrist of the first little hand to come out, so that there is no argument later about who was the elder. And then the first one pulls his hand back in and his brother comes out first instead, which I think tells you everything you need to know about twins.

A midwife knew things. She knew how to tell when the baby was coming, and how long it would be, and when something was wrong. She knew which herbs to use, and how to rub a mother's back, and how to say, very calmly, that everything was going well, even when she was not quite sure yet. She knew how to cut the cord and wash the baby and rub it with a little salt, and wrap it up snug in strips of cloth, which is what people did then, and what they still did when another baby was born many centuries later in Bethlehem.

And in Egypt she knew about the stones.

When the king gives his order in a moment, he talks about the birthstool. The Hebrew word is a funny little word, and it means something like the two stones. In the old world, a woman often did not lie down to give birth. She knelt, or crouched, on two bricks or stones, one under each foot, with a little space between them, and the midwife knelt in front of her, ready to catch. The Egyptians took this so seriously that one of their goddesses was the goddess of the birth brick. The only other place in the Bible where that same little Hebrew word appears is in the prophet Jeremiah, where it means the potter's wheel, the two round stones a potter spins, one above the other, to shape the clay. And I think that is rather lovely. The place where the potter makes a jar, and the place where a mother has a child. Both of them, the two stones.

So that is a midwife. Strong hands. Calm voice. Good at waiting, and very good at being woken up.

And in the land of Goshen, at this time, the book names two of them.

One of them was called Shiphrah. The other was called Puah.

Shiphrah probably comes from a word that means beautiful, or bright, or fair, the way the sky is fair after rain. Nobody is quite sure what Puah means. Some think it means a girl, and some think something like splendid. I like to think of them as Shiphrah the Fair and Puah the Splendid, which sounds like two queens from a fairy tale, and that is a little joke, because they were nothing of the kind. They were two working women with tired backs and very clean hands.

Now, you may be thinking, only two? For a whole people, so many that the king was frightened of them? That is a very good question, and readers have asked it for a very long time. Perhaps there were more, and these two were the chief midwives, in charge of all the others, the ones the king would naturally send for. Perhaps the people were not so many in those early years as they would become. The book does not say. It just gives us two names, and that is all we need for tonight.

And there is one more question, which I will tell you about now, so that you can keep it in your pocket. The book calls them the Hebrew midwives. In Hebrew, those words can be read two ways. They can mean midwives who were Hebrews. Or they can mean midwives to the Hebrews, women who served the Hebrew mothers, and might themselves have been Egyptians. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the time of the apostles, thought they were Egyptian women. Most readers have thought they were Hebrews. The book does not settle it, and so, tonight, neither will I. They were midwives. They feared God. That is the part the book wants us to remember.

So let us go and meet them. And let us bring somebody with us, a girl of our own invention.

Her name is Ayala, which means a young deer. She is thirteen years old. She lives two streets away from the midwives' house, with her mother and her grandmother and a great many brothers, and since the spring she has been going round with Shiphrah and Puah, carrying the bag.

The bag is important. It is a big leather bag with a strap, and Ayala has learned exactly what goes in it. Clean linen, folded. Strips of cloth for swaddling. A little jar of oil. A little jar of salt. A knife, very sharp, wrapped in its own cloth, which she is not allowed to touch. Some dried herbs, which Puah sniffs every morning, frowning, to make sure they are still good. A lamp, and a pot of oil for the lamp. And a round flat loaf, wrapped in a cloth at the bottom, because Shiphrah says that a midwife who forgets to eat is a midwife who will faint at the wrong moment, and there is never a right moment.

Ayala has learned other things too. She has learned to boil water without being asked. She has learned that when Puah says, quite softly, Ayala, would you take the little ones outside to look at the goats, it means things are about to get busy, and the little ones should not be in the room. She has learned to hold the lamp very still. And she has learned, which surprised her, that most of a midwife's work is waiting. Sitting with the mother. Talking. Rubbing her back. Telling stories. Laughing, sometimes. And then, all at once, not waiting at all.

The midwives' house is at the end of a street, with a blue-painted door, so that people running in the dark can find it. And on the morning our story begins, there is somebody at the door who is not running, and who is not a frightened father.

It is a man in a white linen kilt, very clean, with a staff, and the look of a man who works for someone important and wants you to know it.

The king, he says, wishes to see the midwives. Today.

Today, she says.

Today, he says.

Ayala does not know what that look means yet. But she will.

They walk to the palace. Or at least, to the place where the king is staying, for the kings of Egypt had more than one great house, and moved between them. Ayala is allowed to come as far as the outer gate, carrying the bag, because Shiphrah says that if they are needed for a birth on the way home she does not want to go back for it.

I will not pretend to know what that palace looked like. But I can tell you what Egyptian palaces of that age were like, in general, and it would have been nothing like anything Ayala had seen. Great courtyards. Painted columns shaped like bundles of papyrus. Ceilings painted blue, with gold stars. Pools with lotus flowers. Servants everywhere, carrying things on trays, and every one of them walking very quietly. It is the sort of place where you feel your feet are too loud.

Ayala waits at the gate, sitting on the bag, for a very long time. Watching the guards. Counting the pigeons. Trying not to be nervous for no reason.

Inside, the two midwives are brought before the king.

And now I am going to tell you exactly what the book says he said, and nothing more, because this is Scripture.

The king of Egypt said to the Hebrew midwives, one of whom was called Shiphrah and the other Puah, When you help the Hebrew women to give birth, and you see them on the birthstool, if it is a son, you shall kill him. But if it is a daughter, she shall live.

That is all he said. And I will not add anything to it, because it is a dreadful thing, and the book does not linger, and neither shall we.

But I would like you to see what kind of order it was. It was a quiet order. A secret order. He did not tell his soldiers to do it. He did not announce it in the market. He wanted it to happen where nobody would see, in a little room in the middle of the night, so that it would look like the sad things that sometimes happen at a birth anyway, and nobody would be able to say for sure that the king had done it. He wanted the people who were trusted most, the ones the mothers called for when they were most afraid, to be the ones who betrayed them.

And he picked midwives because he thought they would be easy. Two women. Ordinary working women, who lived in his country by his leave. What could they possibly do, against the king of Egypt?

The book does not tell us what Shiphrah and Puah said to him. I think it is very likely that they said nothing at all. You do not argue with the king of Egypt in his own hall. You bow. You listen. You go home.

So they bowed. And they listened. And they went home.

Ayala sees them coming out of the gate, and she jumps up, and she knows at once that something is wrong. Puah is walking very fast. Shiphrah is walking very slowly. Neither of them is talking. They do not even tell her to pick up the bag, and she picks it up anyway and hurries after them.

They walk all the way back through the fields, by the long path along the canal, where the reeds are, and the herons stand on one leg in the shallows looking disappointed with the world, as herons do. And still nobody says anything.

At last, at the place where the path crosses the little bridge, Puah stops. She puts both her hands on the wooden rail, and she looks down at the water.

Well, she says.

Well, says Shiphrah.

Ayala waits. She has learned to wait.

Puah says, still looking at the water, I have been doing this for thirty years. I have caught, I do not know how many. Hundreds. I caught your brothers, Ayala. All five of them. And every one of them, the first thing I did was look, and see that he was breathing, and say thank God.

She stops.

Shiphrah says, gently, I know.

And then Puah says something that I think is the whole of tonight's story, in a few words. She says, He is the king. But he is not God.

Now, that sentence is our invention. The book does not give us a word they said to each other. What the book gives us is something better. It gives us the reason. It says, but the midwives feared God, and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them, but let the male children live.

The midwives feared God.

That is an old phrase, and it is easy to misunderstand. To fear God, in the Bible, does not mean to be frightened of him, the way you might be frightened of a big dog, or of a king with a staff and a bad idea. It means something more like this. To know who God really is. To know that he is the one who made every one of those babies, and every one of their mothers, and every one of us. To know that he is real, and good, and that he sees what is done in small rooms in the middle of the night. And so to care more about what God thinks than about what anybody else thinks, even the king.

The Book of Proverbs says, the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. And here are two midwives, who had probably never read a book in their lives, being wiser than all the king's advisers in all the painted halls of Egypt.

Shiphrah looks at Ayala, who is standing very still with the bag, her eyes very big.

Not a word of this to anyone, she says.

Not a word, says Ayala.

Good girl, says Shiphrah. Now. Whose baby is next?

And Ayala, who has been keeping count, says it is the weaver's wife, on the street with the fig tree, any day now.

Then we had better go and see how she is, says Shiphrah. And they did.

So the midwives went on being midwives.

That is all they did. They did not lead a rebellion. They did not make a speech. They did not run away. They just kept getting up in the night, and going where they were called, and kneeling down by the two stones, and catching the babies, and saying thank God, and wrapping them up warm, and giving them to their mothers. Girls and boys alike. Exactly as they always had.

I want to stay with that for a moment, because I think it is one of the bravest kinds of courage there is, and one of the least noticed. It is not the courage of a soldier in a battle, all at once, with a trumpet. It is the courage of somebody who has been told to do something wrong, and who just quietly goes on doing what is right, every day, one baby at a time, knowing that at any moment somebody might notice. It is the courage of people who will never be in a history book, and who keep the world going anyway.

Let us imagine some of those nights.

There is the weaver's wife, on the street with the fig tree, whose baby comes at midnight in a thunderstorm, which is not at all common in Egypt, so that the weaver keeps going outside to look at it and has to be told to come back in and hold the lamp. It is a boy. A big one, with a great deal of black hair, and a voice like his father's. Puah holds him up and says, well, he will not be hard to find in a crowd.

There is the young mother on the edge of the fields, whose first baby is very slow in coming, all one long day and into the night, so that Ayala falls asleep sitting up against the wall and wakes to find it is all over, and a tiny girl is asleep on her mother's chest, and Shiphrah is sitting in the doorway eating the round flat loaf from the bottom of the bag, looking very tired and very pleased.

There are twins in the house by the well, a boy and a girl, and the grandmother tries to tie a scarlet thread on the first one's wrist, because that is what was done for Tamar's twins, and Puah tells her, very kindly, that this is not that sort of story, and the baby has more important things to think about.

There is a family from the tribe of Levi, in a house near the river, with a father called Amram and a mother called Jochebed. They have a daughter, a quick, watchful little girl, and now a baby boy. The book tells us, a little later, that their son Aaron was three years older than his famous brother. Which means that he was born about now, in these very years. We do not know who caught him. Perhaps it was Shiphrah, or Puah, or another midwife whose name the book does not keep. But somebody did. Somebody knelt by the stones, and saw a little boy, and said thank God, and wrapped him up warm. And that little boy grew up to stand beside Moses before Pharaoh, and to be the first high priest of Israel.

You never know, when you catch a baby, who you are catching.

And all the while, in the land of Goshen, there were more children. More boys running about the streets with sticks. More babies crying in the night. More of exactly what the king did not want.

It was not going to stay a secret for ever. Somebody, somewhere, was going to count.

And one day, the man in the white kilt came back to the house with the blue door.

This time Ayala was not allowed even as far as the gate. Shiphrah sent her home. She went home, and sat on the roof of her house, which was flat, as roofs in Egypt were, and watched the road all afternoon, and did not eat her supper, and her grandmother, who knew more than she let on, sat beside her and did not ask why.

In the palace, the king called for the midwives again. And again, I will tell you exactly what the book says.

The king of Egypt called the midwives and said to them, Why have you done this thing, and let the male children live?

And the midwives said to Pharaoh, Because the Hebrew women are not like the Egyptian women. They are lively, and they give birth before the midwife comes to them.

That is their answer. And that is the end of the conversation, as far as the book is concerned. It does not tell us what the king said next. It does not tell us that he believed them, or that he did not. It just moves on.

Now, I promised to be honest with you about what is clear and what is not, and this is one of the places where good readers have disagreed, so let me tell you about it plainly.

The word they use, lively, is a curious one. In Hebrew it is related to the word for life, and it can mean full of life, vigorous, strong. It can also, in some places, mean like the animals, the way a deer or a ewe gives birth out in the field without any fuss and is up and walking about straight afterwards. It is quite possible that there is a sly joke tucked in there, at the king's expense. You think we Hebrews are animals, they might be saying. Well, then. We give birth like them. Quickly. Before anybody can get there.

But was it true?

The book does not say. Perhaps it was partly true. Women who work hard in the fields often do have quicker births, and midwives, then and now, often arrive to find the baby already there and the mother looking very pleased with herself. Perhaps the midwives had begun to be just a little slow, on purpose, in answering the knock at the door. Perhaps the mothers had begun not to send for them until it was too late to matter. Or perhaps it was not true at all, and they said what they said to save their own lives, and the lives of all those children.

Christians have thought hard about this, because Christian teaching holds truthfulness very high, and many great teachers have said that it is never right to tell a lie, even a small one, even to a wicked man. Saint Augustine, around the year four hundred and twenty, wrote a whole little book against lying, and he came to these midwives in it, and he did not dodge them. He said that if what they told the king was a lie, then it was not the lie that God rewarded. It was their mercy. Their kindness to the children. Their fear of God, which would not let them kill. Saint Gregory the Great, late in the sixth century, said much the same, though a little more sternly. He thought their kindness was truly rewarded, but that if there was an untruth in their words, the reward was a smaller one than it might have been, a reward in this life, houses on earth.

I think that is wise, and I am going to leave it there. Notice that the book itself does not praise their answer. It does not say, and God blessed them because they told the king a clever story. It says something quite different. It says the reason twice, as if to make sure we do not miss it. Because they feared God.

Here is the end of the story, as the book tells it. It is only two verses long, and it is very beautiful.

So God dealt well with the midwives. And the people multiplied, and grew very strong. And because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.

The Hebrew says, more exactly, he made them houses.

A house, in the Bible, usually does not mean only a building with a roof and a door. It means a family, and a family that goes on. Children, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren. The house of David. The house of Israel. These two women had spent their whole lives helping other women's houses to grow, catching other people's babies. And God gave them houses of their own. There is a psalm that says, unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labour in vain. Shiphrah and Puah did not build their houses. The Lord did.

There is an old Jewish tradition about these houses, written down by the rabbis many centuries later, and I will tell it to you, as long as you remember that it is a tradition and not Scripture. Some of the rabbis said that Shiphrah and Puah were not strangers at all. They said that Shiphrah was Jochebed, the mother of Moses, and that Puah was Miriam, his sister, and that the houses God gave them were the great families of Israel, the priests who came from Aaron and the kings who came, a long time later, from David. It is a lovely idea. But the book does not say so, and it gives Jochebed and Miriam their own names in their own places, so I think it is kinder to let Shiphrah and Puah be themselves.

And the book does one more thing, which I promised I would come back to.

Remember the king? The great king of Egypt, in his painted hall, with his store cities and his taskmasters and his staff-carrying servants. We do not know his name. The Bible does not keep it. Scholars have argued about it for two hundred years and they still argue.

But the two midwives? We know their names. Shiphrah and Puah. The book took the trouble to write them down, right there in the first chapter of Exodus, before it even tells us the name of Moses. For thousands of years, every time anyone has read this book, in synagogues and churches, in every language in the world, they have said those two names out loud.

I think that is God's sense of humour. The powerful man who wanted to be remembered is forgotten. The two women who only wanted to do their job well are remembered for ever.

I would like to tell you that it all ended well straight away. It did not. The book says that when his quiet plan failed, the king gave a loud one. He commanded all his people that every son born to the Hebrews should be thrown into the Nile. And that is where, a few nights ago, we found a girl standing among the reeds, watching a little basket daubed with pitch, to see what would be done to her brother. That is another story, and if you have heard it, you know how it goes.

But I like to think of Shiphrah and Puah still at work, through all of that. Still getting up in the night. Still kneeling by the two stones. Still saying thank God.

And Ayala, our invented girl, growing up.

I like to think of her, many years later, a midwife herself, with her own leather bag and her own blue door, and a girl of her own, carrying the bag. Teaching her what goes in it. Clean linen. Oil. Salt. The sharp knife you are not allowed to touch. And a round flat loaf at the bottom, because a midwife who forgets to eat will faint at the wrong moment, and there is never a right one.

And perhaps, on the night of a great hurry, a night when all the people of Israel were packing up to leave Egypt in the dark with their bread still in the kneading bowls, perhaps she had to catch a baby in the middle of all that, with the donkeys loaded and everyone shouting. Babies do not wait for history. They never have.

We do not know what happened to Shiphrah and Puah in the end. The book does not tell us. They step into the story for seven verses, and then they step out again, and we never hear of them any more. I think they would have liked that. They were not the sort of women who wanted a fuss. They had done what they were there to do. They had feared God, and let the children live.

Saint Peter, long afterwards, when he was told by the rulers of his own day to stop doing what God had asked of him, said, we must obey God rather than men. He was standing in front of a council, in Jerusalem, and he was not thinking of two midwives in Egypt. But he might have been.

Every one of us, at the very beginning, was caught by somebody. Somebody was there. A mother, a nurse, a doctor, a midwife, a grandmother who happened to be in the house. Somebody looked at us, and saw that we were breathing, and was glad. You did not know their name. Perhaps nobody wrote it down.

But God did.

Now, some notes for the curious.

Tonight's story is from the Book of Exodus, chapter one. The king's order to the midwives, their names, their answer, and God's blessing on them, are all in verses fifteen to twenty-one. The loud order, that every boy should be thrown into the Nile, is verse twenty-two. Aaron's age is in chapter seven. The other midwives, with Rachel and with Tamar, are in Genesis. The potter's wheel, with the same little word as the birthstool, is in Jeremiah, chapter eighteen. Peter's words before the council are in Acts, chapter five.

Whether the midwives were Hebrews or Egyptians who served the Hebrews, the book does not say; Josephus thought they were Egyptians. How many midwives there were, it does not say. Whether their answer to the king was the whole truth, it does not say. Saint Augustine, in his book Against Lying, and Saint Gregory the Great, in his long commentary on the Book of Job, thought that God rewarded their mercy and their fear of him, not any untruth in their words, and Gregory thought an untruth would have made the reward smaller. The tradition that Shiphrah and Puah were Jochebed and Miriam comes from the rabbis, in the Talmud, and so does the reading of the houses as the priests and the kings. They are told as traditions.

And Ayala, her family and her five brothers, the blue door, the leather bag and everything in it, the walk to the palace, the weaver and his thunderstorm, the twins by the well, and every word Shiphrah and Puah say to each other, are our own invention.

David at the Gate of Gath

David runs from Saul with no bread and no sword, eats the holy bread at Nob, and gets out of Gath by acting mad at the gate.

What's true in this story

Who
David before he was king; Ahimelech the priest of Nob; Doeg the Edomite; Achish king of Gath and his servants; David's brothers and the four hundred at the cave of Adullam; the prophet Gad; with an invented old gatekeeper of Gath
Tradition
Catholic and Orthodox; both read the First Book of Samuel and the Psalms as Scripture and honour David as a prophet, a king and a forefather of Christ
Scripture and history
the First Book of Samuel, chapter twenty-one and chapter twenty-two, verses one to five, told straight in a plain rendering, with the titles of Psalms thirty-four and fifty-six, and our Lord's words about David and the bread in Saint Mark, Saint Matthew and Saint Luke
Legend
none told as fact; that David made Psalm thirty-four in the cave of Adullam is our own imagining, since the title says only that it belongs to the time he acted mad, and the name Abimelech in that title is told as a puzzle nobody has settled
What we invented
the old gatekeeper of Gath and his doors, the road, the weather, what David thinks on the way, and the small talk at the cave are our own invention; nothing is added to what David, Ahimelech, Achish, his servants or Gad say or do in the book
Feast day
King David is remembered on the twenty-ninth of December in the Roman Martyrology, and in the Orthodox Church on the Sunday after Christmas, with Saint Joseph the Betrothed and Saint James the brother of the Lord, and with all the forefathers on the Sunday of the Holy Forefathers before Christmas

A thousand years later, on a Sabbath day, a young teacher from Nazareth was walking with his friends through the grainfields of Galilee, and his friends were hungry, and as they walked they picked some of the heads of grain and rubbed them in their hands and ate them. And he said, the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. And in Saint Matthew's telling he adds the words of the prophet Hosea: I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.

So the hungry young man at Nob, with an armful of holy bread, ends up in the Gospels, held up by the Son of David himself as an example of what the law is for.

I should mention one small puzzle there too, since we are being honest tonight. In Saint Mark's Gospel, Jesus says this happened in the time of Abiathar the high priest. In the First Book of Samuel the priest who gave David the bread is Ahimelech, and Abiathar is his son, who escaped from Nob and later became David's priest. Saint Matthew and Saint Luke do not name the priest at all. Scholars and the Fathers have explained it in several ways, and people still discuss it. It does not change the story in the least.

And Psalm thirty-four went on, too, and it went a very long way.

Saint Peter quotes it in his first letter. Like newborn babies, he says, long for the pure spiritual milk, if you have tasted that the Lord is good. And a little later he quotes the line about keeping your tongue from evil.

And then, in the early Church, the psalm found its way to the altar. Saint Cyril of Jerusalem, teaching the newly baptised in Jerusalem in the three hundreds, tells them that when they come up to receive Communion they will hear the singer inviting them, with a holy melody, to the holy mysteries, singing, O taste and see that the Lord is good.

O taste and see. The words of a hungry young man who had eaten the holy bread and escaped from the gate of Gath, sung as Christians came forward to receive the true bread from heaven. In the Orthodox Church those words are still the Communion hymn at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, on the weekdays of Lent. And in Catholic churches the psalm is sung often at Mass, and the line O taste and see has long been sung at Communion.

There is one last thing, and it is the strangest and the loveliest. Saint Augustine, the great bishop of Hippo, preached on this psalm in North Africa, more than fifteen hundred years ago. And the old Latin text he was reading said something odd about David at the gate of Gath. It said that David was carried in his own hands.

And Augustine stopped, the way a good preacher does, and asked his people: how can a man be carried in his own hands? Nobody can do that. A man can be carried in someone else's hands. But in his own?

And then he answered himself. It could not be true of David, he said, not in the plain sense. But it was true of Christ. Because at the Last Supper, when the Lord took the bread into his hands and said, this is my body, he was carrying himself in his own hands.

I think that is a beautiful thing for a preacher to find in a story about a frightened young man scratching on a door. It is the kind of thing the Fathers loved to do: to look at the Old Testament the way you look at a stained glass window, and see the light of Christ coming through it.

As for Gath itself, the city's ruins are still there, on a hill above the plain, at a place called Tell es-Safi. Archaeologists have been digging there for many years, finding walls and houses and pottery from the time of the Philistines. They have not, as far as I know, found any old doors with scratch marks on them. But then wood does not last three thousand years. Even very well oiled wood.

And Nob, the town of priests, is gone so completely that nobody is quite sure where on the hills above Jerusalem it stood.

But the bread is still given. And the psalm is still sung.

A few notes for the curious.

Tonight's story comes from the First Book of Samuel, chapter twenty-one, and chapter twenty-two, verses one to five, told straight in our own plain rendering, not in any one translation. The goodbye in the field is the end of chapter twenty. The rules for the bread of the Presence are in the Book of Leviticus, chapter twenty-four, and the golden table in the Book of Exodus, chapter twenty-five. Ahimelech's family line goes back to Eli through Ahitub and Phinehas, which the book gives in chapter fourteen. The women's song is in chapter eighteen. Nob's view of Jerusalem comes from the Book of Isaiah, chapter ten, and Ruth's family line from the last chapter of the Book of Ruth.

The titles of Psalms thirty-four and fifty-six are quoted as the psalter has them. That David made Psalm thirty-four in the cave of Adullam is our own imagining; the title only ties it to the time he acted mad. The drumming on the doors is in the old Greek translation called the Septuagint. Augustine's sermon is the first of his expositions on what his Bible numbered Psalm thirty-three, and Cyril's words are from his fifth Mystagogical Catechesis. Our Lord's words about David and the bread are in Saint Mark, chapter two, Saint Matthew, chapter twelve, and Saint Luke, chapter six.

The old gatekeeper, his doors, his bench and his bucket are our own invention, and so are the road, the weather, the terebinth tree and the evening at the cave. Where Nob stood is not known for certain. Gath is usually identified with Tell es-Safi, and Adullam with a site in the low hills south-west of Bethlehem.

That's the story of David at the gate of Gath for tonight. King David is remembered on the twenty-ninth of December in the Roman Martyrology, and in the Orthodox Church on the Sunday after Christmas, together with Saint Joseph the Betrothed and Saint James the brother of the Lord, and with all the forefathers on the Sunday before Christmas.

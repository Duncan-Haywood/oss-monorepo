Mephibosheth at the King's Table

Years after the battle on Mount Gilboa, King David asks a strange question in his palace at Jerusalem, whether anyone is left of the house of Saul, so that he can show him kindness for Jonathan's sake; the answer is a grown man who cannot walk, living quietly across the Jordan in a place whose name sounds like nothing, and who is carried before the king expecting the worst and is given a seat at the table for the rest of his life. Told from the Second Book of Samuel, chapter nine, with the promise Jonathan asked for in First Samuel twenty and the day by the Jordan in Second Samuel nineteen.

And Mephibosheth says, "My lord the king, my servant deceived me. I said to him, saddle me a donkey, so that I can ride on it and go with the king, because your servant is lame. And he slandered your servant to my lord the king."

In other words: I told Ziba to saddle a donkey for me. He saddled two. And he took them both, and went to meet you, and left me behind. A man who cannot walk cannot follow a king out of a city on foot.

And then Mephibosheth says something that I think is one of the most beautiful things anyone says in the Books of Samuel.

"But my lord the king is like an angel of God. So do whatever seems good to you. All my grandfather's house were nothing but dead men before my lord the king. And yet you set your servant among those who eat at your table. What right have I, then, to cry out to the king any more?"

He does not argue. He does not ask for his land back. He has had the table. He has had the kindness. That, to him, is the thing that matters.

And David, who has had a very long and very terrible few weeks, and who has two stories in front of him and no way of knowing for certain which one is true, says, "Why say any more about it? I have decided. You and Ziba shall divide the land."

And Mephibosheth says, "Oh, let him take it all. Since my lord the king has come home safely."

Let him take it all.

Now. Here is the honest bit. The Bible does not tell us, for certain, who was telling the truth, Ziba or Mephibosheth. It gives us both stories and lets us decide. It was a long time ago, and there was nobody taking notes on the road.

But it does give us the feet, and the beard, and the unwashed clothes. And it gives us those last words. Let him take it all. That is not the voice of a man who wanted a kingdom. That is the voice of a man who only ever wanted to be at the table.

I know what I think. And I suspect you do, too.

So let us start to wind down now, and let the lamps burn a little lower.

There is one more thing the Bible tells us about Mephibosheth, and it is said in one line. Years later, there came a hard time, when the house of Saul was in danger once more, and others of Saul's family did not come through it. But the king spared Mephibosheth, the son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, because of the oath of the Lord that was between them, between David and Jonathan.

The oath. The promise, on that last day in the field, before David ran. Do not cut off your kindness from my house. Not ever.

David kept it. It held. Not once, but again, and again, for as long as both of them lived.

And then the Bible lets him go, quietly, the way it lets most people go. We are not told when Mephibosheth died, or where. I like to think it was in Jerusalem, near the table, an old man by then, with his sticks beside him and his son's children running about the palace, sliding down the steps.

Because his family did go on. In the First Book of Chronicles, there is a long list of names, the kind of list people tend to skip when they are reading the Bible straight through. But if you look at it closely, you will find Merib-baal, the son of Jonathan, and his son Mica, and Mica's sons, and their sons after them, generation after generation, a whole family tree growing out of one small lame boy who should not, by all the rules of kings, have lived to grow up.

And near the end of that list, many generations on, it says that some of them were mighty men, archers, with many sons and grandsons.

Archers. Like Jonathan. Who could put an arrow wherever he liked.

When David heard that Jonathan had died, he wrote a song, and in it he said that the bow of Jonathan never turned back. I like to think that, in a way, it never did.

There is one more table I want to mention before we go.

Many centuries later, a teacher was at a dinner party, on a sabbath, in the house of an important man, and he noticed how the guests were all trying to get the best seats. And he told them a story about a man who gave a great banquet, and when the important guests made their excuses, the man said to his servant, go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the town, and bring in the poor, and the crippled, and the blind, and the lame.

That teacher was a son of David, a long way down that family tree. And his table, too, is for the people who think of themselves as dead dogs, and who come from places called Nothing, and who have to be carried in.

Christians have always heard something of that in tonight's story. The king who goes looking for the one who is hiding. Who says, do not be afraid. Who does not wait for him to earn it, or to walk in on his own two feet. Who just says his name, and sets a place for him, and keeps it set.

Always, the book says. He ate at the king's table, always.

So let us leave him there. Late in the evening, in the long room in Jerusalem. The lamps are low. The musicians are playing something slow. The princes have gone off to bed, and the bread has been cleared away, and the last of the wine is in the jars.

And at the table, near the king, there is a man with a pair of sticks propped against the wall beside him, and a cup in his hand, and a small boy asleep with his head on his knee.

He is not afraid any more.

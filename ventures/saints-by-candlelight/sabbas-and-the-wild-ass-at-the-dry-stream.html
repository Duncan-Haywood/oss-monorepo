Sabbas and the Wild Ass at the Dry Stream

A boy from Cappadocia who once stole an apple grows into the old man of the desert gorge east of Jerusalem, where a wild ass shows him where to dig, a lion is asked to share a cave, and a monastery hung on a cliff is still praying fifteen hundred years later. The life of Saint Sabbas the Sanctified, from Cyril of Scythopolis.

It is night, in a deep, dry gorge in the desert, and the moon is up.

On a ledge high on the cliff, an old monk is sitting very still, with his knees drawn up and his cloak around him, looking down. Below him, at the bottom of the gorge, there is a stream bed. It is made of stones and gravel, and there is no water in it. There has not been water in it for months.

And down there, on the stones, in the moonlight, there is a wild ass.

It is a small, pale, scruffy animal, with long ears and a dark stripe down its back. And it is digging. It is pawing at the gravel with one front hoof, scrape, scrape, scrape, the way a dog digs for a bone. Then it puts its head down into the hole it has made.

And it drinks.

The old monk on the ledge lets out a long breath, and very quietly, he begins to laugh.

Last night we were in the green, wet Basque country with Saint Ignatius and his mule. Tonight we are going about as far from green and wet as you can get. We are going back east, and back in time, about a thousand years before Ignatius, to the desert just outside Jerusalem.

If you stand on the Mount of Olives and look east, away from the city, the land does something strange. It falls. It drops away in long brown folds, down and down, towards the Dead Sea, which is the lowest place on the dry face of the earth. That falling country is the Judaean desert. It is not a desert of sand dunes, like in the films. It is a desert of rock and chalk and gravel, cut through by deep, steep gorges, where it hardly rains at all for most of the year, and then, in winter, it can rain so hard all at once that a dry stream bed turns into a roaring brown river for an afternoon and then dries up again by the next morning.

It is a hard place to live. Which is exactly why, in the four hundreds and five hundreds, it filled up with monks.

There were, at one time, dozens and dozens of monasteries out there. Some of them were great walled houses where the monks lived all together, ate together, prayed together. Those were called coenobia, which just means places of common life. And some of them were something different, a kind of monastery that grew up especially in this desert, called a lavra.

Lavra is a Greek word that means a lane, or an alley. And a lavra was more or less exactly that. A path running along a gorge, with little cells scattered along it, caves mostly, or a cave with a bit of wall built on the front, each one with a monk inside it. All week, the monks stayed in their own cells, praying, and working with their hands, usually weaving baskets or ropes out of palm leaves. And then on Saturday and Sunday, they all came down the lane to the church in the middle, and prayed together, and ate together, and handed in their baskets, and collected their bread and their palm leaves for the week. And then off they went again, back to their caves, until next weekend.

I think of it as a sort of holy village where everyone works from home.

Our saint tonight founded the greatest of all the lavras. In Greek, his name is Sabas, with one b, and in Latin and English it is usually Sabbas, with two. In the East he is called Saint Sabbas the Sanctified, and he is one of the great fathers of all monks, of the Catholic and Orthodox Churches both. He lived long before the Churches of East and West went their separate ways, so he belongs to everyone.

And we know a surprising amount about him, because of a man called Cyril. Cyril came from the town of Scythopolis, in the north of the Holy Land, and he became a monk in the same desert, and he spent years going round the monasteries collecting stories from the old monks who had known Sabbas, and writing them down. He wrote his Life of Sabbas in about the year five hundred and fifty-five, about twenty-three years after Sabbas died. Historians like Cyril a great deal. He is careful with his dates, and he names his witnesses. But he also loved a good miracle story, and he wrote down what the old monks told him. So tonight, when I say Cyril tells us, it means just that. Some of it is plain history. Some of it is the kind of story that old monks tell young monks around a fire. I will try to let you know which is which.

Sabbas was not born in the desert. He was born in the year four hundred and thirty-nine, a very long way to the north, in Cappadocia, which is in the middle of what is now Turkey. It is high, cold country, with snowy winters, and strange rock towers made of soft volcanic stone. It is also, as regular listeners will know, the home of Saint Basil and his brother Saint Gregory of Nyssa and their sister Saint Macrina. Sabbas was born about sixty years after Basil died, in a village called Moutalaska, not far from Basil's city of Caesarea. So he grew up in the land of Basil, and the monks there lived by the rules Basil had written.

His father was called John, and his mother was called Sophia. John was a soldier, an officer in the army. And when Sabbas was about five years old, his father's regiment was posted to Alexandria, in Egypt. And John went, and Sophia went with him. And for reasons Cyril does not explain, they left the little boy behind, with his uncle.

That does sound sad, and I think it was. Armies have been splitting families up for as long as there have been armies.

The uncle was called Hermias, and he was kind enough. But his wife, Cyril says, was not. She was hard on the boy. So after a few years, Sabbas ran away, to his other uncle, whose name was Gregory. And then, which is the sort of thing that happens in families everywhere and in every century, the two uncles began to quarrel. Not about the boy, exactly. About the boy's property. His parents had left some land and some money to keep him, and both uncles thought they ought to be the one looking after it.

And the boy, who was about eight years old, listened to the grown-ups arguing about who should have his father's fields. And he thought, I do not want any of this.

Not far from the village, about two miles away, there was a monastery called Flavianae. And Sabbas walked there, and knocked on the door, and asked to be allowed to stay.

The monks took him in. Eight years old. And as far as we know, the uncles stopped arguing after a while, and said sorry, and asked him to come home, and he said, very politely, no thank you.

Now Cyril tells two stories about Sabbas as a boy in that monastery, and they are the sort of stories that are told about young saints. I will tell you both.

The first is about an apple.

The monastery at Flavianae had a garden, and in the garden there was an apple tree. And one morning, the boy was working in the garden, and he saw a beautiful apple on the tree, ripe and red and shining, and it was early in the day, long before the time the monks ate, and the thought came into his head, very clearly, I want that apple.

So he picked it.

And then, before he took a bite, he stopped. He stood there with the apple in his hand. And he thought, no. I am a monk, or I am trying to be. I am not going to be ruled by an apple.

And he threw the apple down on the ground and stamped on it. And Cyril says that from that day on, for the rest of his life, Sabbas never ate an apple again.

Now. I want to be careful here. I am not telling you that apples are bad. Apples are a very good gift of God, and if you have one in your lunchbox tomorrow you should eat it with thanks. The point of the story is not the apple. The point is that the boy noticed that a small wanting had got hold of him, and he decided that he would be the one holding the reins, not the wanting. Every monk in every century would understand that. I think most parents would understand it too.

Although I do think that ninety-four years without a single apple is very thorough.

The second story is stranger. The monastery had a bakery, with a big bread oven, the kind you build a fire inside, let it burn down, rake out the ashes, and then bake in the heat that is left in the bricks. And one day, Cyril says, a monk who looked after the bakery had put some clothes to dry inside the oven, as you might put a wet coat on a radiator. And he forgot about them. And the baker came along, and lit the fire, and the oven roared up.

And somebody remembered the clothes.

And the boy Sabbas climbed straight into the hot oven, and gathered up the clothes, and climbed out again, and his own clothes were not even scorched.

That is the story as Cyril tells it, and as the old monks told it to him. It is told on purpose to remind you of the three young men in the Book of Daniel, who walked about in the fiery furnace and came out without even smelling of smoke. Whether it happened exactly that way, I cannot tell you. But the monks who knew Sabbas as an old man believed that God had been looking after him since he was a boy, and that is what the story is for.

I would ask you, very seriously, not to try it.

Sabbas stayed at Flavianae for about ten years. He learned the psalms by heart, all hundred and fifty of them, which is what young monks did. He learned to work. He learned to keep the rule.

And then, when he was eighteen, he asked permission to go to Jerusalem.

His abbot did not want him to go. But Sabbas kept asking. And in the end, around the year four hundred and fifty-six, off he went, walking south for hundreds of miles, to the Holy City. He visited the holy places. He spent a winter in a monastery near the city. And then he went out into the desert, to find the most famous monk of all.

This was Saint Euthymius, who was by then an old man of about eighty, with a long white beard and a great reputation for holiness, living with his monks in a lavra in the desert east of Jerusalem. Sabbas went to him and asked to join.

And Euthymius looked at him. He looked at this eager eighteen-year-old from Cappadocia, who did not even have a proper beard yet. And he said, more or less, no.

Not unkindly. He said that a lavra, where every monk lives on his own in a cave, is not the right place for a young man. Too much solitude, too young, too soon. A young man needs a community first. He needs brothers around him, and an abbot to keep an eye on him, and a lot of work to do. Go down to the monastery of my old friend Theoctistus, he said. It is just down the valley. Live with the brothers there. Learn obedience. And then we will see.

I think a great deal of wisdom is packed into that no. It would have been easy to be flattered. Here was a bright, keen, holy young man who had walked all the way from Cappadocia to be your disciple. But Euthymius did not want a young man who was keen. He wanted a young man who would last.

So Sabbas went down the valley to the monastery of Saint Theoctistus. And he was, by every account, a wonderful young monk. Cyril says he worked harder than anybody. He carried the water. He carried the wood. He did the jobs nobody else wanted. And there is a nice detail that he was very strong, the kind of strong young man who would carry three loads of firewood when everyone else was carrying one.

There is one more story from those years that I like very much. Some time while he was at the monastery, he went with one of the older monks on an errand to Alexandria, in Egypt. And who should be in Alexandria but his mother and father, still there with the army after all those years.

And they recognised him. And I imagine there were a great many tears.

But his father, being a soldier, had a soldier's idea of a good life for a son. He wanted Sabbas to stay in Alexandria and join the army, where he could have a good rank and a good career. And Sabbas said no. Gently, but no. I serve a different king, he said, or something like it. And his parents, Cyril says, tried to give him money to take back with him, and he refused it, and in the end he took only a very little, to please them, and went back to the desert.

And then Euthymius started taking him on Lent.

Every year, Euthymius had a custom. In the middle of January, he would leave the lavra and go off, with one or two of his closest monks, deep into the most empty part of the desert, down towards the Dead Sea, a place called Rouba, and stay out there in the wild until Palm Sunday, praying, and eating almost nothing but the roots of the desert plants. And now he began to take young Sabbas with him. And Cyril says that Euthymius, who had a dry sense of humour, gave him a nickname. He called him the young old man.

Which I think is a lovely thing to be called. Young in years. Old in good sense.

When Sabbas was about thirty, the abbot gave him permission to live, for most of each week, alone in a cave near the monastery. So he did. From Sunday evening until Saturday morning he stayed in his cave, praying, and working with his hands. And then on Saturday he came back to the monastery with what he had made, and stayed for the weekend, and then went back again.

And what he made was baskets. Palm leaf baskets. Cyril tells us he made fifty of them every week.

Fifty. Every week. I have tried to imagine it, and I think that is about one basket every two hours, all day and half the night, for five days, while praying the psalms out loud the whole time, because that is how the monks did it. Weave, weave, weave, the Lord is my shepherd. Weave, weave, weave, I shall not want. And by the end of the week, a great tottering stack of fifty baskets carried down the path, and the brothers, I imagine, saying, Sabbas, we really have quite enough baskets now.

The monks sold the baskets, in the villages and in Jerusalem, to buy bread. That was how the desert monasteries lived. The baskets went to market, and the bread came back. Regular listeners may remember that some centuries later, Saint John of Damascus was sent to sell baskets in the market as a lesson in humility. That was at a monastery in this same desert. In fact, it was the one Sabbas was about to build.

In the year four hundred and seventy-three, Saint Euthymius died, at the age of ninety-five. And Sabbas, who was by then in his thirties, went further out into the desert to be alone. He wandered for a few years. And then, around the year four hundred and seventy-eight, he came to the gorge.

It is the valley of the Kidron. The Kidron is the little valley that runs along the east side of Jerusalem, between the city and the Mount of Olives, the valley Our Lord crossed on the night before He died. And if you follow it east, out of the city and down into the desert, it gets deeper and deeper, and steeper and steeper, until it is a great twisting canyon with sheer cliffs on either side, hundreds of feet high, all the way down to the Dead Sea.

And about halfway along, high up in the eastern cliff, Sabbas found a cave.

It was not an easy cave to get into. Cyril says he had to use a rope to get up to it. And the cave was his home, alone, for the next five years. He prayed. He wove his baskets. He drew his water from wherever he could find it, sometimes walking a very long way. And the wind blew down the gorge, and the hawks went round, and the sun went over, and he was, as far as anybody can be, happy.

But a holy man in a cave is like a lamp in a window. People notice.

Around the year four hundred and eighty-three, the first disciples began to come. One or two, and then more. Young men who wanted to live as he lived, and asked to stay near him. And Sabbas, who had once been told by Euthymius that young men need a community, remembered. He did not send them away. He found them caves of their own, along the cliff, up and down the gorge, and he became their father. And by the time a few years had gone by, there were seventy of them.

And this was the beginning of the Great Lavra.

Now I want to give you a picture. This is our own picture, I should say, made up from the kind of thing Cyril describes, with a young monk we have invented, called Zenon.

Zenon is nineteen. He arrived at the gorge in the spring, with a walking stick and a cloak and a great many ideas about holiness, and Abba Sabbas has given him a cave about a hundred yards down the cliff, which is small, and has a scorpion in it, which the two of them have since removed together, very politely.

And now it is late summer, and Zenon's main problem in life is water.

Because here is the thing about the gorge. At the bottom of it there is a stream bed. In winter, when it rains, a stream runs there, and sometimes a flood. But by the summer, it is dry. All of it. Stones and gravel and dust. And there are now seventy monks living up and down these cliffs, and every one of them needs water, to drink, and to soak the palm leaves so they can be woven. And the nearest good water is a long, hot walk away, with a jug on your shoulder.

So every few days, Zenon takes his jug and his stick and sets off, up the gorge, along the path, a long way, to the nearest spring, fills the jug, and staggers back, with the sun on his neck and the jug slopping, and arrives at his cave with about two thirds of what he set out with.

"Abba," he says to Sabbas, one evening, putting down his jug with a groan. "There must be water closer than that."

"There must," says Sabbas.

"Can you not pray for some?"

"I have been praying for some," says Sabbas, "since before you were born."

"Then why hasn't any come?"

And the old man smiles, and says, "Perhaps it has, and I have not been looking in the right place."

And now we come to the night we started with.

Cyril tells this story. One night, Sabbas was awake on the cliff, praying, as he usually was in the middle of the night. And the moon was bright. And he looked down into the dry stream bed at the bottom of the gorge, and he saw a wild ass.

Wild asses lived in that desert in those days. They were small, tough animals, a bit like a donkey but wilder and quicker and much less interested in carrying your shopping. And this one was standing in the dry stream bed, digging. Pawing at the gravel with its hoof, deeper and deeper, at one particular spot. And then it put its head down into the hole, and it drank.

Animals know things. A wild ass in the desert knows, in a way we have mostly forgotten, where water hides. And here, under the stones of the dry stream bed, just below the lavra, there was water, running underground, where nobody had thought to look.

So in the morning, Sabbas went down, to the place where the wild ass had been digging. And he dug. And there was water.

Not a great fountain. A small, cool, steady flow, that came up into the hole and filled it. And Sabbas dug it out properly, and lined it, and made a well.

And Cyril says, writing seventy years later, that the well was still there in his own day, giving water to the monks. Whether it is exactly the same hole today, after fifteen hundred years of winter floods, I cannot promise you. But the monks of the gorge have been drinking from under that stream bed ever since.

And I like to picture Zenon, the next morning, coming down the cliff path with his jug on his shoulder, ready for his long walk, and finding the old man sitting in the stream bed, very muddy, with a big smile, and a hole full of water.

"Look," says Sabbas. "We were not looking in the right place."

"How did you find it?"

"I didn't. A wild ass did. I just watched."

Zenon looks at the water for a long moment. "Did you pray for a wild ass?"

"I prayed for water," says Sabbas. "God sent an ass. He has a sense of humour, you know. Remember Balaam."

And Zenon fills his jug, and walks the ten steps back to the bottom of the path, and does not spill a drop.

There is another story from those early years, and it is about the church.

When there were only a few monks, they could pray together in the open air, or in somebody's cave. But now there were seventy, and more coming, and they needed a church. And the cliffs were steep, and there was very little flat ground to build on.

Cyril says that one night, Sabbas was walking along the gorge, praying, and he saw a pillar of fire. A column of light, standing up from the ground to the sky, on the other side of the stream bed. And it stood there, shining, and then it was gone.

And in the morning, he went to the place where it had stood. And he found there a great cave in the cliff, wide and high, with a shape, Cyril says, just like a church. A long room going back into the rock, with an apse at the east end, the curved part at the front where the altar goes, as if somebody had carved it on purpose. But nobody had. And so the monks always called it the God-built church.

They put an altar in it. And it became the first church of the lavra. You can still go into it today. It is still a chapel.

Now, as for the pillar of fire. That is Cyril's story, and it is told, I think on purpose, to remind you of the pillar of fire that went before the people of Israel in the desert, after they came out of Egypt, showing them the way. Whether Sabbas saw it with his eyes, or in prayer, or whether the old monks told it that way because that is what it felt like when God had shown them where to put their church, I cannot tell you. But the cave is real. And it does look like a church.

Now I have to tell you something slightly less cheerful. And I have to tell it, because it is true, and because it is a comfort to anyone who has ever found that doing a good thing makes some people cross.

Not all of Sabbas's monks were happy.

As the lavra grew, some of the brothers began to grumble. They said that Sabbas was a simple countryman from Cappadocia, and that he was not a priest, and that he did not know how to run a big monastery properly, and that he was too strict, or not strict enough, or both at once, the way people do. And in the end, a group of them went up to Jerusalem, to the patriarch, the bishop of Jerusalem, whose name was Sallustius, and they asked him to give them a new abbot.

You can imagine how that felt. You have built a community out of nothing, on a cliff, with your own hands, and some of the people you took in go behind your back to the bishop.

And Cyril tells what happened next, and I think it is one of the most satisfying scenes in the whole Life.

The patriarch Sallustius listened to the complaints. Then he sent for Sabbas, and sent for the complaining monks, and had them all come to the church in Jerusalem together. And there, in front of the grumblers, he ordained Sabbas a priest.

And then he turned round to them and said, more or less, You wanted a proper father for your lavra. Here he is. I did not choose him. You did not choose him. God chose him, and I have only made it official.

And that, for the time being, was that.

This was in about the year four hundred and ninety. Sabbas was fifty-one. And a few years after that, the patriarch made him the chief over all the hermits and lavras in the whole desert, so that the countryman from Cappadocia, who never quite stopped being a countryman, became the father of all the monks of the Holy Land.

The grumbling did not entirely stop. It came back more than once over the years, and at one point, Sabbas got so tired of it that he simply left for a while, and went off on his own, to let things calm down. In the end, some of the unhappy monks went off and founded a monastery of their own, called the New Lavra, and Sabbas, instead of being cross, helped them. He sent them supplies. He found them a leader. He made peace.

Around this time too, Cyril tells us, his mother came. His father had died, and Sophia, now a widow, travelled all the way to the Holy Land to be near her son, and lived out her last years nearby. And she gave him the money she had. And he did not spend it on himself. He spent it on building guesthouses, for pilgrims and travellers and the poor, one in Jericho and others nearer Jerusalem, so that anybody who came to the desert would have somewhere to stay.

I find that a quietly lovely ending to the story of the boy who was left behind when he was five. The family came back together, in the end, in the desert. And his mother's money became bread and beds for strangers.

There is one more animal in tonight's story. A larger one. And this is my favourite.

Cyril tells that once, when Sabbas had gone off alone into the wilderness to pray, as he often did, he came to a cave in the wilderness, and decided to spend the night there. He said his evening prayers, and he lay down on the floor of the cave, and he went to sleep.

What he did not know was that it was somebody else's cave.

At about midnight, the owner came home. The owner was a lion.

Now there were still lions in the Holy Land in those days. Not many, but some, living in the thickets along the river Jordan and in the wild places. And this lion came padding into its cave in the dark, and found a man asleep on its floor, and was, I imagine, quite surprised.

And the lion did not attack him. Instead, Cyril says, the lion took hold of the edge of his cloak in its teeth, very carefully, and began to drag him towards the door. Out you go.

Sabbas woke up. And he found himself being towed across a cave floor by a lion.

And he did not scream, or run, or fight. He got up. And since it was midnight, which is the time the monks said the night office, the long prayers in the middle of the night, he simply began to say them. Out loud. Psalm after psalm. There in the cave, with the lion standing in the doorway, watching him.

And the lion, Cyril says, went out, and waited outside until he had finished.

And when Sabbas lay down again to sleep, the lion came back in. And took hold of his cloak again. And began to drag him out again.

And at this point, Sabbas sat up, and looked the lion in the face, and spoke to it. And I am going to give you what he said in our own plain words, as near to the sense of Cyril's story as I can.

He said, "Listen. This cave is quite big enough for the two of us. We were both made by the same hands. If you want to share it, you are very welcome. If you don't, then off you go. But I am staying."

And the lion, Cyril says, went away. And left him the cave.

I love that story for many reasons. I love that Sabbas does not try to frighten the lion, or command it, or do anything clever. He just says his prayers, because it is time to say his prayers. And then he offers to share. We were both made by the same hands. There is room for both of us. That is a very good thing to say to a lion. It is quite a good thing to say to almost anyone.

Though I note that the lion chose to leave rather than share. Lions are like that. And there is no story of the lion coming back to say thank you for the offer. I expect it found another cave, and told all the other lions about the very odd man who sang at midnight.

As Sabbas grew older, the world beyond the desert began to ask for him.

The Holy Land was part of the great Roman Empire of the East, with its capital at Constantinople, far away to the north. And the monks of the desert were caught up, as everybody was in those years, in long arguments in the Church about how best to speak of Christ, true God and true man. Those arguments are not tonight's story. But it is enough to say that twice, when things were difficult for the churches and the people of Palestine, Sabbas was sent all the way to Constantinople, to speak to the emperor himself.

The first time was in the year five hundred and eleven, when he was seventy-two, to the emperor Anastasius. And Cyril tells a story about it that I think everyone who has ever been underdressed for an occasion will appreciate.

Sabbas arrived at the palace with a group of other monks. And the other monks looked like the sort of people who visit emperors. But Sabbas looked, frankly, like what he was, a very old man who had spent more than fifty years in the desert. His cloak was patched and dusty. His beard went everywhere. And the doorkeepers of the palace took one look at him and decided he was a beggar who had wandered in with the delegation by mistake. And they kept him outside.

So all the smart monks went in to see the emperor. And the emperor looked at them, and looked at the letter from the patriarch of Jerusalem, which had told him that the great Sabbas was coming, and said, And which of you is Sabbas?

And nobody was.

So they went out to look for him. And they found him, Cyril says, in a corner by the door, out of everybody's way, quietly saying his psalms.

The emperor came to respect him a great deal. And Sabbas spent that whole winter in Constantinople, and then went home to his cliff.

The second time, he was ninety-one. That was the year five hundred and thirty-one, and the emperor was the great Justinian. The people of Palestine had suffered terribly after a revolt and its putting-down, and Sabbas went to ask for help for them, for the taxes to be eased, and for the burned churches to be rebuilt, and for a fort to be built to protect the monasteries from raiders. And Justinian, Cyril says, received him with great honour, and granted what he asked.

And Cyril tells a small story about that visit, too. While the emperor was busy at a long meeting, dictating orders, and everyone was standing round watching, Sabbas quietly slipped away. And when one of his monks found him, and asked him why he had left the emperor's presence, the old man said, more or less, It was the hour for prayer. The emperor is doing his work. I must do mine.

Sabbas came home to his lavra for the last time, an old, old man.

He was ninety-three. He had lived in the desert for more than seventy-five years, and on his cliff for more than fifty. And by now, his lavra was not just a lane of caves. It was a great monastery hanging on the cliff, with towers, and a church, and a bakery, and a hospital, and a guesthouse, and gardens on little terraces, and the well of the wild ass at the bottom. And all around the desert, there were other monasteries he had founded, or helped to found, or sent monks to. Cyril lists them, a good handful of lavras and monasteries that looked back to Sabbas as their father, and that is a great many monks.

In the late autumn of the year five hundred and thirty-two, he fell ill. The patriarch came out to see him, and asked to take him up to Jerusalem to be looked after. But Sabbas did not want to be in Jerusalem. He wanted to be in his lavra, in his own cave, on his own cliff. So they brought him home.

He gave the brothers his last instructions. He named the monk who should be abbot after him. He told them to keep the rule and to love each other, which is more or less what every holy abbot has told his monks since the very first ones.

And on the fifth of December, he received Holy Communion, and gave his soul back into the hands of God, and he went home.

He was ninety-four. He had never eaten another apple.

They buried him there, at the lavra, between the God-built church and the main church, in the middle of the courtyard. And his monks went on.

And here is the part that I find the most wonderful of all. They are still going on.

The monastery that Sabbas founded is still there, on its cliff, in the gorge of the Kidron, about ten miles from Jerusalem. In Arabic it is called Mar Saba, which means Saint Sabbas. It is one of the oldest monasteries in the world that has been lived in, more or less without stopping, ever since it was founded. It has been raided. It has been shaken by earthquakes. It has been rebuilt more than once. But there have been monks there praying, for about fifteen hundred years. Today they are Greek Orthodox monks, under the patriarch of Jerusalem. They still keep a very old and strict rule. They still welcome men as guests. And women, I should say, are still not allowed inside the walls, as was the custom of the lavra from the beginning, though there is a tower outside the walls, called the women's tower, from which they can look across.

Over the centuries, the prayer that the monks of Mar Saba sang every day, the order of their psalms and hymns through the night and the day and the year, spread out from that gorge, and was taken up by monasteries all over the Christian East. A great deal of the way Orthodox monks and churches pray today, and Eastern Catholics too, owes something to the rule of Saint Sabbas.

And his relics have had an adventure of their own. In the time of the crusades, they were taken away to Venice, and they stayed there for about seven hundred years. And then, in nineteen sixty-five, Pope Paul the Sixth sent them home. They were carried back to Mar Saba, and the monks received them with great joy, and they are there now, in the church, in the place where he lived. A Catholic pope, sending an Orthodox saint home to his Orthodox monks. I think Sabbas would have liked that very much. There is room for both of us, he might have said. We were made by the same hands.

The same psalms Sabbas said to a lion in a cave in the wilderness.

Now the notes for the curious.

Our main source tonight is the Life of Sabas by Cyril of Scythopolis, written in about five hundred and fifty-five, along with his Life of Euthymius, Sabbas's teacher. Cyril was a monk of the Judaean desert himself, and he gathered his stories from monks who had known Sabbas. There is a good English translation by Richard Price, with notes by John Binns. For the history of the desert monasteries, John Binns's book Ascetics and Ambassadors of Christ, Joseph Patrich's study of Sabbas, and Yizhar Hirschfeld's book on the Judaean desert monasteries, which uses the archaeology of the caves and cells, are the places to go.

What is history. His birth in Cappadocia in four hundred and thirty-nine, his father's posting to Alexandria, his uncles, his monastery at Flavianae, his coming to Jerusalem, Euthymius sending him to Theoctistus, his years in a cave making baskets, the founding of the Great Lavra in four hundred and eighty-three, his ordination by the patriarch Sallustius, the grumbling monks and the New Lavra, his mother's coming and the guesthouses, his two journeys to the emperors in five hundred and eleven and five hundred and thirty-one, his death on the fifth of December five hundred and thirty-two, and the return of his relics in nineteen sixty-five. Cyril's own childhood blessing is his own report.

What is Cyril's story, told as he tells it. The apple, the clothes in the oven, the wild ass at the dry stream, the pillar of fire over the God-built church, the lion in the cave, and the doorkeepers who took him for a beggar. Some of these are probably plain memories and some have grown in the telling, and we have not tried to sort them out further than Cyril does.

What we invented. Zenon and his jug, the scorpion, the conversations about the water, and nearly every word anybody says, apart from the sense of Sabbas's speech to the lion and his answer at Justinian's court, which follow Cyril in our own plain words.

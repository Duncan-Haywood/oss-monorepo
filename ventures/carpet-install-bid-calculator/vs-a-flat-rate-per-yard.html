This calculator vs. a flat rate per yard

“$55 a yard installed” is not a price for a carpet job — it is a price for one specific scope, pattern, and stair count, quietly applied to every floor that calls in.

What a flat rate per yard is

A flat rate per yard is the fastest way to quote: multiply the floor's billed yardage by a number and read off a price. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — a flat living-room stretch-in over pad, solid carpet, no stairs, no tear-out — and then reused for every floor afterward, whether or not the next floor looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the floor square footage, stair count, scope (stretch-in, glue-down, double-stick, or carpet tile), pattern (solid, textured, patterned, or berber), transitions, tear-out, furniture move, and haul-away, plus your own labor hours and material costs. It prices the floor, the stairs, the scope, and the pattern waste separately, then returns the walk-away price and a bid price above your target margin — for this job, not a generic one.

What a flat rate per yard doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/yard whether the job is a stretch-in over pad or a glue-down — even though glue-down alone swaps pad for adhesive and adds installer hours per yard. Prices scope separately from a starting kit for stretch-in, glue-down, double-stick, or carpet tile, so a glue-down is never quoted off a stretch-in rate.
Has no line for stairs, regardless of whether the job has none or fourteen. Prices stairs per step — labor hours and stair-wrap material — on top of floor yardage, so stairs are never folded into "a little more floor."
Prices the floor's billed yardage at one waste percent, with no separate line for the extra waste a patterned match leaves on the cutting-room floor. Applies a pattern-specific waste percent (solid, textured, patterned, or berber) to the floor yards, so a patterned job buys more carpet than a solid one on the same footprint.
Has no line for tear-out, a furniture move, transitions, or haul-away, regardless of how many the job needs. Prices tear-out hours and dump weight, furniture-move time, transitions per opening, and haul-away separately, so a job needing all four costs more than a bare install on an empty room.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against what a flat rate would have quoted the same job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one floor it was built for, and nothing about a per-yard number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per yard get a carpet install quote wrong?

Because the cost driver is scope and stairs, not floor area. A patterned glue-down with fourteen stairs, a tear-out, a furniture move, and a haul-away needs stair labor and material, extra pattern-match waste, adhesive instead of pad, tear-out hours and dump weight, and furniture-move time that a flat living-room stretch-in never sees. A flat rate per yard charges all of that the same as the cheap special it was built on.

What does a flat rate usually leave out entirely?

Stairs, pattern-match waste, tear-out, furniture moves, and haul-away. Stairs alone are not yards -- they are priced per step, with their own labor and stair-wrap material -- and a job that needs the old carpet torn out, the furniture moved, and the scraps hauled away is doing four things a bare stretch-in over pad is not. A per-yard number has no line for any of them.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped presets: a 400 sq ft stretch-in living room with no stairs prices at $2,708.76, which works out to $55.41 a yard. Applying that same $55.41-a-yard rate to a 400 sq ft patterned glue-down job with 14 stairs, a tear-out, a furniture move, and a haul-away quotes $3,681.45 -- $272.64 below that job's own $3,954.09 break-even cost, before a dollar of profit, and $1,261.17 below the calculator's own bid price.

Is a flat rate per yard ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same scope, same pattern, no stairs, no tear-out either time -- a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which job it was built on and neither does the estimator until the floor and stairs are actually measured.

Does this calculator know what carpet or labor costs in my market?

No. It has no data on local mill invoices or labor rates. It takes the floor square footage, stair count, scope, pattern, transitions, tear-out, furniture move, and haul-away, plus the labor hours and material costs you enter, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

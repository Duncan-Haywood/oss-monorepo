This calculator vs. a flat $395 removal fee

"$395 removal fee" is not a price for a wildlife exclusion job — it is a price for one visit and one trap, quietly reused for every attic the truck rolls out to, whatever it actually holds.

What a flat $395 removal fee is

A flat removal fee is the fastest way to advertise: one number, easy to post online and easy for a homeowner to compare between companies. It is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's ad will call and ask a shop to match, whatever the attic actually needs.

The number behind it was set for one job — a single visit, a single trap or a ladder, and nothing to clean up afterward — and then reused for every job afterward, whether or not the next attic looks anything like the one it was priced on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the species, the height tier the exclusion work is reached at — ground level up through a lift — how many days traps stay out and how many checks a day, linear feet of exclusion and one-way devices and the trip back to pull them, attic decontamination and insulation by the square foot, disposal, repairs, and a reserve sized against a multi-year warranty. It prices the visits, the height, and every extra separately, and returns a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this job, not a generic one.

What a flat $395 removal fee doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same fee whether the job is one visit for a ground-level groundhog with a single trap, or a raccoon job with five days of daily trap checks and a two-story seal. Prices the trap days, checks per day, and height tier from what was entered, so the two jobs above come out at different numbers instead of one.
Has no line for a return trip, so a job needing daily trap checks for five days gets quoted the same as a job needing one visit and done. Counts every trap-check visit and prices its own drive time and labor, so five days of checks cost more than none.
Assumes ground level, so a two-story eave in a harness or a job that needs a lift gets quoted the same as work reachable from the ground. Applies a height-tier multiplier to exclusion and one-way-device labor, so a lift job costs more than a ground-level one.
Has no line for a one-way device's removal trip, attic decontamination, insulation replacement, or a warranty reserve, regardless of how much the job needs. Prices each of those separately from its own inputs, so a fully loaded job costs more than a bare removal.
Gives the shop an ad price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper one-visit special on a multi-day job. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against the flat fee, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the crew commits to it.

A flat fee is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied without checking it against a real cost base, and nothing about an ad price tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat fee skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $395 removal fee get a bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is visits, height, and decontamination, not "an animal removal" alone. A one-visit, ground-level groundhog job is a different job from a raccoon job with five days of daily trap checks, a two-story seal reached with an extension ladder, a one-way door and the trip back to pull it, and an attic to decontaminate -- and a flat removal number cannot see which one it is looking at.

What does a flat removal fee usually leave out entirely?

The trap-check visits, the height-tier labor, the one-way device and its removal trip, the decontamination and insulation, and the warranty reserve. A published removal fee is priced on one visit and one trap or one ladder -- it has nowhere to put the hours for four more days of checks, a harness at a steep pitch, a lift, or an attic full of contaminated insulation.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shop-rate defaults ($25/hr crew wage, 52% burden, 14% overhead, a 22% target margin, $0.85/mile and 22 drive minutes each way): a one-visit, ground-level groundhog job with a single trap and no decontamination has a $645.18 break-even cost, already $250.18 above the flat $395 before a dollar of profit. This calculator's own default raccoon job -- a two-story seal, five days of daily trap checks, one one-way door, and 250 square feet of attic decontamination -- breaks even at $2,740.42. A bat job needing a lift, three one-way devices, and 400 square feet of decontamination breaks even at $4,866.77.

Is a flat removal fee ever the right way to quote a wildlife exclusion job?

Not on this calculator's own numbers, even for the cheapest job in the comparison: a single one-visit, ground-level groundhog job with one trap and no decontamination already breaks even above the flat $395, before a single day of trap checks, a ladder, or an attic full of insulation enters the bid. The fee is not close on the hard case -- it is already short on the easy one.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor rates, wildlife-control pricing, or what any state permits. It takes the species, the height tier the work is reached at, trap days and checks per day, one-way devices, decontamination and insulation by the square foot, disposal, repairs, and your loaded labor and warranty reserve, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe -- the arithmetic a flat fee skips, not a market survey.

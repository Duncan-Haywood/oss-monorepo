Method and citation

Sort the m p-values so that p(1) ≤ … ≤ p(m). Each method's adjusted p-value is the smallest error rate at which it would keep that test, so a test is kept when its adjusted value is at or below your level.

Benjamini-Hochberg (1995): keep the k smallest, where k is the largest i with p_(i) <= i q / m; adjusted value min over j >= i of m p_(j) / j. It controls the false discovery rate at q for independent tests and, as Benjamini & Yekutieli (2001) showed, for positively dependent ones (PRDS). Benjamini-Yekutieli divides q by 1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/m and holds under any dependence. The two-stage BKY procedure (Benjamini, Krieger & Yekutieli 2006) runs BH at q / (1 + q), estimates the number of true nulls as m minus its rejections, and runs BH again at the correspondingly higher level; it is shown as rejections only.

Storey's q-value (Storey 2002; Storey & Tibshirani 2003) is the BH adjustment multiplied by pi0 = #{p > lambda} / (m (1 - lambda)), the estimated share of true nulls, capped at 1. Below the histogram, the dashed line is that estimate: if the right-hand bars sit near it, the nulls look uniform as they should.

Bonferroni multiplies by m; Šidák (1967) uses 1 - (1 - p)^m; Holm (1979) steps down, max over j <= i of (m - j + 1) p_(j), and like Bonferroni holds under any dependence; Hochberg (1988) steps up with the same factors and needs independent or positively dependent tests. All four control the family-wise error rate.

e-BH (Wang & Ramdas 2022): with e-values sorted largest first, keep the k largest where k is the largest i with e_[i] >= m / (alpha i). An e-value (Vovk & Wang 2021) is a non-negative statistic with expectation at most 1 under the null.

Verified by tests in the source against statsmodels 0.15.0: every adjusted p-value for Bonferroni, Šidák, Holm, Hochberg, BH and BY to 1e-12 on six lists (the example, all-null and all-significant edge cases, 200 tests with ties, 1,000 tests, and 120 with p-values down to 1e-14 and a block of exact 1s), the two-stage BKY rejections at four levels, Storey's q-values at three lambdas, and e-BH against a direct implementation of the paper's definition. On the example the tool gives the counts that statsmodels' own tests pin to the Benjamini, Krieger & Yekutieli (2006) paper: one-stage BH keeps 4 and the two-stage procedure 8, with second-stage level 0.0649 (Bonferroni keeps 3).

Limits

  • The p-values must be valid. Every correction assumes each p-value is uniform (or larger) when its null is true. p-hacked, data-dredged or already adjusted p-values give corrections that mean nothing.
  • Count every test you ran. m is the number of rows used. Leaving out tests you looked at, or pre-filtering to the promising ones, makes every method too lenient.
  • Dependence. Benjamini-Hochberg, Hochberg, Šidák and the two-stage procedure need independent or positively dependent tests. Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini-Yekutieli and e-BH hold under any dependence.
  • Storey's pi0 needs many tests. With fewer than about 100 it is noisy, and q-values can be optimistic. It is also biased upward when null p-values pile up near 1 (discrete or conservative tests), and it breaks under strong correlation: in this tool's seeded simulation (1,000 one-sided z-tests, 100 real effects, every pair of statistics correlated 0.5, 400 runs) q-values at 0.05 had an actual false discovery rate of 0.109, while Benjamini-Hochberg's was 0.029 (eval/simulate.mjs).
  • FDR is an average. A 5% false discovery rate bounds the expected share of false results over repeated studies, not the share in your list.
  • Not modelled: local false discovery rates, weighted or grouped (hierarchical) procedures, online FDR for tests that arrive over time, resampling methods such as Westfall-Young, Hommel's procedure, closed testing and gatekeeping for clinical endpoints, and boosted e-BH.

Questions

Why correct p-values at all?

Each test at the 5% level has a 5% chance of a false positive when nothing is going on. Run 20 such tests and you expect one false positive; run 20,000 and you expect a thousand. A correction decides which results still stand once you account for how many you ran.

What is the difference between FWER and FDR?

Family-wise error rate (FWER) methods such as Bonferroni and Holm bound the chance of even one false positive among everything you call significant. False discovery rate (FDR) methods such as Benjamini-Hochberg bound the expected share of false positives in that list. FDR keeps more results and suits screening; FWER suits a few confirmatory claims where any error is costly.

What is an adjusted p-value?

The smallest error rate at which that test would be kept by the method. Compare it with your chosen level: a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value of 0.03 means the test is kept at any false discovery rate of 3% or more.

What is a q-value, and how is it different from a BH-adjusted p-value?

Storey's q-value is the Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value multiplied by pi0, an estimate of the share of tests where nothing is going on. When many tests are real effects pi0 is below 1 and q-values keep more results. The estimate needs many tests, ideally hundreds; with few, use Benjamini-Hochberg.

Which method should I use?

Decide before you look. For a screen (genes, features, many A/B metrics) use Benjamini-Hochberg, or Benjamini-Yekutieli if the tests could be dependent in ways other than positively. For a handful of primary claims use Holm, which is never worse than Bonferroni and valid under any dependence.

What are e-values and e-BH?

An e-value is a non-negative test statistic whose average is at most 1 when the null hypothesis is true, such as a likelihood ratio or the final value of a betting martingale in a sequential test. e-BH (Wang and Ramdas 2022) controls the false discovery rate for e-values under any dependence between tests.

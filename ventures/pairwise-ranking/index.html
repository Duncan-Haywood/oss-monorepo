Who is really the strongest, from who beat whom?

A league table counts points; it does not ask who you played. Paste head-to-head results (sports fixtures, chess games, a blind taste test, design A-or-B picks, a debate tournament) and this tool fits the Bradley-Terry model to all of them at once: a rating for each player with its standard error, the chance each is really ahead of the next, a rank range, draws by Davidson's model, a tested home advantage, and win, draw and loss chances for any pairing.

Questions

What is the Bradley-Terry model?

It gives every player a strength and says the chance that one beats another is its strength divided by the sum of the two. The strengths are chosen to make the results you actually saw as likely as possible, using every game at once, so beating a strong side counts for more than beating a weak one. Zermelo proposed it for chess tournaments in 1929; Bradley and Terry rediscovered it in 1952 for taste tests and other paired comparisons.

How is this different from Elo ratings?

Elo updates a rating after each game, so the result depends on the order the games were played and on a K factor someone chose. Bradley-Terry fits all the results together and does not care about order. The ratings here are shown on Elo's scale (1500 for an average player, 400 points for 10 to 1 odds) so they read the same way, but they are maximum-likelihood estimates with standard errors.

How are draws and ties handled?

By default with Davidson's (1970) model, which adds a tie parameter: two equal sides tie with probability nu / (2 + nu), and ties become rarer as the gap between the sides grows. You can instead count a tie as half a win for each side, or leave ties out.

What does home advantage mean here?

The first-listed side in each row gets a multiplier on its strength, estimated from the data, with a likelihood-ratio test of whether it is there at all. It switches on when the columns are headed home and away. It works for any order effect: the home team, the player with white, the option shown first, the speaker who went first.

Why does it say no finite ranking exists?

If some group of players never lost to anyone outside the group, the results are best explained by making them infinitely stronger, so the maximum-likelihood ratings do not exist (Ford 1957). The same happens when a home side won every game. The tool says which players cause it, then shows ratings from a penalised fit (a normal prior), whose gaps depend on that choice.

How sure is the order?

Each rating has a standard error from the curvature of the likelihood. The tool also draws thousands of plausible sets of strengths from that uncertainty and ranks each one, giving every player a rank range and a chance of being top. In the 2023-24 Premier League example Manchester City finished first, but the results alone give them about a 59% chance of being the strongest side.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Every fit is computed by JavaScript in this browser tab. There are no accounts, cookies or analytics vendors.

Method and citation

The model. Player i has log-strength λ_i. In a game where i is listed first, with h = 1 for a home or first-mover advantage, the chances of a win, a tie and a loss are proportional to H π_i, ν √(H π_i π_j) and π_j, where π = e^λ, H = e^α and ν = e^δ. Without ties and home advantage this is Bradley & Terry (1952), first given by Zermelo (1929); the tie term is Davidson's (1970) and the order effect follows Davidson & Beaver (1977), with H also inside the tie term so the model stays a softmax of utilities linear in the parameters.

Fitting. In that form the log-likelihood is concave, so Newton-Raphson with step halving from zero reaches the maximum in a handful of iterations; the observed information gives the standard errors, and strengths are centred to average zero (the reported covariance is transformed to match). Hunter's (2004) MM algorithm, a different method, is in the engine as a check. Ratings are 1500 + 400 log10(π), Elo's (1978) display scale only. Rank ranges and the chance of being top come from simulating the strengths from their normal approximation (4,000 seeded draws). The home test is a likelihood ratio against the same model without α.

When there is no answer. The maximum exists only if, however the players are split in two, someone on each side beat someone on the other (Ford 1957), checked here by strongly connected components of the who-beat-whom graph. With ties or a home term that check is necessary but not sufficient (one win and one tie between two players, or a home side that won every game, still pushes a parameter to infinity), so the fit also watches for a runaway parameter. Either way the tool says so and refits with a normal prior (standard deviation 1.5 on the log scale unless you set one).

Checked against 30 independent fits made in Python (numpy, scipy and statsmodels, versions in eval/reference.json): statsmodels' binomial GLM on a ±1 design for the models without a tie parameter, and scipy's BFGS on Davidson's likelihood written out directly, with a numerical Hessian, for those with one. Strengths agree to 1e-7 (GLM) and 2e-5 (BFGS), standard errors to 1e-6 and 0.1%. Newton and the MM algorithm agree to 1e-9. Implemented from the papers; no third-party code was ported.

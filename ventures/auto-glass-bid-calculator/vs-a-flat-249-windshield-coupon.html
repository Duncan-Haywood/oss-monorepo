This calculator vs. a flat $249 windshield coupon

"$249 windshield installed" is not a price for a windshield — it is a price for one job with no ADAS camera and no trip, quietly applied to every calibration and mobile job the truck pulls up to.

What a flat $249 windshield coupon is

A flat coupon rate is the fastest thing a shop can put on a search ad or a storefront banner: one number, read back as the whole job. It is also the number customers already compare between competing quotes, since "windshield installed" sounds like one job whatever vehicle it is on.

The number behind it was set for one job — economy aftermarket glass, no ADAS camera or sensor on it, done in the shop, paid in cash — and then reused for every windshield afterward, whether or not the next vehicle carries a forward-facing camera at all.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the glass position, the glass source, whether an ADAS camera or sensor requires calibration, whether the job is mobile or in-shop, and how it is paid, plus your loaded labor and overhead. It prices the glass, the calibration, and the trip separately, and returns a walk-away price for the job you describe — for this vehicle, not a generic one. If a calibration requirement is selected but never priced, it says so before the bid goes out.

What a flat $249 coupon doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate whether or not a forward-facing camera or radar unit is mounted to or sighted through the glass, even though most vehicles built since roughly 2018 carry one. Prices ADAS calibration as its own line — none, dynamic, static, or both — with starting hours and a sublet or equipment cost, and flags a bid where a required calibration was never actually priced.
Prices "a windshield" at one glass-source tier, so an OEM windshield is quoted the same as economy aftermarket glass, even though OEM glass alone can cost more than the entire coupon. Applies the glass source's own starting cost — economy aftermarket, OEM-equivalent aftermarket, or OEM — so a costlier tier shows up as more material, not a rounding error.
Has no separate line for a mobile trip, ignoring the trip fee and the technician's travel hours a customer who drives to the shop never generates. Prices a trip fee and trip hours only when the job is mobile, and drops both for an in-shop swap.
Bakes payroll burden into a round coupon number nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the technician's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives one number regardless of how the job is paid, with no way to show a customer what a cash discount actually saves or what an insurer is being billed versus what the customer collects. Applies a cash discount to the target-margin price and reports the savings separately for a cash job, or splits the same price into the customer's deductible and the insurer's bill for an insurance job.

A flat coupon rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a search ad tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat coupon skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $249 windshield coupon get the price wrong?

Because a windshield's cost is mostly the glass, the moldings, the urethane kit, and — on most vehicles built since roughly 2018 — the ADAS camera or sensor calibration that replacing the glass requires. None of that scales with a single flat number the way a coupon rate assumes. Economy aftermarket glass with no ADAS camera, done in-shop, is one job; OEM glass with a static-and-dynamic calibration, done mobile and billed through insurance, is a different job entirely. A flat coupon charges both the same.

What job is the $249 number actually built around?

One specific job: economy aftermarket glass, no ADAS camera or sensor on it, done in the shop, paid in cash. It is a marketing price meant to get the phone to ring, not a number derived from any particular vehicle's glass cost or calibration requirement.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped job presets, at a $28 hourly wage, 22% payroll burden, 14% overhead, and a 32% target margin: even the coupon's own job — economy aftermarket glass, no ADAS, in-shop — costs $428.77 to run, so the $249 coupon is $179.77 under that job's own cost before overhead or margin ever enter into it, and this calculator prices the job at $630.55. A common ADAS sedan — OEM-equivalent aftermarket glass, dynamic calibration, mobile service, billed through insurance — costs $773.57 to run against the same $249 coupon, a $524.57 shortfall, and this calculator prices it at $1,137.60. A vehicle with both a camera and a radar mounted to the glass — OEM glass, static-and-dynamic calibration, mobile, insurance — costs $1,266.70 to run, a $1,017.70 shortfall against the coupon, and this calculator prices it at $1,862.80.

Is a flat coupon rate ever the right way to quote a windshield?

Not on this calculator's own numbers — even the exact job the $249 rate names does not clear its own cost, let alone a target margin. A coupon can work as a below-cost loss leader meant to get the phone to ring, as long as the shop knows that is what it is doing and does not extend the same number to a calibration job or a mobile trip. The risk is quoting an ADAS or mobile job at the coupon rate sight unseen, because the rate does not know whether a camera is mounted to the glass until someone looks.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local glass, labor, or calibration-sublet rates, and it is not a determination that any ADAS or safety system is functioning correctly after the work. It takes the glass position, source, ADAS calibration requirement, and service type you enter, plus your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this job — the arithmetic a flat coupon skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat coupon rate is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest way to advertise that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

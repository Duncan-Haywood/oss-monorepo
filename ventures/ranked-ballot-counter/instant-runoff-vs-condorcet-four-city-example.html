Why instant runoff and Condorcet can pick different winners

This is the standard teaching example from Wikipedia's articles on instant-runoff voting and the Condorcet method: 100 voters rank four cities, each by how close it is to home. The ballots are:

Most first choices: Memphis

Memphis has 42 first choices, more than any other city, but 58 voters rank it last.

Chattanooga is last and its 15 ballots move to Knoxville. Then Nashville is last; its 26 ballots pass over the eliminated Chattanooga to Knoxville, which wins with 58 of 100.

Head to head: Nashville

Nashville beats every other city one on one: 58 to 42 against Memphis and 68 to 32 against both Chattanooga and Knoxville. A candidate who wins every head-to-head contest is the Condorcet winner, and the Schulze method, like every Condorcet method, elects it. Its full ranking here is Nashville > Chattanooga > Knoxville > Memphis.

Instant runoff never asks whether Nashville beats Knoxville. It only sees first choices among the cities still standing, and Nashville runs out of them in round 2. That is the trade: instant runoff is simple to explain and count by hand; Condorcet methods find the candidate a majority prefers to each rival, at the cost of a count that needs the whole table.

Questions

Can instant runoff elect someone other than the Condorcet winner?

Yes. In the four-city example Nashville beats every other city head to head, but it has only 26 first choices and is eliminated in the second round, so instant runoff elects Knoxville.

Which method should a club use?

The one its rules name. Instant runoff is the most widely used for one seat, STV for several seats, and Condorcet methods such as Schulze are used by some organisations that want the head-to-head winner. Agree the method and the tie rule before the vote, not after.

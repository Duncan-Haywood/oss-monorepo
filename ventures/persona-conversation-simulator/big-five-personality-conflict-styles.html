Big Five personality traits and conflict: who clashes, and why

Most arguments between colleagues, housemates or a buyer and a seller are not about personality. But personality shapes how a disagreement goes: who speaks first, who gives way, who holds a line, and when it boils over. This page sets out the rules the simulator uses to turn the five broad traits into those tendencies. The rules are written out so you can check them and disagree with them.

From traits to a conflict style

The Thomas-Kilmann model sorts conflict behaviour on two axes: how hard you push for your own outcome (assertiveness), and how much you try to meet the other person's needs (cooperativeness). That gives five styles: competing, collaborating, compromising, accommodating and avoiding. The simulator approximates the two axes from the Big Five:

  • Assertiveness = 0.6 × extraversion + 0.4 × (100 − agreeableness)
  • Cooperativeness = 0.75 × agreeableness + 0.25 × openness

Both near the middle reads as compromising. Otherwise the quadrant picks the style. This is a heuristic mapping written for the tool, not a validated psychometric conversion.

Which trait gaps cause friction

  • Two low-agreeableness people. Two hard lines and little giving way. This is the strongest friction signal the tool uses.
  • Two high-neuroticism people. Small disagreements escalate quickly. A calm partner (low neuroticism) steadies an anxious one instead.
  • A wide agreeableness gap. The softer person may concede more than they meant to.
  • A wide conscientiousness gap. A planner and an improviser argue about pace and detail more than about the goal.
  • A wide extraversion gap. Rarely open conflict, but the quieter person's view surfaces late or not at all. Ask them directly.
  • Shared high openness or conscientiousness counts as rapport: good at finding a third option, or at leaving with owners and dates.

Research on bargaining points the same way for agreeableness and extraversion. In a straight haggle over one number, both traits have been found to be liabilities rather than strengths (Barry and Friedman, 1998, on distributive negotiation). The simulator's concession sizes follow that direction. Its exact weights are hand-set, not fitted to any study.

Worked example: same two people, roles swapped

Two invented characters. Rosa Juniper scores O70 C80 E75 A20 N30. Linus Oakhaven scores O40 C30 E20 A80 N70. The tool rates the pair high friction:

  • Rosa is far blunter than Linus; Linus may concede more than they meant to.
  • Rosa will take most of the airtime; Linus's view may surface late or not at all.
  • Rosa wants a plan and dates; Linus wants to keep it loose. Pace and detail are the likely sore point.
  • Rosa's calm can steady Linus when things get tense.

If you concede easily, decide your limit before the conversation, and say less after you have made your offer.

Build your own cast and run it

Which Big Five traits matter most in a conflict?

Agreeableness and neuroticism do the most work. Agreeableness sets how readily someone gives way, and neuroticism sets how quickly pressure turns into escalation. Extraversion decides who holds the floor, and conscientiousness and openness show up as arguments about pace, detail and new ideas.

Which personality pairs clash the most?

Two low-agreeableness people (two hard lines), two high-neuroticism people (fast escalation), and pairs with a wide conscientiousness gap (a planner and an improviser arguing about pace and detail). A wide extraversion gap rarely causes open conflict, but the quieter person's view tends to surface late or not at all.

What this is not

Trait scores describe broad tendencies across many people. They do not tell you what one person will do on a given day, and nothing here is a way to assess a real person. The simulator builds invented characters only and refuses profile links, handles and contact details.

The job

A square is 100 sq ft of wall. Waste covers cuts, starter, and damaged pieces — vinyl often needs about 10%, fiber cement more. Stories multiply install hours. None of this is a manufacturer table or a weathertightness finding — it is a time and material budget you can edit.

Linear trim

Trim is where a price-per-square-foot bid goes quiet. A small house with many windows can spend as much on J-channel and corners as on a whole extra square of siding.

Your costs

Payroll burden covers employer taxes, workers’ compensation (ladders and tear-off are not cheap), liability insurance, and paid time off. An installer paid $32 often costs more than $42 on the wall. Underestimating that is how an $8-a-foot special loses money.

    Common questions

    How do I work out how many squares of siding a house needs?

    Enter each elevation as a length × height row. The calculator adds them into gross wall area, subtracts the window and door area you enter, divides the remainder by 100 to get net squares, then adds waste to get the billable squares that material and install hours are priced from. Waste starts at 10% for vinyl and aluminum and 12% for fiber cement and engineered wood — those are common starting points, not a manufacturer allowance, and a cut-up elevation with lots of gables needs more.

    If I subtract the windows, why is there still a charge per opening?

    Because the two are different costs. Subtracting opening square footage stops you buying siding for glass. Cutting and fitting around that opening is a labor line of its own — about 0.35 h per opening here — and the J-channel or window wrap around it is priced separately by the linear foot. A wall with fourteen windows and a wall with two can have the same net square footage and are nowhere near the same day's work; a per-square-foot quote cannot tell them apart.

    How much does a second or third storey add?

    Install hours are multiplied by 1.0 for one storey, 1.35 for two, and 1.7 for three or more. That multiplier is height and staging — carrying material up, moving pump jacks, working off a plank — not "a bigger house", so it applies to install hours only and not to tear-off, housewrap, trim, or the flat add-ons. Scaffold or pump jacks are their own add-on line with hours and rental cost, because that is a job-level decision rather than something that scales with area.

    Does tear-off cost the same as installing?

    No, it is its own hours-per-square figure, and the presets put it at roughly a third to a half of install: about 2.0 h/square against 4.5 for vinyl, 2.5 against 7.0 for fiber cement. Tear-off also brings the dumpster, which is a separate flat cost, and it is optional — a siding-over job turns tear-off hours off entirely while keeping housewrap, which is priced from net squares rather than billable squares because wrap does not need the cut waste.

    Why does a small siding repair still price at more than the material?

    Round-trip drive time comes from the miles and speed you enter, setup is its own line, and a minimum on-site hours floor applies when the takeoff comes in under it — a crew that mobilizes has those hours gone whether the wall is four squares or forty. The trip or truck fee sits on top as a flat line. Making the floor visible is the point: it is what a "just a couple of panels" call actually costs you.

    Why isn't a 20% margin the same as adding 20% to my cost?

    Margin here is a share of the final price — price = job cost ÷ (1 − margin) — which is how the quote reads to the homeowner and how your books read it. Overhead is a separate markup on the job's direct cost, applied before margin and never to itself or to profit. A $14,000 cost marked up 20% is $16,800, a 16.7% margin; a 20% target margin is $17,500.

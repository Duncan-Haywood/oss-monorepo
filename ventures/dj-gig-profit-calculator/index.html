How it is calculated

Slot: event hours plus setup and planning hours. Overhead per hour: monthly overhead × 12 ÷ (hours per week × weeks per year). Contribution: price × (1 − card fee) − gig costs. Profit per hour: contribution ÷ slot hours − overhead per hour. Price for target: ((target + overhead per hour) × slot hours + gig costs) ÷ (1 − card fee). Lead marketplace: price × (1 − lead fee), then the same costs and overhead. Agency: your share of price plus tips, less gig costs and the share of overhead you still carry.

Not modelled: payroll and self-employment tax, sales tax, deposits and cancellations, overtime clauses, the extra bookings a marketplace or agency may bring, seasonality, and local market prices. Unverified: tested on hand-calculated examples, not against a real DJ's books.

Common questions

How much should a DJ charge for a wedding?

Work backwards from the hours. Add the event hours to setup, travel and consultation time, multiply by the hourly take-home you want plus your overhead per working hour, add the gig's own costs such as fuel, a helper and lighting rental, then divide by one minus your card fee. That is the price floor. This tool does the arithmetic from your own figures; it does not know what weddings cost in your town.

Why does setup and travel time matter so much?

A five-hour reception is rarely five hours of work. Load-in, soundcheck, travel, teardown and planning calls often double the time, so an hourly rate quoted on event hours alone overstates what you earn per hour worked. Enter your real setup and prep hours and the table shows profit per hour worked.

What should go in monthly overhead?

Gear replacement set-aside, insurance, music pool and software subscriptions, website, vehicle costs not tied to one gig, storage and marketing. Overhead is spread over the hours you can work each week, not the hours you happen to fill.

Is a lead marketplace or an agency worth it?

The tool replays your monthly mix three ways: direct clients, a lead marketplace that takes a percentage of each booked gig, and subcontracting for an agency that keeps part of the price. It compares weekly income and income per hour worked. It does not value the extra bookings a marketplace or agency might send you, so compare the result with how many more gigs you expect.

Is a price raise worth the clients I might lose?

The raise table shows the share of gigs you could lose before a price rise costs you money. Overhead does not shrink when clients leave, so the break-even loss is the extra revenue divided by the new total contribution.

Does it include tips?

Enter a tip percentage and it adds tips to the model comparison. Tips are not included in the price floor.

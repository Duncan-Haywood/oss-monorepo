This calculator vs. a flat per-square-foot attic price

A published “$3.50 a square foot” special multiplies one rate by attic floor and stops. This page names no company or product — it compares against that generic pricing method, and what it cannot see once the job is not the one the rate was written for.

What each approach actually does

A flat per-square-foot rate is fast: measure the attic floor, multiply by a published number, quote it. It works because it is the same arithmetic every homeowner and every competing bid uses, so it is easy to compare and easy to explain. It has one input — floor area — and treats every job at that area as the same job.

This calculator starts from the same measurement and then prices what the flat rate cannot: board feet (square feet times inches, the unit foam is actually bought and sprayed in, not floor), a pitch-adjusted cathedral area instead of flat floor, a closed-cell rim in lineal feet, an ignition coating on exposed foam, waste by cell type, tight-access hours, and the permit allowance — then loads a wage, marks overhead, and divides by one minus a target margin to print the walk-away price below which the job loses money.

Common questions

Why not just multiply attic square feet by a rate?

Because spray foam is bought and sprayed in board feet — square feet times inches — not attic floor. A flat rate written for one thickness on plain attic floor cannot see a closed-cell rim, a switch from open- to closed-cell, an ignition coating on exposed foam, or a cathedral roof's pitch-adjusted area. Two jobs that read the same on the tape measure can be a different number of board feet, a different cell mix, and a different price.

What does a flat per-square-foot rate get wrong first?

The rim. A published attic-floor rate prices the floor and stops. An 80-foot closed-cell rim at 9.25 inches sprayed 3 inches deep is roughly 185 board feet of closed-cell foam that never shows up in an attic-square-foot number, because the floor area did not change — only the board feet did. The second most common miss is cell type: open-cell and closed-cell are different drums at different rates, and a flat rate written for one silently prices the other the same.

Is a flat rate ever fine to quote from?

For a fast phone estimate on a plain attic floor with no rim, no coating, and one cell type, a flat rate and this calculator can land close. It stops matching once any of that changes — a rim, a coating, a cell switch, a cathedral pitch, or tight access — which is most jobs that are not the one the rate was written for.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type — spans, wages, costs, or the price you send — is sent to a server.

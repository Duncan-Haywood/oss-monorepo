    Common questions

    Why is the unit board feet rather than square feet?

    Because foam is sold and yielded by volume, and thickness is the variable that moves cost most. The default attic floor is 40 × 30 ft, or 1,200 sq ft, sprayed at 8 inches — which is 1,200 × 8 = 9,600 board feet. Doubling the thickness doubles the material without touching the square footage, so a per-square-foot price is only meaningful once the depth is pinned down.

    Why does the order quantity exceed what actually gets sprayed?

    Because of the waste allowance, which defaults to 10%. The plane needs 9,600 board feet, so the estimate orders 10,560. Real losses come from overspray, trimming to depth, hose purge, and the tail of a set you cannot use — a set you paid for either way. Set the allowance to zero only if you are genuinely billing at consumed yield rather than purchased yield.

    Why is material almost nine times the labor cost?

    That is the shape of a spray foam job, and it is worth seeing plainly: on the defaults the foam and consumables come to $5,993.00 against $707.10 of labor for 16.74 hours. It means your exposure is chemical pricing and yield, not crew speed. A 10% swing in foam cost moves this bid about $600; a 10% swing in labor productivity moves it about $70.

    What R-value do the defaults actually deliver?

    About R-29.6 — 8 inches of open-cell foam at roughly R-3.7 per inch. That is the nominal centre-of-cavity figure for the product, not an assembly rating, and it does not account for framing, compression, or the air-sealing benefit that is often the real reason for the job. If you are quoting to a code minimum, check the required assembly value rather than the nominal number.

This calculator vs. a flat price per square foot

“$X a square foot, sprayed” is not a price for a spray-foam job — it is a price for one thickness, one cell, no rim, and no coating, quietly applied to every attic that gets called in.

What a flat price per square foot is

A flat price per square foot is the fastest way to quote a spray-foam job over the phone: multiply the floor or roof area by a number and read it back. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's quote will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one kind of job — usually an open attic floor at a common open-cell thickness, no rim-joist band, no ignition coating required — and then reused for every job afterward, whether or not the next one needs closed-cell or hybrid foam, a cathedral or crawl-wall assembly, a rim band sprayed separately, or an intumescent ignition barrier.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the span, the area type (attic floor, cathedral, or crawl wall), the pitch, the thickness, the cell (open, closed, or hybrid), any rim-joist band, whether an ignition barrier is needed, and your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, travel, and target margin. It converts that into board feet on the sprayed plane and the rim, prices open-cell and closed-cell foam separately by cell share, prices coating hours and material only when a barrier is needed, and returns the job price, the walk-away price, and the price per square foot — for this job, not a flat number for every attic.

Charges close to the same rate whether the plane is sprayed 5 inches of open-cell or 5.5 inches of closed-cell, even though closed-cell costs roughly twice as much foam per board foot and takes more hours per 1,000 board feet to spray. Prices open-cell and closed-cell board feet separately, at each cell's own material cost per board foot and spray hours per 1,000 board feet, so a closed-cell or hybrid job never prices at open-cell's rate.
Has no line for a rim-joist band — a job with 80 lf of rim sprayed 3 inches closed-cell gets the same square-foot rate as a job with no rim at all. Prices rim-joist board feet from lineal feet, rim height, and rim thickness as their own board-foot figure, spray hours, and closed-cell material, on top of the plane.
Quotes the same rate whether or not code requires an ignition or thermal barrier over exposed foam, with no line for coating hours or material. Prices ignition-barrier coating hours per 100 sf and coating material per square foot as their own line, only when the barrier preset calls for one.
Has no line for the waste allowance on ordered foam — the flat rate was set on square feet, not on what actually ships in the drums after overspray and trim. Applies a waste percent by cell type to the board feet sprayed, and orders (and prices) that larger quantity rather than the bare plane figure.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the price per square foot, so a shopped rate can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

Two jobs, one flat rate

Using this calculator's own shipped starting examples:

A 1,200 sf attic floor, sprayed 8 inches of open-cell, no rim, no ignition coating, prices at $10,978.82 — $9.15 per square foot — on a job that costs $8,234.11 to run.

Take a 1,800 sf cathedral roof instead — 1,609.92 sf once the 6/12 pitch is adjusted for — sprayed 5.5 inches of closed-cell with an intumescent ignition barrier and a permit, and the job costs $18,297.90 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator prices it at $24,397.19: $15.15 per square foot, about 1.7 times the attic job's rate, on about 1.3 times the pitch-adjusted area. Quoting the cathedral at the attic job's flat $9.15-per-square-foot rate prices it at $14,729.18 — $3,568.72 below its own break-even cost — before counting the $9,668.01 it falls short of this calculator's own price at a 25% target margin. Close square footage on paper, one job that pays and one that loses money the moment the closed-cell foam and the ignition barrier show up.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per square foot get a spray-foam job wrong?

Because the cost driver is board feet -- square feet times inches of thickness times the cell -- not square feet alone. A 1,200 sf attic floor sprayed 8 inches of open-cell is 9,600 board feet of a cheaper foam. A 1,800 sf cathedral roof sprayed 5.5 inches of closed-cell plus an intumescent ignition barrier is a smaller floor plan that still yields more board feet of a pricier foam, plus coating hours and material a flat rate has no line for. A price per square foot charges both close to the same because it was set on floor area, not on board feet, cell, or coating.

What does a flat price per square foot usually leave out entirely?

Thickness, cell type, a rim-joist band, and ignition coating. A flat price has no line for open-cell versus closed-cell, which differ in both material cost per board foot and spray hours per 1,000 board feet; no line for a rim-joist band sprayed separately in closed-cell, which is board feet a floor-area number never counted; and no line for an intumescent ignition barrier's coating material and hours where code requires exposed foam to be covered. It also has no line for the waste allowance on ordered foam, since the flat number was set on square feet, not on what actually ships in the drums.

Is a flat price per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of similar jobs -- an open attic floor at a common open-cell thickness, no rim band, no ignition coating required -- a flat per-square-foot number can land close, because that is probably the job the rate was set on. The risk is quoting a closed-cell or hybrid job, a cathedral or crawl-wall assembly with a different pitch or perimeter shape, a job that needs a rim-joist band, or one that needs an ignition barrier the same way sight unseen, because the rate does not know the board feet, the cell, or the coating until someone measures the job, and by then the flat number has often already been quoted.

Does this calculator know my local foam prices or labor rates?

No. It has no data on your local foam distributor's pricing or your labor market. It takes the span, the thickness, the cell, the rim, the ignition coating, and your own wage, burden, overhead, travel, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat price per square foot skips, not a market survey.

This calculator vs. pricing a transcript by spreadsheet or mental math

The arithmetic is the same either way: an invoice total and an effective hourly rate, from a page count, a per-page rate, a turnaround multiplier, and a handful of add-on fees. The difference is what happens when that structure gets rebuilt from memory for the next job, on a deadline, for whatever turnaround tier that client asked for.

What each approach actually does

A spreadsheet or mental math can produce exactly the same invoice total as this calculator — the math is multiplication and addition, not anything exotic. What a blank spreadsheet or a phone screen does not give you is a place that already has every line a deposition invoice needs: appearance fee, per-page rate, the right turnaround multiplier for that job (ordinary, expedited, daily, or same-day), certified copies, exhibits, rough draft, realtime, and travel — each billed as its own line. Rebuilding that structure correctly, from memory, for a job with a different turnaround tier than the last one, is where a skipped line or the wrong multiplier creeps in.

This calculator is that structure already built, plus the one division a per-page number alone does not show: an agency's flat offer divided by every hour the job actually costs, so a per-page rate that looks fine can still be checked against what it pays per hour once travel and production time are counted.

Common questions

Isn't a rate-sheet spreadsheet just as accurate as this calculator?

A correctly built one is, because the arithmetic is multiplication and addition: page count times a per-page rate times a turnaround multiplier, plus appearance fee, copies, exhibits, rough draft, and realtime as separate line items. The risk is not the math, it is rebuilding that structure correctly from memory for every job, on a deadline, for whatever turnaround tier that particular client asked for. A spreadsheet has no built-in reminder to apply the daily-turnaround multiplier instead of the expedited one, or to add the certified-copy fee before totaling — and a single skipped line changes the invoice total without showing an error.

When is a spreadsheet actually the better choice?

When the job has a billing wrinkle this calculator does not model — a sliding per-page rate that steps down past a page threshold, a client contract with its own capped turnaround fee, or a shared realtime feed billed to two attorneys separately. A spreadsheet you build yourself can represent any rule you can describe. This calculator trades that flexibility for a form that is quick to fill in correctly and hard to get wrong by accident, for the common case of a per-page rate, a turnaround multiplier, and a handful of flat add-on fees.

What does mental math miss that either a spreadsheet or this calculator would catch?

The agency-offer comparison, almost every time. It is easy to glance at an agency's flat per-page offer and judge it against a mental sense of what transcripts usually pay, and much harder to also divide that offer by every hour the job actually costs — travel both ways, the appearance itself, and production time — without writing the hours down. Both a spreadsheet and this calculator force that division onto the page as its own step, which is what actually shows whether a per-page number that looks fine pays a reasonable hourly rate once the whole day is counted.

Does this replace knowing my state's official transcript-rate schedule or my own contract?

No, and it is not built to. This calculator and a spreadsheet both stop at the arithmetic: an invoice total and an effective hourly rate from the numbers you supply. Neither one knows any court's official transcript-rate schedule, any agency's contracted rate, or which fees a specific bar or client will accept — that still has to come from your own rate card and any governing contract, checked before you invoice.

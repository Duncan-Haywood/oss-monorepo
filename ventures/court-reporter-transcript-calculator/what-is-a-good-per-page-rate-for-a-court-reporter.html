What is a good per-page rate for a court reporter?

It is the question every freelance reporter asks when an agency emails an offer, and it does not have an answer in the form it is asked. A per-page rate is a price per unit, and the units — pages, and the hours behind them — move from job to job. Two assignments at the same rate can pay wildly different amounts per hour of your life, and the rate itself gives you no way to tell which one you are looking at.

The same rate, twice, with opposite answers

Take an agency offering $4.50 a page plus a $75 appearance fee on a deposition you estimate at 120 pages. That is $615 either way. What decides whether it is a good day is the hours:

The jobTotal payYour hoursPer hour
Across town, straightforward, ordinary turnaround $6155.5$111.82
Two hours each way, a long waiting period, heavy exhibits $6159$68.33

Against an $85-an-hour target, the first job clears it comfortably and the second misses by $16.67 an hour — roughly $150 across the assignment. Nothing about the offer changed. Only the hours did, and the per-page rate cannot see them.

A higher rate is not automatically the better job

This is the part that catches people out, because it runs against the instinct that a bigger number is better. Compare the 120-page job above with a shorter one at a rate that looks stronger:

OfferTotal payYour hoursPer hour
120 pages @ $4.50 + $75 appearance$6155.5$111.82
60 pages @ $6.25 + $100 appearance$4755.5$86.36

The $6.25 rate is nearly 40% higher per page and pays $140 less in total, because a rate multiplies a page count and the page count halved. For the same 5.5 hours it returns $86.36 an hour against $111.82. It still clears an $85 target — by $1.36 — but a reporter who took it because the rate looked better gave up a quarter of the hourly return.

So what actually answers the question

One division, done before you accept rather than after you invoice:

(pages × per-page rate + appearance fee) ÷ every hour the job costs you = what it really pays

Then compare that against a target hourly rate you set once, from what you need the year to earn. The target is the figure worth deciding when you are not under deadline pressure; after that, every offer is a division and a comparison.

Count all the hours

  • Travel, both directions. The single biggest reason the two jobs above diverge.
  • On the record, plus waiting. Late starts and long breaks are hours whether or not anyone is speaking.
  • Production. Editing, proofreading, formatting, certifying. This is the block most often left out of a mental estimate, and it is the one a rush turnaround inflates.

That last point is why a turnaround multiplier is not free money. An expedited or daily tier raises the per-page rate precisely because it compresses production into fewer nights — so it belongs in the same division, with the extra hours counted, not treated as a bonus on top.

Common questions

Can a per-page rate on its own tell me whether a job is worth taking?

No, and that is the whole difficulty. A per-page rate multiplies a page count you cannot know in advance and says nothing about the hours the job will cost you. The same offer of $4.50 a page plus a $75 appearance fee on a 120-page deposition pays $615 either way — but spread over 9 hours of travel, appearance and production that is $68.33 an hour, and over 5.5 hours it is $111.82 an hour. One of those clears an $85 target and the other misses it by $16.67, from an identical headline rate.

Why can a higher per-page rate pay less than a lower one?

Because the page count moves too, and the page count is what the rate multiplies. A 60-page job at $6.25 a page with a $100 appearance fee totals $475 — $140 less than the 120-page job at $4.50 that totals $615. If both cost you 5.5 hours, the smaller job pays $86.36 an hour against the larger one's $111.82. The higher rate is the worse deal here despite looking better on paper, because a rate is a price per unit and the units are not fixed.

Which hours should I count when working out an effective hourly rate?

All of them, or the figure flatters the job. Count travel in both directions, the time you are actually on the record plus any waiting, and production time — editing, proofreading, formatting and certifying the transcript, which on a rush turnaround is the part that grows. Production is the hour block most often left out of a mental estimate, and it is the one that decides whether an expedited multiplier is compensation for extra work or just a bigger number next to a longer night.

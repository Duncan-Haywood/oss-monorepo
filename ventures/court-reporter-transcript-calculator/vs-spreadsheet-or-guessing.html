This calculator vs. a spreadsheet or a guess under a deadline

The arithmetic is the same either way: an appearance fee, a per-page rate multiplied for turnaround, certified copies, exhibits, a rough draft, realtime, and travel, added into an invoice total and divided by hours for an effective hourly rate. The difference is what happens when that arithmetic gets built from scratch, under a deadline, for a rush job or a second agency offer that just came in.

What each approach actually does

A spreadsheet or mental math can produce exactly the same invoice total as this calculator — the math is multiplication and addition, not anything exotic. What a blank spreadsheet or a phone screen does not give you is a place that already has every line a deposition invoice needs: appearance fee, per-page rate, the turnaround multiplier that applies to the transcript fee only and changes with the tier the client asked for (ordinary, expedited, daily, or same-day), certified copies, exhibits, a rough draft, a realtime uplift, and travel. Building that structure correctly once, on a deadline, and then building it again identically for the next job at a different turnaround tier, or for a second agency's offer, is where a dropped line or the wrong multiplier creeps in.

This calculator is that structure already built. Fill in the same fields for the invoice or the agency offer check and the same formula runs every time, so a rush job's price is not whatever felt about right in the moment. It also does the one division a per-page number alone never shows: an agency's flat offer divided by every hour the job actually costs, travel and production time included.

Common questions

Isn't a spreadsheet just as accurate as this calculator?

A correctly built one is, because the arithmetic itself is multiplication and addition. The risk is not the math, it is building it correctly under a deadline — often between depositions on a phone — with no built-in reminder to apply the same turnaround multiplier to the transcript fee but not the appearance fee, or to price certified copies per page rather than per copy. A single dropped line changes the invoice total without showing an error.

When is a spreadsheet actually the better choice?

When the job has a billing rule this calculator does not model — a stepped per-diem for a multi-day deposition, a court's own official transcript-rate schedule that applies instead of a freelance rate card, or a split fee across co-counsel. A spreadsheet you build yourself can represent any rule you can describe. This calculator trades that flexibility for a form that is quick to fill in correctly and hard to get wrong by accident, for the common case of an appearance fee, a per-page rate, a turnaround multiplier, and the usual add-ons.

What does guessing miss that either a spreadsheet or this calculator would catch?

The turnaround multiplier compounding with page count, almost every time. It is easy to estimate a rush job's price in your head by nudging the ordinary rate up a bit, and much harder to also carry certified copies, exhibits, and travel through that same mental estimate without a place to write each one down. Both a spreadsheet and this calculator force every line onto the page on its own, which is what actually closes the gap between an invoice that looks about right and one that undercharges for the rush.

How do I tell whether an agency's flat per-page offer is actually worth the day?

Divide it by every hour the job really costs, not just the hours on the record. It is easy to judge a flat per-page offer against a mental sense of what transcripts usually pay, and much harder to also carry travel both ways, the appearance itself, and production time through that same estimate without writing the hours down. This calculator's agency offer check does that division as its own step, against a target hourly rate you set, which is what shows whether a per-page number that looks fine still pays a reasonable rate once the whole day is counted.

Does this replace checking my state's official transcript-rate schedule or a governing contract?

No, and it is not built to. This calculator and a spreadsheet both stop at the arithmetic: an invoice total and an effective hourly rate from the numbers you supply. Neither one knows any court's official transcript-rate schedule, an agency's contracted rate, or which fees a specific bar or client will accept — that still has to come from your own rate card, state rules, and any governing contract, checked before invoicing.

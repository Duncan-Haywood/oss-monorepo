How to make a Nutrition Facts label for homemade food

A label is arithmetic plus a set of rounding rules. The arithmetic is easy to get wrong in a spreadsheet, and the rounding rules are easy to forget. Here is the method, in the order that avoids the usual mistakes. Open the free generator

1. Weigh every ingredient, in grams

Cups and tablespoons vary by the person scooping. Weigh each ingredient as it goes into the bowl, and write the weight down. Weight also lets you list ingredients in the order the label requires: heaviest first.

2. Get nutrition per 100 g for each one

Use the panel on the package you actually buy, converted from per serving to per 100 g, or the free USDA FoodData Central database for plain ingredients such as flour or eggs. Brand matters: two butters or two flours differ by a few percent, and sodium varies a lot.

3. Weigh the finished batch

Ovens drive off water. A batch that went in at 640 g may come out at 560 g, which makes every serving about 14 percent richer than the raw weight suggests. Weigh the finished product, cooled, and use that number.

4. Pick the serving size

The serving size on a package is not free choice: FDA sets reference amounts for each product category, and your serving has to be a household measure close to that amount. The generator takes your serving weight and household measure as given. Look up the reference amount for your product type before you settle on a number.

5. Add up, scale, and round

Per-serving nutrition is the batch total times the serving weight, divided by the finished weight. Then each nutrient is rounded to its own step before it is printed, and percent Daily Value is worked out against the 2020 Daily Values. The generator does all three and shows the unrounded numbers in its CSV so you can see what the rounding did.

6. Ingredients and allergens

List ingredients by weight, heaviest first. Declare any of the nine major allergens (milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame) that the product contains, and name the type of tree nut, fish or shellfish. The generator reads your ingredient names and prints a Contains line; it cannot see inside a branded ingredient, so ask the supplier about anything called a flavor, a seasoning or a chocolate.

Do you need one at all?

Not always. Cottage food laws in many states exempt some direct sales from a full label, and FDA exempts some low-volume products when the business is small and files a notice. Those exemptions generally disappear if the package makes a nutrition claim such as low sugar or high protein. Check your state agency and FDA before relying on one. Common questions

Do I need a Nutrition Facts label to sell homemade food?

It depends on what you sell and where. Many cottage food laws exempt direct-to-consumer sales from a full label, and FDA exempts some low-volume products from nutrition labeling if the business is small enough and files a notice, but the exemption is lost if the package makes a nutrition claim. Rules differ by state and by sales channel, so confirm with your state agency and FDA before you rely on an exemption.

What do I round the numbers to?

The label rounds each nutrient to set steps: calories to 5 under 50 and 10 above, fats to half a gram under 5 g and a gram above, sodium to 5 mg up to 140 mg and 10 mg above, and carbohydrate, fiber, sugars and protein to a whole gram. The generator applies the steps it reads from 21 CFR 101.9(c); check them against the regulation for your label.

Where do I get nutrition values for each ingredient?

From the nutrition panel of the brand you use (convert per serving to per 100 g) or from USDA FoodData Central, which is free. Use the same form of the ingredient you put in the bowl: raw, not cooked, and unsalted if you use unsalted.

Why does a bigger finished weight matter?

Because per-serving nutrition is the batch total times the serving weight divided by the finished weight. Water lost in the oven shrinks the denominator, so each serving carries more calories. Weigh the finished batch.

This checker vs. Wiz

Two different layers: Wiz continuously inventories a whole cloud account's IAM roles and trust relationships, alongside dozens of other cloud risk categories, as part of a paid platform. This tool reads one pasted trust policy's GitHub Actions OIDC conditions, once, locally — no account, no cloud connection, nothing installed.

What each product is for

Wiz is a cloud-native application protection platform (CNAPP). Per its own public material, it connects agentlessly to a customer's AWS, Azure, GCP, or other cloud accounts and builds a graph correlating roles, permissions, workloads, data, vulnerabilities, and exposed secrets across the whole environment, surfacing risks such as over-permissive IAM trust policies as one of many finding types. Wiz's own security research team has published specifically on GitHub Actions OIDC trust-policy misconfigurations — a missing or wildcarded sub condition letting an unintended repository assume a role — as one instance of the broader IAM risk category its platform inventories continuously. Wiz publishes no public price list on its own site, so no dollar figure for it is stated here as verified fact; only its well-documented product category (an enterprise, account-connected CNAPP) is described.

This tool does none of what Wiz does. It connects to no cloud account, runs no agent, and holds no ongoing inventory of anything. You paste one AWS IAM role trust policy — the one federating GitHub Actions over OIDC — and it classifies that policy's sub and aud conditions against the specific wildcard-scope and operator-semantics traps this misconfiguration class is known for: no sub condition at all, a wildcard reaching past one repository, repo:owner/name:* admitting a fork's pull request, a negated operator, a missing or wildcarded aud, and a literal * under StringEquals that makes the role unassumable rather than open. It runs free in your browser (or as a zero-dependency CLI), with nothing uploaded and no network calls of its own.

Common questions

Does Wiz check GitHub Actions OIDC trust policy conditions the same way this tool does?

Not in the way this tool does. Wiz is a cloud-native application protection platform (CNAPP): per its own public material, it connects to a customer's cloud accounts (AWS, Azure, GCP and others) and continuously maps relationships across every role, permission, and resource to surface risks such as over-permissive trust policies, alongside a broad range of other cloud misconfigurations, vulnerabilities and exposed secrets. Wiz's own security research team has specifically written about the exact class of bug this tool checks for -- a GitHub Actions OIDC trust policy whose sub condition is missing or too broad, letting an unintended repository assume the role -- as one finding among the many categories its platform inventories across a whole account. This tool does the opposite and narrower thing: it makes no connection to any cloud account and holds no ongoing inventory. You paste one IAM role trust policy, and it classifies that policy's sub and aud conditions against the specific wildcard and operator-semantics traps documented for this exact misconfiguration, entirely offline.

Should I use this tool or Wiz?

They work at different layers, and a team can reasonably use both. Wiz connects to a cloud account on an ongoing, agentless basis and correlates findings across IAM, workloads, data, and vulnerabilities into one graph, as part of a paid enterprise platform; no public price list exists on Wiz's own site, so no dollar figure is stated here as verified fact. This tool needs no cloud account connection, no agent, and no ongoing subscription: paste one trust policy and get its sub/aud conditions classified in your browser, free, in under a second, with nothing uploaded. A platform team standardizing least-privilege across every role in a large cloud estate is better served by Wiz or a comparable CNAPP; a developer wiring up one new GitHub Actions OIDC role right now, who wants an immediate, free, local answer to whether its trust policy is scoped correctly, is in the right place here.

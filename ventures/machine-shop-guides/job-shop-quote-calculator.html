Before you send it

    What this calculator does and does not do

    It adds up the lines you give it and spreads the once-per-lot costs (setup, programming, any flat lot charge) over the quantity. That spreading is why a 5-off costs more per part than a 100-off: the quantity breaks are arithmetic, not a discount schedule we invented. It does not estimate a cycle time from a drawing, look up a material price or tell you what the market will pay; those numbers are yours.

    The calibration factors multiply your estimated setup hours and cycle time before they are priced, so the estimate stays yours and the correction comes from how your past jobs actually ran (quoted vs actual). Programming time and outside operations are not scaled.

    Markup here is applied to cost (price = cost × (1 + markup)). If you think in margin instead, a 20% margin on price is a 25% markup on cost. For a deeper model with bar and plate yield, tool life and outside-process minimums, there is also a separate CNC machining quote calculator.

    Common questions

    How do job shops calculate a quote?

    Material, setup, programming, cycle time, secondary operations and markup, from the shop's own rates. This calculator adds those lines and spreads the once-per-lot costs over the quantity, then shows the price per part at every quantity break.

    Why does a small quantity cost more per part?

    Setup, programming and any flat lot charge are paid once per lot. Spread over 5 parts they add far more to each part than spread over 100, so the quantity breaks are arithmetic, not a discount schedule.

    What is the difference between markup and margin?

    Markup is on cost: price = cost × (1 + markup). Margin is on price. A 25% markup on cost is a 20% margin on price.

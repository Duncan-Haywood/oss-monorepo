Quoted vs actual: calibrate your quotes from your own job history

Every quote is a guess at setup hours and cycle time. Your finished jobs say how good those guesses were. Paste the history (an ERP export or a spreadsheet) and this page shows, for each machine, how far the quoted hours ran from the actual ones, what the misses cost, and the factor to multiply the next estimate by. It runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded.

Your job history

One row per job. Columns it looks for (names are matched loosely, so Est Setup (min) or Actual Cycle Time (hrs) work too): job, machine or work center, qty, quoted_setup_hours and actual_setup_hours, quoted_cycle_min and actual_cycle_min (per part), outcome (won / lost). You need at least one quoted-and-actual pair. Quotes with an outcome but no actuals still count toward the win rate.

      How to read it

      Median actual ÷ quoted is the middle job's ratio. 1.20 means the typical job on that machine took 20% longer than quoted. It is the suggested factor because one scrapped-and-rerun job should not move every future quote; that job shows up in the list of jobs worth a look instead.

      Middle half is the 25th to 75th percentile of the ratios. A wide range (0.8 to 1.6, say) means the estimates are scattered, not just biased: a single factor helps less than finding what the slow jobs had in common (material, tolerance, a particular programmer, first articles).

      Hours missed adds up actual minus quoted hours, with cycle time multiplied by the quantity, so a 500-piece run weighs more than a one-off. Positive means you gave the hours away. Times your shop rate, that is the money the quotes left on the table, or the money that made you lose jobs if it is negative.

      Factor is offered with at least 5 jobs and called rough below 15. Use it in the job-shop quote calculator: the "use these factors" link fills in its setup and cycle-time factor fields, which multiply your raw estimate before it is priced. Re-run this every quarter; a factor that drifts toward 1 means the estimating has improved.

      Win rate counts won and lost quotes, including quotes that never became jobs. Set it next to the factor: if a machine is under-quoted and you win nearly everything on it, your prices there are probably low. If it is over-quoted and you rarely win, the padding may be costing you work. The page shows the two numbers side by side and leaves the judgment to you.

      Where the numbers come from

      Most job-shop ERPs record estimated and actual hours per operation (it is usually called a job cost, estimate-vs-actual or variance report); export it to CSV. A shop without an ERP can keep the same columns in a spreadsheet from the traveler and the time clock. This page does not ship any industry average or benchmark: the only numbers on it are yours.

      What this does not do

      It does not know why a job ran long, and a factor from last year's jobs is only as good as the match between those jobs and the next one. It does not look at material, outside processing or programming time. It is a check on your estimating, not a price list.

      Common questions

      How do I compare quoted and actual hours?

      Export the job cost or estimate-vs-actual report from your ERP, or keep the same columns from travelers and the time clock, and paste it here. The page shows, per machine, the median of actual ÷ quoted, the hours missed and what they cost at your rate.

      What is a quote correction factor?

      The median ratio of actual to quoted hours on a machine: 1.20 means the typical job took 20% longer than quoted. The job-shop quote calculator multiplies your setup and cycle-time estimates by it. It is offered with at least 5 jobs and called rough below 15.

      Why use the median instead of the average?

      So one scrapped-and-rerun job does not move every future quote. Jobs that took more than twice, or under half, the quoted time are listed separately as worth a look.

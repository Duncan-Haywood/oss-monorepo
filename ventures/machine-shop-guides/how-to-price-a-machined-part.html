How to price a machined part

A quote is a cost estimate plus a decision. The estimate should come from your own records, line by line, so that when a price is wrong you can see which line was wrong. This guide gives the method. It deliberately contains no rates: a published “typical” machine rate or markup would be someone else's shop, not yours.

1. Know your shop rate before you quote anything

Every hour on the quote is priced at a rate. Build it from your books: the year's overhead (rent, utilities, insurance, software, equipment payments or depreciation, indirect wages) plus direct labour cost, divided by the hours you actually bill, not the hours the doors are open. Many shops keep separate rates for a manual mill, a 3-axis machining centre and a 5-axis or mill-turn machine because the equipment cost behind each hour differs. If your billable hours are an optimistic guess, every quote inherits the error.

2. Material, from the invoice

Start from what you pay for the stock form you will actually buy: a bar, a plate, a casting. Work out how many parts one stock unit yields after saw kerf, facing stock, the remnant you cannot use and workholding grip. Material per part is the stock price divided by that yield, plus an allowance for setup pieces and rejects. Ask for a current quote on anything volatile; a price from last quarter's invoice can be out of date. To size the stock from the customer's model (bounding box, round-bar diameter, length with your facing and cut-off), drop the STEP or STL file on the stock size page.

3. Setup and programming: the once-per-lot costs

Setup (fixturing, loading tools, touching off, first-piece checks) and CAM programming are paid once per lot, or once per program, regardless of quantity. Estimate them in hours from comparable past jobs. Programming can fairly be spread over the lots you expect off the same program; a one-off with no repeat should carry all of it.

4. Cycle time per part

Cycle time is spindle time plus load, unload and in-process gauging. A CAM simulation gives spindle time; your own past jobs on similar parts tell you how far real cycle times run from the simulation. Multiply minutes per part by the machine's rate. If an operator tends two machines, decide whether the labour is in the machine rate or charged separately, and do it the same way every time.

Price the starts, not just the order. If a job usually loses some parts to scrap, you run more parts than you ship, and every scrapped part has already taken its cycle time, its material and any per-part finishing. To ship q good parts at an expected fallout f, start q / (1 − f) parts, rounded up: 100 parts at 3% fallout means starting 104. Price cycle time, material (whole bars, if you buy by the bar) and per-part outside processes on those starts. Setup stays once per lot. Hardware and certificates go only on the parts you ship. Take the fallout figure from your own history on similar parts, not from a rule of thumb, and keep it separate from the saw-kerf and facing allowance in step 2: one is lost material on every part, the other is whole parts lost.

5. Other operations

Deburring, inspection, first-article inspection, heat treatment, plating, anodizing, marking, packaging. In-house operations are minutes per part at a rate. Outside processes usually carry a per-part price and a lot minimum; for short runs the minimum is what you pay, so quote it as a flat charge per lot rather than burying it in the part price.

6. Markup, margin and quantity breaks

Add the lines for one lot, then apply markup. Markup on cost and margin on price are different numbers: a markup m on cost gives a margin of m / (1 + m), so a 25% markup is a 20% margin. Then re-run the whole calculation at each quantity your customer might order. Because setup, programming and lot charges are fixed, the price per part falls as the quantity rises. That is your quantity-break table, and it is honest because it comes from your costs rather than a discount you made up.

7. Sanity checks before the quote goes out

  • Does the price per part at the smallest quantity still cover the setup? Many shops set a minimum lot charge for this reason.
  • Is any single line far bigger than you expect? That is usually where the estimate is wrong.
  • Did you include inspection and paperwork the drawing or purchase order requires (first-article reports, certifications, material certs)?
  • If the drawing is marked CUI or export-controlled, can your systems handle it under the contract? See CMMC Level 1 vs Level 2.

Common questions

How do I work out a machine shop rate?

From your books: the year's overhead (rent, utilities, insurance, software, equipment payments or depreciation, indirect wages) plus direct labour, divided by the hours you actually bill, not the hours the doors are open. Many shops keep separate rates per machine type.

How do I calculate material cost per part?

Take the price of the stock form you will buy and divide by the number of parts one unit yields after saw kerf, facing stock, unusable remnant and workholding grip, then add an allowance for setup pieces and rejects.

How should outside processes go on a quote?

Outside processes such as plating, anodizing or heat treatment usually carry a per-part price and a lot minimum. For short runs the minimum is what you pay, so quote it as a flat charge per lot rather than burying it in the part price.

How it measures the part

STEP: it reads the solid's edges, not a mesh. Every corner is on the part. A circular or elliptical edge adds the extremes it actually reaches, following the arc from its start to its end the way the file says, so a concave scallop whose full circle would stick out of the part adds nothing. Faces on planes, cylinders and cones reach their extremes on their edges, so that is the exact box. Spheres, fillet tori and free-form (B-spline) surfaces can bulge past their edges; for those it adds a shape known to contain them and tells you the box may be larger than the part, and by up to how much.

STL: the box and the round bar come from the mesh's corners (exact for the mesh), and the volume from the triangles, so the page can also give the part's weight and how much of the bar becomes chips.

Orientation: a part modelled at an angle is boxed square to its own faces and hole axes, and the page says when it did that. The round bar is measured about the part's own turning axis when it has one.

We checked it against OpenCascade's own measurements on 9 test parts and 59 generated ones (plates, shafts, ball ends, fillets, rotated brackets, inch files): it never gave a box smaller than the part, 60 of the 68 boxes were exact, and the rest were at most 2.8 mm too large and were flagged as such. The test files and the script that made them are in the source repository.

How it estimates the cycle time

This is the volumetric method an estimator uses for a first number before anyone opens CAM. Roughing time is the metal to remove (the first stock option's volume minus the part's volume, from the STL) divided by your removal rate, which is width of cut × depth of cut × feed. Finishing time is the part's surface area divided by your stepover × finishing feed. Your non-cutting allowance is added on top of both, then your load and unload time.

It counts every face as finished, so it runs long on faces left as raw stock, and it knows nothing about holes, threads, setups or tool reach. Treat it as a starting line for the quote, then let your own jobs correct it: the quoted vs actual page turns your past quoted and actual cycle times into a factor the quote calculator applies.

What it does not do

  • It does not read drawings (PDF, DWG) or tolerances. Check the stock against the drawing: a thread, a flatness callout or a heat-treat allowance can change the size you need.
  • An assembly is measured part by part where each is modelled. Export the single part you are quoting.
  • It does not price material. The density presets are typical values; the price is yours.

For the rest of the quote (setup, programming, cycle time, outside processes, markup), use the job-shop quote calculator, and see how to price a machined part.

Common questions

How do I find the stock size from a STEP file?

Drop the .step, .stp or .stl file on this page. It works out the part's bounding box, the round bar it would turn from, and the stock to cut with your allowance per side, facing and cut-off, and rounding. The file is read in your browser and never uploaded.

How accurate is the bounding box?

For STEP it reads the solid's edges, so faces on planes, cylinders and cones give the exact box; spheres, fillets and free-form surfaces get a containing shape and a warning. Checked against OpenCascade on 68 parts, it never gave a box smaller than the part, 60 were exact and the rest were at most 2.8 mm too large and flagged.

Can it estimate cycle time?

As a first number, from your own cutting data: roughing time is the metal to remove divided by your removal rate, finishing time is the surface area divided by your stepover times feed, plus your non-cutting allowance. It knows nothing about holes, threads or setups, so treat it as a starting line.

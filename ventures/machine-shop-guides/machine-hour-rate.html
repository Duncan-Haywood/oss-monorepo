Machine hour rate calculator: what an hour on each machine costs you

A 5-axis hour and a manual mill hour do not cost the same, so one shop rate misprices both. Enter each machine's cost or lease, floor space, power, maintenance and tooling, how many hours it is scheduled and how many of them you can bill, plus the shop's overhead, and this page works out a cost per billable hour for each machine, with and without the operator. It ships no rates, lives or utilization figures: every number is yours. It runs in your browser and keeps nothing.

How much utilization moves it

      How each line is worked out

      Billable hours are the hours the machine is scheduled (shifts × hours × working days) times utilization: the share of those hours that end up on a job you can charge for. Setup you charge separately, warm-up, waiting for material, breakdowns and idle time are not billable. This one number moves the rate more than any other, so take it from your time records, not from hope.

      Ownership is the yearly lease or loan payment if you enter one. Otherwise it is straight-line depreciation, (cost − salvage) ÷ useful life, plus the cost of money on the average investment, (cost + salvage) ÷ 2 × your rate: what the cash tied up in the machine costs you in interest or forgone return. Include tooling packages, probes, installation, rigging and the foundation in the cost. A machine that is paid off still has a replacement coming; leaving ownership at zero quotes as if it never will.

      Space is the machine's footprint, with its share of the aisle and chip handling around it, times what a square foot of the building costs you a year. Maintenance is service contracts, repairs, way oil and coolant you buy per machine.

      Overhead is the shop's costs that belong to no machine, shared out by billable hours: a machine that bills twice as many hours carries twice as much. If you would rather share it some other way (by floor space, or more on the machines that need the inspection room), work the share out yourself and enter it as that machine's maintenance with the shop overhead left blank.

      Power is rated kW × the average load × the price. Spindles run well below their rating most of the time; if you have a meter reading, use kWh ÷ hours run instead (enter it as the kW with 100% load). Tooling is inserts, end mills, drills and holders worn out per spindle hour, from your tooling spend ÷ spindle hours.

      Operator is the loaded wage (pay, payroll taxes, benefits, workers' comp) ÷ the machines one person runs ÷ utilization. The operator is paid for every scheduled hour and only the billable hours recover that pay. The shop rate calculator builds the loaded wage from a base wage.

      Machine rate is the cost of the hour without the person; quote that for unattended running or when labour is priced on its own line. With operator is the full cost of an attended hour. Neither includes profit: the quoted column adds your markup.

      Using the rates

      Put each machine's rate into the job-shop quote calculator (the link under the table fills in the first machine's), or into the Excel quote template.       What this does not do

      It does not know what your machines cost, how busy they are or what other shops charge, and it ships no industry average. It works out cost, not price: what the market will pay for an hour on a machine is a separate question. It is not tax depreciation; the straight-line figure is for pricing, not your return.

      Common questions

      How do I calculate a CNC machine hour rate?

      Add up what the machine costs a year (depreciation or lease, the cost of money tied up in it, floor space, maintenance and its share of shop overhead) and divide by the hours you can bill, then add the per-hour costs: power, tooling and, for an attended hour, the operator. This page does that per machine from your own numbers.

      Why does utilization change the machine rate so much?

      Most of an hour's cost is yearly cost spread over billable hours, so the same machine billing fewer hours costs more per hour. The page shows each machine's rate at 10 points more and less utilization, so an optimistic guess is easy to see.

      Should a machine shop have one shop rate or a rate per machine?

      A rate per machine prices work more accurately when the machines cost very different amounts to own and run: a single blended rate undercharges hours on the expensive machines and overcharges hours on the cheap ones. The page gives both the per-machine rates and the blended one.

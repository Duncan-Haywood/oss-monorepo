    How it is worked out

    Weight is the cross-section area × the length × the density. The areas: round, π/4 × diameter²; hex, √3/2 × across-flats² (about 0.866 × AF²); square, side²; flat, width × thickness; round tube, π/4 × (OD² − ID²) with ID = OD − 2 × wall; square tube, outside² − inside², worked with sharp corners, so real tube with its corner radius weighs a little less. One g/cm³ is 0.036127 lb/in³, so 6061 at 2.70 is about 0.0975 lb/in³ and carbon steel at 7.85 about 0.284 lb/in³. A 1 in round steel bar comes out at 2.67 lb per foot, the figure steel weight tables print.

    Density varies a little by alloy and temper, so the list holds typical values only: the stock-size page's presets, which SOURCES.md lists as handbook nominal values. For a weight you invoice by, use the mill certificate or the supplier's data sheet.

    Parts per bar: each part takes its finished length, the facing stock and one cut-off width. The unusable end is set aside first. Parts = the whole number of (part + facing + cut-off) that fit in (bar − unusable end). The drop is everything left over, the unusable end included. Yield is the finished length of all the parts as a share of the bar.

    Material per part is the bar's cost ÷ the parts it yields, so the drop is charged to the parts that made it, which is what it costs you unless you sell or reuse the offcuts. A scrap allowance for setup pieces and rejects goes on top in the quote calculator, not here.

    What this does not do

    It does not know what your supplier charges, and it ships no price or surcharge. It does not nest parts from plate or plan saw cuts across several lengths, and it does not size stock from a drawing. Bar sold to a length tolerance can run long; the weight here is for the nominal size.

    Common questions

    How do I calculate the weight of bar stock?

    Multiply the cross-section area by the length and the density. The areas: round, pi/4 x diameter squared; hex, about 0.866 x across-flats squared; square, side squared; flat, width x thickness; tube, the outside area minus the inside. A 1 in round carbon steel bar at 7.85 g/cm3 weighs about 2.67 lb per foot.

    How many parts can I get from a bar?

    Set aside the end the chuck or bar feeder can't run, then divide what is left by the part length plus the facing stock plus one cut-off width, and round down. Everything left over, the unusable end included, is the drop.

    How do I work out material cost per part for a machining quote?

    Divide what the bar costs by the parts it yields, so the drop is charged to the parts that made it. Add a scrap allowance for setup pieces and rejects on top when you price the part.

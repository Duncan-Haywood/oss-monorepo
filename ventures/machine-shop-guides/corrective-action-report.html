    What a corrective action report is

    A corrective action report (CAR, sometimes CAPA for corrective and preventive action, or SCAR when a customer issues it to you as a supplier) records one nonconformity from the moment it is found to the check that the fix held. ISO 9001 asks for it in clause 10.2, and the parts themselves are handled under clause 8.7, the control of nonconforming outputs. AS9100 shops follow the same clauses with more on top. The standards are sold by ISO and SAE; this page does not reproduce them. What a report has to show, in plain terms:

    • The nonconformity: the requirement and what was found instead, with the part, revision and quantity. A nonconformance report (NCR) often records this first; the CAR picks it up when the cause needs removing.
    • The correction: what was done with these parts, and with any others that could be the same, before anything else: hold, sort, rework, scrap, use-as-is with the customer's agreement, tell the customer.
    • The root cause: why it happened and why it was not caught, down to something the shop can change.
    • Whether it could happen elsewhere: the same cause on other parts, machines or shifts.
    • The corrective action: the change that removes the cause, who does it and by when, and the documents that now say the new way.
    • The effectiveness check: a later look at what was made after the change, recorded with what was looked at. Only then is it closed.

    Correction or corrective action?

    The most common reason a CAR comes back is that the two are the same text. "Sorted and scrapped 18 parts" is a correction: it deals with the parts. "Added an in-process probe check after the finish pass" is a corrective action: it deals with why the parts were bad. A report needs both.

    Why "operator error" is sent back

    A root cause that stops at a person leaves the process as it was, so the next person can make the same slip. Customers' supplier quality engineers and registrar auditors ask the next why: why was the slip possible, and why did the inspection not catch it? In the invented example on this page, "the bore was undersize" leads to tool wear, to an in-process check every 50 parts, to a check interval set when the job ran in aluminium and never revised when it moved to stainless. That last line is a cause the shop can change; "operator error" is not. In the same way, an action that is only "retrained the operator" changes nobody's process, so pair any training with the change it trains people on.

    The same report as an 8D

    Automotive and many aerospace customers ask for the eight disciplines (8D) format. The fields map across: D1 the team; D2 the problem (the nonconformity); D3 containment (the correction); D4 the root cause, often with the whys and an Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram, and why it escaped; D5 the chosen corrective action; D6 putting it in place and checking it worked; D7 preventing recurrence (the documents changed and the "could it happen elsewhere" answer); D8 recognising the team. If the customer sends their own form, use it; the content is the same.

    Effectiveness: when to check, and what counts

    The standard sets no interval. Ninety days after the action is done is a common choice (it is what the CMMC Workspace log suggests), or the next few lots of the part, whichever says more. Write down what you looked at: "14 lots since the change, no undersize shafts, probe logs kept". If it did not work, the report goes back to the root cause, not straight to a new action.

    Common questions

    What is the difference between a correction and a corrective action?

    A correction deals with the parts already made: quarantine, sort, rework, scrap, tell the customer. A corrective action removes the cause so the problem does not come back. A report needs both, and an auditor sends it back when the action only repeats the correction.

    Why is "operator error" not accepted as a root cause?

    Because it stops at a person. Auditors and customers ask why the slip was possible and why the check did not catch it; asking why three to five times usually reaches a process cause the shop can change, such as a hand-typed offset or an inspection interval set for another material.

    How do you show a corrective action was effective?

    Set a date to check, often about 90 days after the action is done, then look at the parts, lots or records made since and write down what was checked and what was found. If it did not work, go back to the cause and record a new action. Close the report only after a check that found it worked.

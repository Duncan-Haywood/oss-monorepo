DFARS 7012 cyber incident reporting: the 72-hour clock

If your contract carries DFARS 252.204-7012 and a cyber incident touches a system that holds covered defense information, you have 72 hours from discovery to report it to DoD, and you keep images of the affected systems for at least 90 days after you report. What the clause says, in order

DFARS 252.204-7012, Safeguarding Covered Defense Information and Cyber Incident Reporting, is the clause that brings NIST SP 800-171 and cyber incident reporting into a DoD contract or subcontract; look for its number in yours, including purchase orders flowed down from a prime under paragraph (m). The current text is dated MAY 2024. The duties after an incident are in paragraphs (c) to (m):

  • Review, (c)(1)(i). Look for evidence that covered defense information was compromised: the computers, servers, data and user accounts involved, and any other system on your network the attacker may have reached.
  • Report, (c)(1)(ii). "Rapidly report cyber incidents to DoD at https://dibnet.dod.mil." Paragraph (a) defines it: “Rapidly report” means within 72 hours of discovery of any cyber incident. The clock starts at discovery. Waiting for the review to finish is not a reason to file late.
  • Medium assurance certificate, (c)(3). You need a DoD-approved medium assurance certificate to file. You get it through the External Certification Authority program (the address the clause gives). Get it before an incident, not inside the 72 hours.
  • Malware, (d). Malicious software you isolate goes to the DoD Cyber Crime Center (DC3), following DC3's or the Contracting Officer's instructions. Do not send the malicious software to the Contracting Officer.
  • Preserve, (e). Keep images of the affected systems and the relevant monitoring and packet capture data for at least 90 days from the submission of the cyber incident report. The 90 days start when you submit the report, not when you discover the incident. Rebuilding a machine before it is imaged destroys exactly what this paragraph asks you to keep.
  • Access and damage assessment, (f) and (g). On request, DoD gets access to the information or equipment it needs for forensic analysis; for a damage assessment the Contracting Officer asks for what you preserved.
  • Marking, (h). Mark information that identifies your company or is proprietary, so DoD can limit its release.
  • Subcontractors, (m)(2)(ii). A subcontractor reports to DoD itself and gives the incident report number to the prime, or the next tier up, as soon as practicable.
  • Cloud providers, (b)(2)(ii)(D). A cloud service provider that holds CDI for you has to meet the same reporting, malware, media, forensics and damage-assessment paragraphs.
  • Other reports, (l). The clause does not replace reporting that other clauses or laws require.

Where this fits with NIST SP 800-171 and CMMC

The incident-response family of NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2 (3.6.1 to 3.6.3) asks for an incident-handling capability, tracking and reporting, and testing it. The 72-hour report is part of what 3.6.2 has to cover for a DoD contractor, and your system security plan should say who files on DIBNet and who holds the certificate. In the SPRS score calculator, 3.6.1 and 3.6.2 are 5-point requirements each. A shop that has never walked through this list has a gap there.

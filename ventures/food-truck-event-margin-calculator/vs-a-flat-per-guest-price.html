This calculator vs. a flat per-guest price

“$15 a guest” sounds like a rate. It is really one booking's price, divided by that booking's own headcount, offered up as if it applied to the next one too.

What a flat per-guest price is

Dividing a quote by its guest count is the fastest way to compare two bookings, and the fastest way to quote a new one: take the figure that worked last time and multiply by the new headcount. It travels well — a client can repeat a $/guest number back to you from a competitor's flyer — which is exactly why it gets applied to bookings it was never built for.

The number was set for one booking's own prep time, drive, and permit requirements, and then reused for every booking after, whether or not the next one shares any of them.

Common questions

Why is a flat price per guest the wrong way to quote a food truck booking?

Because it is not actually a rate for a guest — it is one booking's total price divided by that booking's own headcount, then reused on a different booking as if it were a market rate. Commissary prep, travel time, truck and equipment amortization, and the one-off special-event permit fee do not scale with guest count, so a per-guest number set from one booking silently drops all of them when applied to a smaller or farther one.

How big is the gap in practice?

On this calculator's own defaults, a 100-guest booking 15 minutes from the commissary prices at $1,509.29, or $15.09 a guest. Apply that same $15.09 a guest to a 40-guest private booking 1 hour 15 minutes away that needs its own $75 event permit, and the flat rate quotes $603.72 — $464.26 below that smaller booking's own $1,067.97 break-even cost, before a dollar of profit, and $921.96 below the calculator's own $1,525.67 price.

Why does the smaller booking cost more per guest, not less?

Because most of a booking's cost does not depend on the guest count at all. Commissary prep and breakdown, the drive, the commissary fee, truck and equipment amortization, and the permit fee are paid once per booking whichever way the headcount falls, so a booking with fewer guests spreads that same fixed cost over fewer heads — and a farther venue and its own permit requirement add to that fixed cost rather than shrinking it.

Is a per-guest rate ever a reasonable shorthand?

For quickly comparing two bookings of similar size, similar distance, and similar scope, a per-guest figure can be close enough to sanity-check against. The risk is carrying that number to a booking that differs on drive time, headcount, or permit needs without re-deriving it — at which point it is no longer describing the booking in front of you, only the one it was set from.

Does this calculator know what a per-guest rate should be in my market?

No. It has no data on what other trucks charge and makes no claim about local pricing norms. It takes your own prep, service, and breakdown hours, drive time and mileage, staffing, food cost, fuel, commissary fee, equipment amortization, and permit fee, and turns them into a price for the booking you describe — the arithmetic a flat per-guest rate skips, not a market survey.

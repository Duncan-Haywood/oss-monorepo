What's a defensible reasonable-compensation salary for your S-corp?

An S-corp owner-employee has to be paid a reasonable W-2 salary before the rest of the profit can be taken as a distribution — and only the salary portion pays FICA payroll tax. This estimates a low-to-high range across the three approaches IRS guidance and Tax Court cases cite (cost, market, and the independent-investor income test), plus what each end of that range costs in payroll tax. Common questions

Why does an S-corp owner need a "reasonable" salary at all?

An S-corp owner-employee must be paid reasonable W-2 compensation for services performed before any remaining profit is taken as a distribution. Only the W-2 wage is subject to FICA payroll tax (Social Security and Medicare); a distribution is not. Set the salary too low relative to the work performed and the IRS can recharacterize distributions as wages, assessing back payroll tax, interest, and an accuracy-related penalty — the fact pattern behind the IRS's own S-corporation compensation guidance and Tax Court cases such as Watson v. Commissioner.

What are the three approaches this calculator uses?

The cost approach sums what it would cost to hire separate employees to perform each duty the owner actually performs. The market approach is what an outside, unrelated employer would pay someone else to fill the owner's primary role. The income approach (the "independent investor" test) takes net profit before owner compensation and subtracts a fair rate of return on the shareholder's invested capital — the residual is what is available for compensation without shortchanging an outside investor. This tool reports the low-to-high span across all three, never their average.

Can the income approach make the reasonable range LOWER than the market rate?

Yes, and that is one of the more commonly missed parts of this methodology. If paying the cost- or market-approach salary would leave less than a fair return on the capital invested in the business, the independent-investor test caps a defensible salary below that figure, with the remainder available as distribution instead. This calculator flags explicitly whenever the income approach is the binding constraint.

Where do the occupational wage figures come from?

They are approximate national median annual wages for common owner-operator roles, rounded to the nearest $1,000, in the range published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) program. They are reference figures for a starting estimate, not a live BLS data feed and not specific to your metro area — check bls.gov/oes for the closest SOC code and location before treating a number from this table as your final market-approach figure.

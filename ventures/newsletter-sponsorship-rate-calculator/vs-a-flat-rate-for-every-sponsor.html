This calculator vs. one flat rate for every sponsor

One flat rate is the fastest way to answer "what do I charge": pick a number once -- from a media kit template, a number a peer mentioned, or last quarter's best deal -- and quote it to whoever asks, for whatever slot they want. It names no product or company; it is just a single number reused for every sponsor.

This calculator instead prices a dedicated send, a primary in-issue placement, a secondary placement, and a classified text link separately, from your own subscriber count, open rate, and niche. Those four placements are not the same product -- a dedicated send is a solo email with no competing content, a classified is a line of text under the real issue -- and commonly cited market ranges for them differ by roughly 5x.

On this calculator's own shipped example — a 10,000-subscriber general-interest list with a 40% open rate — the four placements price out as dedicated $120–$200, primary $80–$140, secondary $48–$80, and classified $24–$40. A creator who has settled on one flat number near the primary midpoint, around $110, would quote that same $110 for a classified slot worth $24–$40 — nearly 3× even the top of that range, the kind of quote a smaller advertiser just declines — and the same $110 for a dedicated send worth $120–$200, leaving up to $90 unbilled on a single sale.

Common questions

Why not just pick one rate and quote it to every sponsor?

Because a dedicated send, a primary in-issue placement, a secondary placement, and a classified text link are not the same product -- a dedicated send is a solo email with no competing content, while a classified is a line of text below the real issue. Market CPM ranges for those four placements differ by roughly 5x -- on the shipped example, the dedicated range ($120-$200) is exactly 5x the classified range ($24-$40) at both ends. One flat number applied to all of them either overcharges the cheap placements, undercharges the valuable ones, or both at once.

How much difference does it actually make?

On this calculator's own shipped example -- a 10,000-subscriber general-interest list with a 40% open rate -- the placement prices are: dedicated $120-$200, primary $80-$140, secondary $48-$80, classified $24-$40. A creator who settles on one flat number near the primary midpoint, around $110, would quote that same $110 for a classified slot worth $24-$40 -- nearly 3x even the top of that range, a quote a sponsor will simply decline -- and the same $110 for a dedicated send worth $120-$200, leaving up to $90 unbilled on a single sale.

Isn't picking one number simpler for sponsors?

It is simpler to say, not simpler to be right about. A sponsor buying a classified line and a sponsor buying a dedicated send are buying different amounts of attention, and a rate card that does not distinguish them either scares off the smaller advertiser with an inflated price or hands the larger one a discount nobody decided to give. Four numbers on one screen costs the creator nothing extra to produce -- this calculator generates all four from the same three inputs -- and it removes the guess about which placement the flat number was actually calibrated for.

Does a flat rate also hide differences between lists, not just placements?

Yes. A flat per-subscriber number applied without checking open rate treats a 10,000-subscriber list at 40% open the same as one at 15% open, even though the first reaches roughly 2.7x as many real readers per issue. This calculator prices from opens rather than raw subscriber count for exactly that reason -- see the FAQ on the calculator's own page for why sponsors pay for eyes on the ad, not names on a list.

This calculator vs. guessing a flat rate

The fastest way to answer a sponsor's "what do you charge?" is to copy a number — a round CPM figure from a creator-economy blog post, or whatever a bigger newsletter in your niche lists on its own public rate card. It costs nothing and takes ten seconds.

What a copied number carries with it is someone else's audience: their open rate, their niche, and whichever single ad placement they happened to publish a price for. A dedicated send and a classified text link are not the same product, and a newsletter with a 55% open rate is not the same inventory as one with a 15% open rate at the same subscriber count — a flat number can't tell the two apart, but a sponsor doing the math on their own return eventually will.

Common questions

What's wrong with copying a competitor's rate card?

Their rate reflects their subscriber count, their open rate, and their niche — not yours. Two newsletters with the same subscriber count can have very different open rates, and open rate is what a CPM-per-opens benchmark actually prices against. Copying their number carries their audience quality into your quote, in either direction.

Isn't a round number like $20 CPM close enough?

It can land close by coincidence, but a single flat CPM cannot represent both a dedicated send and a classified text link at once — the calculator's own benchmark ranges price a dedicated send several times higher than a classified slot in the same niche, because the ad gets the whole email instead of one line inside it. A flat number picks one placement's price and applies it to all four.

When is a flat rate the better choice?

When you have no subscriber count or open rate to work from yet — a brand-new newsletter with no send history — there is nothing for this calculator to price, and a market rule of thumb is the only starting point available. Once you have a few issues of open-rate data, your own numbers are the better anchor.

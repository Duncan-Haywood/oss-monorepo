    Common questions

    How do I turn a house's square footage into roofing squares?

    You cannot, directly — that is the mistake this tool exists to stop. Enter each section's footprint (the area it covers on the ground) and its pitch, and the calculator multiplies the footprint by √(1 + (rise ÷ 12)²) to get actual roof surface, then divides by 100 to get squares. A 1,800 sq ft footprint at 6/12 is about 20 squares of roof, not 18. At 12/12 it is over 25. Bidding the footprint number buys you roughly one square of material short for every eight you ordered.

    Why does pitch change the price twice?

    Because it changes two different things. Steeper pitch means more surface for the same footprint, which is a material and a labor quantity. It also means slower, harder work on that surface, which is a labor rate. The second effect is the steepness multiplier: 1.0 up to 6/12, 1.25 through 9/12, 1.5 through 12/12, and 2.0 above that, applied to install and tear-off hours. Those steps are common shop starting points, not OSHA categories or a safety determination — a 12/12 walk-and- stage decision is yours, not the calculator's.

    Does waste apply to the tear-off as well as the new roof?

    No, and that is deliberate. Waste covers cuts, starter, ridge, and valleys on the new covering, so it is added only to the material squares that underlayment and covering are priced from. Tear-off follows the existing surface multiplied by how many layers are actually coming off, and dump cost follows those tear-off squares. A two-layer strip on a section is twice the tear-off and twice the landfill, but it does not double the shingles.

    Why price the garage and the porch as separate sections?

    Because they are usually a different pitch and a different number of existing layers, and a blended rate cannot see that. A steep detached garage or a porch cover carrying three layers is priced at its own pitch factor, its own steepness multiplier, and its own tear-off squares. One averaged "roof" row quietly moves cost from the hard section onto the easy one and leaves the bid short by whatever the hard section actually is.

    Why isn't a 20% margin the same as adding 20% to my cost?

    Margin here is a share of the final price, which is how the customer's number is quoted and how your P&L reads it: price = job cost ÷ (1 − margin). Overhead, separately, is a markup on the direct cost of the job — office, sales, and the insurance that is not already in payroll burden — and it is applied before margin, never to itself or to the profit. Marking a $10,000 cost up by 20% gives $12,000 and a 16.7% margin. Targeting a 20% margin gives $12,500. That gap is the whole job's profit on a small reroof.

    How much more does a metal roof cost than asphalt shingles?

    More than the material line alone shows. The covering presets here start 3-tab asphalt at $95 a square and architectural asphalt (this calculator's default) at $140, but standing-seam metal starts at $480 a square — a bit over three times the architectural material cost, and stone-coated steel and concrete tile sit in between at $360 and $420. Install labor climbs with it: 1.5 hours a square for 3-tab, 1.8 for architectural, up to 3.5 for standing-seam metal, because seaming and flashing panel roofing takes longer per square than nailing a shingle course. Picking a covering in the calculator fills material, underlayment, and install hours together, so the price gap you see includes both the pricier material and the slower crew day, not material cost by itself.

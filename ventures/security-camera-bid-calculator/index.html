    What this does, and what it does not

    Why "days the drives hold" is a separate number from "storage needed"

    The arithmetic asks for a figure like 10.4 TB. You can buy 8 TB or 12 TB, not 10.4, and once the filesystem and the recorder's overwrite headroom take their slice you keep about 90% of the label. Quoting 30 days and handing over a system that holds 11 is the most common failed handover in this trade, so both numbers are shown and the shortfall is named.

    Why the PoE check matters more than it looks

    A switch over its budget does not fail cleanly. It drops cameras intermittently, often months later when heaters come on, and every one of those is a callback the bid never priced. The tool flags anything over 80% of the budget, because a system at its ceiling has nothing left for the camera added next spring.

    Bitrates are planning figures

    Figures assume roughly 15 fps and typical scenes. H.265 is credited at half of H.264, and smart-codec modes conservatively rather than at the marketing number, because their saving only materialises on static scenes. A camera watching traffic or moving foliage will exceed these. Nothing here is a manufacturer specification or an ONVIF profile.

    This is not legal advice about recording

    Where cameras may point, whether audio may be recorded, signage, and how long footage may be kept are governed by law that varies by country, state, and sometimes by the room. This tool prices an install; it does not tell you whether the install is lawful.

    Common questions

    How much should I charge to install security cameras?

    There is no flat per-camera price. Eight cameras through an accessible attic is a different job from eight through masonry with a lift. This calculator prices the drops, the cable, the head end, and the labour from your own wage, burden, overhead, and margin, and gives a walk-away price to hold when a customer asks you to match a lower bid.

    How many terabytes do I need for 30 days of recording?

    It depends on resolution, codec, camera count, and hours recorded per day. Eight 4 MP cameras on H.265 recording continuously use about 0.35 TB per day, so 30 days needs roughly 10.4 TB before usable-space headroom. This calculator computes it and, separately, the retention the drives you actually buy will deliver — those are different numbers, and the gap is where a handover fails.

    Is this a manufacturer specification or a code ruling?

    No. Outputs are arithmetic on the numbers you enter. Bitrates are typical planning figures at 15 fps, not a guarantee any camera hits them — a scene full of moving foliage will exceed them. Nothing here is an ONVIF profile, a manufacturer specification, or a building, fire, or privacy-law ruling.

    How many cameras can one PoE switch power?

    It depends on the switch's rated budget and what each camera actually draws, not a fixed camera count. A fixed dome or turret with no heater is a light load at about 6 W; a bullet with IR climbs to about 9 W; add a heater for winter and it is closer to 13 W; a PTZ needs PoE+ and can pull 25 W. A switch with a 120 W budget — this calculator's example default — powering eight bullet cameras with heaters is already at 104 W, 87% of budget, before the recorder or a spare port are counted. That is over the 80% planning ceiling this calculator flags, because a switch at its rated ceiling has nothing left for the camera added next spring, and running over budget does not fail cleanly — it drops cameras intermittently, often months later when the heaters come on. Standard PoE (802.3af) delivers 15.4 W at the switch port; PoE+ (802.3at) delivers 30 W, which is why a 25 W PTZ needs a PoE+ port and a PoE+-capable switch, not a standard one.

This calculator vs. pricing from your income goal alone

The fastest way to price a retainer is take-home income divided by billable hours. This page is about what changes when business costs and a scope buffer are added to that division instead of left out.

Pricing from income alone means taking the monthly take-home a freelancer wants, dividing it by the hours they expect to bill, and quoting a retainer off that single number — nothing added for software, insurance, self-employment tax, or the revisions a real engagement tends to produce. It is one division, it takes no time, and because dividing two numbers always produces an answer, the result looks like a real rate even though half of what it needs to cover was never in the calculation.

This calculator takes the same income goal and adds the two figures that method skips: monthly business costs and a scope buffer for the extras a client relationship generates. It runs once, in the browser, on nothing that leaves the device, and it prices the retainer from what actually has to be covered rather than from take-home pay by itself.

The gap is not rounding error. On this calculator's own shipped example — a $6,000 income goal, 160 available hours, 65% utilization, $1,800 in monthly business costs, a 20-hour retainer, and a 15% scope buffer — dividing income alone by billable capacity (6,000 ÷ 104 billable hours × 20 hours) prices the retainer at $1,153.85. This calculator, folding in the $1,800 of costs and the 15% buffer, prices the same 20-hour block at $1,725.00. That is $571.15 a month — $6,853.80 a year — that the income-only guess quietly treats as free: money spent running the business, or hours spent on scope drift, delivered to the client at no charge.

Common questions

Why not just divide my income goal by my billable hours?

Because a retainer priced that way only ever covers take-home pay. Software, insurance, a laptop replacement fund, and self-employment tax do not stop needing money because a client pays on time, and none of that is in an income goal by itself. A scope buffer is missing too: a retainer without one prices the hours a client is expected to use, not the revisions and small extras that actually show up. Skip both and the number still looks like a real rate, because dividing two numbers always produces one -- it is just the wrong one.

How much difference does it actually make?

On this calculator's own shipped example -- a $6,000 income goal, 160 available hours, 65% utilization, $1,800 in monthly business costs, a 20-hour retainer, and a 15% scope buffer -- dividing income alone by billable capacity prices the retainer at $1,153.85. This calculator, adding the $1,800 in costs and the 15% buffer, prices the same retainer at $1,725.00. That is $571.15 a month, or $6,853.80 a year, quietly absorbed as an unpaid business expense instead of billed to the client who is using the hours.

Where would the extra costs come from?

From whatever it costs to run the practice for a month: software subscriptions, business insurance, a coworking desk or home-office share, an accountant, marketing, hardware replacement set aside monthly, and the self-employment tax portion an employee's paycheck would have withheld automatically. None of that has to be exact to be worth entering -- a rough monthly total from a bank statement is already closer to the truth than leaving the field at zero, which is what pricing from income alone does by omission.

What if I don't have a scope buffer figured out yet?

A buffer between 10% and 20% is a reasonable default until a freelancer has enough retainer months behind them to know their own pattern of small unplanned requests. The buffer is not padding for its own sake -- it is priced-in room for the revision or two that almost every ongoing engagement produces, so that a normal month does not quietly run over the hours a client bought.

Is this the same as looking at last year's tax return?

No, and it does not replace a tax return, a bookkeeper, or an accountant. Those confirm what already happened and what is owed. This calculator does the one thing neither hands over on its own: turning an income goal, real billable capacity, monthly costs, and a scope buffer into a single retainer price, before the work starts. It is arithmetic on numbers a freelancer supplies, not a tax or accounting determination.

This calculator vs. working out your HSA limit by hand

The headline numbers — $4,400 self-only, $8,750 family — are easy to find. What goes wrong by hand is prorating the catch-up correctly, not double-counting the last-month rule, and remembering the testing-period tax hit that comes with it.

A hand estimate that prorates only the base and leaves the catch-up at its full $1,000 would overstate this by $500 -- money that, if actually contributed, becomes an excess contribution with its own 6% excise tax until corrected.

Common questions

What's the most common mistake prorating an HSA limit by hand?

Prorating the base limit by month but forgetting to prorate the $1,000 age-55 catch-up the same way. The catch-up is not a flat add-on regardless of eligible months -- for someone eligible 6 of 12 months, the catch-up is $500, not $1,000, unless the last-month rule applies instead.

What's the second most common mistake?

Treating the last-month rule as a bonus stacked on top of the prorated amount rather than a full replacement for it. Electing the last-month rule swaps the prorated limit for the FULL annual limit -- it is one or the other, never both -- and it only applies if you were (or will be) HSA-eligible on December 1 of the tax year.

What consequence gets missed most often?

The last-month rule's testing period. Taking the full limit under this rule requires staying HSA-eligible through December 31 of the FOLLOWING year. A hand estimate that stops at "I qualify for the full amount" often misses that losing eligibility before the testing period ends (for a reason other than death or disability) turns the extra amount into taxable income plus a 10% additional tax.

What HDHP-qualification mistake gets made most often?

Checking only the deductible, or only the out-of-pocket maximum, instead of both. A 2026 plan needs a deductible of at least $1,700 self-only / $3,400 family AND an out-of-pocket maximum no higher than $8,500 self-only / $17,000 family -- a plan can clear one bound and still fail the other, and a plan marketed as "high-deductible" is not automatically HSA-qualified.

Does this calculator know my complete eligibility situation?

No. Neither this calculator nor a by-hand worksheet built from this page checks for other disqualifying coverage -- a general-purpose FSA, Medicare enrollment, or being claimed as a dependent -- which can eliminate HSA eligibility even during otherwise-eligible months. Both apply the same published 2026 IRS figures to the numbers you enter; verify against IRS Publication 969 or a qualified tax professional before relying on either.

This calculator vs. doing the HSA proration math by hand

Prorating a 2026 HSA contribution limit is arithmetic anyone can do with the right figures: the annual limit times eligible months over 12. The part a hand check most often gets wrong isn't the base limit -- it's remembering that the age-55 catch-up gets the same treatment, and that the last-month rule is a different election with its own separate condition, not just a bigger number.

Common questions

Is the age-55 catch-up prorated the same way as the base HSA limit?

Yes, under the ordinary monthly-proration method -- and this is the step a manual check most often skips. The $1,000 catch-up is fixed by statute rather than adjusted for inflation, which reads as if it were a flat add-on regardless of how many months you were eligible. It isn't: for someone eligible 6 of 12 months, the catch-up itself is prorated to $500, on top of the prorated base limit. A hand calculation that adds the full $1,000 catch-up to a correctly prorated base limit overstates the total by the unprorated portion of the catch-up.

What's the most common way a manual proration ends up wrong?

Prorating the base limit correctly and then adding the full, unprorated age-55 catch-up on top of it. For family coverage with 6 eligible months and the catch-up, the correct total is $4,875 ($4,375 base plus a prorated $500 catch-up) -- a hand check that forgets to prorate the catch-up lands on $5,375 instead, $500 too high.

Why would someone contribute the full annual limit under the last-month rule instead of the prorated amount?

Because the last-month rule is a genuine, separate election, not a rounding shortcut: anyone HSA-eligible on December 1 of the tax year may contribute the full annual limit even if eligible for only part of the year, catch-up included at its full, unprorated amount. It is not the same total as a proration mistake that happens to look similar -- it is the correct total for a real election that comes with its own separate condition, the testing period below.

What is easy to miss about the last-month rule's testing period when checking it by hand?

That the extra amount is conditional, not final, until the testing period ends. Electing the last-month rule requires staying HSA-eligible through December 31 of the following year. If eligibility lapses before then for a reason other than death or disability, the amount contributed beyond what ordinary proration would have allowed becomes taxable income in the year eligibility lapsed, plus a 10% additional tax. A hand check that stops at "I get the full limit this year" and never carries the condition forward has only computed half the rule.

Does checking my own HDHP deductible and out-of-pocket maximum by hand carry the same risk?

It has its own separate failure point: mixing up the self-only and family bounds. For 2026 the deductible floor is $1,700 self-only versus $3,400 family, and the out-of-pocket ceiling is $8,500 self-only versus $17,000 family -- a plan checked against the wrong coverage type's bounds can read as HDHP-qualified when it isn't, or the reverse.

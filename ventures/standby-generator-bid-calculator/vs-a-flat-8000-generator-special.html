This calculator vs. a flat $8,000 generator special

"$8,000 installed" is not a price for a standby-generator install — it is a price for one specific kW, fuel, service, and transfer switch, quietly applied to every house the truck pulls up to.

What a flat generator special is

A flat special is the fastest way to quote: one headline number on a search result, a postcard, or a Facebook ad, read off and repeated. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's ad will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one house — one kW, one fuel (usually natural gas), one service size, one transfer switch, beside the house, no trench — and then reused for every generator afterward, whether or not the next house looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the generator size, load mode, fuel, service amps, transfer switch type and amps, pad type, location/setback, and gas meter, plus your loaded labor and overhead. It sizes the feeder from the nameplate current, prices the transfer switch that actually matches the service you entered, and returns a walk-away price for this house — not a generic one.

Prices the feeder, fuel line, trench, and pad from the entered kW, fuel, and setback, so the two jobs above come out at $6,106.00 and $13,679.38 — about 2.2× — instead of one shared number.
Assumes the transfer switch on the truck matches the house, so a 400 A service quoted with a 200 A switch left over from the last job prices out at $6,607.83 with nothing to say the switch will not carry the whole house. Flags it directly: "The 200 A transfer switch is smaller than the 400 A service — it will not carry the whole house unless the load is split." Sizing the switch correctly for that same house prices at $9,685.14 — $3,077.31 more, and a switch that actually carries the load.
Sets a "target margin" by marking cost up a flat percentage — cost × 1.25 for a "25% margin" — which is a markup on cost, not a share of price. Solves for the price that makes margin exactly the stated share of that price. On the same $4,579.50 job, a 25% markup on cost prices at $5,724.37 and actually clears only 20.0% margin; this calculator's $6,106.00 clears the full 25% the target asked for.
Bakes payroll burden into a round "labor rate" nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the installer a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match the ad price. Reports the walk-away price below which the job loses money, plus how many times a typical 22 kW natural-gas hang the entered house really is, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the installer commits to it.

A flat special is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one house it was built for, and nothing about a postcard rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $8,000 generator special get the price wrong?

Because the feeder, the transfer switch, the fuel line, and the pad all scale with the generator's kW, the service amps, the fuel, and the setback, not with the word "generator" alone. A 22 kW natural-gas hang beside the house is a different job from a 26 kW propane set in the far yard with a 60-foot trench and a 400 A automatic transfer switch. A flat special quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat special usually leave out entirely?

Whether the transfer switch it assumes actually matches the service. A 400 A house still needs a 400 A automatic transfer switch, not the 200 A unit that happens to be on the truck or left over in a reused estimate. Swapping in the smaller, cheaper switch drops the price by real dollars and leaves a switch that will not carry the whole house on the panel.

Why does the transfer-switch warning matter if the dollar total looks fine?

Because the total by itself does not say which switch is behind it. A job priced with a correctly sized 400 A automatic transfer switch and the same job priced with an undersized 200 A one land at two different, both-plausible-looking totals — a flat number or a reused spreadsheet total gives no way to tell which total you are looking at. This calculator names the mismatch directly instead of leaving it to be read off a dollar figure that cannot carry that information.

What does solving margin as a share of price get right that a flat number misses?

A flat special, and most rough estimates built around one, price backward from a markup on cost — add 25% to what the job costs and call that the target margin. That is not the same number: margin is profit divided by price, not profit divided by cost, and a 25% markup on cost actually clears a lower margin than stated. This calculator solves for the price that hits the target margin exactly, so a target set at 25% comes out at 25%, not a smaller number wearing the same label.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, equipment cost, or what a competitor's postcard charges. It takes the generator size, load mode, fuel, service amps, transfer switch, pad, setback, and your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat number skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat generator special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

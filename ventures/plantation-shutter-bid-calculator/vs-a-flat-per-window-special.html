This calculator vs. a flat per-window special

"$250 a window installed" is not a price for a job — it is a price for six small inside-mount paint-grade openings hung from the truck, quietly reused for every house that also asks for plantation shutters.

What a flat per-window special is

A flat per-window special is the fastest way to quote a shutter job over the phone or on a Facebook ad: quote the published $250-a-window number, skip the walkthrough. Homeowners shop it because a flyer or a post already gave them a figure, and it is the figure they will ask you to match — even for a job the number was never priced on.

The number behind it was set for one kind of job — small inside-mount openings, paint-grade, hung straight from stock with nothing to remove — and then reused for every house afterward, whether or not the next one needs an outside mount, a stain-grade mill invoice, a measure-and-template trip, or a second story.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the openings, the typical width and height, panels per opening, the mount (inside or outside), the grade (paint or stain), the scope (product-on-site hang, measure-and-install, or template-and-custom), and whether the job needs treatment removal, a specialty opening, a bypass track, or a cafe split, plus your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin. It returns the bid price, the price per opening, and the walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this mount and this grade, not a flat number for every house.

What a flat per-window special doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same figure per window for an inside-mount paint-grade hang and an outside-mount stain-grade template job — even though the mill invoice and the hang hours on the second job can run several times the first. Prices the mount, the grade, and the scope separately, from panels per opening and square footage, not a blended per-window number.
Has no line for treatment removal, a specialty or arched opening, a bypass track, or a cafe split, regardless of whether the job the customer wants actually needs them. Prices each of those as its own labor and material line, applied only when the job calls for it.
Quotes the same rate for a one-story ranch and a second-story job that needs a ladder or scaffold on every opening. Prices extra stories as their own labor line, applied per opening above the ground floor.
Has no separate line for a measure trip or template-and-custom milling, which a stain-grade or unusual-opening job usually needs and a product-on-site hang does not. Prices measure and template hours separately by scope, so a millwork job is not priced like a hang-only job.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against what a flat per-window special would have quoted the same job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

Common questions

Why does a flat "$250 a window" special get a plantation shutter install wrong?

Because the cost driver is the mount, the grade, and the scope, not the panel in the truck. Six small inside-mount paint-grade openings hung straight from stock are a different job from eight wide outside-mount stain-grade openings on a second story that need a measure trip, custom milling, treatment removal, and a bypass track. A flat per-window special charges both the same because it was priced on the cheapest opening and mount combination, not the job in front of the estimator.

What does a flat per-window rate usually leave out entirely?

The mount, the grade, the scope, and the extras. A published $250-a-window number is set on an inside-mount, paint-grade, product-on-site hang -- it has no line for outside-mount trim and hardware, no line for a stain-grade mill invoice, no line for a measure trip or template-and-custom millwork, and no line for treatment removal, a specialty opening, a bypass track, a cafe split, or a second story.

Is a flat per-window rate ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of similar jobs -- small inside-mount paint-grade openings hung straight from stock, no removal, no bypass, no second story -- a flat rate can land close, because that is probably the job the rate was set on. The risk is quoting an outside-mount stain-grade template job the same way sight unseen, because the rate does not know the mount, the grade, the scope, or which extras apply until someone counts the openings, and by then the flat number has often already been quoted.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or material costs?

No. It has no data on your local labor market, your mill or hardware supplier's invoice pricing, or your shop's overhead. It takes the openings, the mount, the grade, the scope, and which extras apply, plus your own wage, burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat per-window special skips, not a market survey.

How it is calculated

For each month, temperature falls through the wall in proportion to thermal resistance, with surface resistances of 0.13 m²K/W inside and 0.04 outside. The saturation vapour pressure at each layer interface comes from the ISO 13788 formulas (over water above 0 C, over ice below). Vapour pressure falls from the indoor to the outdoor value in proportion to vapour resistance (one over permeance). Where that straight line would rise above saturation, the real profile is pulled down to touch saturation at the interface it crosses, like a string pulled taut under a ceiling; the condensation rate there is the vapour arriving minus the vapour leaving.

Condensed water is carried from month to month. While an interface is wet its vapour pressure stays at saturation, so in warmer months the flow reverses and it dries, inward or outward. The year starts in the first month that condenses after a dry month, as in ISO 13788, and runs twice so you can see whether anything is left over a second winter. The safe indoor humidity is found by bisection: the highest constant indoor RH at which no interface collects any water in any month.

Not modelled: air leakage (often the larger moisture source in real walls), rain, wet cladding and solar-driven inward vapour, built-in construction moisture, moisture stored in materials, liquid water movement, and permeance that changes with humidity. Condensation is checked at interfaces only, as ISO 13788 does. Unverified: checked by a hand calculation and unit tests only, not against WUFI, a field measurement or a worked example from the standard. Use it to screen assemblies, not to sign one off.

Common questions

Will my wall get condensation inside it?

It depends on where the cold side of the wall is, how humid the inside is, and how easily vapour passes through each layer. Vapour moves from the warm, humid side toward the cold side. If it reaches a layer that is colder than its dew point and that layer, or the one beyond it, slows the vapour down, it condenses there. This tool finds that layer and works out how much collects each month.

What is the Glaser method?

A steady-state calculation from the 1950s that is still the basis of ISO 13788 and of many code commentaries. Temperature falls through the wall in proportion to thermal resistance and vapour pressure falls in proportion to vapour resistance. Wherever the vapour pressure would rise above the saturation pressure at that temperature, the excess condenses. Run month by month with the condensed water carried forward, it shows whether a wall dries out over the year.

Is some winter condensation acceptable?

Often a small amount is, if it dries out completely by summer and the material can hold it without damage. ISO 13788 treats an assembly as acceptable when everything that condenses evaporates within the year and the material's own limits are not exceeded. Wood sheathing that stays wet for months is a different matter: the tool reports how long each interface stays wet as well as the peak amount.

Where do I find the perm rating of a layer?

On the product's data sheet, usually as water vapour permeance in US perms (ASTM E96) or in metric units. The library values on this page are typical rounded figures to start from. Replace them with your own products' values, and note that wood sheathing, kraft and variable-permeance membranes become far more permeable when damp.

Why does exterior foam help?

It keeps the sheathing warmer. With insulation outside the sheathing, the sheathing's temperature rises toward the indoor temperature and its saturation pressure rises with it, so less vapour condenses there. Too little foam can make things worse if the foam also slows drying to the outside, which is why the amount of foam matters.

What does the model leave out?

Air leakage, which in real walls often moves far more moisture than diffusion; rain, built-in construction moisture and wet cladding; solar-driven inward vapour in summer; moisture stored in materials; and liquid transport. Use a transient hygrothermal model such as those described in ASHRAE Standard 160 for a design decision.

This calculator vs. a flat $400-a-zone special

"$400 a zone installed," or "$2,400 for 6 zones," is not a price for an irrigation install — it is a price for one brochure system, quietly reused for every trench, soil type, and backflow size the truck rolls out to.

What a flat $400-a-zone special is

A flat per-zone rate is the fastest way to advertise: one number, easy to post online and easy for a homeowner to compare between shops. It is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's coupon will call and ask a shop to match, whatever the yard actually needs.

The rate behind it was set for one system — a spray system in dirt, the smallest backflow device, a short trench — and then reused per zone for every job afterward, whether or not the next yard's trench, soil, and backflow size look anything like the one it was priced on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter zones, extra heads, trench footage, soil (dirt, clay, or rock), the controller, the backflow device (a 3/4-inch PVB up through a 1.5-inch RPZ), and whether the job adds a drip conversion, a rain sensor, or a master valve. It prices the trench, the soil, the backflow, and every extra separately, and returns a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this job, not a per-zone average.

What a flat $400-a-zone rate doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate per zone whether the trench runs 180 feet through dirt or 420 feet through rock. Prices trench footage and a soil-hour factor from what was entered, so a rock trench costs more than a dirt one at the same zone count.
Has no line for backflow-device size, so a 3/4-inch PVB gets quoted the same as a 1.5-inch RPZ with a test-cock assembly and a larger tap. Prices the backflow device's own hours and kit cost from the size selected.
Assumes a bare spray system, so a drip conversion, a rain sensor, or a master valve gets quoted the same as a job with none of them. Prices each of those separately from its own toggle, so a fully loaded system costs more than a bare one at the same zone count.
Scales with zone count only, so two 6-zone jobs with different trenches, soil, and backflow devices get the identical price. Reports a price per zone that reflects the job entered, so two jobs at the same zone count can come out at different numbers.
Gives the shop an ad rate to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper brochure-system special on a rock-trench job. Reports the walk-away price, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the crew commits to it.

Common questions

Why does a flat $400-a-zone special get a bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is trench footage, soil, backflow size, and the extras package, not zone count alone. A 6-zone front-yard spray system with a 3/4-inch PVB in 180 feet of dirt trench is a different job from a 6-zone drip conversion with a 1-inch RPZ in 240 feet of clay -- and a rate that only counts zones prices them identically.

What does a flat per-zone rate usually leave out entirely?

Trench footage, soil type, backflow-device size, the controller, and any drip conversion, rain sensor, or master valve. A published per-zone rate is set on one brochure system -- a spray system in dirt with the smallest backflow device -- and reused for every zone count afterward, whatever the trench and soil under it actually are.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shop-rate defaults ($38/hr crew wage, 32% burden, 15% overhead, a 30% target margin): the 6-zone spray system the $400-a-zone rate is built on -- 3/4-inch PVB, dirt, 180 feet of trench -- has a $2,699.17 break-even cost, already $299.17 above the $2,400 flat rate for 6 zones before a dollar of profit. A 6-zone drip conversion with a 1-inch RPZ in 240 feet of clay trench -- the same zone count, so the same $2,400 flat rate -- breaks even at $4,121.60, a $1,721.60 gap. A 10-zone job with a Wi-Fi controller, a 1.5-inch RPZ, rock trench, and drip, rain, and master-valve extras breaks even at $8,098.17 against a $4,000 flat rate, a $4,098.17 gap.

Is a flat per-zone rate ever the right way to quote an irrigation install?

Not on this calculator's own numbers, even for the brochure job the rate is set on: a 6-zone spray system in dirt with the smallest backflow device already breaks even above the flat $400-a-zone rate, before a foot of rock trench, a larger backflow device, or a drip conversion enters the bid. Two jobs can carry the identical zone count and the identical flat-rate price while one costs 53% more to deliver -- the rate cannot see the difference because it never asked about the trench.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor rates or irrigation pricing in any market. It takes zones, extra heads, trench footage, soil, controller, backflow-device size, drip conversion, rain sensor, master valve, and your loaded labor, overhead, and target margin, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe -- the arithmetic a flat per-zone rate skips, not a market survey.

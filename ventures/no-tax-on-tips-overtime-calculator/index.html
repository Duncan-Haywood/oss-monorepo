What's the "no tax on tips" and "no tax on overtime" deduction worth?

Two new federal deductions apply to tax years 2025 through 2028: up to $25,000 of qualified tips, and up to $12,500 ($25,000 married filing jointly) of qualified overtime compensation — but only the half-time premium portion of overtime pay, not your full time-and-a-half wages, and both shrink once your income clears $150,000 (single) or $300,000 (joint). Common questions

How much can I deduct under 'no tax on tips'?

Up to $25,000 of qualified cash tips per year, for tax years 2025 through 2028, if your occupation is on Treasury's published list of occupations that customarily and regularly receive tips. The deduction phases out for higher earners -- see the phase-out question below.

How much can I deduct under 'no tax on overtime'?

Up to $12,500 per year (single or head of household) or $25,000 (married filing jointly) of qualified overtime compensation, for tax years 2025 through 2028 -- but only the FLSA 'half-time' premium portion of overtime pay (the extra 0.5x for hours over 40 in a week), not your full time-and-a-half overtime wages. If you earned $30/hr on overtime hours from a $20/hr regular rate, only the $10/hr premium counts, not the full $30/hr.

What is the income phase-out for these deductions?

Both deductions are reduced by $100 for every $1,000 (or part of $1,000) that your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds $150,000 (single or head of household) or $300,000 (married filing jointly). The tip deduction reaches $0 at $400,000 MAGI single / $550,000 joint. The overtime deduction reaches $0 at $275,000 MAGI single or head of household (its cap is smaller) / $550,000 joint.

Do these deductions reduce Social Security, Medicare, or state income tax?

No. Both are federal income-tax deductions only. Your tips and overtime pay remain fully subject to Social Security and Medicare (FICA) tax if you are an employee, or self-employment (SECA) tax if you are self-employed, and most states will still tax them in full unless that state has separately chosen to conform to the federal deduction.

Can I claim these if I file married filing separately?

No. Both deductions require married taxpayers to file a joint return; married filing separately is not eligible for either one.

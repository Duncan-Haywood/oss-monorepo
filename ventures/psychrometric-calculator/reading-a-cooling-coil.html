Reading a cooling coil on the psychrometric chart

A cooling coil is rated with air entering at 80 °F dry bulb and 67 °F wet bulb, the entering condition used for AHRI ratings of residential equipment. Suppose you measure the air leaving it at 55 °F dry bulb and 54 °F wet bulb, with 1,200 cfm across it at sea level. What is the coil doing? Every number below is what the calculator gives for those inputs; load the example there to follow along.

1. Fix the two points

Two readings fix a point. At sea level (14.696 psia) the entering air holds 78.2 gr/lb of moisture, is at 51% relative humidity, and has a dew point of 60.3 °F. The leaving air holds 60.4 gr/lb at 94% relative humidity.

2. Total load from the enthalpy drop

1,200 cfm of air at 13.85 ft³ per pound of dry air is 5,199 pounds an hour. Each pound gives up 31.44 − 22.57 = 8.88 Btu, so the coil removes 46,178 Btu/h, or 3.85 tons.

The field rule "4.5 × cfm × Δh" gives 47,927 Btu/h here, 3.8% high. The 4.5 assumes standard air at 13.33 ft³/lb; air at 80 °F and 51% humidity is lighter than that. Using the entering air's own specific volume removes the error.

3. Sensible heat ratio

Moisture fell by 17.7 gr/lb. That water, 1.58 gallons an hour of condensate, carries 14,169 Btu/h of latent heat. The remaining 32,008 Btu/h lowered the temperature, so the sensible heat ratio is 0.69: 69% of the coil's work cooled the air and 31% dried it.

4. Apparatus dew point and bypass factor

Draw a straight line from the entering point through the leaving point and keep going until it meets the saturation curve. It meets it at 52.5 °F, the apparatus dew point: the effective temperature of the coil surface. The leaving air sits (55.0 − 52.5) / (80.0 − 52.5) = 0.09 of the way back toward the entering air, so the bypass factor is 0.09. About a tenth of the air behaves as if it missed the fins.

Switch the calculator to the "apparatus dew point and bypass factor" process, enter 52.5 °F and 0.09, and it gives back the same leaving air. That is the way to ask what-if questions: a deeper coil lowers the bypass factor, and a colder refrigerant lowers the ADP.

5. The same readings in Denver

Take the same 80/67 entering and 55/54 leaving readings and the same 1,200 cfm at 5,280 ft (12.10 psia). The thermometers read the same, but the air does not hold the same water. Entering air now holds 99.9 gr/lb, not 78.2, and leaving air 73.9. The total load falls to 43,428 Btu/h because each cubic foot holds fewer pounds of air. The split shifts too: 17,004 Btu/h latent and 26,424 sensible, an SHR of 0.61, not 0.69.

A sea-level chart read in Denver gets the moisture wrong by more than a fifth. It gets the coil's split between cooling and drying wrong too. Set the altitude before you trust a psychrometric reading.

What this leaves out

The fan's heat, duct gains, the enthalpy of the condensate (under 1% of the load), and readings taken in poorly mixed air are all left out. A wet bulb read with too little airflow over the wick reads high. A dry bulb read in a stratified duct can be off by several degrees, and a 1 °F wet-bulb error here moves the load by several percent. The equations are those of ASHRAE Fundamentals Chapter 1; the method is described on the calculator page.

Questions

Why does the 4.5 × cfm × Δh rule read high here?

Because 4.5 is 60 minutes times 0.075 pounds per cubic foot, standard air at 13.33 cubic feet per pound. The entering air in this example takes up 13.85 cubic feet per pound, so each cubic foot carries less air and the rule overstates the load by 3.8%.

Does a bypass factor of 0.09 mean 9% of the air goes around the coil?

Not literally. It describes the leaving air as a mix of air cooled all the way to the apparatus dew point and air not cooled at all. Deeper coils and slower face velocities lower it.

Why does altitude change the sensible heat ratio?

The same wet bulb at lower pressure means more water in each pound of air: 99.9 instead of 78.2 grains per pound in this example. More of the coil's work goes to condensing that water, so the latent share rises and the sensible heat ratio falls from 0.69 to 0.61.

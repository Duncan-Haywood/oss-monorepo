This calculator vs. a flat rate per lineal foot

“$X a lineal foot, any ramp” is not a price for a ramp build — it is a price for one rise, one layout, and one surface, quietly applied to a rise twice as tall that needs a rest landing, a switchback turn, and both-side handrail the coupon never saw.

What a flat rate per lineal foot is

A flat rate per lineal foot is the fastest way to quote a ramp over the phone: multiply the run by a number and read it back. Homeowners like it because it is easy to compare against a competitor's quote, and it is the number a customer holding one will ask you to match.

The number behind it was usually set for the simplest version of the job — a short, straight run at the minimum slope, needing only the two required landings and a basic wood surface — and then reused for every ramp afterward, whether or not the next one is tall enough to need a rest landing, turns a switchback, or goes on a pricier surface.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the rise, slope, layout (straight or switchback), surface, width, whether to demo existing stairs, and whether to include a permit, plus your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin. It converts rise and slope to run, adds every landing the layout and rise require, prices the walking surface by material, prices both-side handrail and footings separately, and returns the price, the price per lineal foot, the effective hourly rate, and the walk-away price — for this ramp, not a flat rate for every ramp.

What a flat rate per lineal foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same per foot whether the rise needs only the two required landings or a rest landing and a switchback turn on top of them. Counts every landing the rise and layout require — intermediate rest landings at the maximum-rise interval, plus a turn landing for a switchback that did not already rest — and prices the walking surface from run plus every landing, not run alone.
Has no line for both-side handrail, even though handrail lineal footage grows with the run and every landing, not with the run alone. Prices handrail from the run and landing perimeter as its own line, separate from the walking surface.
Has no line for footings, which scale with both the run length and the landing count. Prices footings by count — a pair at the house, a pair per landing, and posts along the run — as its own line.
Charges the same rate for pressure-treated wood and for composite, aluminum, or poured concrete, even though the material cost per square foot can be several times higher. Prices the walking surface by the selected material's own decking and framing cost per square foot.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price, the profit, and the effective hourly rate, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per lineal foot get a taller ramp wrong?

Because lineal feet of run is not the walking surface, and it is not the parts list. A flat rate is usually set on a small straight run with the minimum two landings; a taller rise needs a rest landing the coupon never counted, a switchback needs a turn landing, and both-side handrail and footing counts grow with the landings, not with the run alone. Using this calculator's own figures, a 24-inch straight wood ramp costs $4,601.99 to run and prices at $6,135.98 -- $255.67 per lineal foot. A 48-inch switchback aluminum ramp with a rest landing, demo, and permit costs $13,910.58 to run and this calculator prices it at $18,547.43 -- 3.02 times the small ramp's price, not twice, on exactly twice the run.

What does a flat rate per lineal foot usually leave out entirely?

Landings, handrail, footings, and the surface material. A flat per-lf rate has no line for a rest landing a taller rise requires, no line for a switchback's turn landing, no line for both-side handrail lineal footage, no line for the footing count a longer run and more landings both add, and no line for a pricier surface like aluminum or concrete instead of wood. It also has no line for demoing existing stairs or pulling a permit.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own figures: the 24-inch straight wood ramp prices at $6,135.98, on a job that costs $4,601.99 to run, at $255.67 per lineal foot. Apply that same per-lf rate to the 48-inch switchback aluminum ramp (rest landing, demo, permit) and it quotes at $12,271.96 -- $1,638.61 below the ramp's own $13,910.58 break-even cost, before counting the $6,275.47 it falls short of what this calculator prices the ramp at ($18,547.43, 3.02 times the small ramp).

Is a flat rate per lineal foot ever the right way to quote?

For a ramp close to the rise, layout, and surface the rate was set on -- typically a short straight run needing only the two required landings -- a flat per-lf number can land close, because that is probably the ramp the rate was set on. The risk is quoting a rise tall enough to need a rest landing, a switchback, a pricier surface, a demo, or a permit at the same rate, because the rate does not know the rise, the layout, or the surface until someone measures the site, and by then the flat number has often already been quoted over the phone.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or material costs?

No. It has no data on your local labor market, your supplier's material pricing, or what other ramp builders in your area charge. It takes rise, slope, layout, surface, width, handrail, footings, demo, permit, and your own wage, burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this ramp -- the arithmetic a flat rate per lineal foot skips, not a market survey or a code determination.

What this checks

Every delivery and rideshare app shows gross pay per hour or per trip. None of them subtract the car. The decision a driver actually faces is whether this session, after gas and wear, still cleared what it looked like it cleared on the app screen — and what is left once a tax reserve comes out too. How much to set aside for gig-driving taxes walks through the reserve in search-query words.

It does not tell you which platform to drive for, which order to accept, or what your car is worth. The gap between gross pay and net pay is arithmetic on the numbers you typed.

Common questions

How do I calculate my real hourly pay driving for DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Instacart?

Take everything the app paid out this session — base pay, tips, promotions — and subtract what the vehicle actually cost to run: fuel for every mile driven (including the drive to go online and back home), a per-mile reserve for maintenance and tires, and a share of your monthly insurance and other fixed costs spread across your typical monthly mileage. Divide what is left by hours logged, not by hours with a delivery in progress. On this page’s starting figures — $180 gross, 90 miles, 6 hours, 28 MPG, $3.49 gas — the vehicle costs about $30.64 for the session, so $30.00/hour gross becomes about $24.89/hour net, before income tax.

What miles count toward the cost, active delivery miles or every mile?

Every mile the car moved this session, not just miles with an order in progress. The drive to a good starting spot, the loop between orders with nothing assigned, and the drive home all burn the same gas and put the same wear on the car as an active mile does. Undercounting miles by only logging active-delivery mileage understates vehicle cost and overstates how much the session actually netted.

How much should I set aside for taxes as a DoorDash, Uber, or Instacart driver?

This page will not tell you a percentage — that depends on your filing status, state, total income, deductions, and quarterly-estimate schedule, and picking one for you would be a claim about what you owe. What it does is arithmetic once you tell it your own reserve percentage: it holds that share out of session profit only (never out of a losing session, which reserves nothing), and shows both the pre-tax profit and what is actually left after the reserve. Ask a tax preparer for your percentage, then use this page to see what it does to a session’s take-home.

Am I actually making minimum wage after gas and car costs?

Enter an hourly wage to compare against — a state minimum wage, a job offer, whatever you want to check against — and the page reports the gap between that number and this session’s net-per-hour, both stated before income tax so they compare on the same basis. It does not assume a minimum wage for you; wages vary too much by state and city to guess at one.

Does this use the IRS standard mileage rate?

No. The standard mileage rate is a single blended figure the IRS sets once a year for a tax deduction, and it changes annually — hardcoding a specific year’s rate here would go stale the first time it changed. This page instead asks for your own gas price, MPG, and a maintenance reserve per mile, plus your monthly insurance and other fixed costs spread across your typical monthly miles, so the cost per mile reflects your actual car rather than a national average. If you prefer to use the current standard mileage rate as a single per-mile figure, put it in the maintenance-per-mile box and set gas price to 0.

What gross pay do I need to hit a take-home goal?

Type an hourly take-home goal and the page solves backwards: it takes the session’s vehicle cost, adds the profit needed to clear that goal after the tax reserve, and reports the gross pay — and gross pay per mile — that would take. At the starting figures with a 25% tax reserve, a $20/hour take-home goal on a 6-hour, 90-mile session solves to about $190.64 of gross pay, roughly $2.12 per mile.

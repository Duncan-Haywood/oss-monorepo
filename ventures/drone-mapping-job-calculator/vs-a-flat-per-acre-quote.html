This calculator vs. a flat per-acre quote

“$X an acre” is not a price for a mapping flight — it is a price for one specific overlap setting, altitude, and camera, quietly applied to every site that calls in, however that site is actually going to be flown.

What a flat per-acre rate is

A flat per-acre rate is the fastest way to quote aerial mapping work: multiply the site's acreage by a number and read off a price. It is the number a client holding a competitor's quote will ask you to match, whatever overlap or resolution the job actually needs.

The number behind it was set for one flight — one overlap setting, one altitude, one camera — and then reused for every site afterward, whether or not the next job is flown anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter your camera's sensor width, true focal length and image dimensions, the site area, altitude, front and side overlap, ground speed, battery window, and your field and processing rates. It computes ground sample distance, flight-line spacing, image count, airtime, sorties, and processing hours from those terms directly, then prices the job at your target margin — for this flight, not a generic one.

What a flat per-acre rate doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/acre at 70/70 overlap and 80/80 overlap on the identical site, even though 80/80 is 2.19x the images to capture and process. Prices image count, flight lines, and processing hours directly from the overlap you enter, so a higher-overlap flight of the same site costs more.
Has no line for altitude or ground sample distance, regardless of whether the client asked for a coarse GSD or a fine one. Computes GSD from sensor width, focal length, and altitude, and flags a target GSD that would need more altitude than the 400 ft Part 107 ceiling allows.
Has no line for battery swaps, ground control points, or the processing time that follows image count. Prices sorties, battery swaps, ground control, and processing hours separately, so a longer or more image-heavy flight costs more than a short one.
Gives the pilot a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price at your target margin and the dollar gap against a flat per-acre rate, so a shopped price can be scored before you commit to it.

A flat per-acre rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one flight it was built for, and nothing about a per-acre number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a per-acre rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat per-acre rate get a drone mapping quote wrong?

Because the cost driver most of the time is the overlap specification, not acreage. Image count goes as 1/((1-front)x(1-side)), so going from 70/70 to 80/80 overlap on the exact same site is 2.19x the images to fly and process -- a job whose footprint did not change at all. A per-acre rate has no line for overlap, so it charges a 70/70 flight and an 80/80 flight of the same site identically.

What does a per-acre rate usually leave out entirely?

Front and side overlap, ground sample distance, and the processing time that follows image count. A finer GSD needs a lower altitude, a lower altitude means a smaller footprint per frame, and a smaller footprint means more flight lines and more exposures -- all before acreage enters the arithmetic at all. Acreage says nothing about which resolution or overlap a given job was agreed to.

How big is the gap in practice?

On this calculator's own 100-acre worked example (Mavic 3E at 120 m, 40% target margin): a 70/70 flight prices at $618.72 -- $6.19 an acre. Setting a per-acre rate from that job and applying it to the identical 100-acre site flown at 80/80 still quotes $618.72, because acreage did not change. The 80/80 flight's own price at the same 40% margin is $754.03, so the flat rate is $135.30 short. Quoted at the flat rate, the realized margin on the 80/80 job falls from the intended 40% to about 26.9% -- more than 13 points of margin given up on a job that looked identical on paper.

Is a flat per-acre rate ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same overlap, same altitude, same camera, similar site shape -- a per-acre rate can land close, because none of the terms that actually move cost are varying. The risk is applying the same rate outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which overlap or altitude a new job was agreed to and neither does the pilot until the flight is actually planned.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local field or processing rates. It takes your camera's sensor geometry, the site area, altitude, overlap settings, and your own hourly rates, and turns that into ground sample distance, image count, airtime, and a price at your target margin -- the arithmetic a flat per-acre rate skips, not a market survey.

How it is calculated

    
The service transformer's impedance in ohms is %Z/100 x V² / VA, on the secondary, split into R and X by its X/R; the %Z can be reduced first to cover the nameplate tolerance. A utility fault level adds V² / VA ahead of it; blank means an infinite bus. A utility figure instead sets the source impedance to V / (1.732 x I). Running motors are a second source at the service, in parallel, sized so they add the multiplier x their full-load amps there.

    
Each feeder adds (R + jX) x length / sets per phase (twice the length on single-phase, out and back). R is computed: conductor area from the AWG series, kcmil or metric size; copper 1.7241 x 10⁻⁸ and aluminium 2.8264 x 10⁻⁸ ohm m at 20 C, corrected to your temperature; 2 % for stranding; and the IEC 60287 skin-effect factor at 50 or 60 Hz. A step-down transformer refers everything upstream through the square of its turns ratio and adds its own impedance. Fault current = V / (1.732 x |Z|) on three-phase, V / |Z| single-phase.

    
Arc flash follows the IEEE 1584-2018 model: the arcing current at 600 V, 2.7 kV and 14.3 kV from the bolted current and the conductor gap (its Eq. 1 and Table 1), interpolated to your voltage or, at 600 V and below, carried to it by Eq. 25; the enclosure size correction factor (Eqs. 11-15); the incident energy at the working distance (Eqs. 3-6, Tables 3-5) and the distance where it falls to 1.2 cal/cm², the arc flash boundary. The reduced arcing current (the variation factor of Eq. 2) is run with its own clearing time and the higher energy is reported. The code is a port of the FSL-1.1-MIT-licensed arcflash Python library and reproduces every intermediate and final figure of the standard's worked examples in Annex D.1 (4.16 kV) and D.2 (480 V) in its unit tests.

    
Not modelled: line-to-ground and line-to-neutral faults (on a center-tapped secondary they can exceed the line-to-line figure), the protective device's clearing time (you read it from its curve at the arcing current shown), arc flash on single-phase systems and above 15 kV, motors away from the service, generators and inverters, series ratings, current-limiting fuses letting less through, and the cable reactance of your actual installation unless you type it. Unverified: fault currents checked by hand arithmetic and against the point-to-point formulas in unit tests, arc flash against the IEEE 1584-2018 Annex D examples; neither against a commercial study package.

  


  

    
Common questions

    
How do I calculate available fault current at a transformer?

    
Divide the transformer's full-load current by its per-unit impedance. A 1500 kVA, 480 V three-phase transformer carries 1500000 / (1.732 x 480) = 1804 A at full load; at 3.5 % impedance the fault current on an infinite bus is 1804 / 0.035 = 51,549 A. Many designers reduce the impedance by 10 % to cover the nameplate tolerance, which gives 57,277 A.

    
What does NEC 110.24 require?

    
Service equipment in other than dwelling units must be field marked with the maximum available fault current and the date the calculation was done, and the marking must be updated when changes to the electrical installation change the available fault current. Check the edition your jurisdiction has adopted.

    
Is the point-to-point method accurate?

    
Its multipliers add the impedances of the transformer and the cables as plain magnitudes, as if they had the same angle. A transformer is mostly reactance and a long small feeder mostly resistance, so the true complex sum is smaller and the real fault current higher. In this page's example the magnitude sum reads 5.6 percent low at a panel 150 ft down a 1/0 feeder. This tool shows both.

    
Should I include motor contribution?

    
Running induction motors feed a fault for the first few cycles. A common allowance is four times the full-load current of the motors running at the service, added there. Enter the motors' full-load amps and the multiplier your method uses, or leave it blank.

    
Where do the conductor impedances come from?

    
Resistance is computed from the conductor's area and the resistivity of copper or aluminium at the temperature you choose, with 2 percent for stranding and the IEC 60287 skin-effect formula. Reactance defaults to 0.050 ohm per 1000 ft, a quarter higher in steel conduit, and you can type your own figure for each feeder.

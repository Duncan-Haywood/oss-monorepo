# Reorder points replayed on a real store's year (2026-11-22)

**Question.** If a seller followed this planner's reorder points for a year, re-running it every
four weeks on their latest orders, how often would they run out during a lead time, how many units
would they sell, and how much stock would they carry? And does a reorder point taken from the SKU's
own lead-time demand do better than the bell-curve rule the page shipped with?

## Data

UCI Machine Learning Repository, "Online Retail II" (Daqing Chen; CC BY 4.0;
<https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/502/online+retail+ii>): every order line of a UK online
giftware seller, many of whose customers are wholesalers, 2009-12-01 to 2011-12-09. 1,067,371
lines; 1,061,278 are products (StockCode of five digits and an optional letter; postage, fees and
manual adjustments are dropped), 5,070 SKUs, 739 days. UCI's host refuses this session, so the
workbook came from a public GitHub mirror (`aryuhhhhh/sales_etl`, `data/online_retail_II.xlsx`,
45,622,278 bytes, sha256 `bcbe73b35f5b7babf197fb0cb983a11f5d9ff929078d4aa53d171b1f2df2e980`).
Both sheets were written to one CSV with `openpyxl` (`iter_rows(values_only=True)`, header from
the first sheet). The data is not committed.

The file is read through the page's own `parseCsv` and `normalizeSales`, which is how two faults
turned up (both fixed in this PR, both tested):

- `analyze()` took the first and last order date with `Math.min(...lines)`; on this file that
  throws "Maximum call stack size exceeded". Any export past roughly 120,000 lines would have failed
  on the page. Now a loop (`calc.test.mjs`: 200,000 lines).
- An invoice-style export (`StockCode`, `Description`, `InvoiceDate`) was refused for lack of a SKU
  column. These names are now aliases.

## Method (`eval/backtest.mjs`)

Holdout: the last 364 days (2010-12-11 to 2011-12-09), 13 plans 28 days apart. Each plan runs
`analyze()` on the trailing 365 days of orders (180 in one run). Between plans a daily
continuous-review policy runs on the real daily demand: when on hand plus on order is at or below
the reorder point, order up to `velocity x (lead + cover) + safety`; orders land `lead` days
later; demand that finds no stock is lost; returns are netted within a day and never count as
demand. Stock starts at the first plan's target. Cover = 28 days.

- **CSL** (cycle service level): of the lead times that finished inside the year, the share with
  no lost sale. This is what the "service level" setting promises.
- **Fill**: units sold / units demanded.
- **Stock**: mean units on hand x the SKU's average selling price (no costs in the file).
- 3,078 SKUs sold in the year before the holdout and in the holdout (3,036 with a 180-day window).
  On Syntetos-Boylan's classes from the year before: 2,711 lumpy, 319 intermittent, 25 erratic,
  **0 smooth**. This is a lumpy-demand store; steady daily sellers were not tested.
- Confidence intervals: paired bootstrap over SKUs, 2,000 resamples, fixed seed.

Rules compared:

- `normal`, the rule the page shipped: safety = z x weekly sd / sqrt(7) x sqrt(lead).
- `auto`, shipped now as the default: SKUs that sold on fewer than 1 day in 1.32 of the window
  (Syntetos-Boylan's ADI cut-off) take the reorder point as the service-level quantile of their
  own demand over every run of `lead` consecutive days (`leadTimeQuantile`); others keep `normal`.
  On this store that is nearly every SKU.

Two other variants were tried on lead 14 and dropped: scaling the quantile by the recent-to-average
sales ratio (1.9-5.5 million GBP of stock for 73-85% CSL, far worse) and a lead+1 day quantile to
cover the overshoot past the reorder point (the same frontier as plain `ltd`, shifted).

## Results

Lead 14 days, window 365 (`online-retail-ii-lead14.txt`):

| setting | rule | fill | CSL | stock (GBP) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 90% | normal | 91.2 | 73.7 | 1,374,193 |
| 90% | auto | 90.3 | 74.3 | 1,190,136 |
| 95% | normal | 92.6 | 77.5 | 1,515,221 |
| 95% | auto | 92.8 | 80.7 | 1,462,822 |
| 98% | normal | 93.6 | 79.8 | 1,676,258 |
| 98% | auto | 94.8 | 86.0 | 2,027,869 |
| 99% | normal | 94.2 | 81.3 | 1,783,521 |
| 99% | auto | 95.4 | 86.9 | 2,172,098 |

Paired, auto minus normal, at the default 95%: **CSL +3.2 points [2.5, 3.9], fill +0.3 [0.1, 0.5],
stock -3.5% [-5.7, -1.5]**: better on all three at once.

Other settings (`online-retail-ii-lead7.txt`, `-lead28.txt`, `-lead14-window180.txt`), at 95%:

| run | CSL normal / auto | paired CSL | paired fill | paired stock |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| lead 7 | 81.8 / 82.9 | +1.1 [0.6, 1.6] | -0.1 [-0.3, 0.1] | -7.9% [-9.9, -6.0] |
| lead 28 | 71.4 / 80.0 | +8.5 [7.6, 9.6] | +1.5 [1.2, 1.8] | +8.7% [6.6, 10.9] |
| lead 14, 180-day window | 70.4 / 74.6 | +4.2 [3.5, 4.9] | +0.5 [0.2, 0.7] | +7.1% [5.1, 9.1] |

Where the two rules carry different stock, compare them at equal stock: interpolating `normal`'s
CSL and fill at each `auto` point that falls inside `normal`'s range, `auto` keeps more lead times
stocked in all six comparable points (+1.3 to +5.4 points of CSL); fill is higher in four and
0.3 points lower in two (180-day window, 95% and 98%).

**Neither rule delivers the promised service level on this store.** At a 95% setting the shelf
stayed stocked through 77-83% of lead times (70-75% on a 180-day window); at 99%, 77-90%. Big
single orders larger than the whole buffer, and lines whose sales fade between plans, account for
most of it. The page now says so next to the setting.

## Limits

- One store, wholesale-heavy, all lumpy demand. The default only changes the rule for lumpy and
  intermittent SKUs; steady sellers keep the old rule because nothing here tested them.
- Lost sales are what the replay assumes; a store that backorders would count differently.
- Demand while a SKU was out of stock in the real data is not visible, as in any orders export.
- Stock is valued at selling price because the file has no costs.

## Reproduce

```
python3 -m venv venv && venv/bin/pip install openpyxl
venv/bin/python - <<'PY'
import openpyxl, csv
wb = openpyxl.load_workbook("online_retail_II.xlsx", read_only=True)
w = csv.writer(open("orii.csv", "w", newline=""))
for i, ws in enumerate(wb.worksheets):
    for j, row in enumerate(ws.iter_rows(values_only=True)):
        if j == 0 and i > 0: continue
        w.writerow(row)

node --max-old-space-size=4000 eval/backtest.mjs orii.csv --lead 14        # ~2 min
```

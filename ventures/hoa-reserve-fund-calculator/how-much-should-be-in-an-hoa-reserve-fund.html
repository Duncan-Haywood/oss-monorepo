How much should be in an HOA reserve fund?

There is no single dollar figure or flat percentage of the budget that answers this for every association — the right number depends on what your specific roof, elevator, repaving, painting, and other capital components will cost to replace and how soon. What follows is that arithmetic worked through on a real, modeled 120-unit association, not a rule of thumb.

The target that matters: percent funded

Reserve studies measure a fund against percent funded: the balance on hand divided by the prorated, wear-based obligation every component has already accumulated toward its next replacement — not the balance divided by the sum of every future replacement cost, and not a flat share of the operating budget. Most studies flag anything under roughly 30% funded as a special-assessment risk.

A balance can look adequate in isolation and still fail that test. The association modeled below holds $150,000 against a $40,000/year contribution across six components (roof, repaving, exterior painting, pool resurfacing, elevator modernization, and common-area HVAC) on a 30-year horizon at 3.5% cost inflation and 2% credited interest.

What doing nothing costs over 30 years

Left at $40,000/year, the same association's projected balance by year 30 is −$2,890,659.64. Inflation raises every component's replacement cost every year while the contribution stays flat, so each replacement cycle borrows further against the next and the gap compounds rather than holding steady.

Common questions

How much should be in an HOA reserve fund?

Enough to cover the prorated, wear-based share of every capital component's eventual replacement cost — not a flat percentage of the budget and not a round dollar figure picked without a schedule. Most reserve studies treat a fund under roughly 30% funded (balance against that prorated obligation) as a special-assessment risk. A balance that looks comfortable in isolation, such as $150,000 against a $40,000 annual contribution, can already sit below that threshold once the roof, elevator, and repaving schedules are actually modeled.

What happens when an HOA reserve fund is underfunded?

The gap does not stay the same size — it compounds, because inflation raises every component's replacement cost every year while an unfunded obligation earns nothing. Left alone from a $40,000/year contribution, a 120-unit association modeled here goes from a $193,000 balance in year 1 to a deficit of roughly $2.89 million by year 30, because the contribution never grows to match rising replacement costs and each cycle borrows further against the next. The fund does not fail all at once; it falls a little further behind every year until a special assessment or a reserve loan is the only option left.

How is a special assessment calculated from a reserve shortfall?

As the dollar gap between what the target percent-funded threshold requires and what the fund actually holds that year, divided across the unit count. In the example modeled here, that gap is $118.77 per unit as a one-time assessment in the first shortfall year — or, spread out instead of billed as a lump sum, a $54.69 per unit monthly dues increase phased in from today, which is the per-unit cost of raising the annual contribution to the level that clears every year of the 30-year horizon.

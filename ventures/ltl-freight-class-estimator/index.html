Common questions

Why does density decide the class rather than weight?

Because a trailer runs out of space long before it runs out of weight. A carrier is selling floor and cube, so the question that sets the rate is how much weight your freight brings per cubic foot it occupies. Density is total weight divided by total cube, and the class bands here run from class 50 at 50 lb/ft³ and up, down to class 500 below 0.5 lb/ft³. Light, bulky freight is expensive to move for exactly the reason it feels cheap to lift.

How close am I to the next class down?

The estimate tells you, which is the practical use of this page. The worked example is a single 48″ × 40″ × 36″ pallet at 500 lb: 40 ft³, 12.5 lb/ft³, which lands in the class 85 band that starts at 12. The next band down, class 77.5, begins at 13.5 lb/ft³ — so on that pallet you are a lb/ft³ away. Sometimes that gap closes by consolidating two shipments, or by taking four inches off a pallet that is packed taller than it needs to be. Sometimes it does not close and it is worth knowing before you quote.

Which dimensions do I measure?

The packed outside dimensions, including the pallet, the stretch wrap, and anything that overhangs. Not the box, not the product. A carrier dimensioner measures the space your freight actually occupies on the trailer, so measuring the carton and ignoring a 2-inch overhang is how an estimate and a re-class notice end up disagreeing. Height is measured from the floor, pallet included.

Is weight per piece or for the whole shipment?

Per piece — and pieces means identical ones. The estimate multiplies both cube and weight by the piece count, so density is unchanged by how many identical pallets you ship. That is correct for density, and it is also why a mixed shipment cannot be estimated in one pass here: if your pallets differ, run the heavy configuration and the light one separately, because the real shipment classes as its parts rather than its average.

Why isn’t this an official freight class?

Because density is only one of the four things that set an NMFC classification. Handling, stowability, and liability — how fragile, how stackable, how valuable and how prone to theft or damage — can move the class regardless of how dense the freight is, and many commodities have their own NMFC item number that is not density-based at all. This is a planning estimate for the density half. Verify it with your carrier or a qualified freight professional before booking, and expect accessorials (liftgate, residential, inside delivery) to sit on top of whatever the class turns out to be.

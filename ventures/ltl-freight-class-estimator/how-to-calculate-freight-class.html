How do I calculate LTL freight class?

Every LTL quote form asks for a class before it will give you a price, and the number is not arbitrary: for most freight it comes straight out of density — total weight divided by total cubic feet. Work out the density and you know the band. The part that catches small shippers is that density is measured to the outside of the packaging, pallet included, so the box decides the class as much as the product does.

The arithmetic, in order

  1. Multiply length × width × height in inches, then divide by 1728. That is cubic feet for one piece.
  2. Multiply by the number of pieces for total cube, and multiply the per-piece weight by the same count for total weight.
  3. Divide total weight by total cube. That is density, in pounds per cubic foot.
  4. Read the band. 50 lb/ft³ and above is class 50; the bands step up through class 500 as density drops below 0.5 lb/ft³.

Worked example

A single pallet measuring 48 × 40 × 36 inches at 500 lb is 40.00 cubic feet, giving a density of 12.5 lb/ft³ and an estimated class 85. The next lower band, class 77.5, starts at 13.5 lb/ft³ — so it is within reach two ways: add about 40 lb to the same footprint, or take the cube down to roughly 37 cubic feet, which is about three inches off the pallet height. Neither is obvious from the weight and the class number alone, which is why the estimator shows the cube, the density, and the distance to the next band together.

Common questions

How do I calculate LTL freight class?

Multiply length by width by height in inches and divide by 1728 to get cubic feet per piece, then multiply by the number of pieces for total cube. Divide total weight by total cube to get density in pounds per cubic foot. That density falls into a class band: 50 lb/ft3 and above is class 50, and the bands step up through class 500 as density falls below 0.5 lb/ft3. Density is the input you control; carrier rules, stowability, handling, and liability can still change the class the carrier actually bills.

Why is my freight class higher than I expected?

Almost always because the shipment is lighter for its size than you assumed — class rises as density falls. Packaging is usually the cause: an oversized carton, a tall pallet with empty headspace, or dunnage that adds cube without adding weight. The cube is measured to the outside of the packaging, including the pallet, not to the product inside it.

Can I lower my freight class by changing the packaging?

Sometimes, and it is worth checking before you book. Because density is weight divided by cube, you can raise density either by consolidating more weight onto the same footprint or by removing cube that carries no weight — trimming pallet height, using a smaller carton, or stacking rather than shipping two half-full pallets. The estimator shows the density the next lower class band needs, so you can see whether the gap is inches or out of reach.

Is a density estimate the same as the class the carrier bills?

No. Density gives you the class band to expect and to sanity-check a quote against, which is what makes the carrier's form answerable. The billed class can still differ: many commodities have a fixed class regardless of density, and carriers can reclassify on stowability, handling, or liability, or re-measure the shipment at the terminal. Treat the estimate as preparation for the quote, not a substitute for it.

How to calculate a bar bending schedule

A bar bending schedule lists every reinforcing bar on a job: its mark, size, shape and dimensions, and how many there are. Three numbers come out of it: the cut length of each bar, the weight of steel, and the cutting plan that says which bars to cut from each stock length. This page works all three by hand for a small made-up schedule; the free tool does the same for yours.

1. Cut length: outside dimensions minus what the bends take

Why is a bent bar shorter than the sum of its dimensions?

Schedule dimensions are measured to the outside of the bar, and the bar goes round each corner on a curve. At a 90° bend each straight leg stops r + d short of the outside corner, and the centreline runs round an arc of radius r + d/2, so one bend takes 2(r + d) − π/2 (r + d/2) off the sum of the legs.

How much shorter depends on the bend radius, and BS 8666 sets a minimum scheduling radius r for each size: 2d for bars up to 16 mm (24 mm for a 12 mm bar) and 3.5d from 20 mm (70 mm for a 20 mm bar).

Shape-code charts print a total-length formula for each shape. The ones the tool uses:

ShapeWhat it isTotal length
00StraightA
11L-bar, one 90° bendA + B − 0.5r − d
21U-bar, two 90° bendsA + B + C − r − 2d
51Closed link with hooks2(A + B + C) − 2.5r − 5d

For a 12 mm bar one bend takes 2 × 36 − 1.571 × 30 = 24.9 mm; the chart's 0.5r + d gives 24 mm. The tool computes both and its tests fail if they drift more than a tenth of a bar diameter apart at any standard size. Shape R in the tool uses the geometry directly for any bar with only 90° bends.

Worked: mark 02, a 12 mm L-bar with A = 1800 and B = 450: 1800 + 450 − 0.5 × 24 − 12 = 2226 mm, rounded up to the next 25 mm: 2250 mm. Mark 05, a 10 mm link 350 × 550 with 100 mm hooks: 2 × 1000 − 2.5 × 20 − 5 × 10 = 1900 mm.

2. Weight

How much does a rebar weigh per metre?

Steel weighs 7850 kg/m³, so a bar of diameter d mm weighs π d²/4 × 7850 / 10⁶ kg per metre, about d²/162: 0.617 kg/m for 10 mm, 0.888 for 12 mm, 1.579 for 16 mm, 2.466 for 20 mm.

Mark 01, 48 bars of 12 mm at 5400 mm: 48 × 5.4 × 0.888 = 230 kg. The example schedule in the tool comes to 1,241.6 kg.

3. The cutting plan: where the money is

Bars arrive in stock lengths, usually 12 m. Every offcut too short to use is steel paid for and thrown away. The 12 mm bars in the example are:

01  48 × 5400
02  32 × 2250
09  16 × 2100

Cutting by hand, the usual rule is longest first, each piece into the first bar it fits (first-fit decreasing). Two 5400s go in each of the first 24 bars, leaving 1200 mm that no remaining piece fits. Then five 2250s per bar, and the 2100s. That takes 34 bars.

The tool finds this instead:

16 x 12000: 1 x 5400 + 2 x 2250 + 1 x 2100, offcut 0
16 x 12000: 2 x 5400, offcut 1200

32 stock bars, two fewer, because one 5400 + two 2250s + one 2100 uses a 12 m bar exactly. The steel itself is 364.8 m, 30.4 bars, but that is not the bound: the column-generation bound for these three lengths is exactly 32.0 bars, so no plan can use fewer, and 32 is proven optimal.

On a real schedule the combinations are too many to try by hand. The tool solves the cutting-stock problem the way it has been solved since Gilmore and Gomory (1961): the linear relaxation by column generation (a simplex over cutting patterns, with a knapsack programme that finds the best new pattern from the simplex prices), which gives a lower bound no whole-bar plan can beat, then several ways of turning it into whole bars, keeping the cheapest. If the plan meets the bound, it is optimal and the page says so; if not, the page says how far it could at most be from optimal.

How it does on schedules generated at random (200 of them, 4 to 25 different lengths per bar size, 600 to 11,000 mm, cut from 12 m): the plan met the proven bound in 197 of 200 and was at most one bar over in the others; first-fit decreasing bought more bars in 122 of the 200, about 1% more steel overall. These are generated schedules, not real jobs, so take the 1% as an order of size: on a job that buys 50 tonnes of rebar it is about half a tonne. The script that produced these numbers is in the tool's source (check/benchmark.mjs).

When to use something else

When should I use something else?

Detailing software (RebarCAD, aSa and similar) draws the bars from the model, covers every shape code and lap rule, and feeds the bending machines. This tool is for checking a schedule someone sent you, weighing it for an order, or planning the cutting on site or in a small shop.

It covers shapes 00, 11, 21, 51 and any 90° shape; other shapes, laps, couplers and bend radii larger than the minimum are not modelled.

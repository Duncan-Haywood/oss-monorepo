This calculator vs. doing depreciation recapture by hand

Depreciation recapture is arithmetic anyone can do with a calculator or a spreadsheet. The problem is that IRC Section 1250 requires splitting the gain into two pieces taxed at different rates, capping the recapture piece two different ways, and reordering the whole calculation again if any of the proceeds come out of a 1031 exchange as boot — and a hand calculation that misses any one of those still produces a total that looks reasonable.

Common questions

What's the most common mistake when estimating depreciation recapture by hand?

Taxing the entire gain at your long-term capital-gains rate and skipping the recapture split. IRC Section 1250 pulls the portion of the gain equal to depreciation actually claimed (capped at 25%, taxed at ordinary rates) out of the gain first — only what's left over is taxed at the lower capital-gains rate. Applying one blended rate to the whole gain understates the bill whenever your capital-gains rate is below 25%.

Why can't recapture just be "depreciation taken" times 25%?

Because unrecaptured Section 1250 gain is capped at the smaller of the depreciation actually claimed or the total gain itself — you cannot recapture more than you gained, even if you claimed more depreciation than that over the years you owned the property (a sale close to break-even after a long holding period). A hand calculation that multiplies the full depreciation figure by 25% without capping it at the gain overstates the tax.

Does land value matter for this calculation?

Yes — land is never depreciable, so it should never be subtracted from your adjusted basis as if it were. A hand calculation that treats the full purchase price as depreciable, or forgets to add back capital improvements, produces the wrong adjusted basis and therefore the wrong total gain before recapture is even split out.

If I do a 1031 exchange, is the boot taxed at my capital-gains rate?

Not necessarily all of it. Boot — cash or net debt relief you actually receive from the exchange — is recognized gain, and recognized gain is taxed in the same recapture-then-capital-gains order as an outright sale: the recapture portion (up to the depreciation taken, capped at 25%) comes out of the boot first, at ordinary rates, before any remainder is taxed at your capital-gains rate. A hand estimate that applies one capital-gains rate to the whole boot amount understates the tax on a partial exchange.

Does this calculator know my state's tax on the sale?

No — neither this calculator nor a hand calculation built from the FAQ here includes state income tax. Most states tax this gain too, often as ordinary income with no capital-gains discount. Both this tool and a manual estimate need your state's own rules added on top; check your state's tax agency or a CPA before relying on either total.

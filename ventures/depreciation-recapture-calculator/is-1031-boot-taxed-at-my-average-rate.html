Is 1031 exchange boot taxed at my average rate?

No. It's tempting to take the blended rate your full sale would owe and apply it to whatever boot you pull out of an exchange. That understates the bill, because boot is not a random sample of your gain — it comes out of the higher-taxed recapture portion first, in full, before any of it reaches your lower capital-gains rate.

Worked example

A rental bought for $200,000 ($40,000 land, $160,000 depreciable), with $100,000 of depreciation claimed over the holding period, sold for $500,000 minus $30,000 of selling costs:

Blended rate on the full sale ($65,500 ÷ $370,000)17.70%

Now suppose this seller does a 1031 exchange but takes $50,000 of cash out — boot — instead of reinvesting every dollar:

The $50,000 boot is smaller than the $100,000 of depreciation taken, so under Section 1250's recapture-then-remainder order, all of it is unrecaptured Section 1250 gain — taxed at the 25% recapture rate, none of it at the 15% capital-gains rate. $50,000 × 25% = $12,500, exactly. Where the crossover sits

The pattern holds for any boot up to the amount of depreciation actually taken — every dollar of it is recapture, taxed at up to 25%, regardless of how large or small the boot is relative to the total gain:

Boot receivedRecognized as recaptureRecognized as capital gainTax due
$20,000$20,000$0$5,000
$50,000$50,000$0$12,500
$100,000 (equals depreciation taken)$100,000$0$25,000
$150,000 (past the crossover)$100,000$50,000$32,500

Past $100,000 of boot — the depreciation-taken crossover on this example — the extra dollars finally reach the 15% capital-gains rate, which is why the effective rate on the whole boot amount keeps falling as the boot grows past that point, but never below the rate on an outright sale.

Common questions

Is 1031 exchange boot taxed at my average or blended tax rate?

No. Boot is recognized gain, and recognized gain is taxed in the same recapture-then-capital-gains order as an outright sale — the unrecaptured Section 1250 portion comes out of the boot first, at your ordinary rate up to 25%, and only the remainder (if any) gets your lower capital-gains rate. Averaging the two rates across your whole gain and applying that single rate to the boot understates the tax whenever your depreciation taken is larger than the boot itself.

Why does the boot get the worse rate first instead of a proportional share?

Because Section 1250 does not ask which dollars you received — it asks how much of the recognized gain is unrecaptured depreciation, and that amount is capped at the smaller of the depreciation actually taken or the recognized gain. When the boot is smaller than the depreciation taken, the entire boot fits inside that cap, so all of it is recapture. Only once the boot exceeds the depreciation taken does the excess spill into the capital-gains bucket.

At what boot amount does the rate improve?

Once boot received exceeds the depreciation actually taken on the property. Below that crossover, 100% of the boot is taxed at the recapture rate (up to 25%). Above it, the amount over the crossover is taxed at your capital-gains rate instead, so the blended rate on the boot gradually improves as the boot grows past that point.

Are your lien deadlines still open?

A subcontractor or supplier who is not paid usually keeps the right to file a mechanics lien only if two clocks are respected: an early preliminary notice, and a later lien filing. Both run from dates on your own job. Enter the day you first furnished labor or materials and the day you last did, and this tool shows both dates for California, Texas, Florida, Arizona, or Washington — and how many days are left.

Common questions

What is a preliminary notice in a mechanics lien claim?

A preliminary notice (called a Notice to Owner in Florida, a notice of unpaid balance in Texas, and a 20-day preliminary notice in Arizona) is a written notice a subcontractor or supplier sends to the owner and general contractor early in a job, to preserve the right to file a lien later. Several states make it a condition of lien rights, so a missed deadline can cost the claim.

How long do I have to file a mechanics lien after finishing the work?

It depends on the state. California allows 90 days after completion of the project, or 30 days after the owner records a notice of completion. Florida allows 90 days after your last furnishing, Washington 90 days after your last furnishing, Arizona 120 days after completion or 60 days after a recorded notice of completion, and Texas the 15th day of the 4th month on commercial work or the 3rd month on residential work.

When does the preliminary notice clock start?

For California, Arizona, Florida, and Washington it starts on the day you first furnished labor or materials: 20 days in California and Arizona, 45 days in Florida, and 60 days in Washington (10 days on new single-family residential work). Texas instead counts months: the 15th day of the 3rd month after the month of unpaid work on commercial jobs, or the 2nd month on residential jobs.

Why does the calculator use my last furnishing date when I do not know the completion date?

Some states measure the lien deadline from completion of the whole project. Your own last day on the job can only be the same as or earlier than completion, so using it gives the same date or an earlier, safer one. Entering the real completion date can only move the deadline later.

No. This tool applies a simplified reading of published state timing rules to the dates you enter, for a first-tier subcontractor or supplier. It does not model second-tier claimants, public projects, required notice content, delivery method, or foreclosure deadlines, and lien rights can be lost by missing a deadline.

This calculator vs. a flat panel-upgrade price

“A panel upgrade runs about $2,500” is not a price for a panel upgrade — it is a price for one feeder length, one circuit count, one path, and one answer about the utility, quietly quoted on every job that calls in.

What a flat panel-upgrade price is

A flat price is the fastest way to answer the phone: one number for “a panel upgrade”, sometimes dressed up as a dollars-per-amp rate so it looks like it flexes with the job. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between contractors, and it is the number a customer holding a neighbour’s invoice or a competitor’s quote will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set on one job — one amp rating, one overhead mast, a short feeder in an unfinished garage, the existing grounding left alone, no meter socket, no temporary power, no utility coordination — and then reused on every panel afterward, whether or not the next one looks anything like it.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the existing and new amp rating, whether the service is overhead or underground, whether the panel is a main-breaker can or a meter-main combo, whether it sits in a garage, a basement, or finished living space, how many feeder feet the run takes, how many circuits have to be landed and how many of those need AFCI or GFCI breakers, and whether the job includes a new meter socket, new grounding electrodes, temporary power, or utility coordination. Against your own loaded wage, overhead, travel, permit and utility fees, and target margin, it returns the bid price, the price per amp of new service, the effective hourly rate, and the break-even price below which the job loses money — for this panel, not a generic one.

What a flat panel-upgrade price doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same whether the feeder is eight feet across an open garage or forty feet through finished walls and ceilings. Prices feeder feet at their own hours-per-foot and cable cost, and adds finished-space access hours as a separate line.
Treats “landing the circuits” as one lump, whether there are ten of them or twenty with AFCI on every one. Prices circuits and AFCI/GFCI breakers separately, at both their own labour hours and their own material cost per breaker.
Prices an overhead mast swap and an underground service lateral as the same job. Carries a different cable cost per foot, a different mast kit, and separate underground path hours for each service type.
Has no line for a new meter socket, new ground rods, temporary power, or the hours spent coordinating with the utility. Each is its own toggle with its own hours and material, because each is present on some panel jobs and absent on others.
Gives the electrician a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the break-even price and the effective hourly rate, so a shopped or matched price can be scored against real cost before anyone commits to it.

All three are “a panel upgrade”. The same flat number is generous on the first, loses money on the second, and is not close on the third. Run your own job in the calculator to see which side of that gap it falls on.

Why a per-amp rate does not fix it

The usual repair is to quote dollars per amp instead, so the number at least appears to scale with the service. On this calculator’s own defaults it scales the wrong way. The 100A same-amp garage swap prices at $29.41 per amp of new service. The 200A → 400A class change with a new meter-main prices at $27.63 per amp — less per amp, on a far larger job, because the fixed work (getting there, the permit, coordinating the utility, landing what is already there) does not double when the amp rating does.

So a per-amp rate derived from a small job overcharges every large one, and a rate derived from a large job undercharges every small one. Carry that per-amp rate onto the 200A underground job above and it quotes $5,881.71 — $2,231.12 under that job’s break-even. The rate is not merely imprecise; it points away from the cost.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a flat number or a per-amp rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat panel-upgrade price get the bid wrong?

Because almost none of the labour scales with the words “panel upgrade”. The hours come from feeder feet, how many circuits have to be landed, how many of those need AFCI or GFCI breakers, whether the path is overhead or underground, whether the panel sits in a garage or in finished living space, and whether the utility has to be coordinated with at all. A flat price was set on one job with one answer to each of those questions, and then quoted on jobs with different answers.

Is a dollars-per-amp rate any better than a flat price?

No, and it can be worse, because it looks like it is adjusting for job size when it is not. Run this calculator’s own presets on its default rates: the 100A same-amp garage swap prices at $29.41 per amp of new service, while the 200A to 400A class change with a new meter-main prices at $27.63 per amp. The larger service is cheaper per amp, so a per-amp rate derived from a small job overcharges the large one and a rate derived from a large job undercharges the small one. The rate moves in the opposite direction from the cost.

What does a flat price leave out entirely?

The line items that turn a one-day job into a two-day one: the feeder run and its cable, the per-circuit landing time, AFCI and GFCI breakers at both their hours and their material cost, a new meter socket, new grounding electrodes, temporary power, utility coordination hours, and the permit and utility fees themselves. Each is its own input here, because each is present on some panel jobs and absent on others.

How far under water can a flat price actually put a job?

On this calculator’s defaults, a flat $2,500 panel-upgrade quote against its 100A to 200A underground preset - a forty-foot feeder through finished space, twenty AFCI circuits, temporary power and a utility disconnect - lands $5,612.83 below that job’s break-even price of $8,112.83. That is before any profit: the job loses more than twice the flat quote itself.

Is a flat panel-upgrade price ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs it can land close - same amp rating, same path, same panel location, a similar circuit count and a similar answer on grounding and the utility. A shop that only does same-amp garage swaps in one neighbourhood may quote a flat price for years without being bitten. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because nothing about a flat number tells you when the next job has stopped matching the one the number was built on.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It holds no data on local rates, competitors, or what a homeowner in your area expects to pay. It takes the wage, burden, overhead, travel, and target margin you enter and turns them into a break-even price and a bid price for the feeder, circuits, path, grounding, and utility work you describe. It replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not your judgment about your own market.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat panel-upgrade price is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the number was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. It names no company or product: the comparison is against a pricing method, not against a competitor.

Your costs

Payroll burden covers employer taxes, workers’ compensation, liability insurance, and paid time off — the part of a cleaner’s cost that never shows up on their pay rate. Underestimating it is the most common reason a janitorial bid that looked profitable was not.

If they cut the schedule

A prospect who asks for 3 days a week instead of 5 will often offer 60% of the current monthly price. Labor hours do drop with the visits. Supplies, equipment, and travel stay on the job, so the price that still holds your margin is higher than a linear cut. Why 60% of a 5-day price is not a 3-day bid.

If they want to buy the paper

A facility manager who says “we’ll buy the paper, soap, and liners — take the supplies off” is not asking you to drop the whole line. Restroom stock is only a share of it. Chemicals, finish, and glass stay yours. And if they run out, the emergency fill you still make can cost more than stocking it yourself. Should I let the building buy the paper?

If they want the initial thrown in

A neglected or vacant building’s first visit is several regular cleans of labor plus extra chemicals. Prospects ask to “just include it.” Month 1 then carries a project on a periodic price — and if they cancel after 30 days, that giveaway is the whole relationship.       Common bidding questions

      How do I price a day porter on a janitorial contract? Price the person, not the floor. Hours = people × hours per shift × shifts per week × 52/12, then the same loaded wage, overhead, and margin as the night clean. An 8-hour weekday porter is about 173 hours a month — usually more labor than the entire after-hours production clean.

      Should I bake the day porter into the monthly cleaning price? No. The night clean is production-rate work; the day porter is coverage during business hours. Combining them hides how much of the bid is a body on site. This calculator keeps the two numbers separate and adds them into one monthly contract price.

      How do I price strip and wax with a janitorial contract? Treat it as one-time project work. Hours are the floor area divided by a production rate, times the number of finish coats. Add finish, stripper, and pads as materials. Keep that total off the monthly service price so you are not giving away eleven months of wax after the first invoice.

      Should the initial deep clean be in the monthly price? Usually no. An initial or post-construction clean is a different production rate and a one-time invoice. Mixing it into the recurring bid is how a contract that looked fine in month one loses money for the rest of the year.

      Why not bid the whole building by square foot? Restrooms, kitchens, and exam rooms take several times the labor of open office. Two 8,000 sq ft buildings can differ by 30% in hours. The labor split is there so that mix is visible before the price goes out.

      Common questions

      How do I price a day porter on a janitorial contract?

      Price the day porter as scheduled coverage: people × hours per shift × shifts per week × 52/12, then the same loaded wage, overhead, and margin as the night clean. Do not convert the porter into extra square feet or a faster production rate.

      Should I bake the day porter into the monthly cleaning price?

      No. The night clean is production-rate work; the day porter is a person on site during business hours. Combining them hides how much of the bid is coverage.

      How do I price strip and wax with a janitorial contract?

      Treat strip and wax as one-time project work invoiced separately from the monthly service so the remaining months are not underpriced.

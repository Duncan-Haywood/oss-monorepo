FOR COMMERCIAL CLEANING CONTRACTORS

Bid the building, not the square footage.

Price a janitorial contract the way the trade actually does: each space divided by the production rate your crew really hits, then wage burden, supplies, overhead, and the margin you intend to keep. Add strip and wax, carpet extraction, or an initial clean as one-time project work so it does not get baked into the monthly price. You get the recurring bid, the first-month invoice, and the number below which the job loses money.

Your costs

Payroll burden covers employer taxes, workers’ compensation, liability insurance, and paid time off — the part of a cleaner’s cost that never shows up on their pay rate. Underestimating it is the most common reason a janitorial bid that looked profitable was not.

Strip and wax, carpet extraction, and the initial deep clean are invoiced once. Pricing them inside the monthly contract is how a contractor works eleven months at a loss after the building is caught up. Hours = square feet ÷ production rate × coats or passes.

    Where the labor goes

      Transparent arithmetic from your own inputs. The starting production rates are common ballpark figures, not an industry standard, a benchmark, or a guarantee — time your own crews and replace them.

      Common bidding questions

      How do I price strip and wax with a janitorial contract? Treat it as one-time project work. Hours are the floor area divided by a production rate, times the number of finish coats. Add finish, stripper, and pads as materials. Keep that total off the monthly service price so you are not giving away eleven months of wax after the first invoice.

      Should the initial deep clean be in the monthly price? Usually no. An initial or post-construction clean is a different production rate and a one-time invoice. Mixing it into the recurring bid is how a contract that looked fine in month one loses money for the rest of the year.

      Why not bid the whole building by square foot? Restrooms, kitchens, and exam rooms take several times the labor of open office. Two 8,000 sq ft buildings can differ by 30% in hours. The labor split on the right is there so that mix is visible before the price goes out.

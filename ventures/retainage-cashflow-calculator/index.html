Common questions

How much retainage will be withheld on my contract?

Retainage withheld is the contract value times your retainage percentage — it does not depend on how much has been paid to date. On the worksheet defaults, a $100,000 contract at 10% retainage withholds $10,000.00. The calculator separately tracks retainage still unpaid, which is capped at whichever is smaller: the full retainage withheld, or the earned-but-unpaid balance.

Is retainage the same thing as an unpaid invoice?

No. Earned but unpaid is the contract value minus what has actually been paid — $25,000.00 on the defaults ($100,000 contract, $75,000 paid). Retainage still unpaid is a narrower figure inside that: the smaller of the retainage withheld ($10,000.00) or the earned-but-unpaid amount. On the defaults the full $10,000.00 of retainage is still inside that $25,000.00 owed, not on top of it.

When does retainage typically release?

The calculator models a common AIA-style rule: the full retainage rate applies to billed work until the job passes a reduction point (50% complete by default), then the rate drops to a lower reduced rate (5% by default). On the worksheet defaults — a job 75% complete against that 50%-complete reduction point and 5% reduced rate — the modeled proper hold is $5,000.00 of the $7,500.00 progress retainage billed, meaning about $2,500.00 is typically already releasable. This is a planning model built from a common contract pattern, not a reading of your actual subcontract — check the release terms your contract actually states.

What does a payment delay actually cost me?

The calculator applies your own annual carrying-cost assumption only to the earned-but-unpaid balance, not the full contract value. On the defaults — $25,000.00 earned but unpaid at a 12% annual carrying-cost assumption — each additional day models about $8.22, and another 30 days of delay models about $246.58. Over the 90 days already outstanding in the default scenario, that comes to $739.73 in modeled carrying cost on top of the $25,000.00 owed. This is a planning figure using your own rate assumption, not a contractual interest claim.

Should I bid higher to cover retainage?

The calculator compares what it takes to keep the same progress-payment cash at 0%, 5%, and 10% retainage. On a $100,000 job at 10% retainage, keeping the same cash flow as a no-retainage job means bidding $111,111.11 — $11,111.11 more — because the withheld 10% has to be grossed back up from progress payments. That is different from the smaller economic add-on ($296.77 on the defaults) that only covers the modeled carrying cost of the hold. Most contractors price the cash-now gap, not just the interest, when retainage is negotiable.

Should I take a partial retainage check now?

On the worksheet defaults — a $100,000 contract with 10% retainage, $75,000 paid, and a 12% carrying-cost assumption — waiting 60 days for the remaining $10,000 models $197.26 more carrying cost on that retainage, leaving $9,802.74. A $5,000 check that closes the rest is $4,802.74 behind that wait. If the other $5,000 still arrives in 60 days, taking the check now leaves $9,901.37. The calculator shows the arithmetic; it does not tell you to sign a waiver.

This calculator vs. a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet multiplies contract value by a retainage rate fine. Where it quietly goes wrong: the reduction-point release schedule, comparing a partial check today against waiting, and writing the follow-up email from the same numbers.

What each tool does

A lot of contractors already track retainage in a spreadsheet: one column for contract value, one for the retainage percentage, a formula that multiplies them. That arithmetic is trivial and a spreadsheet does it fine. What it does not do on its own is model the reduction-point release schedule many contracts use — the full retainage rate on completed work until a stated percent complete, then a lower rate on the whole contract from there. Reproducing that correctly means a conditional formula built and maintained per project, not a single multiplication.

It also does not compare a partial check offered today against waiting. That decision has three different shapes — take the check and close the rest, take it while the remainder still arrives later, or wait for the full amount — each with its own carrying-cost math, and building all three correctly in cells is where a quick formula stops being quick. And it cannot write the payment follow-up: a spreadsheet holds numbers, not the sentence that turns them into a factual status request.

Common questions

Isn't retainage withheld just contract value times the retainage percentage?

That multiplication is trivial and a spreadsheet does it fine. What it does not do on its own is model the reduction-point release schedule many contracts use — the full rate on completed work until a stated percent complete, then a lower rate on the whole contract from there. Reproducing that in a spreadsheet means writing and maintaining a conditional formula per project; this calculator applies the rule from the percent complete, reduction point, and reduced rate you enter.

How is comparing a partial retainage check to waiting harder than a spreadsheet formula?

A partial check today can mean three different deals — take it and close the rest, take it while the remainder still arrives later, or wait for the full amount — and each has its own carrying-cost math against the days involved. Modeling one path in a cell is straightforward; modeling and comparing all three, with the carrying cost applied only to the unpaid retainage and not the rest of the earned balance, is where a spreadsheet formula stops being quick to build correctly. This calculator computes and ranks all three from the same inputs.

Can a spreadsheet write the payment follow-up email?

Not on its own — a spreadsheet holds numbers, not the sentence that turns them into a factual status request naming the project, invoice reference, due date, and unpaid amount. This calculator generates that follow-up draft from the same figures already in the worksheet, so the numbers in the email and the numbers behind it can't drift apart.

Does this replace a project's own payment-tracking spreadsheet?

No. A spreadsheet or ledger that tracks every pay application, every retainage line, and every payment received across a whole project is still the right tool for that ongoing record. This page answers one question at a time — what is this project's retainage, release schedule, and partial-offer decision worth right now — and nothing it computes is saved anywhere once you leave the page.

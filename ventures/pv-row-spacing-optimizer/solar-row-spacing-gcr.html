Solar row spacing: the 9-to-3 rule against the money answer

Every ground-mount solar plant has to answer one question early: how far apart do the rows go? Too close and the rows shade each other on winter mornings; too far and you lease land, run cable and build fence for spacing that earns nothing. This guide explains the usual rule, what it leaves out, and what it cost on two real weather files.

Ground coverage ratio in one line

GCR = table width / row pitch. The table width is the slant width of a row of modules (two modules in portrait is about 4 m); the pitch is the distance from one row to the same point on the next. A GCR of 0.4 with 4 m tables is a 10 m pitch. Fixed-tilt plants are commonly built between about 0.3 and 0.5, trackers between about 0.3 and 0.4.

The 9-to-3 rule

The usual starting point: space the rows so that no row shades the next between 9:00 and 15:00 solar time on the winter solstice, the day the sun is lowest. It is easy to compute and it is safe. But it ignores everything that decides whether a spacing pays: the price of land, the value of the energy, how much of the year's light is diffuse rather than direct, and the electrical effect of a shadow on a module's bypass-diode substrings. It also treats every latitude the same, and at high latitudes the winter sun at 9:00 is so low that the rule spreads rows very far apart.

A worked example

The tool's example site: 40 acres near Albuquerque, NM (35.0° N), a clear sky every day, 4 m fixed tables with 2 electrical bands, modules 21% efficient, installed at $1.00/W, energy worth $50/MWh, land leased at $800 per acre a year, 7% discount rate over 30 years. Searching every tilt from 0° to 40° and every GCR from 0.20 to 0.70:

  • The best fixed layout is 28° at GCR 0.46: a 8.7 m pitch, 5.2 m clear between rows, 2117 kWh/kWp a year with 0.74% lost to the rows, net present value $498,686.
  • The 9-to-3 rule at the same tilt gives GCR 0.51 (a 7.9 m pitch). It fits more capacity on the 40 acres, but loses 1.45% to the rows, and its net present value is $459,665, $39,021 less.
  • A backtracking single-axis tracker at GCR 0.39 (6.2 m pitch, $0.10/W more) is worth $2,109,066 on the same land: at this sunny, clear-sky site trackers win by far.

Here the rule packs rows tighter than the money answer; at a high-latitude site it does the opposite. Clear sky every day overstates the yield and the shading loss, which is why the tool takes a real weather file.

What the model leaves out

Long rows on flat ground, no horizon, no bifacial rear-side gain, no snow, no inverter clipping, and racking and cable costs that do not change with spacing. It is a screening model for choosing a spacing and tilt to take into detailed design, not a replacement for it.

Questions

What GCR should a fixed-tilt solar plant use?

There is no single number: it is where the energy the next step of spacing saves from shade is worth less than the land it takes. On the two real weather files tested here, the cheapest fixed layouts were GCR 0.40 at 36 degrees north and 0.24 at 55 degrees north, with the page's default costs.

Does the 9-to-3 rule pack rows too tight or too loose?

Either, depending on the site. In the clear-sky Albuquerque example it packs rows slightly tighter than the best layout; at 55 degrees north it spreads them nearly three times farther apart than the cheapest layout.

Why not design on clear-sky weather?

Clear sky has far more direct beam than any real climate, so it overstates the yield (by 41 percent at Greensboro and 94 percent at Sand Point here) and the shading loss. Use a typical-year file for the site.

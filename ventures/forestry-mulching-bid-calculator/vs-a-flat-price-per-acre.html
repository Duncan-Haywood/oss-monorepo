This calculator vs. a flat price per acre

A price per acre is the number a landowner asks for on the phone — one rate, times the acreage, whatever size the lot turns out to be. That rate is learned on one job and then quoted on the next, and the cost it cannot see is mobilization: the trailer trip that costs the same whether the lot is five acres or three-quarters of one.

What a flat price per acre is

It is the fastest number to quote over the phone: one rate, times the acreage. It is usually learned on a job that was easy to price — a several-acre lot where the trailer trip out and back was a small share of the total — and then quoted again on the next job that calls, regardless of how big that job is.

Nothing about "how many acres" tells the operator what share of this particular job is the trailer trip rather than the cutting. The flat rate does not ask, because it was learned on a job where the answer did not matter much.

What this calculator does instead

What a flat price per acre doesWhat this calculator does instead
Divides mobilization — loading, floating out, unloading, floating back — evenly across whatever acreage the rate happens to be scaled to. Prices mobilization as its own line, from trips, hours, and miles, so it does not scale with acreage at all.
Charges a small lot the same rate as the large lot the rate was learned on, even though the trailer trip is a much bigger share of a small job's true cost. Reports mobilization's share of the price for this specific acreage, so a mobilization-dominated small job is visible before it is quoted.
Has no line for production rate — the same rate is quoted whether the machine covers this ground fast or slow. Derives acres per machine-hour from head width, advance speed, passes, and terrain, so the price reflects how long this specific ground actually takes.
Gives the operator a number to quote before the trailer has even left the yard. Reports the break-even cost and the price at your target margin for this job's own acreage, so a flat rate can be checked against real cost before you match it.
Cannot tell you when quoting the same rate on a smaller job turns a healthy margin into a thin one. Prices the job from its own inputs, so a rate that compresses margin on a small lot shows up as a lower margin instead of a surprise at the bank.

The gap is $245.89 on one job, on a rate that was never wrong for Job A, only quoted on a lot too small to carry it.

When a flat rate still works

If every lot on a route runs close to the same acreage, a flat per-acre number can land close to cost, because mobilization really does stay a similar share of every job. The risk is the small lot that breaks the pattern — a fence line, a driveway clearing, a half-acre corner — quoted at the same rate because the phone call never asked how big the job actually was.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per acre lose money on a small job?

Because mobilization -- loading the machine, floating it to the site, unloading, and floating it back -- costs the same $202.50 whether the lot is 5 acres or 0.75, and a flat per-acre rate spreads that cost evenly across whatever job it was quoted from. A rate of $965.91 an acre, priced from a 5-acre job where mobilization is only 4.2% of the bill, carries almost none of that fixed cost on the acre it is quoted against. Apply the same rate to a 0.75-acre job, where the identical $202.50 trip is 20.9% of what the job should cost to run, and the rate collects far less than the trip alone requires a fair price for.

How much does that actually cost on a small job?

Take the same crew and machine pricing a 5-acre medium-brush job at a 30% target margin: the job costs $3,380.69 to run and prices at $4,829.56, or $965.91 an acre. Quote that same $965.91-an-acre rate on a 0.75-acre job that actually costs $679.23 to run -- including the same $202.50 mobilization trip -- and it collects $724.43: a profit of $45.21, a 6.2% margin, against the 30% target and the $291.10 the job was supposed to clear. The rate was never wrong for the 5-acre job it came from; it is $245.89 short on the small one.

Is a flat per-acre rate ever fine to quote?

For jobs close to the size the rate was learned on, yes -- a 5-acre lot quoted at a rate derived from other 5-acre lots carries its fair share of mobilization and machine time. The risk is the small job that calls next: a half-acre fence line or a driveway lot gets the same rate as the acreage it was priced from, and the trailer trip that does not shrink with the lot is the difference between a margin and a loss.

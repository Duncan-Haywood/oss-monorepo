What a small app can be liable for, and how to check

Most small apps never meet a regulator. The ones that get hurt are usually hurt by something cheap to have fixed: a chargeback wave from a subscription nobody knew renewed, a privacy complaint about an analytics pixel, a leaked key that ran up a five-figure cloud bill. Each of these leaves a trace in the code, so a scan can catch it before launch.

Every finding is phrased as an observation with its evidence, a file and line, so you can check it. None is a determination that something is or is not lawful.

What we found running it on our own projects

On 16 October 2026 we ran node cli.mjs sweep over this repository's 1,274 product directories and 506 research projects. It took about 14 seconds.

  • The product portfolio: 4 low-severity findings, all comparison pages naming another product without a non-affiliation line. These are static, browser-only tools that collect nothing and carry their disclaimers, which is the house rule. On a portfolio built to that rule, the scan's job is to keep it true as it grows.
  • The one-click deploy templates: the OpenTofu templates that deploy an app into a stranger's AWS, Google Cloud or Azure account declared no budget or cost alarm. That is the financial exposure the rule exists for, and it lands on the person deploying. The same change that added this tool fixed it: each template now takes an optional alert address (or, on Google Cloud, a billing account) and creates a monthly budget that warns at 80% of actual and 100% of forecast spend.
  • Getting the rules right took the sweep. The first version raised 153 findings, almost all false. Titles like "Pallet vs Carton" read as competitor comparisons. "Do I need to register for GST?" read as a sign-up form. Pattern lists inside scanners read as live LLM calls. A password box for an API key read as a login. Each is now a test case.

What a scan cannot see

  • Whether your privacy notice is true, or what your vendors do with data.
  • Your contracts, employment, tax registration, or sector licensing (health, finance, insurance).
  • Text in images, a CMS or a database; anything behind a login.
  • Runtime behaviour: the scan reads files, it does not watch traffic. For spend, pair it with a guard in the request path such as LLM Spend Guard.

Run it continuously

Add node cli.mjs scan . --fail-on high to CI so a new high-severity exposure fails the pull request. Post --format md as a PR comment, or upload --format sarif to GitHub code scanning to see findings inline. For a monorepo, sweep gives one row per app. The repository's AGENT.md is a playbook for an AI coding agent: run the scan, triage each finding against the code, fix what is clearly a fix, and bring the judgment calls to a person.

Questions

Which liabilities can an automated scan find?

The ones that leave a trace in files: a tracker script without a consent manager, subscription checkout code without renewal and cancellation wording, data-collecting forms without a privacy notice, affiliate links before any disclosure, AI calls without a statement that AI is used, live keys, LLM endpoints with no limit, cloud IaC with no budget, and copyleft licences in a lockfile.

Which can it not find?

Anything that depends on facts outside the files: what your vendors do with data, whether your privacy notice is accurate, contracts, employment, tax registration, sector rules such as health or finance licensing, and anything in images or a CMS. A clean scan is not a compliance opinion.

Why treat runaway cloud or AI spend as a liability?

Because it is one: the account owner owes the bill. A leaked key, an endpoint anyone can drive on your model key, or an autoscaler with no budget alarm can produce a charge larger than the business earns in a year, and providers do not have to forgive it.

What size should each duct be?

Give each room's airflow and how long its run is, and the static pressure the blower has. The page works out the friction rate the ducts can afford, then the smallest round size for every supply run, trunk and return that stays under it and under a velocity limit, and how much each short run's damper must throttle. The physics is shown below. It is free and runs in your browser.

Room airflow comes from the room-by-room load calculator: export the house there and load it here. How the load is worked out.

1. The blower and what is in the way

The blower's rated external static pressure at your design airflow is on the air handler's fan table. The losses below are example values: use your coil's, filter's and register maker's figures at your airflow.

2. Supply runs

Total effective length is the run's straight feet from the air handler to the register, trunk included, plus the equivalent length of every elbow, boot and takeoff on the way. The page has no fitting table: use your fitting maker's equivalent lengths. Rooms with the same trunk name share a trunk.

How the sizes are worked out

The friction rate. The blower can push its airflow against a rated external static pressure. The coil, filter, supply register and return grille take their share at that airflow; what is left is all the ducts may use. Spread over the longest supply path plus the longest return path (in total effective length, straight feet plus fittings), it gives the design friction rate: FR = (ESP − component losses) × 100 / (TEL supply + TEL return), in inches of water per 100 ft. Sizing every run to that rate means the longest run still gets its air, and every shorter run has pressure to spare.

The loss in a duct. For each candidate round size, the velocity is airflow over area, and the loss per 100 ft is Darcy-Weisbach: Δp = f × (100 / D) × ρ V² / 2gc, converted at 5.2023 lbf/ft² per inch of water. The friction factor f solves the Colebrook equation, 1/√f = −2 log10(ε/3.7D + 2.51/(Re √f)), with Reynolds number Re = ρVD/μ. Air density is 0.075 lb/ft³ at sea level, corrected for elevation with the same standard atmosphere the load uses; viscosity is 1.217×10−5 lb/ft·s. Roughness ε is 0.0003 ft for sheet metal, the value usually given for galvanized duct. For flex the page takes 0.003 ft, a rough assumption for flex pulled tight: sagging or compressed flex loses far more, so the maker's friction chart wins.

The size. Each run takes the smallest size from 4 to 24 in. whose loss is at or under the friction rate and whose velocity is under the limit you set (900 ft/min for branches and trunks and 700 for returns are common choices for quiet residential systems, not code limits). Its own loss is its friction times its effective length; the damper column is what the blower has left minus that loss and the longest return's.

A check on the units. The tests compare the engine with a widely quoted power-law fit for galvanized duct, Δp = 0.109136 q1.9 / D5.02: at 0.0003 ft the engine comes out 3 to 9% lower over 50 to 2,000 cfm, and at 0.0005 ft within 5% of it.

What it does not do

It is not ACCA Manual D and uses none of ACCA's tables or fitting equivalent lengths. It does not size a reducing trunk section by section (each trunk is sized for the air it carries at the start), check register throw or noise, add duct heat gain or leakage, or check that the blower's fan curve meets the airflow at the resulting pressure. Flex duct installed with sag or bends can lose several times the figure here.

No warranty. This is an estimate, not engineering advice and not an ACCA Manual J or Manual D design. Fitting equivalent lengths and component losses are yours to supply. Many permits require duct design from approved software or a qualified designer; build ducts on their design.

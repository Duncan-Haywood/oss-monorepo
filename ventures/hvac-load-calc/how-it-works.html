How the room-by-room load is worked out

A load calculation answers one question: how many Btu per hour must the equipment add in winter, and take out in summer, to hold the indoor temperature on a design day? This page lists every step the calculator takes, so you can check it or redo it by hand. It is an open first-principles method. It is not ACCA Manual J, whose tables and procedure belong to ACCA, and it simplifies in the ways listed at the end.

1. How easily heat crosses each surface (U-values)

A wood-framed wall is two heat paths side by side: through the insulated cavity and through the studs. Each path's resistance is the sum of its layers, and the wall's U-factor is the area-weighted sum of the two paths' conductances (the parallel-path method):

U = (1 − f) / R_cavity_path + f / R_stud_path

With inside air film R 0.68, ½" drywall 0.45, sheathing 0.62, siding 0.62, outside film 0.17, softwood at R 1.25 per inch and a framing fraction f of 0.25, a 2x4 wall with R-13 batts works out at U ≈ 0.084 Btu/h·ft²·°F, about R-11.8 overall rather than the R-13 on the bag. The attic floor is 1 / (R_insulation + 0.45 + 2 × 0.61). Windows and doors use the U-factor from their label. A slab loses heat mostly at its edge, so it is counted as F × perimeter × ΔT; the default F of 0.73 Btu/h·ft·°F is the usual figure for an uninsulated slab edge, and an insulated edge is lower.

2. Heating: the cold night

Heating is steady state at the winter design temperature, with no credit for sun or people, since the worst hour is a cloudy pre-dawn:

Q_heat = Σ U·A·ΔT  +  F·P·ΔT  +  1.08 · ρ · cfm_leak · ΔT

Air leakage comes from a blower-door number. A house's natural air change rate is roughly ACH50 divided by an N factor (about 20 for a typical single-storey house; lower for tall or windy sites), so 6 ACH50 is 0.3 air changes an hour. That airflow is shared among rooms by their outside wall area. 1.08 is the sensible heat of air (Btu/h per cfm per °F) at sea level; ρ scales it down with altitude by the standard atmosphere (about 83% at 5,000 ft). Mechanical ventilation adds 1.08 · ρ · cfm · (1 − recovery) · ΔT to the whole house, and ducts outside the conditioned space add a percentage on top.

3. Cooling: a whole design day, hour by hour

Summer loads swing with the sun, so the calculator runs 24 solar hours of a clear 21 July:

  • Outdoor temperature follows the standard ASHRAE daily-range profile: the design dry bulb at 3 pm, falling by the daily range to its low around 5 am.
  • Sun position from latitude, declination and hour angle; sunlight from the ASHRAE 1985 clear-sky model for July (direct normal = A·e^(−B / sin altitude), A = 1085 W/m², B = 0.207; diffuse = C × direct with C = 0.136; ground reflectance 0.2). Beam on each wall depends on the angle between the sun and the direction the wall faces.
  • Windows gain A · SHGC · shade · (beam · K(θ) + 0.9 · diffuse), where K(θ) trims the beam at steep incidence, plus conduction U · A · (T_out − T_in).
  • Walls see the sol-air temperature, the outdoor air temperature raised by the sun the surface absorbs (T_out + α·I / 3.0, with α 0.45, 0.7 or 0.9 for light, medium or dark). A frame wall passes 80% of the sol-air swing about its daily mean through to the inside, two hours late; masonry passes 35%, six hours late.
  • Ceilings see the attic, which runs above outdoor air in proportion to the sun on the roof, peaking about 35 °F over outdoor under dark shingles (20 °F under a light roof; you can change this in the JSON as atticRiseF).
  • People add 230 Btu/h sensible and 200 latent each; appliances add what you enter.
  • Moisture: leakage and ventilation air carries water. The latent load is 0.68 · ρ · cfm · Δgrains, with outdoor humidity from the design dew point and indoor from the set point and RH (75 °F at 50% is about 65 grains per pound).

4. The house peak is not the sum of the rooms

An east bedroom peaks around 9 am, a west living room near 4 pm. The equipment sees the hour when the house total is largest, which is smaller than adding every room's worst hour. The calculator reports both: the house peak sizes the equipment, and each room's own peak sizes its duct. Design airflow is sensible load / (1.08 · ρ · supply ΔT), shared among rooms by the larger of each room's heating and cooling share.

Worked example: the example house

The page loads an illustrative 1,800 ft² single-storey ranch on a slab (36°N, 600 ft, 22 °F winter and 93 °F summer design, 72 °F dew point, R-13 walls, R-38 attic, U-0.32 / SHGC-0.27 windows, 6 ACH50, 50 cfm ventilation, ducts in the attic at 10%). It is not a measured house. The result:

The heating load is 26,901 Btu/h. The 1-ton-per-500-ft² rule of thumb would have put a 3.5 to 4 ton unit in this house; the load says a 2.5 ton unit covers it, at about 820 ft² per ton. That gap is the reason to run the numbers: an oversized system costs more, short-cycles and pulls less moisture out of the air. Adding up each room's own peak gives 17,910 Btu/h sensible, against 16,987 for the same rooms at the house's 3 pm peak (both before ventilation and ducts), a 5% gap: most of this house's glass faces south and west, so its rooms peak within a few hours of each other. A house with large east glass would show a bigger gap.

What it leaves out

  • Overhangs, trees and neighbouring buildings shading windows (it assumes full sun, which overstates the load of shaded glass).
  • Basements and below-grade walls; multi-storey stack effects beyond the N factor; skylights.
  • Heat stored in furniture and floors (it treats window sun as an instant load, which overstates the peak slightly).
  • Duct leakage modelled in detail; it takes a percentage.
  • Design temperatures: you enter them; the tool has no weather database.

For a permit, or before buying equipment, have a full load calculation done with approved software. Use this one to know whether the number on a quote is in the right range, and to ask why if it is not.

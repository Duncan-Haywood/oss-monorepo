This calculator vs. Housecall Pro

Two different questions: Housecall Pro is account-based field-service software for running a trade business, and its Price Book is where prices live once they are decided. This page is a free, private worksheet for the question that comes first — what does one billable crew hour actually cost you?

What each tool is for

Housecall Pro is field service management software for home service businesses, sold as an ongoing subscription account. It covers online booking, scheduling and dispatch, automated customer texts, a mobile app that lets a crew pull up the work order in the field, estimates and invoicing with automated payment reminders, card payments, and a Price Book that holds the prices and rates a business quotes from — a system meant to run a whole company across every technician and every job. It publishes that it is used by over 200,000 field service professionals across HVAC, plumbing, electrical, cleaning, and other trades. Housecall Pro's own pricing page is not reachable from this session to confirm today's exact plan names or dollar figures, so no price for it is invented here: only its well-documented identity and category — field-service management software for trade contractors, sold as a subscription account — are stated, and no dollar amount for Housecall Pro appears on this page.

This calculator does none of what Housecall Pro does. It does not book a job, dispatch a crew, send invoices, or collect payments, and it stores no customer or job record on a server. It takes your own annual figures — payroll, paid and billable hours, crew size, vehicles, insurance, rent and office cost, materials and subcontractor spend — and turns them into four numbers about your business rather than about any one job: the payroll burden per billable hour, the overhead rate as a percentage of direct cost, the break-even hourly rate, and the rate a target net margin actually requires.

It also runs the arithmetic backwards, which is the direction most contractors actually need. Enter the rate you charge today and it solves for the net margin that rate really earns, the annual size of the gap across your crew — stated over labour revenue only, since materials and subs are billed on top of the hours rather than through the rate — and the billable hours that rate would need in order to work. When the hours it would require exceed the hours anyone actually works, the answer is that no schedule saves the rate.

The two are complementary rather than alternatives, and the relationship is one-directional: something has to decide the number that goes into a price book, and a price book does not derive that number from your payroll. The output here is an hourly rate and an overhead percentage you can type into whichever system quotes from them.

Does a Price Book not already tell me my rate?

A price book stores and applies the price you decided on; it does not derive it from your payroll. Whatever system holds your prices — Housecall Pro's Price Book, a spreadsheet, or a laminated card in the van — something has to decide the number that goes in, and that decision is arithmetic about your business rather than about any one job. This calculator is that arithmetic. It is also why the two are complementary rather than alternatives: the output here is an hourly rate and an overhead percentage you can type into whichever system quotes from them.

Why does this calculator say my payroll burden is far above 20%?

Because it counts the paid hours you never bill. The textbook burden figure — employer taxes, workers' compensation, and liability expressed as a percentage of wages — lands near 20% and assumes every paid hour is sold. A field employee is paid for roughly 2,080 hours a year; holidays and vacation come off that, and of the hours actually worked, drive time, loading, estimating, shop time, and warranty callbacks appear on nobody's invoice. Bid calculators multiply a loaded wage by the hours estimated for a job, which are billable hours, so the rate they need is the one that recovers a person's whole annual cost across only the hours that get sold. On this tool's shipped example that turns a 28-dollar wage into 53.76 per billable hour — a 92% burden, not 20%. Using 20% under-recovers labour on every job in the same direction.

Which input changes the answer most?

The billable share — the fraction of paid hours that actually get sold. In this arithmetic ten points of utilization moves the required rate further than the entire target margin does, which is the argument for measuring it rather than estimating it, and it is why the tool's notes call it out when it falls below 60%. That sensitivity is also why a rate that looks survivable can stop working after a slow quarter without any wage or overhead changing.

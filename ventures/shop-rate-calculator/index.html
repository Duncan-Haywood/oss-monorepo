FOR TRADE CONTRACTORS AND SHOP OWNERS

You do not charge what you think you charge.

A $28 wage is not a $28 hour. By the time payroll taxes, workers’ comp, the truck, the phone, the paid holidays and every hour spent driving, estimating and fixing callbacks are in it, the hour you sell costs far more — and the overhead behind it still has to come out of somewhere. Put your own year in below and see the burden rate, the overhead rate, and the number a crew hour has to be sold at.

What should I charge per hour as a contractor?

The crew and the hours

The hours are the part most shops get wrong. You pay for 2,080 hours a year and you sell far fewer, and the gap has to be recovered inside the ones you do sell.

Paid days off means holidays, vacation and sick days — paid, and on nobody’s invoice. The non-billable share is everything else that is worked but not sold: drive time between jobs, loading, estimating, shop and yard time, warranty callbacks and rework. Twenty per cent is a common figure for a small crew; measure yours if you can.

What a person costs on top of the wage

Per field employee, per year. These are the costs of employing somebody rather than the costs of a job.

Workers’ comp varies more than any other figure here — a low-risk trade may be under 4% of payroll and a roofing classification many times that. Use your own declarations page, not a rule of thumb.

Overhead: the business, not the job

Annual, whole business. Anything you would still be paying in a month with no work booked.

Owner pay belongs here for the hours you spend estimating, quoting and running the shop. If you also work on the tools, count those hours in the crew above instead — splitting your own pay between the two is the most common thing to get wrong on this page. Materials and subs is your annual spend on both; it is the rest of the direct cost the overhead rate is measured against.

What you want to make

Net margin is a share of the price, not a mark-up on cost. Ten per cent margin on a $10,000 job is $1,000 of profit; a 10% mark-up on $10,000 of cost is $909.

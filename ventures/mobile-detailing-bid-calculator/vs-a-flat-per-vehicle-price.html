This calculator vs. a flat per-vehicle price card

Names no specific product or company. This compares a free calculator against the generic method every mobile detailer already has: a price card by vehicle size — sedan, SUV, truck — quoted the same regardless of where the job is.

First — the price card is right, and you should have one

A website, a flyer, or a text back to a lead needs one number per vehicle size before you know the address. "$180 for a sedan full detail" is a fine headline price, and no calculator replaces having one. What follows is not an argument that the card is wrong. It is what the same flat number stops showing you the moment the job is real and you know how far it is.

The two places the price card goes quiet

1. Drive time and drive cost never separate from the job

A price card answers "what do I charge for a sedan," not "what does it cost me to reach this one." Both live inside the same $180 either way. The calculator splits them out — $16.08 of vehicle cost on the near job, $40.20 on the far one — so the card fee, the supplies, and the trip are visible as three separate numbers instead of one guess that happened to work out.

2. The same price pays a falling hourly rate as the drive grows

This is the one that costs real money over a full week. The card charges $180 whether the stop is 12 minutes away or 45, but the hours it occupies are not the same, so the price that felt right on the near job pays 31% less per hour on the far one — $30.24 down to $20.88 — with nothing on the card to say so. A detailer working off the card alone can fill a day with "the same $180 job" and end it having earned noticeably less than the schedule implied, purely from which addresses landed on the route.

When the price card is still the better tool

For advertising, it plainly is. A website or a flyer cannot show a live calculator, and a customer calling for a quote wants one number, not a form. The sensible arrangement is both: post the card price to get the call, then run this calculator once you have the address to see whether that day's version of the job still clears your floor — and, on a job far enough out, whether a trip surcharge or a decline is the honest answer instead.

Every figure on this page is this calculator's own worked example, not a claim about any real detailer or an industry benchmark. Your mileage, rates, and supply cost will produce different numbers — the point is which numbers to produce, not what they should come out to.

Common questions

Is a flat per-vehicle price card wrong?

No, and it should not go away. A website or a flyer needs one number per vehicle size, and "$180 for a sedan full detail" is a perfectly good headline price. The point of this page is not that the card is wrong — it is that the card is the same number whether the job is 5 minutes away or 45, and only one of those two jobs actually clears the margin the card was priced to deliver.

How much does drive time actually cost on a mobile job?

Two things, and a flat card shows neither: the mileage cost of getting there and back, and the clock time it occupies that a shop-based detailer never spends. On this calculator's own defaults, a 24-mile round trip at $0.67 a mile is $16.08 in vehicle cost alone, on top of 45 minutes that never touches the car. Move the same $180 job to a 60-mile round trip and the vehicle cost alone triples to $40.20, before the extra clock time is even counted.

Why does the same flat price pay a different hourly rate on different jobs?

Because the flat price is fixed but the whole-stop time behind it is not. On the near job below, $180 nets $128.52 after costs and the card fee, spread across 4.25 hours the job actually occupies — $30.24 an hour. Charge the identical $180 for a job with double the drive and the net drops to $104.40 spread across 5 hours — $20.88 an hour, a 31% cut the price card never shows, because the card only ever displays the $180.

Does the calculator replace the price card?

No, and it is not trying to. The price card is what you advertise before you know the mileage. This calculator is what you run once you know the address, to decide whether that day's version of "the $180 sedan detail" still clears your target margin — and, if a job is far enough, whether it is worth a trip surcharge or a decline instead.

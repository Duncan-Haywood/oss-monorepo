How to balance carry-over in a round robin

Every round robin decides not just who you play, but who your opponent played the week before. If the strongest team in the league is always followed by the same opponent, that opponent keeps meeting sides who have just been beaten up, rested their stars or travelled. Sports schedulers call this the carry-over effect, and the usual hand-made schedule, the circle method, is close to the worst case for it.

A worked example: 8 teams, 7 rounds

Carry-over value (sum of the squared counts)148

So if the Lions are the strongest side, the Hawks meet five opponents straight after the Lions have played them. The searched schedule for the same eight teams has every count at 0 or 1, a carry-over value of 48, which is the least any eight-team round robin can have, and still 3 or 4 home games per team. The numbers come from the same code the page runs, and a test recomputes them, so they cannot drift from the tool.

How the tool finds it

It starts from the circle method and makes small changes that keep a valid round robin: swapping the order of two rounds, swapping two teams' opponents over the fewest rounds that keeps every pair meeting once, and exchanging the games of two rounds along one cycle. A seeded search keeps the changes that lower the carry-over value, then a second pass trades a little of it for fewer back-to-back home or away games, and home and away are set for the fewest breaks it can find. The same seed gives the same schedule. It runs in your browser in about a second for 20 teams.

For 4, 8, 16 or 32 teams (and 3, 7, 15 or 31, where one team sits out each round) there is a better start than the circle method: a construction from finite-field arithmetic (Russell, 1980) in which every carry-over count is exactly 0 or 1, the least possible value. The tool uses it at those sizes. For 16 teams that is a value of 224, where the search from the circle method stopped at 316; for a 16-team home-and-away season, 912, the least possible, where it had 1,048.

Most other sizes from 6 to 40 teams get a second start: a starter timetable, built by shifting one fixed set of pairings round by round (the circle method is the simplest starter, and one of the worst for carry-over). The tool holds the best starter a search found for each size, runs the search from both it and the circle method, and keeps the better. For 20 teams (and 19) the starter has every count at 0 or 1: a value of 360, where the search from the circle method stopped at 448. 22 teams (and 21) reach that floor too: 440, where the circle start stopped at 654, and a 22-team home-and-away season scores 1,782, the least it can have. At 17, 18 and 23 to 28 teams it lowers the value by 13 to 28%, and at 30 teams by 9%.

Why a double round robin should not just be mirrored

The usual way to write a home-and-away season is to play the first half again with the venues swapped. That repeats every carry-over step, so every count doubles and the value roughly quadruples. For a double round robin the tool now searches the second half as a round robin of its own: it starts from the first half played backwards (which turns "A then C" into "C then A", so the counts spread instead of doubling), keeps every pair meeting once in each half with the return game at the other ground, never has a team meet the same side twice running across the halfway point, and weighs home/away breaks with the same exact count it reports. For 18 teams that takes the value from 1,902 to 1,556, for 4 more back-to-back home or away games (76). The one exception is a first half with every count at 0 or 1, as at 16 and 20 teams: mirrored, every count becomes 2 except one per team, which is already the least possible, so the tool keeps the mirror whenever it scores better. For 20 teams that is 1,460 with 116 breaks, below every one of the 14 professional 20-team seasons (inside their 70 to 146 breaks).

Questions

What is the carry-over effect in a round robin?

Team A carries over team B to team C when B plays A and then plays C in its very next game. If A is the strongest side, or plays a bruising style, C keeps meeting opponents who have just been through A. The carry-over matrix counts, for every pair (A, C), how many teams play A and then C next; Russell's carry-over value adds up the squares of those counts.

Is the circle method bad for carry-over?

Yes. The circle method, the usual way to write a round robin by hand, repeats the same carry-over again and again. With 8 teams one team meets five of its seven opponents straight after they have played the same other team. A searched schedule for 8 teams gets every count down to 0 or 1, which is the best possible.

Does balancing carry-over cost home and away balance?

Somewhat. The circle method has the fewest back-to-back home or away games possible (teams minus two in a single round robin), and a schedule with even carry-over usually has more. The tool weighs both and tells you the numbers, so you can keep the circle method when home venues matter more.

Do professional leagues balance carry-over?

Some more than others. On the published fixture lists for the English, Spanish, Italian, French and German top flights from 2021-22 to 2024-25, the Bundesliga's carry-over value ran 1.4 to 2.4 times the others' (3,474 to 4,342 against 1,840 to 2,564), with the fewest possible home/away breaks. The tool's 20-team double round robin scores 1,460 with 116 breaks, the least carry-over a 20-team home-and-away season can have, against 2,278 to 2,564 and 70 to 146 breaks for the 20-team leagues in those seasons.

When does carry-over matter for a rec league?

When one or two teams are much stronger than the rest, when games are physical, or when teams play on consecutive nights. If every team plays at the same venue, home/away breaks do not matter at all, so even carry-over is a free improvement.

How to run a Swiss-system tournament, with a worked example

A Swiss tournament is for a club night or a weekend event with more players than there is time for a round robin. Nobody is knocked out, everyone plays every round, and each round pairs people who are doing about equally well. Chess clubs, darts leagues, board-game and card-game events use it.

A worked example

Six invented players, seeded in the order they signed up. Six players need 3 rounds. Round 1 has no scores yet, so the seed order decides: the top half plays the bottom half. The top seed has white on board 1 and colours alternate down the boards, so the top three seeds do not all start with white.

Round 1WhiteBlack
Board 1AnaDee
Board 2EliBen
Board 3CyFay

Suppose Ana, Ben and Fay win. The three winners now have 1 point and the three losers 0, so round 2 pairs winners with winners and losers with losers, with nobody meeting a previous opponent:

Round 2WhiteBlack
Board 1BenAna
Board 2FayEli
Board 3DeeCy

Three players on 1 point cannot all meet each other, so one board must pair a 1-point player with a 0-point one: the tool calls that a "floater" and says so rather than hiding it. Which three boards? FIDE's Dutch system splits the 1-point group by seed: the top half (Ana) meets the bottom half in order (Ben), and the lowest player left over (Fay) floats down to the 0-point group. Ana had white in round 1 and is due black, Ben had black and is due white, so board 1 gives both their colours. Fay (due white) meets Eli (due black), and Dee (due white) meets Cy (due black): every player gets the colour they are due, with the single float. Colours alternate, so nobody is white three times running or two games ahead on one colour. Score groups are paired from the top down, as the Dutch system does: the top group is settled first (as many boards inside it as possible, then colours, then the top-half-against-bottom-half order), its leftover players float to the next group, and at every step the tool checks that everyone below can still be paired without a rematch. Near the end of a long event it also avoids a round that would leave no fresh opponents for the next one. Replayed against six real chess events, it reproduces about 95% of the boards the arbiters published from round 2 on. It also remembers who floated: a player paired down last round (or given the bye) is kept from being paired down again, and a player paired up from being paired up again, weighed after score gaps and colours as FIDE's Dutch rules rank it. The pairing is checked by a separate validator before it is shown.

Questions

How many rounds does a Swiss tournament need?

Enough that one player can be clearly ahead: the next whole number above log base 2 of the player count. Six or eight players need 3 rounds, sixteen need 4. More rounds sort the middle of the table more finely, but the top places are usually settled by this number.

How are players paired in a Swiss round?

Everyone is sorted by score, then players with the same score are paired top half against bottom half, so the strongest of a group meets the weakest of that group. Nobody plays the same opponent twice. In round 1 nobody has a score yet, so the seed order decides.

What happens with an odd number of players?

One player gets a bye each round and is scored as a win. The bye goes to the lowest-ranked player who has not had one yet, so nobody gets two byes before everyone has had one.

How are ties in the standings broken?

By Buchholz: the sum of your opponents' scores. Beating players who went on to do well is worth more than beating players who lost everything. Still level? Sonneborn-Berger is next: the full score of each opponent you beat plus half the score of each you drew, so a win over the leader outweighs a win over the player who lost everything. Players still level after that keep their seed order.

This calculator vs. a flat-rate boiler special

"$4,500 boiler installed" is not a price for a boiler swap — it is a price for one specific MBH, fuel, and conversion type, quietly applied to every basement the truck pulls up to.

What a flat boiler special is

A flat special is the fastest way to quote: one headline number on a postcard or a website, read off and repeated. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one input MBH, one fuel, one conversion type (usually the simplest: hot-water like-for-like), one zone count, no liner, no tank change — and then reused for every boiler afterward, whether or not the next basement looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the input MBH, whether the job is hot-water or steam, like-for-like versus a condensing upgrade versus a steam-to-hot-water conversion, fuel, zone count, and extras such as a chimney liner or an indirect tank, plus your loaded labor and overhead. It prices the plant and the extra-work hours a conversion adds separately, and returns a walk-away price and an extra-work share below which the job loses money — for this basement, not a generic one.

Charges the same headline price whether the job is an 80 MBH gas hot-water like-for-like swap or a 140 MBH oil steam-to-hot-water conversion — a several-times difference in plant cost and labor for the same word "boiler". Prices the plant from the entered input MBH, fuel, and conversion type, so the two jobs above come out at different numbers instead of one.
Prices "a boiler" as one swap, with no separate line for venting changes, a condensate path, or the piping conversion a steam-to-hot-water job drags in. Times venting, condensate, and conversion labor as their own extra-work hours, so that work shows up instead of being absorbed into the plant price or skipped.
Assumes the existing chimney and hot-water setup carry over, so a job needing a new liner or an indirect tank gets quoted the same as one that does not. Adds a chimney-liner and indirect-tank line only when that scope is turned on, so the two jobs price differently.
Bakes payroll burden into a round "labor rate" nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the tech a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when an owner asks to match a cheaper number. Reports the walk-away price and the extra-work share of the job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the tech commits to it.

A flat special is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a postcard rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat boiler special get the price wrong?

Because the plant and labor scale with MBH input, fuel, and conversion type, not with the word "boiler" alone. An 80 MBH gas hot-water like-for-like swap on two zones is a different job from a 140 MBH oil steam-to-hot-water conversion on four zones with an indirect tank and a chimney liner. A flat special quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat special usually leave out entirely?

The extra work a steam-to-hot-water conversion drags in on top of the plant swap: venting changes, a condensate path if the new plant needs one, and the piping conversion itself. A rate built around "install a boiler" has nowhere to put those hours, so a tech pricing off the special alone is short before the truck is loaded.

What happens when the chimney or the tank has to change too?

A flat installed rate assumes the existing venting and hot-water setup carry over. A masonry chimney that needs a liner for a new appliance, or a plan that adds an indirect tank, is its own line of material and labor that a rate never asked about charges the same price for a job that takes longer and costs more.

Is a flat rate ever the right way to quote a boiler swap?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same fuel, same conversion type, same zone count, no liner, no tank change — a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the postcard rate does not know which band a given basement is in and neither does the tech until the walkthrough.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, equipment cost, or what competitors charge. It takes the MBH, scope, fuel, zones, and your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price and extra-work share for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat boiler special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

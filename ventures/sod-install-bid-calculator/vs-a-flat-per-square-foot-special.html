This calculator vs. a flat per-square-foot special

"$0.55 a square foot installed" is a price for one number, quoted at a lawn whose square footage can look like any other lawn's while everything that actually costs money — the grass, the prep, the pallets, the margin — is different.

What a flat per-square-foot special is

A flat special is the fastest way to quote: one headline rate, read off a yard sign, a Facebook post, or a competitor's postcard and repeated for every lawn afterward. Homeowners shop it because a single number is the easiest thing to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner will ask you to match at a lawn that took the crew a fraction of the time — or several times longer.

The rate behind it was usually set for one lawn — ready soil, common grass, a pickup at the farm — and then reused for every job afterward keyed only to square footage, whether or not the next lawn has an old lawn to strip out, clay to grade, or three watering visits ahead of it.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the lawn's length and width, grass choice, waste allowance, pallet coverage, soil prep, delivery, slope, and watering visits, plus your loaded labor and overhead. It turns that into whole pallets after waste, the dirt-work hours a strip-out or a till-and-grade actually takes, and a walk-away price for this lawn, not a generic one.

Common questions

Why does a flat $0.55-a-square-foot special get the price wrong?

Because it charges every lawn the same rate per square foot, and that rate cannot tell a ready-soil job from a strip-and-haul job. This calculator's own numbers for a 2,000 sq ft fescue lawn on ready soil and an 8,000 sq ft zoysia lawn that needs the old lawn stripped, hauled off, and watered three times come out to $0.93 and $1.84 a square foot — about 1.98x apart — even though both are the same kind of job, sod on a lawn.

What does a flat per-square-foot rate leave out?

The dirt work. Stripping out an existing lawn, hauling it off, and the dump ticket that comes with it do not live in a per-square-foot number for "sod install" — that number is usually set from an easy, ready-soil job and then reused for every lawn afterward. This calculator prices 19.2 hours of prep labor and an $85 dump ticket for the strip-and-haul job, versus zero for the ready-soil job, because prep hours and dump fees are their own inputs.

Why do whole pallets matter if the special is priced by the square foot?

Because a farm does not sell a fraction of a pallet, and a per-square-foot rate has no way to show that. This calculator orders whole pallets from the lawn area plus waste: 5 pallets (2,250 sq ft bought) for the 2,100 sq ft ready-soil order, and 22 pallets (8,800 sq ft bought) for the 8,800 sq ft strip-and-haul order, so the bid reflects what the farm actually invoices.

What does solving margin as a share of price get right that a flat number misses?

A flat special, and most rough estimates built the same way, price backward from a markup on cost — add 20% to what the job costs and call that the target margin. That is not the same number: margin is profit divided by price, not profit divided by cost, and a 20% markup on cost actually clears a lower margin than stated. This calculator solves for the price that hits the target margin exactly, so a target set at 20% comes out at 20%, not a smaller number wearing the same label.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local farm prices, labor rates, or what a competitor's coupon charges. It takes the lawn size, grass, waste, pallet coverage, prep, delivery, slope, and watering, plus your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-square-foot special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the next lawn stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

This calculator vs. a flat-rate water heater special

"$1,200 water heater installed" is not a price for a job — it is a price for a same-day tank swap in an easy garage, quietly reused for every house that also asks to replace a water heater.

What a flat water heater special is

A flat special is the fastest way to quote over the phone: read off the postcard or search-result number, skip the walkthrough. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one kind of job — usually a 50-gallon electric tank swapped in a garage or basement with easy access, the existing circuit reused, no vent change, no gas work — and then reused for every house afterward, whether or not the next one needs an attic carry, a condensing tankless, a gas-line upsize, or a panel rebuild.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the heater type (electric tank, atmospheric or power-vent gas tank, condensing gas tankless, electric tankless, or heat pump), tank gallons or tankless GPM, location, venting and vent length, gas line and gas-line length, electrical path, and add-ons such as an expansion tank, drip pan, condensate, recirculation pump, haul-away, seismic straps, a mixing valve, and tankless service valves, plus your own loaded wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin. It returns the installed price, the effective hourly rate, and the walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this heater and this house, not a flat number for every garage.

Charges the same headline price whether the job is a same-day 50-gallon electric garage swap or a condensing tankless on an attic carry with a gas-line upsize — several times the labor and materials for the same phrase "water heater". Prices the heater from the entered type, capacity, location, vent, and gas or electrical path, so the two jobs above come out at different numbers instead of one.
Has no line for upsizing a 1/2-inch gas line to 3/4 inch, regardless of whether the existing line can actually flow enough gas for a condensing tankless. Prices the gas line separately by scope and length, and flags when a tankless is being priced on an existing line that "looks adequate" — the check the flat rate never runs.
Quotes the same number for a heater set on the floor and one carried into an attic or crawl space, with no separate line for access hours. Times attic, crawl, closet, and basement access as their own labor hours, on top of the base swap.
Has no line for a concentric sidewall vent, a drip pan, a condensate line, or tankless service valves — the extras a condensing or heat-pump unit usually needs that the old atmospheric tank did not. Prices venting by type and length and each add-on as its own labor and material line, applied only when the job calls for it.
Gives the installer a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper flyer. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against what a flat special would have quoted the same job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

Common questions

Why does a flat water heater special get the price wrong?

Because the labor and materials scale with heater type, location, vent, and gas or electrical path, not with the word "water heater" alone. A same-day 50-gallon electric tank swapped in an easy garage is a different job from a condensing gas tankless carried into an attic on a 3/4-inch gas upsize with a concentric sidewall vent, a drip pan, and a condensate line. A flat special quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat special usually leave out entirely?

The vent, the gas or electrical path, and the attic or crawl-space access hours. A rate built around "swap the tank" has no line for upsizing a 1/2-inch gas line to 3/4 inch, no line for a concentric or PVC sidewall termination a condensing unit needs instead of the old atmospheric stack, and no line for the extra hours an attic carry takes over a garage that is already at floor level.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped defaults, at a $48 hourly wage, 30% payroll burden, 16% overhead, and a 25% target margin: the easy job -- a 50-gallon electric tank in a garage, existing circuit, no vent, no gas work -- prices at $1,035.40, on a $776.55 break-even cost. Move the same house to a condensing gas tankless in the attic instead -- concentric sidewall vent, a 3/4-inch gas upsize, a drip pan, a condensate line, and tankless service valves -- and the job costs $2,984.49 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator bids it at $3,979.33: 3.8 times the easy job. Quoting the attic tankless at the easy job's own $1,035.40 flat rate prices it $1,949.09 below its own break-even cost.

Is a flat rate ever the right way to quote a water heater swap?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same heater type, easy garage or basement access, no vent change, no gas or electrical work -- a flat rate can land close, because that is probably the job the rate was set on. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the postcard rate does not know which band a given house is in and neither does the installer until the walkthrough.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor rates, equipment cost, or what competitors charge. It takes the heater type, location, vent, gas or electrical path, and add-ons, plus your own loaded wage, burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a walk-away price for the job you describe -- the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat water heater special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

Why the numbers and the bench disagree

The timing equations have no opinion about whether a design is buildable, which is where most 555 projects actually go wrong:

  • A standard astable can't do 50% duty or below. Charging goes through R1 + R2, discharging through R2 alone, so high time always exceeds low time. It approaches 50% as R2 grows relative to R1 and never reaches it. A diode across R2 (so charging bypasses R2) buys you 50% and below.
  • R1 sets the discharge current. When discharge begins, R1 alone limits current into the discharge pin, which sinks roughly 200 mA at most. This page checks that against your supply voltage.
  • Big timing resistors drift. Past about 1 MΩ the threshold pin's own leakage stops being negligible next to the charging current on a bipolar NE555, so the real period wanders — with temperature, and between chips. A CMOS 555 (7555/TLC555) is far more tolerant.
  • Electrolytic timing caps are approximate. −20%/+80% is a common tolerance, so a microfarad-scale design's frequency is a target, not a specification. Trim with R2 if it matters.

Common questions

Why can't a 555 astable produce a 50% duty cycle?

In the standard two-resistor astable the timing capacitor charges through R1 + R2 but discharges through R2 alone, so the output-high time is always longer than the output-low time. The duty cycle approaches 50% as R2 grows relative to R1 but never reaches it. To get 50% or below, put a diode across R2 so charging bypasses it, or use a CMOS 555 in a 50%-duty configuration.

What is the 555 astable frequency formula?

The period is ln(2) × (R1 + 2 × R2) × C, which is where the familiar f = 1.44 / ((R1 + 2 × R2) × C) comes from — 1.44 is 1 / ln(2). The output-high time is ln(2) × (R1 + R2) × C and the output-low time is ln(2) × R2 × C.

How long is a 555 monostable pulse?

ln(3) × R × C, usually quoted as the rule of thumb 1.1 × R × C. The capacitor charges from 0 V to the 2/3 Vcc threshold, and ln(3) is the time constant multiple that takes.

How small can R1 be in a 555 astable?

About 1 kΩ in practice. During the discharge phase R1 is the only thing limiting current into the 555's discharge pin, which sinks at most around 200 mA. A smaller R1 satisfies the timing equations but can exceed that rating.

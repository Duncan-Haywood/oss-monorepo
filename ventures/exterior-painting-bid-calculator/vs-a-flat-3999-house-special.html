This calculator vs. a flat $3,999 house special

"$3,999 whole-house exterior paint" is not a price for a house — it is a price for one specific house, quietly applied to every house that calls for a quote.

What a flat house special is

A flat special is the fastest number a company can put on a postcard or a search ad: one price, no takeoff, easy for a homeowner to compare between bids. It works for the shop as a lead magnet and works for the homeowner as a round number to shop around with. Nothing about the number says which house it was built for.

The number was set for one job — commonly a smaller, one-story house with sound existing paint, standard trim, no scrape, no shutters, and easy ground-level access — and then quoted to every caller afterward, whether or not the next house looks anything like the one the special was priced on.

What this calculator does instead

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the house's elevation square footage, story count, trim length, shutters, and flags for scrape, primer, an occupied yard, and hard second-story access, plus your crew's wage, payroll burden, and target margin. It turns that into the crew hours the job actually consumes, the price per elevation square foot, and a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this house, not the house the special was built on.

Common questions

Why does a flat $3,999 house special get exterior painting wrong?

Because the crew hours and paint a house needs come from its elevation square footage, story count, trim length, scrape/prime condition, shutters, occupancy, and access — not from being "a house." A one-story 1,800 sq ft ranch with sound existing paint and a two-story 2,800 sq ft colonial with 240 lf of extra trim, twelve shutters, an occupied yard, and hard second-story access are both "a house," and a flat special charges them the same number.

What job is the $3,999 number actually built around?

One specific job, usually the easiest one a company is willing to advertise: a smaller, one-story house with sound existing paint, standard trim, no scrape, no shutters, and easy ground-level access. It is a marketing price meant to get the phone to ring, not a number derived from any particular customer's elevation, stories, or access.

What happens when the real job is bigger than the special's job?

On this calculator's own 2-story, 2,800 sq ft colonial preset — extra trim, twelve shutters, an occupied yard, and hard second-story access, priced at this tool's own shop-rate defaults — the walk-away cost of paint, labor, and overhead alone comes to about $4,526.61, and the bid price at a 28% target margin is about $6,286.96. Matching a $3,999 special on that job is about $527.61 under the walk-away cost, before any profit is counted — the job loses money the day it is scheduled, not just when it runs long.

What happens when the real job is smaller than the special's job?

On this calculator's own 1-story, 1,800 sq ft ranch preset — sound paint, no scrape, no shutters, easy access — the same shop-rate defaults price the job at about $1,940.12, with a walk-away cost of about $1,396.89. A $3,999 special on that house is more than double what the job itself costs to deliver, which is not a defect in the special so much as a sign the number was never tied to any one house's scope in the first place.

Is a flat house special ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of houses close to the one the special was built around — similar size, one story, sound paint, standard trim, easy access — a flat number can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the postcard price does not know which band a given house is in and neither does the crew until the elevation, stories, and access are actually measured.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, paint prices, or what competitors advertise. It takes the wage, paint cost, and overhead you enter and turns them into a walk-away price for the elevation, stories, trim, and access you describe — the arithmetic a flat special skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat house special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest way to advertise that works until the house stops matching the one the special was priced on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. The $1,940.12, $1,396.89, $6,286.96, and $4,526.61 figures above are this calculator's own arithmetic on the two worked jobs built into its own preset list (open the calculator and pick "1-story ranch" or "2-story colonial" from Starting house to reproduce them), not a claim about any specific contractor's real costs or any specific company's advertised price.

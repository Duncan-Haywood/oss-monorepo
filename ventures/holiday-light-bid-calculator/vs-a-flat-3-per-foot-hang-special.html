This calculator vs. a flat $3-a-foot hang special

Names no company or product. This compares a free calculator against the generic pricing habit holiday lighting flyers are shopped against every year: one flat rate per lineal foot — commonly “$3 a foot, hang-only” — advertised the same regardless of stories, take-down, light type, or extras.

First — the flyer rate is right, and you should have one

A flyer or a Facebook post needs one number before anyone has walked the roofline. “$3 a foot, hang-only” is a fine headline rate for the job it is honestly built for: a one-story ranch, C9 lights the customer already owns, no January take-down. What follows is not an argument that the flat rate is wrong. It is what that same rate stops showing you the moment the job is a two-story colonial with tree wraps and a take-down, and you know the eave footage, scope, stories, light type, and extras.

The two places the flyer rate goes quiet

1. Stories, take-down, and extras never separate from the hang

A flat per-foot rate answers “what do I charge per foot of eave,” not “how many stories, tree wraps, bush nets, column wraps, and take-down trips this house needs.” Both live inside the same $3 either way. The calculator splits them out — no extras on the ranch, $539.80 of extra material and a second crew trip on the harder job — so a second story, tree wraps, and a January take-down are visible line items instead of one flat rate that happened to work out on a good day.

2. The same advertised rate does not scale with the house

This is the one that costs real money. The flyer charges $3 a foot whether the footage belongs to the ranch or the harder job, but the crew hours behind it are not close — 7.16 hours against 26.02 hours, more than 3.6× the labor for a rate that only rose from $420 to $540. On this calculator's own defaults, the flat rate falls $11.54 short of total cost on the ranch and $1,388.98 short on the colonial, with the colonial's shortfall reaching into the crew's own loaded wage: $20.75 an hour actually paid, against a $41.60 loaded wage. A shop quoting the flyer rate on every call can sell every job at the advertised number and still cut its own crew's pay in half on the ones that turn out to need a second story, tree wraps, and take-down, with nothing on the flyer to say so.

When the flyer rate is still the better tool

For getting the phone to ring, it plainly is. A flyer cannot show a live calculator, and a homeowner scrolling past wants one number, not a form. The sensible arrangement is both: run the flyer rate to get the call, then run this calculator once you know the eave footage, scope, stories, light type, and extras, to check whether that house's version of the $3-a-foot job still covers cost — and, on a house far enough from the ranch, whether it needs a real bid instead.

Every figure on this page is this calculator's own worked example, computed from its own built-in starting jobs and shipped default shop rates, not a claim about any real shop's business or an industry benchmark. Your rates, wages, and overhead will produce different numbers — the point is which numbers to produce, not what they should come out to.

Common questions

Is a flat $3-a-foot hang special wrong?

No, and it should not go away. It is a real, narrow job priced honestly: a one-story ranch, C9 on clips, customer-owned lights, hang-only, no January trip. A flyer needs one number, and $3 a foot is a fine one for that job. The point of this page is not that the rate is wrong — it is that the same $3 is charged whether the job is that ranch or a two-story colonial with tree wraps and a take-down, and only a full bid tells the two apart.

Does the $3-a-foot special even cover the ranch it is built for?

Almost, and not quite. This calculator's own “cheap special” preset — a 140-foot one-story ranch, C9 hang-only — costs $431.54 in total cost on the calculator's shipped shop defaults. The flat rate prices that same job at $420.00: $11.54 short of total cost, though it clears the $297.86 loaded-labor cost with room to spare.

How much worse does it get on a harder job?

A 180-foot two-story colonial with C9, two tree wraps, six bush nets, two column wraps, a timer, and a January take-down costs $1,928.98 in total cost on this calculator's own defaults. The same $3-a-foot rate prices that job at only $540.00 — $1,388.98 short of total cost, and $542.58 short of covering the crew's own loaded labor alone, before a dollar of material, storage, or overhead is counted.

Does the calculator replace the flyer rate?

No, and it is not trying to. The flyer rate is what gets the call. This calculator is what you run once you know the eave footage, scope (hang-only, hang-and-takedown, or full-service), stories, light type, and extras, to check whether that house's version of the $3-a-foot job still covers cost — and, on a house far enough from the ranch, whether it needs a real bid instead.

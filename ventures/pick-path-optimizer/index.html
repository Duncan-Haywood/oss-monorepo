Walk less to pick the same orders

For small warehouses, 3PLs and online sellers who pick from shelves: paste where each SKU lives and a day of orders. The tool groups the orders onto carts, gives every cart a pick list in walking order on the provably shortest route through your aisles, and shows how many feet, hours and wages that saves against picking one order at a time the usual S-shape way.

Who this is for

Anyone who sends people down aisles with a pick list: a 3PL with a few thousand square feet of racking, an online shop picking from a rented unit, a parts counter, a pharmacy back room. If your pickers carry one order at a time and walk every aisle end to end, most of their day is walking, and that is the part this cuts.

A worked example

Press Load the example: 160 placeholder SKUs in 10 aisles of 20 four-foot bays, and 120 orders of one to four lines. One order per trip on an S-shape route walks about 29,200 ft. The same orders on the shortest route save about 13%; carts of four in arrival order save about 59%; carts of four grouped by the tool save about 71%, or roughly 1.7 picker-hours a day at 200 ft a minute.

When to use something else

Several blocks of aisles, one-way aisles, zone picking with hand-offs, or more than about 1,500 orders a wave are beyond this page; a warehouse management system with a routing module handles those. To decide which SKUs belong near the dock in the first place, use the warehouse slotting optimizer.

How it works

The shortest route is exact, not a rule of thumb: the dynamic programme of Ratliff and Rosenthal (1983) walks the aisles left to right and keeps only what matters about the partial route at each aisle's two ends. The test suite checks it against an exhaustive search on 600 random small layouts. Carts are filled seed-first (the order with the longest walk, then whichever order adds least walking) and then improved by moving and swapping single orders between carts while the total walk falls; every candidate cart is scored by the exact route. The grouping is good, not proven best.

Questions

What is the shortest way to walk a pick list in a warehouse?

In a warehouse with parallel aisles joined by a front and a back cross aisle, the shortest walk can be found exactly with the Ratliff-Rosenthal method, which this tool runs. It usually beats the S-shape rule (walk the whole length of every aisle with a pick) and the largest-gap rule, by an amount that depends on how many picks there are and where.

What is order batching?

Picking several orders on one cart trip, each into its own tote. Orders that need the same aisles share most of the walk. This tool groups your pasted orders onto carts of the size you set so the total walk is short, and gives each cart a pick list in walking order that says which tote each item goes in.

How much walking can batching save?

It depends on your orders. On this page's example day (120 placeholder orders of one to four lines in a 10-aisle, 20-bay layout), carts of four grouped by the tool and walked by the shortest route came to about 71% less walking than one order per trip on an S-shape route. Paste your own orders to see your figure.

Do I need a warehouse management system?

No. Paste SKU locations as SKU, aisle, bay and paste orders one per line. A WMS or Shopify export can be reshaped into that with a spreadsheet. Nothing is uploaded.

Is this safety or operations advice?

No. It is walking distance on the layout you type. Cart capacity, weight, aisle congestion, one-way aisles, reach height and pick accuracy are yours to judge.

Not decided

Cart weight and volume, congestion when several pickers share an aisle, pick time at the shelf (the same for every option, so it does not change the saving), sorting at the pack station, order cut-off times, and what the saved hours are worth to you if they cannot be used for other work.

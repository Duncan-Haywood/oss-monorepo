This calculator vs. a flat hang fee

"$X to install an EV charger" is not a price for an EV charger job — it is a price for one specific run, one panel situation, and no trench, quietly applied to every house that calls in.

What a flat hang fee is

A flat hang fee is the fastest way to quote: one number for "an EV charger install," regardless of the house. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner holding a Facebook ad or a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — a short indoor run to a panel with a spare breaker slot, no trench, no service upgrade — and then reused for every house afterward, whether or not the next house looks anything like the one the fee was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the charger's continuous amps and install type (hardwired or a NEMA 14-50 receptacle), the mounting location (indoor garage, outdoor wall, detached garage, or pedestal), the one-way run and any trench footage, the panel work the job needs (a spare slot through a service upgrade), and the existing service and estimated existing load, plus journeyman wage, payroll burden, permit and inspection fee, charger hardware, overhead, and target margin. It sizes the breaker at 125% of the continuous load, checks voltage drop over the actual run, and returns the price, the labor hours behind it, and the walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this job, not a generic one.

What a flat hang fee doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same number whether the run is 25 indoor feet to a spare slot or 70 feet of trench to a detached garage. Prices run and trench footage separately, by indoor or outdoor labor minutes per foot, so a longer or outdoor run costs more than a short indoor one.
Has no line for a service upgrade or subpanel, regardless of whether the existing panel has headroom for the new breaker. Compares existing service capacity against estimated existing load and the new breaker, and prices panel work from a spare slot (0.4 h) through a full service upgrade (8 h).
Prices "a charger install" with no line for trench and backfill, regardless of how much trench the job needs. Prices trench setup and per-foot trenching hours plus trench-restoration material, so a job that needs 70 feet of trench costs more than one that needs none.
Has no line for voltage drop, regardless of how far the run is or how much copper it takes to hold it under 3%. Checks voltage drop against the actual run and recommends the wire size that holds it, so a long run that needs heavier copper is priced for the copper it needs.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and, if you already have a number on the table, whether it clears that floor.

Common questions

Why does a flat hang fee get an EV charger quote wrong?

Because the cost driver is the run, the trench, and the panel work, not the charger itself. A 48 A charger on a spare breaker slot 25 feet from the panel is a short conduit run and a half-hour of panel work. The same 48 A charger on a 70-foot trench to a detached garage that also needs a service upgrade is 8 hours of panel work and over 9 hours of trenching before the run is even wired -- and a flat per-charger number charges both the same.

What does a flat hang fee usually leave out entirely?

Trench and backfill hours, a service upgrade or subpanel when the existing panel has no headroom for the new breaker, and the extra copper and labor a longer or outdoor run needs to hold voltage drop. A job that needs 70 feet of trench and a service upgrade is doing three things a spare-slot indoor hang is not -- and a flat hang fee has no line for any of them.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped defaults: a 48 A charger on an indoor spare slot, 25 feet of run, prices at $1,263.36. The same 48 A charger on a 70-foot trench to a detached garage with a service upgrade prices at $4,832.10, with a $3,624.07 break-even cost -- 3.82 times the indoor job. Quoting the detached job at the indoor job's $1,263.36 flat price leaves it $2,360.71 below its own break-even cost, before a dollar of profit.

Is a flat hang fee ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same charger amps, a short indoor run, a panel with a spare slot every time -- a flat fee can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the fee does not know which job it was built on and neither does the estimator until the run, the panel, and any trench have actually been checked.

Does this calculator know electrician labor rates or material costs in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor or material rates. It takes the charger amps, install type, mounting location, run and trench length, panel work, existing service and load, plus the wage, burden, overhead, and material costs you enter, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat hang fee skips, not a market survey.

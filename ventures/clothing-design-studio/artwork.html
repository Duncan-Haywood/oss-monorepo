Find a public-domain painting, and check it prints sharp

Old paintings of saints, angels and classical scenes make strong garment prints, and several museums release photographs of public-domain works for any use. Search two of those collections here, then check each image's pixels per inch at the width and method you plan to print. A painting that looks fine on screen can be far too small for a 25 cm chest print.

How the check works

Pixels per inch = image width in pixels ÷ (printed width in cm ÷ 2.54). A 2953-pixel-wide image printed 25 cm wide gives 300 ppi. The verdict compares that with the method's typical figure: inkjet methods (DTG, DTF, sublimation) want 300 and accept about 150; raised inks want sharper edges (floor 200) because the relief exaggerates them; embroidery is redrawn as stitches by a digitiser, so 150 is plenty. For a halftone screen the image should give at least 1.5 times the ruling. The Art Institute reports the pixel size of its original; for The Met, the size is measured when you open the image.

"Use in the studio" puts a preview copy on your garment in the design studio and remembers the original's size, so the studio can warn when you make the print bigger than the file allows. Send your printer the full-size file from the museum's own page.

Questions

What resolution do I need to print a painting on a t-shirt?

Printers usually ask for about 300 pixels per inch at the printed size for direct-to-garment, DTF and sublimation, and accept about 150 for prints seen at arm's length. A 25 cm (9.8 inch) wide chest print therefore wants an image about 2950 pixels wide, and at least about 1480. Your printer's own file spec wins.

Can I use a museum's public-domain image on clothing I sell?

The Art Institute of Chicago and The Met release images of works they consider public domain for any use, including commercial use, under their open-access policies. This page only shows works the museum itself flags as public domain, but it does not check rights: read each image's terms on the museum's page before you print.

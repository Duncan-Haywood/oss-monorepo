This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

“$X a square foot, any hardscape job” is not a price for a patio build — it is a price for one base depth, one pattern, and one haul-away situation, quietly applied to a driveway pad two and a half times the area that needs a deeper base, a fussier pattern, and a dump run the coupon never saw.

What a flat rate per square foot is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote a hardscape job over the phone: multiply the finished face by a number and read it back. Homeowners like it because it is easy to compare against a competitor's quote, and it is the number a customer holding one will ask you to match.

The number behind it was usually set for the simplest version of the job — a small patio at the minimum base depth, a plain running-bond pattern, and spoils left on site — and then reused for every job afterward, whether or not the next one is a driveway pad on a deeper base, a pattern that wastes more material and takes longer to cut, or a lot too tight to leave the dug-out base material behind.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the length and width (or area), the pattern, the base depth, edge restraint, and whether spoils need to be hauled away, plus your own wage, payroll burden, material costs, overhead, and target margin. It prices pavers by the square foot including pattern waste, base and bedding by the cubic yard from the actual excavation depth, edge restraint by the lineal foot, and haul-away from the actual excavation volume — for this job, not a flat rate for every job.

What a flat rate per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same per square foot whether the job sits on 6 inches of base for foot traffic or 10 inches for a driveway pad. Prices base and bedding by the cubic yard from the actual excavation depth the selected use requires, not a single depth for every job.
Has no line for pattern waste or the extra cutting time a fussier layout adds, even though a circular or herringbone pattern buys and cuts more pavers per finished square foot than running bond. Prices pavers on the finished area plus pattern waste, and applies a separate labor factor for the extra cuts each pattern needs.
Has no line for edge restraint, even though lineal feet of perimeter does not scale with area the way a long, narrow walkway shows. Prices edge restraint from the actual perimeter (or an entered edge length) as its own line, separate from the paved face.
Has no line for hauling spoils off site, even though a tight lot with nowhere to dump the dug-out base material adds a real trucking cost a backyard patio never needed. Prices haul-away from the actual excavation volume as its own line, only when the job needs it.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price, the profit, and the effective hourly rate, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

Two jobs, one flat rate

Using this calculator's own figures, at a 22% target margin, a $32 hourly wage, 30% payroll burden, and $375 in travel and equipment:

A 12 × 16 ft running-bond patio on 6 inches of base with spoils left on site costs $3,065.87 to run and this calculator prices it at $3,930.60 — $20.47 per square foot. A 20 × 24 ft circular driveway pad on 10 inches of base — more pattern waste, a slower cutting pace, and a haul-away run — costs $9,259.22 to run and this calculator prices it at $11,870.79, 3.02 times the patio's price on just 2.5 times the area. Quote the driveway pad at the patio's flat $20.47-per-square-foot rate instead, and 480 square feet prices at $9,826.50 — still above the job's $9,259.22 break-even cost, but by only $567.28, and $2,044.29 short of what this calculator prices the job at. A contractor targeting a 22% margin on that rate actually realizes about 5.8%, most of it eaten by the deeper base, the extra waste, and the haul-away the flat rate never priced.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get a bigger or different job wrong?

Because finished square footage is the face the homeowner measures, not the hole under it or the parts list. A flat rate is usually set on a small patio at the minimum base depth with a simple pattern and spoils left on site; a driveway pad needs a deeper base, a fussier pattern wastes more pavers and costs more labor per square foot, and hauling spoils off site is its own line the coupon never counted. Using this calculator's own figures, a 12 x 16 running-bond patio on 6 inches of base with spoils left on site costs $3,065.87 to run and this calculator prices it at $3,930.60 -- $20.47 per square foot. A 20 x 24 circular driveway pad on 10 inches of base with haul-away costs $9,259.22 to run and this calculator prices it at $11,870.79 -- 3.02 times the patio's price, not 2.5 times, on 2.5 times the finished area.

What does a flat rate per square foot usually leave out entirely?

Base depth, pattern waste, edge restraint, and haul-away. A flat per-square-foot rate has no line for the extra excavation and compacted base a driveway pad needs over a patio, no line for the higher paver waste and slower cutting a circular or herringbone pattern adds over running bond, no line for edge restraint scaling with perimeter rather than area, and no line for trucking spoils off site when they cannot stay in the yard.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own figures: the 12 x 16 running-bond patio prices at $3,930.60, on a job that costs $3,065.87 to run, at $20.47 per square foot. Apply that same per-square-foot rate to the 480-square-foot circular driveway pad and it quotes at $9,826.50 -- $2,044.29 short of what this calculator prices the job at ($11,870.79). The job still clears its $9,259.22 break-even cost, but only by $567.28: a contractor pricing off the patio's rate and targeting a 22% margin actually realizes about 5.8%, most of it consumed by the deeper base, the extra waste, and the haul-away the flat rate never priced.

Is a flat rate per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a job close to the base depth, pattern, and haul-away situation the rate was set on -- typically a simple patio at minimum base depth in running bond with spoils left on site -- a flat per-square-foot number can land close, because that is probably the job the rate was set on. The risk is quoting a driveway pad, a fussier pattern, or a job that needs haul-away at the same rate, because the rate does not know the base depth, the pattern, or where the spoils go until someone measures the site, and by then the flat number has often already been quoted over the phone.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or material costs?

No. It has no data on your local labor market, your supplier's material pricing, or what other hardscape contractors in your area charge. It takes length and width (or area), pattern, base depth, edge restraint, haul-away, and your own wage, burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat rate per square foot skips, not a market survey.

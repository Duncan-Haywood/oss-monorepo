This calculator vs. a flat "$8 a foot" crown molding special

"$8 a foot installed crown" is not a price for a crown molding install — it is a price for one specific grade, one profile, and one flat ceiling, quietly applied to every room the truck pulls up to.

What a flat "$8 a foot" special is

A flat per-foot rate is the fastest way to quote: one headline number on a postcard or a website, multiplied by a tape measurement. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match on their own dining room.

The number behind it was set for one job — usually the cheapest profile (paint-grade simple cove, mitered and caulked), the cheapest ceiling (flat, stepladder access), no coping, no stacked pieces, no scaffold — and then reused for every crown run afterward, whether or not the next room looks anything like the one the rate was set on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the linear feet, inside and outside corners, room count, and travel, plus grade (paint or stain), profile (simple cove, colonial, or built-up), ceiling shape (flat, cathedral, or tray), and height/access (standard, tall, or a two-story foyer), along with extras such as demo of existing crown, blocking, an LED cove, and matching casing. It prices the run, the corners, the grade, the ceiling, and the access separately, and returns a walk-away price and extra-work share below which the job loses money — for this room, not a generic one.

Common questions

Why does a flat "$8 a foot" crown molding special get the price wrong?

Because the labor and material scale with the grade, the profile, the ceiling shape, and the access, not with linear feet alone. Paint-grade simple cove in one flat 8-ft room is a different job from stain-grade built-up crown in a two-story cathedral foyer with fourteen coped inside corners and an LED cove. A flat per-foot rate quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat per-foot rate usually leave out entirely?

The corners, the coping, the ceiling, and the ladder. A published per-foot price is set on the cheapest run it was ever quoted against — paint-grade, mitered, flat ceiling, a stepladder — and has nowhere to put the hours for coped stain-grade insides, stacked built-up pieces, a spring-angle change at a cathedral ceiling, or a rolling scaffold in a two-story foyer. A carpenter pricing off the per-foot rate alone is short before the miter saw comes out of the truck.

How much do grade, profile, ceiling, and access actually add over the per-foot rate?

On this calculator's own worked examples, at a $42 crew wage, 32% burden, 15% overhead, and a 20% target margin: 80 linear feet of paint-grade simple cove in one flat room walks away at $1,182.95, against an $8/ft quote of $640.00 — already $542.95 short. The identical 80 linear feet as stain-grade built-up crown in a two-story cathedral foyer with an LED cove walks away at $7,710.87 against the same $640.00 quote — $7,070.87 short, thirteen times worse on the same footage. 180 linear feet of paint-grade colonial across three rooms walks away at $3,302.54, $1,862.54 over its $1,440.00 quote. Grade, profile, ceiling, and height move the number far more than linear feet alone.

Is a flat per-foot rate ever the right way to quote crown molding?

For the one job it was actually priced on — paint-grade simple cove, mitered and caulked, a flat ceiling, a stepladder, no demo, no blocking, no LED cove, no casing match — a flat rate can land close, and on this calculator's own numbers it does. The risk is applying it to a stain-grade or built-up job, a shaped ceiling, or a two-story access without noticing, because the per-foot rate does not know which job it is looking at and neither does the carpenter until the corners are cut.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the linear feet, corners, grade, profile, ceiling shape, height, and extras, plus your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price and extra-work share for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat "$8 a foot" special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

This calculator vs. doing it by hand or a spreadsheet

Base fee, plus extra-attempt, rush, and skip-trace add-ons, plus mileage revenue past what's included, minus your vehicle cost — that's arithmetic anyone can do on a notepad or in a spreadsheet. The part that's easy to get wrong, or to skip entirely, is doing it the same way for every job and turning the result into what the job actually paid per hour, especially once you're tallying a second or third client's fee schedule against the same numbers.

What each approach actually does

By hand or in a spreadsheet, you can add up a serve's revenue — base fee, extra attempts beyond what's included, a rush or skip-trace surcharge, and mileage past the included distance — and subtract your vehicle cost and the affidavit and fixed-cost share. All of that is straightforward addition and subtraction, and a correctly built spreadsheet gets it exactly right. What it does not give you, unless you build it in yourself, is a place that keeps the client's mileage rate and your own vehicle cost per mile as two clearly separate numbers — it is easy to tally only the billed miles as a cost and overstate profit — or a built-in step that turns a dollar figure into an effective hourly rate, which most hand tallies skip until after a run of bad jobs makes the gap obvious.

This calculator runs the identical formula every time: extra attempts beyond what's included, rush and skip-trace add-ons, mileage revenue only for miles past the included distance, vehicle cost for every mile actually driven, and net profit divided by total time (drive, on-site, and paperwork) to show effective hourly rate and the break-even base fee. The built-in client comparison table then applies that same formula consistently across two, three, or four clients' fee schedules side by side, so the ranking reflects what each client actually pays per hour for the same job — not which tally happened to be built more carefully that day.

Common questions

Can't I just tally a serve's profit by hand or in a spreadsheet?

Yes — base fee, plus extra-attempt, rush, and skip-trace add-ons, plus mileage revenue past what's included, minus your vehicle cost and the affidavit and fixed-cost share, is straightforward addition and subtraction. A correctly built spreadsheet gets that part exactly right. Where a hand tally or a quick spreadsheet formula stops short is turning that dollar figure into an effective hourly rate, and doing the same arithmetic the same way for a second and third client's fee schedule without dropping a line — that's the part this calculator exists for.

What does a spreadsheet not catch that this calculator does?

Two things a blank spreadsheet has no built-in reminder for. First, a client's mileage rate and your own vehicle cost per mile are two different numbers — one is what the client pays you past the included miles, the other is what fuel and wear actually cost you for every mile driven — and it is easy to tally only the billed miles as a cost, which overstates profit. Second, a dollar profit on its own does not say whether the job paid well: this calculator always divides net profit by total time (drive, on-site, and paperwork) to show effective hourly rate, which a hand tally usually skips because it takes an extra step most people don't bother adding until after a bad month.

Why is comparing several clients' fee schedules harder than it looks?

Because it means building the same formula correctly two or three times, under time pressure, and applying every add-on and cost the identical way for each client. A dropped extra-attempt fee for one client or a skipped skip-trace charge for another changes which client looks like the better one to keep serving for, without showing an error anywhere. This calculator's client comparison table runs the exact same formula for every row, so the ranking reflects what each client actually pays per hour for the same job, not which tally happened to be built more carefully.

# Parametric CAD and manufacturing packages

Code-CAD for Open Tennis Robots, built on [build123d](https://github.com/gumyr/build123d)
(OpenCascade). One command turns the parameters into everything a fabricator
needs:

```
pip install -r requirements.txt        # build123d, pinned
python -m otr_cad collector --out ../manufacturing/collector
python -m otr_cad feeder --out ../manufacturing/feeder
```

| Output | For |
| --- | --- |
| `laser/*.dxf` | Laser or waterjet cutting service. Flat aluminium 5052, true mm, no bends, no tapped holes |
| `print/*.stl` | Any FDM printer or print service, PETG |
| `step/*.step`, `step/assembly.step` | Any CAD package (Fusion, FreeCAD, Onshape, SolidWorks), CNC/CAM |
| `cut_list.csv` | Wire, tube and bar cut to length |
| `bom.csv` | Every part: made, cut, bought, and the fasteners |
| `drawings/*.svg` | Top, side and front views of the assembly |
| `manifest.json` | Parameters, derived numbers, check results, SHA-256 of every file |
| `collector-package.zip` | All of the above, ready to send |

The ready-built package is committed at
[`../manufacturing/`](../manufacturing/) and served from the project page.
You only need to run the generator if you change a parameter.

## What the build checks

Every build runs these checks, and the generator exits non-zero if one fails:

- **Design rules** (`params.Collector.check`): the rod gap squeezes a ball by
  15-30%, there is edge distance around holes, the drum clears the base, the
  motors fit, and the electronics tray fits.
- **Collisions**: every pair of parts, bought-in parts included as
  envelopes, intersects with zero volume.
- **Ground contact**: wheels, caster and drum all touch z = 0.

`tests/test_cad.py` adds hole alignment: every pilot hole in a printed part
that sits on the base plate must land on a hole in the plate. It also checks
that the DXFs read back in millimetres, that two builds are byte-identical,
and that the committed package matches a fresh build.

## Changing the design

Edit `otr_cad/params.py`, or override from the command line:

```
python -m otr_cad collector --out /tmp/wide --set drum_len=550 --set base_half_w=300
```

Bought-in parts (motor, wheel, bearing, caster, Raspberry Pi) are drawn
from the dimensions in `params.py`. Check them against the datasheet of
the part you actually buy before cutting metal.

  **Azimuth drive (2026-11-10, `Feeder.azimuth_drive`, `check_azimuth`, `feeder.az_gear_sector`, `package.azimuth_sweep`, `feeder.turning_inertia`):** a NEMA 17 stepper hangs flange-up under the mast plate on +y, 123.75 mm from the turntable axis, and turns a bought steel pinion (module 1.5, 15 teeth, 5 mm bore) meshing with a printed PETG gear sector (150 teeth on the full circle, pitch radius 112.5 mm, cut over +-38 deg) bolted under the turntable plate's +y edge with three M4 screws into heat-set inserts.   **Shuttle drive (2026-11-11, `Feeder.shuttle_drive`, `check_shuttle_drive`, `feeder.shuttle_drive_parts`, `package.feed_stroke_sweep`):** the shuttle had a clevis hole in its rear and a "100 mm stroke linear actuator" in the BOM, with no mount. Sizing a drive first showed that actuator is the wrong part: at the 20 mm/s assumed for the elevation actuator's class, 95 mm out and back takes 9.5 s, about 9.7 s a ball with the dwell and refill, against a 2 s interval for a fast drill and the drill file's 3 s default. A slider-crank is quicker. A standard-size servo outboard of the motor plate (+y; the elevation actuator is on -y) turns a printed crank with a 47.5 mm throw through 180 deg, and a printed 120 mm rod joins the crank pin to an M4 shoulder bolt. The bolt runs through a 100.6 mm slot in the motor plate (z +3, x -239.8 to -139.2 head frame) into a heat-set insert in the shuttle's +y side, 20 mm ahead of its rear end. Both ends of the stroke are the crank's dead centres, so the servo's end-angle error barely moves the shuttle (10 deg short costs 0.7 mm), and nothing pushing on the shuttle can turn the servo back. A printed bracket holds the servo by its tabs on three legs bolted through the plate; the legs stand clear of the crank's disc, the rod's sweep and the launcher motors (3.5 mm to the upper motor). Sizing, all assumed: a 23-ball column standing on the shuttle (sleeve, hose and column full, plus a tub's depth, all weight on the bottom ball) at mu 0.6 is 7.8 N. The 0.45 kg shuttle (from the CAD) slides at mu 0.35 and climbs at +45 deg. The pin is 48.5 mm off the shuttle's centre line on a 192 mm guide, so pushing cocks the shuttle between the plates (x1.21; it jams at 274 mm off), and the rod's 23 deg angle presses it on the floor or roof. That gives a peak of 19.3 N at the pin and 0.98 N m at the crank, 1.8x under an assumed 1.75 N m (18 kg cm class) stall. On a linear torque-speed line the stroke takes 0.76 s out and 0.68 s back (0.48 s unloaded), so a cycle is 1.68 s with 0.1 s held forward and 0.14 s for the next ball to drop: 36 balls a minute at most. The stroke sweep poses the shuttle, rod and crank at 45, 90, 135 and 180 deg, nose down and nose up, against every other part, and finds no collisions. The servo also passes the elevation and azimuth sweeps. **Firmware, same change:** the feed gate sent a 150 ms pulse every 550 ms (a 1000-2000 us servo), which would move this crank about 25% of its stroke. It also counted the ball as fed the moment the gate opened, and the Pi's drill runner re-aims as soon as the count rises, so with any real stroke time the head would have turned while the ball was still riding to the wheels. Now `GATE_OUT_MS` (900) holds the shuttle forward until the stroke is over, the count rises only then, and `GATE_BACK_MS` (850) waits for the refill (`firmware/esp32/config.py`; a test checks they cover the CAD's loaded strokes). The simulated board (`tests/feeder_sim.py`) now moves a physical shuttle at those stroke times and releases a ball only when it reaches the wheels. Against it, the old firmware fails 4 of the 15 host tests (no ball reaches the wheels), and counting at gate-open fails 2 (the last ball arrives after the drill has stopped the wheels). Every friction, mass and servo figure is an assumption: weigh the column load with a scale under the shuttle, and time your servo with the crank fitted. Calculated and drawn, not built.
  **Azimuth home switch (2026-11-12, `Feeder.home_switch_design`, `check_home_switch`, `feeder.home_switch_bracket`, `home_switch_parts`, `home_cam_radius`):** the firmware homed the turntable on a switch on GPIO 22 at -32 deg with nothing drawn to trip it. The only free room near the turntable is the 10 mm bearing gap between the bearing (r 75) and the gear sector's inner edge (r 92.6), so a subminiature roller-lever switch (D2F-01L3 class, about 12.8 x 5.8 x 6.5 mm, all its figures assumed) lies flat on a printed bracket on the mast plate at 45 deg round from the front, roller pointing out, 1 mm inside the sector's plain inner arc so the arc never touches it. The cam is part of the sector: its inner edge steps in 1.7 mm (pretravel 1.2 plus half the 1.0 mm overtravel, so the click lands mid-travel) on a 45 deg ramp, and stays in from there to the sector's high end. Under the switch the plate angle is 45 - az, so that lobe holds the switch closed at every angle from -32 deg down to -82.5 deg, well past -37.5 where the pinion runs off the teeth. The lobe first touches the roller at -31.25 deg (a round roller meets a 45 deg ramp 0.6 deg sooner than its tip would; the ramp is set later to keep the click at -32), clear of the +-30 working range. A test pushes the drawn lever into the turned sector by bisection: 0 mm pushed at +30, 0 and -30 deg, 1.21 mm at -32 deg (pretravel 1.2), 1.70 to 1.73 mm from -33.5 deg down. The bracket's M3 holes are radial slots (1.5 mm either way) so the switch can be set to click in the middle of the lobe, which leaves only bearing runout and the lobe's print error (0.3 mm assumed) against the 1.5 to 1.9 mm usable travel.

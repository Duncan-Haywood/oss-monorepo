Questions people ask

What is Natasha's Law?

Since 1 October 2021, food that a business packs on the premises where it is sold to the customer (a sandwich made in the shop and wrapped, a bakery's boxed cakes) must carry a label with the food's name and a full ingredients list, with the 14 allergens emphasised in the list. It applies in England; Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own versions of the same rule. This tool prepares the wording. Check the rule against the Food Standards Agency guidance for your premises.

Which allergens must be declared?

In the UK and the EU, fourteen: celery, cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, lupin, milk, molluscs, mustard, peanuts, sesame, soya, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above 10 mg/kg or 10 mg/litre), and tree nuts. In the US, nine major allergens: milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. Switch the box to see either list.

How does the tool decide something contains an allergen?

It reads the ingredient names you typed against a word list, including the contents of brackets, and handles look-alikes such as coconut milk, cocoa butter, butternut squash and rice flour. It cannot look inside a branded product: for those, add the supplier's allergens in square brackets, like pesto [milk, tree nuts], and the tool uses exactly that.

What does 'check' mean?

Some foods nearly always contain an allergen without naming it: hummus (sesame), Worcestershire sauce (fish), pasta (gluten), dried apricots (sulphites). The tool marks these 'check' so you confirm them against the supplier's label. A 'check' is never printed on a label as a declaration until you confirm it by typing the allergen in square brackets.

What about 'may contain' warnings?

Add | may=peanuts, sesame after the dish name to print a precautionary line. Whether a warning is justified is a risk assessment you make about your kitchen; the tool only prints what you ask for.

Is this legal advice, and where does my data go?

No. It is a drafting aid and it can be wrong; the person who sells the food is responsible for the label. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded.

Not decided

Whether your food counts as pre-packed for direct sale, your local rules (England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland, the EU states and the US states differ in detail), the exact wording your Trading Standards or environmental health officer expects, whether a "may contain" warning is justified in your kitchen, the sulphite level in a product (the 10 mg/kg limit needs the supplier's figure), and every branded product's real recipe. Hidden allergens in recipes you did not list, cross-contact and changes of supplier are yours to manage. The word list is a reading aid, not a legal definition.

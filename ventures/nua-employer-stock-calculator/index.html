NUA or IRA rollover: which leaves you with more after tax?

If your 401(k) holds your employer's stock, you can take the shares out in kind and pay ordinary tax only on the cost basis, leaving the net unrealized appreciation to be taxed as a long-term gain when you sell. Or you can roll it all to an IRA and pay ordinary rates on every dollar later. Enter your figures and see both.

Common questions

    
What is net unrealized appreciation (NUA)?

    
NUA is the growth in your employer's stock while it sat in your 401(k): the market value when it leaves the plan minus the plan's average cost basis. If you take the shares out in kind in a qualifying lump-sum distribution, you pay ordinary income tax only on the cost basis now, and the NUA is taxed as a long-term capital gain when you sell, whenever that is.

    
What is a lump-sum distribution for NUA?

    
The entire balance of all of the employer's plans of the same type, paid out within a single calendar year, after a triggering event such as separation from service, reaching age 59½, disability or death. A partial distribution generally loses NUA treatment. Ask the plan administrator to confirm before you act.

    
Is there a 10% penalty on an NUA distribution?

    
The 10% additional tax can apply to the cost basis you are taxed on now if you are under 59½, unless an exception applies, for example separation from service in or after the year you turn 55. The NUA itself is not hit by it. This page asks your age and whether you separated at 55 or later and adds the 10% when it applies.

    
When does NUA beat an IRA rollover?

    
Roughly when the cost basis is a small share of the stock's value and your ordinary tax rate is well above the long-term capital gains rate. A rollover defers all tax, but every dollar comes out later as ordinary income. The table on this page shows how the gap moves as basis share rises.

    
Does this tell me what to do?

    
No. It is a worksheet that does arithmetic on the figures you enter. Rules differ by plan and by state, and the decision is often irreversible, so check the result with a tax professional and your plan administrator before distributing shares.

No. Everything is worked out in your browser and nothing is sent; a test in the code asserts there are no network calls.

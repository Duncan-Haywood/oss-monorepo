This calculator vs. a flat CPM for every ad slot

A single "my show's CPM" is the easiest number to quote — one figure, applied to every sponsor who calls. It cannot see which placement they bought, whether the spot is host-read or produced, or which niche's market it sits in, and the same number that is fair for one slot is not fair for the next.

What a flat CPM for every slot is

It is one rate the show settled on — often whatever the first sponsor agreed to, a figure copied off a rate-card template, or a single number remembered from an industry report — reused for every ad sold after that, regardless of where in the episode it runs or how it is delivered.

Nothing about "$X per 1,000 downloads" asks whether this spot is a 30-second pre-roll, a 90-second host-read mid-roll, or a produced post-roll, or whether the sponsor is buying into a business-podcast audience or a general-interest one. The flat number does not ask, because it was never built to answer.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter your average downloads per episode, your niche, and the read type, and it prices pre-roll, mid-roll, and post-roll separately from commonly cited market CPM-per-1,000-downloads benchmark ranges for that exact combination — not one number stretched across every deal.

What a flat CPM doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate for a 90-second host-read mid-roll and a 15-second produced post-roll, even though the benchmark market ranges for the two can differ several times over. Prices each of the three placements separately, from ranges set for that placement.
Has no line for read type — a host-read endorsement and a pre-recorded produced spot get the same rate. Applies a produced-spot discount to the host-read benchmark, so a produced ad is not quoted at host-read money.
Has no line for niche — a business audience and a general-interest audience get the same rate. Uses separate benchmark ranges by niche, so a business or tech audience's typically higher CPM is not left unclaimed.
Gives the podcaster one number to quote before knowing which slot the sponsor actually wants. Reports a range per placement up front, so the quote already matches what was actually sold.
Cannot tell you when the flat number is leaving money on the table, or pricing you out of a deal, until a sponsor says so. Prices each slot from its own benchmark, so an off-rate quote shows up as a gap against the range instead of a lost or under-priced deal.

When a flat CPM still holds up

If a show only ever sells one placement, in one read type, to sponsors in one niche, a flat number can land close to the fair range, because the mix genuinely never varies deal to deal. The risk is the sponsor who wants something different — a produced post-roll instead of the usual host-read mid-roll — quoted at the rate the show has always used because nobody re-priced the new combination.

Common questions

Why can't I just use one CPM for every ad on my show?

Because niche, which placement in the episode (pre-roll, mid-roll, or post-roll), and whether the spot is host-read or produced each swing the commonly cited market CPM by a wide margin on their own -- and a single flat number cannot track all three at once. This calculator's own benchmark ranges put a business-niche, host-read, mid-roll spot at $30-45 CPM and a general-niche, produced, post-roll spot at $7-10.50 CPM -- roughly four to six times apart, for two spots on the same show.

Where does a flat "my show's CPM" number usually come from?

Usually whatever the first sponsor agreed to, a single figure copied off a rate-card template, or one number remembered from an industry report, then reused for every future spot regardless of placement, read type, or the sponsor's niche. It is easy to quote from memory, which is exactly why it stops matching the moment the deal mix changes.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own arithmetic for a show averaging 10,000 downloads per episode: a business-niche, host-read mid-roll spot prices at $300-450. A general-niche, produced post-roll spot on the same show prices at $70-105. Quote both at one flat $20 CPM (a plausible "my show's number", and this calculator's own general/mid-roll/host-read floor) and both come to $200 -- $100 to $250 under the fair range on the first spot, and $95 to $130 over the fair range on the second.

Is a flat CPM ever a reasonable shorthand?

If a show only ever sells one placement, one read type, and one kind of sponsor, a flat number can land close to the market range, because the mix genuinely never varies. The risk is the sponsor who wants a different slot: a produced post-roll priced at the same rate as a host-read mid-roll is not a thin margin, it is a rate card that never learned there are three placements and two read types to begin with.

Does this calculator know what my specific sponsor will pay?

No. It applies commonly cited market CPM-per-1000-downloads benchmark ranges, by niche, placement, and read type, to your own downloads -- a starting point for negotiation grounded in your show's actual numbers, not a quote, valuation, or offer performed on your behalf. Actual rates still vary by audience quality, host endorsement strength, and how much a specific sponsor wants your specific audience this quarter.

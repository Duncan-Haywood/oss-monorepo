How to write a monthly construction schedule narrative

Most contracts that require a CPM schedule also require a written narrative with each update: the narrative report in USACE's UFGS 01 32 01.00 10, and the schedule narrative in many state DOT and private specifications. It explains, in words, what the update's numbers show. Schedulers usually build it by hand from P6 layouts each month. Most of it is facts that are already in last month's and this month's XER files, and the free tool drafts that part for you.

The eight sections, and where each comes from

SectionFilled in from the filesLeft for you
1. Schedule statusData dates, forecast completion and how far it moved, the variance against the must-finish date, and a half-step split into the part progress caused and the part revisions causedThe cause of a slip and any notice of delay
2. Critical pathThe driving path's activities, and which joined or left itOne sentence on the area, trade and sequence
3. Work performedEvery activity started or finished, with its actual date and how late it was against last month's forecastNothing
4. Planned work not doneEvery activity last month's update said would start or finish by now that did notThe cause, one per activity
5. Changes to the scheduleEvery duration, relationship, lag, constraint, calendar and activity added or deletedThe reason, one per change
6. FloatActivities that became critical and the most float usedNothing
7. Next periodThe work starting, continuing and finishing in the next weeks (four by default), critical work markedWhat still has to be ready: materials, submittals, permits, crews
8. Delays and corrective actionReported actual dates that were later edited or fall after the data dateDelays with their causes, and the recovery plan when the job is behind

The fill-ins are highlighted on screen and marked [FILL IN: ...] in the text download, so a search finds every one before it goes out.

A worked example

The tool's built-in example is two invented updates of an office fit-out, 9 March and 6 April 2026 (press Load an example pair of updates on the tool page, then Download narrative (text)). The draft opens:

The forecast completion is 2026-07-27, 15 working days later than the previous update's 2026-07-06. That is 4 working days ahead of the contract completion date of 2026-07-31 (the previous update was 19 working days ahead of it). Split by a half-step run: progress during the period moved the finish 8 working days later, and the changes made to the schedule 7 working days later.

It goes on to list the shop drawings finishing 3 working days late, six activities that were due to start or finish and did not (each with a cause to write), and nine changes to logic, durations, a constraint and an activity, including paint shortened from 10 to 8 days on the critical path, each with a reason to write. The draft leaves 20 fill-ins. A scheduler who knows the job can answer them in far less time than it takes to build the lists by hand.

Before you send it

  • Fix what a reviewer will reject. An actual date after the data date, or a reported actual that moved, is listed in section 8. Correct it in P6 and export again, or explain it.
  • Give a reason for every change. The owner's reviewer sees the same changes; the reviewer's side of this comparison flags each one.
  • Check the order your contract asks for. The sections follow the common requirements; move them to match your specification.

The dates come from the tool's own CPM run, which matches P6's stored dates on 99.99% of 19,822 open activities in six public files. On those six files, with a slip and a lag change planted between two updates (18 runs), every planted change appeared in the draft with a fill-in and nothing else was listed. Whether a delay is excusable, and who carries it, is for the contract.

Questions

What goes in a monthly schedule narrative?

The status against the contract completion date and how far the forecast moved since the last update, the critical path, the work started and finished, the work that was planned and not done with its cause, every change to logic, durations, constraints and calendars with its reason, float used, the next period's work, and current delays with the corrective action. The contract's own schedule section says which of these it wants and in what order.

Does the tool write the causes and reasons for me?

No. It fills in every date, activity and day count from the two files and leaves each cause and reason as a highlighted fill-in. A file shows that a duration changed, not why, and a narrative is a contract document, so the reason has to come from the people who made the change.

Is my schedule uploaded?

No. Both files are read in your browser tab and the narrative is built there. Nothing is sent anywhere.

Can I use a Microsoft Project schedule?

Yes. Save both updates as XML in Project and open them where the XER files go; the narrative is drafted the same way.

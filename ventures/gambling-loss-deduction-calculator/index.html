Common questions

What is the new 90% gambling loss deduction limit?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, amended IRC Section 165(d) so that, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, your gambling-loss deduction is capped at the lesser of your actual, substantiated losses or 90% of your winnings. This is on top of the pre-existing rule that losses can never be deducted beyond winnings in the first place.

What is "phantom income" and how can I owe tax if I broke even?

Once your losses are at least 90% of your winnings — meaning you broke even or lost money overall — your deduction is capped at exactly 90% of winnings no matter how much more you actually lost. The remaining 10% of winnings is taxed with no loss to offset it, and it does not carry forward. A gambler who wins $50,000 and loses $50,000 now owes tax on $5,000 of income despite walking away even.

Is this permanent, or does it expire like other OBBBA provisions?

It is permanent law with no scheduled sunset, unlike several other OBBBA individual-tax provisions (the SALT cap increase and the tips/overtime deductions both expire after 2028 or 2029). Multiple repeal bills, including one nicknamed the FAIR BET Act, have been introduced in Congress, but none had passed as of this tool's last fact-check — treat repeal as a watch item, not current law.

Do I need to itemize to claim any gambling-loss deduction?

Yes — this rule predates OBBBA and is unaffected by it. Gambling losses are only deductible on Schedule A, Line 16, for a taxpayer who itemizes. If you take the standard deduction, your full winnings are taxable and none of your losses offset them, cap or no cap.

Does this apply to professional gamblers too?

Yes. OBBBA permanently folded a professional gambler's trade-or-business wagering expenses into this same Section 165(d) limitation — a rule that was otherwise set to expire after 2025. A professional's wagering losses and directly related business expenses are grouped together and capped at 90% of winnings, the same as a casual gambler's losses alone.

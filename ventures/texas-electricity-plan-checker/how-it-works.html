TEXAS ELECTRICITY PLAN CHECKER · HOW IT WORKS

Why the 1,000 kWh price misleads, and how to get your own

Every plan in the competitive Texas market has an Electricity Facts Label with three average prices: at 500, 1,000 and 2,000 kWh a month. Plan lists sort by the 1,000 kWh one. A plan can be built to look cheap at exactly that point, with a bill credit that pays only between 1,000 and 2,000 kWh, and cost more than its neighbours in the months you use 900 or 2,300. Your price depends on your months.

Open the checker

Who it is for

  • Households in Oncor, CenterPoint, AEP Texas, TNMP and other competitive areas choosing or renewing a plan.
  • Anyone who has asked "why was my bill so high when my plan was 9 cents?" — usually a month that missed the credit.
  • People weighing a free-nights or free-weekends plan who want to know whether their own use pattern pays.

What you do

  1. Download your usage from Smart Meter Texas (15-minute or daily CSV), or type twelve months of kWh from your bills.
  2. For each plan you are considering, open its EFL PDF, select all, copy, and paste it into the checker. It reads the base charge, energy charge and tiers, TDU delivery charges, bill credits, minimum-usage fees and time-of-use hours into a small form you can correct.
  3. Press compare. Each plan is billed on each of your months, ranked by the total, with your own cents per kWh beside the label's 1,000 kWh price.

The label checks itself

Reading a PDF's text is fragile, so the checker never trusts its reading silently. From the terms it has, it recomputes the label's own three average prices. The label rounds to a tenth of a cent, so correct terms land within about 0.05 cents of each; anything further off is shown as a mismatch beside the plan and in the ranking, with which kWh level disagrees and by how much. A credit read as $100 when it is $125 misses the 1,000 kWh price by 2.5 cents and is caught.

The bill, step by step

Month's bill = base charge + TDU fixed charge + energy charge + TDU per-kWh charge × kWh − each credit whose kWh band the month falls in + each minimum-usage fee whose condition it meets.

Energy tiers are marginal: "8.2¢ to 1,000 kWh, 12.9¢ above" charges the 1,001st kWh at 12.9¢, not the whole month. Credit bands include both ends. A time-of-use window prices the kWh inside it (from your 15-minute data, binned by weekday or weekend and hour) at its own rate. Taxes and charges the label excludes are left out, and calendar months stand in for billing cycles.

Only have the three prices?

A plan with no credit or tier costs a fixed amount plus a rate per kWh, so its three label points sit on one straight line. Draw the line through the 500 and 2,000 kWh bills and see where 1,000 kWh falls. Our example plan B shows 21.0¢, 8.1¢ and 14.1¢: the line predicts $164.00 at 1,000 kWh, the label says $81.00, and the $83 gap says something pays out near 1,000 kWh. For a credit that stops before 2,000 kWh the gap is exactly the credit; B's still pays at 2,000 kWh, which lowers that point too, so the gap understates its $125.

What billing 200 Texas homes showed

We took 200 homes from NREL's End-Use Load Profiles (ResStock, 2018 weather; building simulations calibrated to the housing stock, not meter readings) in Harris, Fort Bend, Dallas, Tarrant and Collin counties, and billed each home's year on the checker's three example plans. The plans are invented, in label shape, with the same placeholder delivery charges. By the 1,000 kWh price the order is B (8.1¢), C (14.2¢), A (16.0¢).

The plan that looks cheapest by half was the cheapest of the three for 54 of the 200 homes and the dearest for 138. Its credit band caught 37.5% of all home-months: 44.9% were under 1,000 kWh and 17.5% over 2,000, because Texas homes swing from mild-month lows to August highs. The median home collected it in 4.5 of 12 months. Picking B by its label price cost a home a median $241 a year against the cheapest of the three on its own usage, and $649 at the 90th percentile. Multiplying the 1,000 kWh price by a year's kWh underestimated B's true cost by a median $1,178.

Free nights: the median home used 30.8% of its electricity between 9 p.m. and 6 a.m. (10th to 90th percentile 26.9% to 35.0%). With the same base and delivery charges, a free-nights plan beats a flat 9.1¢ energy rate only if its daytime rate is under 9.1 / (1 − night share): 13.2¢ for the median home. The full output, and the scripts that fetch the homes and bill them, are in the check folder.

What this does and does not show: it measures how much three label prices leave out for a realistic spread of Texas usage. It says nothing about any real plan's price, and simulated homes are not your meter: run your own year.

When to use something else

  • Variable or indexed plans: the checker bills the label as printed; the price can change after you sign.
  • Rooftop solar: buyback credits and net-metering terms are not modelled; export rows are dropped from your usage.
  • Searching the whole market: the PUCT's Power to Choose site lists every plan; use it to find candidates, then check them here on your usage.
  • A business meter with a demand charge: use the demand charge interval analyzer.
  • A dispute about a bill already sent: your provider, then the PUCT's complaint process.

Questions

Does the checker know today's prices?

No. It stores no provider's prices. Every number comes from the label you paste or type, including the TDU delivery charges, which change twice a year and are printed on every label.

Can it use my Smart Meter Texas file directly?

Yes. Download your 15-minute or daily usage as a CSV and choose the file; rows marked as surplus generation are left out, and months with fewer than 90% of their days are flagged.

What does a label mismatch mean?

The terms the checker has do not reproduce the label's own average price at 500, 1,000 or 2,000 kWh to within about 0.05 cents. Usually a credit, tier or fee was missed or misread; correct the plan's fields until the check passes.

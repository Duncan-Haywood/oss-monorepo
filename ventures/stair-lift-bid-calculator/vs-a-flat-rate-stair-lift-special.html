This calculator vs. a flat "$3,995 installed" stair lift special

"$3,995 installed" is not a price for a stair lift — it is a price for one specific rail, one chair, and one tread attach, quietly applied to every staircase the template goes up.

What a flat "$3,995 installed" special is

A flat installed rate is the fastest way to quote: one headline number on a postcard or a website, next to a photo of a straight staircase. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number an owner holding a competitor's flyer — or a family arranging a hospital discharge on a deadline — will ask you to match on their own stairs.

The number behind it was set for one job — usually the cheapest configuration (a straight one-flight rail, a standard seated chair, wood treads, no fold, no circuit, no removal) — and then reused for every lift afterward, whether or not the next staircase looks anything like the one the rate was set on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the lift count, travel, plus rail (straight, landing, outdoor, or curved), chair (perch, standard, power swivel, power pack, or heavy-duty), and tread attach (wood, concrete, or open-riser), along with extras such as a folding rail, an extra charge point, a dedicated circuit, removing an existing lift, a permit, and a weatherizing kit. It prices the rail, the chair, the attach, and the extras separately, and returns a walk-away price and extra-work share below which the job loses money — for this staircase, not a generic one.

Common questions

Why does a flat "$3,995 installed" stair lift special get the price wrong?

Because the labor and material scale with the rail, the chair, the tread attach, and the extras, not with "a stair lift" alone. A straight one-flight rail with a standard seated chair on wood treads is a different job from a curved custom-bent rail with a heavy-duty power-swivel chair on an open-riser stair, a folding rail for the door, a dedicated circuit, and pulling the old lift. A flat installed rate quotes both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat installed rate usually leave out entirely?

The bend, the swivel, the fold, and the electrician. A published installed price is set on the cheapest configuration it was ever quoted against — a straight rail, a standard chair, wood treads, no fold, no circuit, no removal — and has nowhere to put the hours for a custom-bent curved rail, a power-swivel or heavy-duty chair, open-riser hardware, a folding rail at the bottom landing, a dedicated circuit run, or pulling and hauling the old lift. A contractor pricing off the flat rate alone is short before the template goes up the stairs.

How much do the rail, chair, attach, and extras actually add over the flat rate?

On this calculator's own worked examples, at a $42 crew wage, 30% burden, 15% overhead, and a 20% target margin: a straight one-flight rail, a standard chair, and wood treads walks away at $4,266.69, already $271.69 over its own $3,995.00 special. The typical aging-in-place job — the same straight rail with a power-swivel chair, a folding rail, a dedicated circuit, and a permit — walks away at $6,846.45, $2,851.45 short of the same $3,995.00. A curved custom-bent rail with a heavy-duty chair, open-riser attach, the fold, the circuit, and pulling the existing lift walks away at $16,643.33 — $12,648.33 short, more than four times the flat rate on the same one-lift job. The rail, the chair, the attach, and the extras move the number far more than "one stair lift" alone.

Is a flat installed rate ever the right way to quote a stair lift?

For something close to the one job it was actually priced on — a straight one-flight rail, a standard seated chair, wood treads, no fold, no circuit, no removal — a flat rate can land close, and on this calculator's own numbers the cheap-special job comes out only a few hundred dollars over it. The risk is applying the same number to a curved rail, a power or heavy-duty chair, an open-riser stair, a folding rail, a dedicated circuit, or pulling an old lift without noticing, because the flat rate does not know which job it is looking at and neither does the installer until the rail is templated.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the rail, the chair, the tread attach, and the extras, plus your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price and extra-work share for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat "$3,995 installed" special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

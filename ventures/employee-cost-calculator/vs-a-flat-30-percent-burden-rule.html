This calculator vs. a flat 30% burden rule of thumb

“Budget 30% on top of wages” is not a law of employer costs — it is a rule fit to one wage level, quietly reused for every role afterward.

What a flat burden percentage rule of thumb is

It is the fastest way to ballpark what a hire costs: take the wage, add a round percentage — 20%, 25%, 30% — and call that the fully-loaded cost. It travels well because it needs no lookup: no state rate, no policy declaration, no benefits quote.

The number behind it was set for one role — usually the full-time hire whose numbers someone actually ran once — and then reused for every other role, whether or not the next one pays the same wage, works the same hours, or gets the same benefits.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter a wage, hours, and your own FICA, FUTA, SUTA, workers' comp, PTO, retirement, health insurance, and payroll admin figures, and it prices each one separately — capping FUTA and SUTA at their wage base rather than charging them on every dollar, and holding health insurance and payroll admin as the flat dollar amounts they actually are — then reports the fully-loaded cost for that role, not a generic one.

What a flat percentage rule doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same burden percentage whether the role pays $16/hr part-time or $55/hr full-time, even though health insurance and payroll admin are dollar amounts that do not shrink with the wage. Prices health insurance and payroll admin as the fixed dollar amounts they are, so a lower-paid or part-time role's burden percentage comes out higher, not the same.
Applies FUTA and SUTA rates to the full annual wage, as if neither tax had a cap. Caps FUTA and SUTA at each tax's own wage base, so a role that clears both bases pays the capped amount, not a percentage of every dollar earned.
Gives one number to remember and reuse for every role. Reports the burden as a percentage of wages for the role actually entered, so you can see when a different wage has moved that percentage rather than assuming it stayed put.
Has no separate figure for one-time onboarding versus the cost every year after the first. Reports an ongoing annual cost and a year-one cost separately, so onboarding is not silently repeated every year in the estimate.

Common questions

Why does a flat "add 30%" burden rule of thumb get an employee's cost wrong?

Because a large share of the burden is fixed-dollar, not a percentage of wages — health insurance is a dollar amount set by a policy, and payroll/HR admin is a dollar amount set by a vendor, so neither scales down for a lower-paid or part-time role the way a flat percentage assumes. FUTA and SUTA are charged on wages only up to a wage base, so a full-time role's effective rate on those two is lower than the posted rate once wages clear the cap. A flat percentage bakes in one wage level's mix of fixed and proportional costs and then applies it to every wage.

What does a flat percentage rule usually leave out entirely?

The FUTA and SUTA wage-base caps, and the fact that health insurance and payroll/HR admin are dollar amounts rather than a percentage of pay — so they are a much bigger share of a lower-paid or part-time role's total burden than of a highly-paid one's. A flat rate has no line for either effect; it applies the same percentage regardless of wage.

How big is the gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped default rates (7.65% employer FICA, 0.6% FUTA capped at $7,000, 2.7% SUTA capped at $9,500, 1.5% workers' comp, 5.8% PTO accrual, $500/month health insurance, $600/year payroll admin): a full-time $28/hr role's true ongoing burden is 26.8% of wages ($73,845 ongoing annual cost), about 2.5% below what a flat 30% rule predicts ($75,712). A part-time $16/hr, 20-hour-a-week role's true burden is 56.4% of wages ($26,026 ongoing) — a flat 30% rule understates that role by $4,394, more than 16% too low. A $55/hr full-time role's true burden is only 21.0% of wages ($138,401 ongoing) — the flat rule overstates that role by $10,319.

Is a flat percentage rule ever close enough?

For one wage level and one benefit package it can land close — a rule tuned to a specific full-time role comes within a few percent on this calculator's own default example. It breaks down the moment the wage or hours change, because the fixed-dollar costs behind the rule did not move with it, and nothing about a flat percentage warns you when that has happened.

Does this calculator know my own tax rates and benefit costs?

No. It has no data on your state's FUTA/SUTA rates, your workers' comp policy, or your health plan's premium. It takes the FICA, FUTA, SUTA, workers' comp, PTO, retirement match, health insurance, and payroll admin figures you enter and turns them into a fully-loaded cost for your role — the arithmetic a flat percentage skips, not a survey of your state's actual rates.

Predictive scheduling: the 14-day window moves across your week

Most fair-workweek and predictive-scheduling rules ask for about 14 days of notice and attach a premium to changes inside that window. The part owners miss: the window is counted shift by shift. A change you make ten days before Monday is late for Monday through Thursday and early for the weekend.

What it shows

Fifteen days out, nothing is owed. Ten days out, only shifts starting in the first four days of the week fall inside the window, so the premium is a fifth to nearly half smaller than at three days. By three days, the whole week is inside. Ana's moved shifts land mostly late in the week, so waiting from 10 days to 3 nearly doubles her case; Gus's land early, so four fifths of his premium is already owed at 10 days. On this invented example the practical rule is: if you must rebuild, do it as early as possible, and look at which days the moved shifts land on before you press publish.

How to check your own week

1. Look up the rule that applies to you

Commonly listed jurisdictions are Oregon, New York City, San Francisco, Seattle, Philadelphia, Chicago, Los Angeles (the city, and the county's unincorporated areas), Berkeley, Emeryville and Evanston. Windows, premiums, industry coverage and exemptions differ and change. Use your city or state labor department, not this page.

2. Enter its window and premium

In the roster builder's changes report (or --changes posted.csv --notice-hours 336 --premium-hours 1 on the command line), choose the roster you posted, say when you are making the change, and add the employee-requested people to the exempt list.

3. Read the list, not only the total

Each added, cancelled or moved shift is listed with its notice in hours, so you can see which ones sit just inside the window and could be left as they were.

Questions

Does a 14-day notice window apply to the whole week or to each shift?

It depends on the city. Seattle, Oregon, Chicago, Philadelphia and Los Angeles, as the team hub models them, count from the moment you change the schedule to the start of the shift: a change made 10 days before Monday leaves Monday inside a 14-day window but Saturday and Sunday outside it. New York City's fast food rules count to 12:00 a.m. on the first day of the work schedule, so a Thursday shift cancelled on the Sunday before a Monday-start week is a change with under 24 hours' notice. Read your local rule.

Which cities have predictive scheduling laws?

Commonly listed are Oregon (the only state), New York City, San Francisco, Seattle, Philadelphia, Chicago, Los Angeles (the city, and the county's unincorporated areas), Berkeley, Emeryville and Evanston. Most guides give 14 days of notice, with exceptions by industry and city. Lists differ and laws change, so confirm with your city or state labor department before relying on any of it.

Does the roster builder know my local scheduling law?

The browser tool does not: you enter the notice window and the premium hours from the rule that applies to you, and the changes report prices them against the roster you posted. The team hub (a hosted service we are building, not open yet) has starting presets for Seattle, Oregon, Chicago, Philadelphia, Los Angeles (city), Los Angeles County (unincorporated areas), New York City (fast food), San Francisco (formula retail), Emeryville, Berkeley and Evanston that log and price every change to a posted week. San Francisco pays a fixed number of hours per change rather than a share of the hours lost: 1 hour under 7 days' notice, 2 or 4 hours under 24 hours depending on how many hours change. Emeryville pays 1 hour under 14 days and, for hours taken away under 24 hours, the lesser of the shift's hours and 4. Berkeley pays the same 1 hour, but a cut under 24 hours pays the lesser of the hours taken away and 4, so cutting a shift 15 minutes short the evening before owes a quarter of an hour, and a shift moved to another day is one change. Evanston, as law-firm summaries describe it, pays 1 hour under 14 days and, for a cut under 24 hours, the lesser of the shift's hours and 4, with no grace minutes. New York City pays a flat sum per change rather than hours of pay: $10 or $20 at 14 to 7 days' notice, $15 or $45 at 7 days to 24 hours, $15 or $75 under 24 hours (the higher figure when hours are taken away), and $100 for a clopening. It is a planning aid, not legal advice.

Is covering a call-out always a premium?

Not everywhere. In Seattle and Oregon, hours an employee accepts from a written offer sent to the whole team after a colleague's absence are exempt, if the offer says taking it is voluntary and they may decline. Handing the shift straight to one person is not that offer. Philadelphia exempts hours someone volunteers for after a written mass communication about a colleague's call-out. San Francisco exempts the employee who covers a colleague's illness, vacation or time off when the employer had less than seven days' notice of the absence, however the cover was found. Los Angeles County, in its unincorporated areas, exempts hours an employee accepts to cover another's absence, whether from a team offer or a one-to-one ask, once the employer has said taking them is voluntary and documented the request and the consent. Chicago, the City of Los Angeles, Emeryville, Berkeley and Evanston have no matching exception that we found, and New York City's fast food rules say volunteers and open-shift pick-ups still owe the premium. The team hub's "Offer to the whole team" button sends that kind of offer and logs who took it. Check the current rules before relying on it.

What about on-call shifts?

An on-call shift you don't call someone in for counts as hours taken away. Seattle and Oregon pay half the hours not worked. Chicago's 2026 rules treat it like other cancelled hours: half pay inside 24 hours, one hour of pay before that. San Francisco pays 2 hours (a shift of 4 hours or less) or 4 hours for an on-call shift not called in, unless it was cancelled 24 hours or more ahead. In the team hub you mark each on-call shift "called in" or "not needed", and one never called in by its end is priced as released.

Is the rest between shifts paid the same way everywhere?

No. Seattle and Oregon pay time and a half on the hours worked inside 10 hours of the last shift; Chicago pays 1.25 times. The City of Los Angeles pays time and a half for the whole shift that follows a rest under 10 hours; Los Angeles County pays it only on the hours inside the 10. Philadelphia sets 9 hours, counts a shift after a prior day's shift (not a same-day split), needs the employee's written consent for that shift, and pays $40 each time. The team hub's changes page lists short rests in the posted week and prices them under the preset you pick.

When these forecasts said 70%, did it happen 70% of the time?

Paste yes/no forecasts and what happened: a model's predicted probabilities on a test set, a weather service's chance of rain, a credit model's default risk, a forecaster's or a pundit's win probabilities. Method and citation

The reliability curve. The forecasts are sorted and the outcomes regressed on them under one constraint, that a higher forecast never gets a lower recalibrated value: isotonic regression, computed by the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm (Ayer et al. 1955), with equal forecasts pooled first. The resulting step function is the CORP reliability diagram of Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan (2021): consistent (a calibrated forecaster's curve tends to the diagonal), optimally binned (the algorithm picks the bins), reproducible and based on pool-adjacent-violators. Unlike a binned chart it has no bin count to choose.

The decomposition. With S the mean score, x the forecasts, c their isotonic recalibration and r the base rate, S(x) = MCB − DSC + UNC, where MCB = S(x) − S(c), DSC = S(r) − S(c) and UNC = S(r). It holds exactly for the Brier score (Brier 1950) and for the log score, because the isotonic fit minimises every proper score at once. The binned Murphy (1973) split, reliability − resolution + uncertainty, is shown for comparison; with forecasts that vary inside a bin it leaves a remainder, reported as the within-bin term.

Is it more than chance? Outcomes are redrawn as if the forecasts were true (each a coin with that probability), the isotonic fit is redone, and the band shows where the middle 90% of those curves fall. The same draws give a p-value: the share of calibrated worlds with at least the observed miscalibration (this tool's addition, using CORP's MCB as the statistic). Alongside: Spiegelhalter's (1986) z test, the observed against expected count of events (calibration in the large), Cox's (1958) calibration intercept and slope from a logistic regression on the log-odds of the forecast, the binned expected and maximum calibration error (Naeini, Cooper & Hauskrecht 2015) and the AUC (Hanley & McNeil 1982), which measures ranking only.

Verified by tests in the source against an independent computation (eval/reference.py): on the example and two further cases, the Brier and log scores and both CORP decompositions match scikit-learn 1.9.1's isotonic regression, brier_score_loss and log_loss to 10⁻¹⁰; the isotonic fit matches value for value; the AUC matches roc_auc_score; the calibration intercept and slope match its unpenalised logistic regression to 10⁻⁶; and Spiegelhalter's and the observed/expected z match numpy with p-values from scipy 1.18.1.

Limits

  • Calibration is not skill. Forecasting the base rate every time is perfectly calibrated and useless. Read miscalibration and discrimination together; the AUC and the discrimination term say how much the forecasts know.
  • In-sample recalibration flatters. The isotonic and Platt maps are fitted to these outcomes, so the "after recalibration" score is optimistic. Fit them on one period and check them on the next.
  • Independence is assumed. The tests and band treat outcomes as independent given the forecasts. Forecasts of the same storm, the same borrower or the same team over a season are often correlated, which makes the p-values too small.
  • A shift can pass the shape test. A forecaster who is a few points too high everywhere can show little miscalibration on the curve while the observed/expected count says otherwise; on the example, the resampling test finds nothing (p = 0.289 with the default 500 resamples) and the count test does (p = 0.025). Read both.
  • Size. Up to 200,000 forecasts; resampling 500 times takes a few seconds per 100,000.
  • Not modelled: forecasts of more than two outcomes (multiclass or ordinal calibration), forecasts of a number (use conformal prediction intervals to check interval coverage) and calibration that drifts over time, beyond splitting the file by period yourself.

Questions

What does it mean for forecasts to be calibrated?

Of all the times the forecast said 70%, the event happened about 70% of the time, and the same for every other probability. Calibration is about honesty, not skill: always forecasting the base rate is calibrated but useless.

What is a CORP reliability diagram?

A reliability diagram whose curve comes from isotonic regression of the outcomes on the forecasts instead of from bins. Dimitriadis, Gneiting and Jordan (2021) showed it does not depend on a bin count, uses every forecast, and gives an exact split of the score into miscalibration, discrimination and uncertainty.

Why not just use expected calibration error (ECE)?

ECE depends on how many bins you choose and where their edges fall. On this page's NFL example it ranges from 0.019 to 0.043 depending on the bins, more than double, while the CORP miscalibration is one number. The tool reports ECE too, for comparison with papers that use it.

What do miscalibration, discrimination and uncertainty mean?

Uncertainty is the score of always forecasting the base rate. Discrimination is how much the recalibrated forecasts improve on that by telling events from non-events. Miscalibration is how much the forecasts lose by not being calibrated: the most an order-preserving recalibration could take off the score. Score equals miscalibration minus discrimination plus uncertainty, exactly.

What does the calibration slope say?

It is the slope of a logistic regression of the outcome on the log-odds of the forecast. A slope of 1 means the spread is right; below 1 the forecasts are too extreme (overconfident), above 1 too timid. The intercept shows whether they run high or low overall.

How many forecasts do I need?

At least 10, but a few hundred before the curve means much. The consistency band shows how far a truly calibrated forecaster's curve would wander with your sample size, so a wide band is the tool telling you the sample is small.

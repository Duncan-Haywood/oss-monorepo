How it is calculated

Rain. An alternating-block storm over the duration: the depth in each time step is the IDF depth of k steps minus that of k - 1, with the largest block in the middle and the rest placed right, left, right of it, so every duration centred on the peak gets its IDF depth.

Runoff. The NRCS curve-number equation on the cumulative rain, Q = (P - 0.2 S)² / (P + 0.8 S) with S = 1000 / CN - 10 (NRCS National Engineering Handbook part 630, chapter 10; TR-55). Each step's runoff is spread by the NRCS triangular unit hydrograph: time to peak = step / 2 + 0.6 x Tc, peak = 484 x square miles / time to peak, base set so it holds exactly one inch (chapter 16).

Routing. Level-pool storage-indication routing (modified Puls): 2S/Δt + O at the end of each step equals the inflows at both ends plus 2S/Δt - O at its start, solved for the water level by bisection on the exact basin volume, a rectangular bottom with sloped sides (prismoidal formula). Outflow is a circular orifice at the bottom, Q = Cd x area x √(2g x head to its centre), plus a sharp-crested weir by Francis's formula, Q = Cw x L' x head1.5. A contracted weir (the default: a notch or riser slot narrower than the box it sits in) has L' = L - 0.2 x head for its two end contractions; a suppressed weir, as wide as its channel, has L' = L. The formula is fitted for heads up to about a third of the crest length, and the tool warns past that: there the opening is acting as an orifice, which it does not model.

Sizing. The orifice diameter is found by bisection so the small storm's peak outflow equals its allowable (the pre-development peak unless you enter one). The weir crest goes at the small storm's peak level, so that storm never reaches it, and the weir length is found the same way on the large storm, releasing everything allowed so the basin is as shallow as it can be. Depth = the highest peak level of any storm + freeboard. The time step is checked against NEH-630 chapter 16's unit duration, about 0.133 x the developed site's time of concentration.

Not modelled: NRCS 24-hour rainfall distributions (Types I to III) and Atlas 14 temporal patterns, several subareas or reaches, infiltration from the basin, tailwater on the outlet, the orifice below its crown (approximated as weir-like, rising with head1.5), outlet barrel or culvert control, the emergency spillway, water quality and channel-protection volumes, and metric units. Unverified: checked by hand calculations, conservation tests and an independent RK4 integration, not against HydroCAD, HEC-HMS or a reviewed design.

Worked example step by step: how to size a detention pond.

Common questions

How do I size a detention pond?

Make the runoff hydrograph for the developed site, choose a basin footprint and an outlet, and route the hydrograph through the basin, one time step at a time, keeping track of storage and outflow. Adjust the outlet until the peak outflow is no more than the allowable, usually the pre-development peak, then read the peak water level and add freeboard. This tool does those steps for a small storm and a large one.

Why two storms?

A single opening sized for a big storm lets the frequent small storms through almost untouched, and one sized for a small storm backs the big one far up the bank. So many ordinances ask for peak control at two or three return periods, and the outlet has two stages: a low orifice for the small storm and a weir higher up that opens for the large one.

What does the example show?

Five acres going from curve number 61 to 85, with a 100 by 60 ft basin bottom and 3 to 1 sides. The 2-year peak rises from 0.77 to 9.81 cfs and the 100-year from 8.30 to 28.81 cfs. A 4.6 in orifice and a 1.10 ft weir with its crest at 2.05 ft hold both to the pre-development peaks (the weir with two end contractions, as in a riser slot); the 100-year storm fills the basin to 4.26 ft, so with 1 ft of freeboard it is 5.26 ft deep. The tool then warns that the weir runs 2.21 ft deep over a 1.10 ft crest, far past the head Francis's formula was fitted for: a real outlet for this site is a second orifice or a wider slot, checked as an orifice.

Where do the IDF coefficients come from?

From your local drainage manual or a fit to the NOAA Atlas 14 table for your site, in the form intensity = a / (minutes + b) ^ n in inches per hour. The example's coefficients are made up for illustration and belong to no real place; replace them before relying on a number.

Is this the same as TR-55 or HydroCAD?

It uses the same NRCS runoff equation and the NRCS triangular unit hydrograph, but builds its storm from an IDF curve rather than the NRCS 24-hour rainfall distributions, and uses one subarea. Treat it as a screening tool for a first layout and an independent check, not a stamped design.

What is the peak rate factor?

The 484 in the NRCS unit hydrograph peak, qp = 484 x area in square miles / time to peak. Flat, swampy country such as the Delmarva peninsula uses lower factors (around 300), which spreads the same volume over a longer, lower hydrograph. The tool keeps the volume at exactly one inch whatever the factor.

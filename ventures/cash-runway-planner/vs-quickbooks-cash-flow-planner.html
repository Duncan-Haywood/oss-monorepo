Cash Runway Planner vs. QuickBooks Online's Cash Flow Planner

QuickBooks Online includes a Cash Flow Planner for people already running their books on the platform. Per Intuit's own help documentation, setting it up means connecting your bank and credit-card accounts so it can pull your transaction history, and it forecasts a 3-to-24-month range from that data plus any items you add by hand. It is a feature of a paid QuickBooks Online (or Intuit Enterprise Suite) subscription — it is not a standalone or free tool, and it is not available unless you are already a QuickBooks Online customer.

This calculator asks a narrower question with none of that setup: given the cash you have right now and what is coming in and out, when—if ever—does the balance go negative, and what single payment would fix it? No bank connection, no bookkeeping platform, no account. Five numbers and up to eight dated one-off rows, entirely in your browser.

Common questions

Should I use this calculator or QuickBooks Online's Cash Flow Planner?

It depends on what you already have. QuickBooks Online's Cash Flow Planner is a feature inside a paid QuickBooks Online (or Intuit Enterprise Suite) subscription: setting it up means connecting your bank and credit-card accounts so it can read your transaction history, and it is only available if you are already a QuickBooks Online customer. This calculator is a free standalone page: no account, no subscription, and no bank connection. You type your starting cash, weekly income, weekly costs, and any one-off payments directly, and the forecast runs in your browser. If your books already live in QuickBooks Online and your accounts are connected, its planner can lean on that transaction history. If you are not a QuickBooks customer, or just want a fast answer before committing numbers to any accounting software, this page answers the same question with five fields and no signup.

Can QuickBooks' Cash Flow Planner handle a one-off payment like a tax bill?

Yes, and so can this calculator — both let you add a dated one-time item separate from your recurring income and costs. QuickBooks' planner calls this an "event": you name it, give it an amount, mark it money in or money out, and pick the date. This calculator's one-off rows work the same way, up to eight of them. The difference is what happens after you enter one: this calculator also names which specific one-off payment to move (or which invoice to collect early) to close a projected shortfall, and separately flags when the gap is not a timing problem at all — when weekly costs simply exceed weekly income — so moving a payment would only postpone the same shortfall rather than fix it.

What forecast range does each tool cover?

QuickBooks Online's Cash Flow Planner supports a forecast range from 3 months out to 24 months, per Intuit's own help documentation. This calculator forecasts up to 52 weeks (about 12 months), with every week shown against its actual calendar date. Neither is strictly longer than the other across their full range — pick QuickBooks' planner for a multi-year view once you are on the platform, or this page for a shorter, week-by-week runway check with no setup.

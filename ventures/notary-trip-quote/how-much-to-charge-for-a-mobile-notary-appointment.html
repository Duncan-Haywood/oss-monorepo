How much should a mobile notary charge for a trip?

The statutory fee per signature is the part that is capped, published, and easy to look up — and on a mobile appointment it is usually the smallest line on the invoice. What actually decides whether the job was worth taking is the drive, the time the drive costs you, and whatever the appointment turns into once you are there. Quoting from the notarial fee alone prices the paperwork and gives the travel away.

Build the quote in parts

  1. Mileage: round-trip miles at your per-mile rate.
  2. Travel time and appointment time, both at your hourly rate.
  3. Statutory fees for the signatures involved, plus a fee per witness you supply.
  4. Waiting time at its own rate, and an after-hours surcharge where it applies.
  5. Compare the total with your appointment minimum, and quote whichever is higher.

Worked example

A 20-mile round trip at $0.70 per mile is $14.00 of mileage. Thirty minutes of driving and a 45-minute appointment at $60 per hour add $30.00 and $45.00. State fees of $15.00 and one witness at $25.00 bring the total to $129.00, comfortably above the $50.00 appointment minimum, so the minimum does not apply. Note the shape of it: $44.00 of that quote — more than the notarial and witness fees combined — is travel the capped fee does not cover. Drop the travel lines and the same appointment quotes at $85.00, and the drive is unpaid.

What varies by state

Statutory per-signature fees are set by your state and are the one line you cannot price yourself. Travel, waiting, and convenience charges are generally not capped the same way, but the rules genuinely differ: some states require the travel fee to be disclosed and agreed separately from the notarial fee, and a few regulate it. Check your own state's notary handbook before you publish a rate card.

Common questions

How much should a mobile notary charge for a trip?

Price it as separate lines rather than one improvised number: mileage at your per-mile rate, travel time at your hourly rate, the appointment itself at that same hourly rate, any statutory per-signature fees, witness fees per witness, waiting time at its own rate, and an after-hours surcharge if it applies. Add them, then compare the total with your appointment minimum and charge whichever is higher. Statutory notarial fees are capped by your state; travel, waiting, and convenience charges generally are not, but the rules differ by state.

Why do I lose money on short notary appointments?

Because the billable part of a mobile appointment is much shorter than the job. A 45-minute signing with a 30-minute round trip is 75 minutes of your day, and the capped per-signature fee covers none of the driving. Quoting from the notarial fee alone prices the paperwork and gives away the travel — which is why an appointment minimum exists: it sets a floor under jobs whose travel and setup cost more than their signatures.

Should I charge for travel time as well as mileage?

They pay for two different things, so most mobile notaries charge both. Mileage covers the vehicle — fuel, wear, and insurance — and is usually set per mile. Travel time covers the hour of your day the drive consumes, which mileage does not compensate at all: a 20-mile trip through city traffic costs far more of your time than 20 highway miles. Pricing them as separate lines also makes the quote easier to explain to a client who asks why a nearby signing costs less.

How should I quote waiting time at a signing?

Quote it in advance as its own rate, usually lower than your working hourly rate, and say up front when it starts — typically after a stated grace period past the appointment time. Waiting for a late signer, a missing witness, or documents that have not arrived is the most common way a booked appointment overruns, and a rate agreed beforehand turns an awkward conversation at the kitchen table into a line the client already accepted.

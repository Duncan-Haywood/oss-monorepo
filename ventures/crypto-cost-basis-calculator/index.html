How it is calculated

Lots: every buy is a lot with a quantity and a basis (USD total plus fee); every income row is a lot whose basis is the USD value you entered. Matching: sales are processed in date order, with same-day buys available to a same-day sale. Each sale consumes lots in the order the method dictates and is split into one row per lot used, with proceeds (USD total minus fee) and basis shared out by quantity. FIFO sorts by acquisition date, LIFO reverses it, HIFO and LOFO sort by cost per coin, and the tax-aware rule sorts by the tax each coin would trigger at the rates you entered, with the holding period of each lot deciding which rate applies. That rule is a greedy choice made one sale at a time: it is not guaranteed to be the best possible plan across every sale. Holding period: long-term only when the sale date is after the first anniversary of acquisition. Rounding: each row's proceeds and basis are rounded to cents and the gain is their difference, so the rows add up the way a form does.

Not modelled: prices (you supply every USD value), exchange or blockchain imports, DeFi, NFTs, hard forks, lost or stolen coins, gifts, wash-sale style rules, state tax and the effect of the loss deduction on your other tax; the 3.8% net investment income tax is estimated only if you enter your other income and is a simplified Form 8960. A sale larger than the lots on record is shown with zero basis and a warning rather than hidden. Unverified: tested on hand-calculated examples and invariant checks, not on a real return.

Common questions

What is crypto cost basis and why does it matter?

Cost basis is what you paid for a coin, including fees. When you sell, swap or spend it, your taxable gain or loss is the proceeds minus the basis of the specific coins you disposed of. If you bought the same coin at several prices, which purchase counts as the one you sold changes the gain, and the choice of method is the lever you control.

What is the difference between FIFO, LIFO and HIFO?

They are rules for deciding which purchase lots a sale uses up. FIFO sells your oldest coins first, LIFO your newest, and HIFO the coins you paid the most for, which usually produces the smallest gain today. LOFO takes the cheapest coins first. This tool runs all of them on your own history and shows the gain and an estimated tax side by side, so you can see what each choice would have done.

Can I choose any method I like?

Not necessarily. In the United States the default for property is first in, first out, and using another method generally depends on being able to identify the specific units you sold at the time of the sale and keeping records that show it. Whether a method is available to you, and how to document it, is a question for a tax professional; this tool computes what each method would produce, it does not decide what you are allowed to file.

Is my transaction history uploaded anywhere?

No. The matching runs in your browser from the CSV you paste or open from your own disk. Nothing is sent to a server, there are no accounts, and it works offline after the page loads. That is the reason to use it for a file that lists everything you have ever bought.

What columns does the CSV need?

A header row with date, type, asset, quantity and either a USD total or a price per coin. Fee and wallet are optional. Type is buy, sell, income, swap or transfer. Common exchange headers such as Timestamp, Transaction Type, Quantity Transacted, Subtotal and Fees are recognised. Enter a coin-for-coin swap as one swap row (asset and quantity given up, to asset and to quantity received, total = USD value that day), or as a sale and a buy.

How is the holding period decided?

A gain is long-term when the coins were held for more than one year, meaning the sale date falls after the first anniversary of the acquisition date; a sale on the anniversary itself is still short-term. Each piece of a sale carries the date of the lot it came from, so one sale can produce both short-term and long-term rows.

What does per-wallet matching do?

When it is on, a sale only uses lots recorded in the same wallet or account column, instead of one pool across everything you hold. Some tax rules now expect basis to be tracked wallet by wallet. Turn it on if your records tag each row with where the coins sat, and check the current IRS guidance for how to treat coins you moved between wallets. Add a transfer row with a to wallet column and the lots you move keep their original purchase date and basis in the new wallet, so a later sale there is not shown with zero basis. Network fees paid in coins are not treated as sales.

This calculator vs. a flat $1,495 well-pump special

“We install a well pump for $1,495” is not a price for a well — it is a price for one specific depth, pitless condition, and scope, quietly applied to every well that calls in.

What a flat well-pump special is

A flat well-pump price is the fastest way to quote: one number, easy to advertise and easy for a homeowner to compare between bids. It is the number a homeowner holding a flyer or a Facebook special will call and ask you to match, whatever their well actually needs.

The number behind it was set for one job — a shallow well, a pitless adapter that comes apart cleanly, no stuck pump, and the existing tank kept — and then reused for every well afterward, whether or not the next job looks anything like the one the special was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the well depth past the included footage, pump type and horsepower, whether the pitless adapter is free or seized, a stuck-pump pull, extra well cable, a pressure-tank replacement, and a constant-pressure drive, plus your own labor hours and material costs. It prices the depth, the pitless, and every extra separately by kind, then returns the walk-away price and a bid price above your target margin — for this well, not a generic one.

What a flat well-pump special doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same flat number whether the well is 60 feet or 280 feet, even though extra depth past the included footage does not bill itself. Prices extra drop-pipe footage separately at your own hours-per-foot rate, so a deep well costs more than a shallow one.
Has no line for the pitless adapter, regardless of whether it comes apart cleanly or is seized and has to be replaced. Prices pitless-replacement hours and fittings separately by condition, so a seized pitless costs more than a free one.
Has no line for a stuck pump, extra well cable, or a pressure-tank replacement, regardless of how many the job needs. Prices each extra separately — a stuck pull, cable past the included run, the tank, and a constant-pressure drive — so a fully loaded job costs more than a bare swap.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against a flat special, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

A flat special is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one well it was built for, and nothing about a flat number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat special skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat well-pump special get a bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is depth past the included footage, whether the pitless adapter comes apart or has to be replaced, a stuck pump, extra cable, a new tank, and a constant-pressure drive -- not "a well pump." A 280-foot 1.5 HP pull with a seized pitless, a stuck pump, a new tank, extra cable, and a VFD is doing several jobs a 60-foot 1/2 HP swap with a good pitless and the existing tank never touches, none of which a flat special's single number can see.

What does a flat well-pump special usually leave out entirely?

Footage past the price's included depth, whether the pitless is free or seized, a stuck-pump pull, extra well cable beyond what a shallow well needs, a pressure tank replacement, and a constant-pressure drive. A shallow well with an easy pitless and no tank swap is doing far less than a deep well needing all of the above -- and a flat number has no line for any of it.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped presets, at its own shipped shop rates: a 60-foot 1/2 HP submersible swap with a good pitless and the existing tank -- the job the $1,495 brochure special is built on -- prices at $1,176.19, with a $764.52 break-even cost. A 280-foot 1.5 HP pull with a seized pitless, a stuck pump, a new tank, extra cable, and a constant-pressure drive prices at $5,915.32, with a $3,844.96 break-even cost. Quoting that second job at the flat $1,495 special prices it $2,349.96 below its own break-even cost -- before a dollar of profit -- and $4,420.32 below the calculator's own bid price.

Is a flat well-pump special ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- shallow depth, a good pitless, no stuck pump, the existing tank kept -- a flat rate can land close, and on this calculator's own easy preset the flat special actually prices above the calculator's own bid. The risk is applying the same number outside that band without noticing, because the flat rate does not know which well it was priced for and neither does the estimator until depth, pitless condition, and extras are actually spelled out.

Does this calculator know what materials or labor cost in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates. It takes the well's depth, pump type and horsepower, pitless condition, stuck-pump pull, extra cable, tank, and constant-pressure drive, plus the labor hours and material costs you enter, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat special skips, not a market survey.

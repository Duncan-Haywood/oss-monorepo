This calculator vs. working out bonus depreciation and Section 179 by hand

The arithmetic looks simple — a cost times a percentage. The part that goes wrong by hand is the rules layered on top: the phase-out counts purchases the calculator in front of you doesn't show, and the two elections fail differently when a deduction is bigger than your income.

Common questions

What's the single most common mistake working this out by hand?

Forgetting that the Section 179 phase-out is based on ALL qualifying purchases for the year, not just the one asset being considered. A hand estimate often checks a single purchase against the $2,500,000 (2025) limit and stops there, missing that other equipment bought the same year counts toward the $4,000,000 phase-out threshold too.

What's the second most common mistake?

Treating an over-large deduction the same way under both elections. A bonus-depreciation deduction that exceeds taxable income creates or increases a net operating loss (NOL), which can offset up to 80% of a future year's income. A Section 179 election that exceeds income instead produces a SECTION 179 CARRYFORWARD -- a different mechanism, still capped by income every future year it's used, with no 80% ceiling but no better guarantee of use. Confusing the two overstates how much benefit either path locks in immediately.

What eligibility mistake gets made most often?

Applying bonus depreciation or Section 179 to an asset used 50% or less for business. Both elections require MORE than 50% business use; at or below that line, the property must use the slower, straight-line Alternative Depreciation System instead -- a rule easy to skip when the arithmetic focuses on the dollar amount rather than the use percentage.

What planning mistake gets made most often?

Assuming bonus depreciation is always the better choice because it is bigger. Section 179 is elected asset-by-asset up to the annual limit, while bonus depreciation is all-or-nothing for an entire property class. A business expecting a loss year sometimes prefers a smaller, income-capped Section 179 election -- or electing out of bonus depreciation entirely -- to avoid creating a large NOL and instead preserve depreciation for a future, higher-income year.

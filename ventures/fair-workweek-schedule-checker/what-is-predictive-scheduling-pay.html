What is predictive scheduling pay? A worked example

Predictive scheduling laws, also called fair workweek laws, make covered restaurant, retail and hospitality employers do three things: post schedules ahead of time, pay extra when they change a posted shift at short notice, and leave a minimum rest between a closing shift and the next opening. The extra pay is easy to miss because it never appears as a line on the schedule. It appears as a change.

The three triggers

Late posting. A schedule must go up a set number of days before the shift, 14 under the Seattle and Chicago presets. A shift that starts sooner than that after posting is flagged.

Short-notice change. Under the Seattle and Chicago presets, adding hours or moving a shift without cutting hours inside 14 days costs one hour of pay at the worker's regular rate. Cutting or cancelling a shift costs half the pay for the hours not worked: Seattle applies that inside 14 days, Chicago only inside 24 hours.

Rest. Ten hours between shifts. Scheduling less needs the worker's written consent and 1.25 times pay for the hours inside the window.

Common questions

What is a fair workweek or predictive scheduling law?

A city or state rule that makes larger retail, restaurant and hospitality employers post work schedules in advance, usually 14 days, and pay the worker extra when the employer then adds hours, moves a shift or cancels one at short notice. Seattle, Chicago, New York City, Philadelphia, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Oregon each have their own version with different thresholds and different premiums.

How do I check a schedule for predictive scheduling violations?

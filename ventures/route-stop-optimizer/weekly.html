How it works, and how it was checked

Problem: this is the periodic vehicle routing problem. Each customer needs a number of visits a week on one of its allowed patterns (spread out: two visits a week are Mon+Thu, Mon+Fri or Tue+Fri on a five-day week), each working day has your drivers' routes from one yard, and a route longer than the shift is penalised so heavily that the planner only runs over when nothing fits. Objective: least weekly driving time, plus your per-move charge for each customer taken off its current days. Search: ruin and recreate with simulated annealing: remove a customer and its nearest neighbours (or a whole route) from every day they are on, put each back on the pattern whose days' cheapest insertions sum lowest, tidy the touched routes with 2-opt, and keep the change by the annealing rule. Early on the overtime charge is low, so the search can pass through an over-full day on the way to a better week; it ends at full strength.

Checked: on 85 small weeks (six customers, one or two drivers, a tight shift, some twice-a-week customers) the planner matched the optimum found by trying every pattern, driver split and visit order. On invented books of 40, 80 and 150 customers (5 books each, a quarter twice a week, an 8-hour shift), it cut weekly drive time by 43%, 40% and 35% against days dealt out at random, as books grow, and by 26%, 21% and 12% against one pie slice of the map per day, with no overtime where the baselines ran up to 4 hours a week over. Five seeds on the same book land within 2 to 4% of each other. Method and table.

Not modelled: the real road network, traffic, time windows inside the day, a customer who must keep the same driver, every-other-week customers, and turn-by-turn directions. Unverified: not yet measured against a published periodic-routing benchmark or a real company's route book.

Common questions

What does re-zoning a route book mean?

Choosing which day of the week each recurring customer is served so that each day's customers sit close together. A book that grew one sign-up at a time puts neighbours on different days, so a driver crosses town every day. Re-zoning puts them on the same day.

How much can changing service days save?

On invented books of 40 to 150 customers with service days dealt out at random, as books grow, the planner cut weekly drive time by 35 to 43 percent; against cutting the map into one pie slice per day it cut 11 to 26 percent. Your saving depends on how scattered your days are today; paste your current days to see it on your own list.

Can customers who need two visits a week keep a sensible gap?

Yes. Give the visits a week and the planner only uses spread patterns: two visits a week are Monday and Thursday, Monday and Friday, or Tuesday and Friday on a five-day week; three are Monday, Wednesday and Friday. You can also limit any customer to certain days.

Will it move every customer to a new day?

No. Moving a customer costs a phone call and some goodwill, so the planner charges a set number of driving minutes per moved customer (10 by default) and only moves one when the saving is larger. Raise it to move fewer customers; set it to 0 to see the best possible week.

Does it respect a driver's shift length?

Yes. Each route's driving plus service time is kept within the shift you set. If the customers cannot fit, the plan says how many hours over the week runs so you can add a driver, a working day or shorter visits.

Is my customer list uploaded?

No. The list is read and planned in this browser tab. There are no accounts, cookies or servers involved, and nothing is stored.

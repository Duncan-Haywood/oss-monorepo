What this does and doesn't do

Both calculators apply the federal FLSA overtime formulas to the numbers you enter. Neither one knows your state's daily-overtime rule, a double-time threshold, your nondiscretionary bonus structure, or your specific pay policy — those can all change the regular rate or the threshold under FLSA and state law. Treat the output as the arithmetic behind your own payroll record or pay question, not a legal determination of what is owed.

Common questions

How do you calculate overtime pay with one hourly rate?

Pay the hours up to the overtime threshold (40 hours a week under federal FLSA rules) at the regular rate, and pay the hours past the threshold at the overtime rate — the regular rate times the overtime multiplier, 1.5x by default ("time and a half").

How do you calculate overtime when an employee worked at two different pay rates in the same week?

Under the federal FLSA weighted-average method (29 CFR 778.115), the regular rate for that week is total straight-time earnings divided by total hours worked — not either rate on its own, and not a simple average of the two rates. The overtime premium is half that blended rate for each hour worked past the threshold, because straight-time pay for every hour, including the overtime hours, is already counted once in the straight-time total.

Why is the overtime premium only half the blended rate, not 1.5 times it?

Because straight-time pay for every hour worked — including the hours past the overtime threshold — is already included once in the total straight-time pay. Adding the full 1.5x rate for those hours would double-count the first 1x. Adding only the extra 0.5x (the "half-time premium") brings each overtime hour up to time-and-a-half in total.

Does this apply in every state?

This calculator applies the federal FLSA rules: a 40-hour weekly threshold and a 1.5x multiplier by default. Several states require more — daily overtime past a set number of hours in a single day, a double-time threshold, or a higher minimum multiplier — and nondiscretionary bonuses or shift differentials can also change the regular rate. Check your state's rules and your specific pay policy before relying on the total.

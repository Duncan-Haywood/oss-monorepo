This calculator vs. a spreadsheet

Most payroll spreadsheets already have an overtime column: hours over 40, times the rate, times 1.5. That formula is correct for one thing — an employee paid a single hourly rate all week. It quietly stops being correct the moment someone works at two or more different pay rates in the same workweek, which is exactly the case a plain =IF(hours>40,...) formula was never built to handle.

What each one is for

A spreadsheet handles standard single-rate overtime well: one formula, one row, done. What it does not do on its own is the federal FLSA weighted-average (blended rate) method required when someone worked at two or more different hourly rates in the same week — the regular rate used for that week's overtime premium is total straight-time pay divided by total hours, not either rate alone and not a simple average of the two. Encoding that correctly, and only that, takes a formula most sheets don't have and most people don't build until a payroll question forces the issue.

This calculator runs both cases: standard single-rate overtime, and the FLSA blended-rate calculation for any number of pay rates in one week, with the regular-rate arithmetic done the same way every time and a plain-text breakdown you can paste into a payroll record or a pay question.

Common questions

What does a typical payroll spreadsheet get right about overtime?

Standard single-rate overtime: hours up to the threshold at the regular rate, hours past it at 1.5x. That is one formula per row and a spreadsheet handles it fine.

Where does a spreadsheet formula usually go wrong on overtime?

The moment someone works at two or more pay rates in the same week. The correct FLSA regular rate is total straight-time pay for the week divided by total hours worked — not either rate on its own, and not a simple average of the two rates. A hand-built formula that multiplies each rate by 1.5x separately, or averages the two rates before applying 1.5x, produces the wrong overtime premium without erroring.

Why is the overtime premium half the blended rate rather than 1.5 times it?

Straight-time pay for every hour worked, including the hours past the threshold, is already counted once in the straight-time total used to compute the blended rate. Adding the full 1.5x rate for those hours again would double-count the first 1x. Adding only the extra 0.5x half-time premium brings each overtime hour up to time-and-a-half in total — a step a spreadsheet formula has to encode correctly by hand, with no built-in check that it did.

Does getting this formula wrong matter in practice?

Yes. The federal weighted-average method is set out in 29 CFR 778.115, and an employer who instead pays overtime on only one of the two rates, or on a simple average, has underpaid the required overtime premium under the FLSA — a wage-and-hour exposure that grows every pay period the formula stays wrong.

Is this calculator a substitute for reviewing your own state's rules?

No, and neither is a spreadsheet. This tool applies the federal FLSA threshold and multiplier you configure to the numbers you enter. Several states require more — daily overtime, a double-time threshold, or a higher minimum multiplier — and this calculator does not know which apply to you.

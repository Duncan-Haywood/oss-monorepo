This calculator vs. a flat rate per acre

“$800 an acre, mowed weekly” is not a price for a lawn-care stop — it is a price for one lot's worth of beds, edging, and drive time, quietly applied to every lot after it.

What a flat rate per acre is

A flat per-acre rate is the fastest way to quote a recurring mowing stop: measure the lot, multiply by a number. Customers shop it because it is easy to compare between crews, and it is the figure a homeowner holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The rate behind it was set for one kind of lot — usually open turf, no beds to weed, no edge-and-blow, and a short hop from the stop before it — and then reused for every lot afterward, whether or not the next one carries beds, a full edge, or sits a long drive away.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter each service's quantity and production rate — turf, edging, beds, hedges, leaf cleanup — your visits per week, service weeks per year, round-trip travel, and your own wage, payroll burden, fuel, dump, equipment, overhead, and target margin. It prices the mowing hours from a turf-specific rate, adds beds, edging, and travel as their own buckets, and returns the price per visit, the in-season monthly, and the walk-away price — for this lot, not a flat rate for every acre.

Two 10,000 sq ft, 0.23-acre stops, one flat rate

Using this calculator's own shipped starting example:

A 10,000 sq ft, 0.23-acre lawn — open turf, no beds, no edging, and a short hop between stops — prices at $184.30 a month on a stop that takes 0.5 hours. Setting a flat rate per acre from that easy stop works out to $802.82 an acre a month.

Take the identical 10,000 sq ft, 0.23 acres of turf and put it on a harder lot instead — 350 sq ft of beds needing weeding, an edge-and-blow, and an 18-minute one-way drive — and the stop takes 2.4 hours, 4.8 times the easy stop's time on the identical turf acreage. Apply the $802.82-an-acre rate to this lot and it quotes $184.30 a month again, because the rate only knows acreage. This calculator prices the harder stop at $459.73 a month — the flat rate lands $275.43 a month short of that price, and $160.49 a month short of covering the stop's own break-even cost of $344.80. Same acreage, same flat rate, one lot that pays and one that loses money the moment the beds, the edging, and the drive show up.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per acre get a lawn-care stop wrong?

Because the cost driver is the beds, the edging, and the drive, not the turf acreage. A 10,000 sq ft, 0.23-acre lawn that is open turf with no beds and a short hop between stops is a 0.5-hour mow. The identical 10,000 sq ft, 0.23-acre lot with 350 sq ft of beds needing weeding, an edge-and-blow, and an 18-minute one-way drive is a 2.4-hour stop -- 4.8 times the time, on the identical turf acreage. A flat per-acre rate charges both the same because it was set on acreage, not on beds, edging, or the drive.

What does a flat per-acre rate usually leave out entirely?

Bed weeding, edging and blowing, and the drive. A per-acre number has no line for square feet of beds at their own slower production rate, no line for the minutes an edge-and-blow takes, and no line for round-trip travel at all -- it prices the turf and assumes the rest of the stop away. On this calculator's own shipped starting example, that hidden work -- edging, beds, and the drive together -- is 79.1% of the total time on the job, on top of the mow itself.

Is a flat rate per acre ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of similar lots -- open turf, no beds to weed, no edge-and-blow, and a short hop from the stop before it -- a flat per-acre number can land close, because that is probably the lot the rate was set on. The risk is quoting a lot with beds, a full edge-and-blow, or a long drive the same way sight unseen, because the rate does not know the beds, the edging, or the drive until someone has walked the property, and by then the flat number has often already been quoted.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or fuel costs?

No. It has no data on your local labor market or your fuel and equipment costs. It takes each service's quantity and production rate (turf, edging, beds, hedges, leaf cleanup), your visits per week, service weeks per year, round-trip travel, and your own wage, payroll burden, fuel, dump, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this stop -- the arithmetic a flat per-acre rate skips, not a market survey.

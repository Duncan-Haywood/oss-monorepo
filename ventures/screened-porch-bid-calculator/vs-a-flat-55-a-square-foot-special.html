This calculator vs. a flat $55-a-square-foot special

"$55 a square foot installed" is a price for one porch — a screen-in under an existing roof, no knee wall, one door — quoted at a porch whose square footage can look like any other porch's while the knee wall, roof, and ceiling underneath it are entirely different.

What a flat per-square-foot special is

A flat special is the fastest way to quote: one headline rate, read off a yard sign, a Facebook post, or a competitor's postcard and repeated for every porch afterward. Homeowners shop it because a single number is the easiest thing to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner will ask you to match at a porch that took the crew a fraction of the time — or several times longer.

The rate behind it was usually set for one porch — a screen-in under an existing roof, no knee wall, standard fiberglass mesh — and then reused for every job afterward keyed only to floor square footage, whether or not the next porch has a knee wall to frame, a new roof to build, or a ceiling to finish.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the porch's width, depth, attachment, wall height, knee wall, roof, ceiling, mesh, panel width, doors, and extras, plus your loaded labor and overhead. It turns that into whole screen panels along the perimeter, the knee-wall, roof, and ceiling hours a flat rate never separates out, and a walk-away price for this porch, not a generic one.

Common questions

Why does a flat $55-a-square-foot special get the price wrong?

Because it charges every screened porch the same rate per square foot, and that rate cannot tell a screen-in under an existing roof from an enclosure with a knee wall, a new roof, and a ceiling. This calculator's own numbers for a 10×12 screen-in with no knee wall and a 12×16 enclosure with a 36-inch knee wall, a new framed roof, and a beadboard ceiling come out to $25.77 and $67.47 a square foot — about 2.6x apart — even though both are the same kind of job, a screened porch.

What does a flat per-square-foot rate leave out?

The knee wall, the roof, and the ceiling. A 10×12 screen-in under an existing roof with no knee wall needs none of those — this calculator prices the walls only. A 12×16 enclosure with a 36-inch knee wall, a new framed roof, and a beadboard ceiling adds 14.0 hours and $1,120 of knee wall, 6.53 hours and $2,304 of roof, and 4.61 hours and $1,440 of ceiling — 25.1 hours and $4,864 that a per-square-foot number for “screened porch” never separates out.

Why does this calculator count screen panels instead of square feet of mesh?

Because a screened porch is built from framed panels at a fixed width, not a continuous roll charged by the square foot — a shop buys and hangs whole panels, not a fraction of one. This calculator counts panels from the perimeter and panel width: 11 panels for the 32-foot perimeter of the 10×12 screen-in, and 14 panels for the 40-foot perimeter of the 12×16 enclosure, so the bid reflects what the crew actually installs.

What happens to margin when the $55 special is quoted on the harder job?

It gets thin. A flat rate special is not priced from cost — it is a headline number set for the easy job and repeated for every job afterward. Quoting the $55-a-square-foot special on the 12×16 enclosure prices at $10,560.00, which clears only 8.0% margin against this calculator's $9,715.20 cost — a fraction of the 25% margin the calculator solves for when it prices that same job at $12,953.61.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local screened-porch prices, labor rates, or what a competitor's coupon charges. It takes the porch's width, depth, wall height, knee wall, roof, ceiling, mesh, panels, doors, and extras, plus your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price for the job you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-square-foot special is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the next porch stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

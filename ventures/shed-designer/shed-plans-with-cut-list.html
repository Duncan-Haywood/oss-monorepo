How much lumber does an 8x12 shed take?

If you are building your own backyard shed, the usual answers are a rule of thumb ("about one stud per foot of wall") or a plan set that lists boards but not how to cut them. Neither tells you that the jack studs beside a 38" door are 80-1/2" long, or that a 6/12 rafter for an 8 ft wide shed with a 12" overhang needs a board at least 69" long once the plumb cut is allowed for.

Open Shed Designer and drag out your shed: it frames every board at its real size and hands you the plans, a cut sheet and the order.

The worked example: 8x12 gable shed

The tool's "8x12 classic" preset (pick it under + New design), which you can change in a few drags: 12 ft along the ridge, 8 ft across the gable, 8 ft walls (91-1/2" studs under three 1-1/2" plates), a 6/12 roof with a 12" overhang, a single 38" x 82" door on one 12 ft side, two 24" x 36" windows on the other 12 ft side, and a 12" x 12" vent in each gable. 2x4 walls at 16" on centre, a pressure-treated 2x6 floor at 16" on three 4x6 skids over a gravel pad, and 2x6 rafters at 24" with 2x4 rafter ties.

A lower shed: the 6'6" door and the 8 ft peak

The tool opens on a different 8x12: a 78" (6'6") door, a ridge beam instead of ties, and a wish to keep the peak under 8 ft so the siding does not look tall. It sets the walls as low as the door allows: 78" + a 5-1/2" 2x6 header + three 1-1/2" plates = 86-1/2". Then it looks for the steepest roof under the limit, and here it finds none. Even a 3/12 roof would put the peak about 9'5-1/2" above the gravel, so it says so and picks 5/12, the lowest pitch at which the gable vents still fit beside the beam post (peak about 10'1"). Its order: 136 pieces from 51 boards, $552.30 of lumber against a $538.28 lower bound, $2,001.60 in all at the example prices, pink paint and a quart of purple for the polka dots included.

Why a cut list beats a board count

With 16 ft 2x4s two 91-1/2" studs come out of one board with about 8-3/4" left; with 8 ft boards each stud wastes 4-1/2". Whether that is cheaper depends on what your yard charges per length, so the tool tries every stock length you tick, packs the pieces longest first into the tightest board, shrinks each board to the cheapest length that still holds its cuts, and keeps the cheapest plan. The cut sheet then lists each board with its marks (A, B, C...) so you can cut at the saw and find each piece on the wall drawings.

When to use something else

The tool frames simple gable sheds on skids: no lofts, hip or shed (single-slope) roofs, ramps, porches or concrete slabs, and it does not size anything for snow, wind or code. If your shed needs a permit, your building department or a designer who can stamp drawings is the right next stop. A shed kit or a prebuilt shed is the faster answer if you would rather not cut at all.

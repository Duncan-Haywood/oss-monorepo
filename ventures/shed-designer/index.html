Shed Designer

Drag out a gable shed and get what you need to build it: framing plans for the foundation, floor, all four walls and the roof, a 3D view, a producer cut sheet that says which 8, 10, 12, 14 or 16 ft board each piece comes out of, and a shopping list with a cost. Every 2x4 to 2x12 is framed at its real size (a 2x4 is 1-1/2" x 3-1/2"). Make a few designs and compare them side by side.

Start from a ready-made design

Ten sheds under 100 sq ft (six in painted lap siding and asphalt shingles, to match a house), each picked from 2,400 designs this tool's search built and checked: every member at 90% of its capacity or less, a peak of 13 ft or less, nothing the model warns about, and the best on the measure its card names. Prices are worked out now at the prices below. Flick through with the arrows or a swipe; open one to see it from every side, what it costs and how much it holds; add two or three to Compare.         Taking these to a building department or an HOA? "HOA packet (PDF)" gives coloured, dimensioned elevations, the finishes and the site plan on four pages; the plan set has a cover sheet with materials and colours, a site plan (type your lot under "Site plan" above), plans, elevations with overall height, a section and details. It is not engineered or code-checked, and it is not "permit-ready": what a submission needs is set locally. Many places need no building permit for a small shed (the IRC, R105.2, exempts a one-storey detached accessory building up to 200 sq ft; the IBC sets 120 sq ft; many towns use less), but zoning setbacks and HOA rules can still apply. Check with yours.

        Options made for you

        Close variations of the design you are editing, each framed, priced and checked. They keep your size, doors, windows, colours and wall framing, and change one thing a builder would weigh. Pick one to add it to your designs.

        Bring the price down, same size

        Counts your time at the hourly rate you set (in the buying settings) as well as materials. Tries cheaper off-the-shelf parts and settings one at a time (stock doors and windows, siding, roofing, the foundation and its gravel, the subfloor, overhang, pitch and spacing), keeps your footprint, the ridge and rafter-seat choices you made and anything you fixed under "Your limits and fixed choices", and keeps a change only if the shed still passes every check and the peak rises by no more than 6". It lists each saving so you can take some and leave others.

        What each foundation, floor, door and window choice costs

        Every alternative for the parts under and around the shed (blocks or a gravel pad, the floor joists and subfloor, a door built on site at 36" to 72" or a stock door, the windows or none), each framed, priced and checked against the design you are editing. Shows what it saves or adds in materials and your time, whether the peak moves, and what it means on site. "Use this" changes your design; Undo takes it back.

        Simplify the build

        Searches small changes (roof pitch, overhang, rafter and stud spacing, connectors or notches, ridge type) for the cheapest build, the one with the fewest saw cuts, and a balance of the two. It counts every saw cut and every angled cut and notch, and keeps only designs that pass every check.

        Questions

        Does it use the real size of a 2x4?

        Yes. Every board is framed at its dressed size: a 2x4 is 1-1/2 by 3-1/2 inches, a 2x6 is 1-1/2 by 5-1/2, a 2x8 is 1-1/2 by 7-1/4, a 2x10 is 1-1/2 by 9-1/4 and a 2x12 is 1-1/2 by 11-1/4. Stud lengths, plate stack heights, rafter plumb cuts and the parts list all use those numbers.

        How does it decide which board lengths to buy?

        Starting prices are Home Depot listings seen on 2026-10-29, each linked. It treats the order as a cutting-stock problem: every piece is placed into 8, 10, 12, 14 or 16 ft boards (18 and 20 ft if you tick them) to minimise cost at the prices you type, with your saw kerf between cuts. It also proves a lower bound, by solving the problem's linear relaxation, and prints it beside the result, so you can see how close the order is to the cheapest possible; often it is exactly the cheapest.

        Is the design checked for strength or against building code?

        Not by an engineer. The Structure tab runs allowable-stress (NDS) checks on the floor joists, rafters, ridge beam and posts, headers and wall studs under the snow and wind loads you set, the kind of check a span table encodes. It also checks the skids between blocks and the blocks' pressure on the soil, the rafter ties against wind uplift, the walls against racking, and that every piece bears on what is under it; the tests check that no piece floats or overlaps another. It works out whether the wind would tip the shed and what anchors would hold it, but does not check hold-downs, the anchors themselves, or your local code. Many places need a permit for a shed over a certain size; ask your building department.

        Can I open the plans in CAD?

        Yes. Each sheet downloads as DXF (full size, in inches, with layers for framing, outlines, hidden lines, openings, dimensions and text) or SVG, and the whole set prints from the browser.

        Is my design uploaded anywhere?

        No. Everything runs in your browser. Your designs are saved in this browser's local storage so they are there next time (you can turn that off), and a shared link carries the design inside the link itself.

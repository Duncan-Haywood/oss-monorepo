Conditional vs unconditional lien waivers

A lien waiver (waiver and release) is a document in which you give up your mechanics lien rights, and often your payment bond and stop-notice rights, for a payment. GCs, owners and lenders ask for one with every pay application. Signed at the right moment it is routine paperwork. Signed at the wrong moment it gives away your strongest collection tool for money you have not received.

The four kinds

Progress (a payment partway through)Final (the last payment)
ConditionalWaives rights for work through a date, only once the stated payment is received and clears. Sign it with the pay app.Waives all rights on the job, only once the final payment clears. Sign it with the final pay app.
UnconditionalWaives rights for work through a date, whether or not you were paid. Sign it only after the money is in your account.Waives all rights on the job, whether or not you were paid. Sign only once the final payment, retainage included, has cleared.

The one rule: never sign an unconditional waiver for money you have not received. A common, safe routine is: with this month's pay app, a conditional progress waiver for this month's amount and an unconditional progress waiver for last month's payment, which has now cleared.

Read the details before you sign

  • Through date and amount: a progress waiver should cover work through a stated date and a stated amount, not "all work to date".
  • Exceptions: list what you are not waiving: retainage, disputed or unapproved change orders, extra work billed but unpaid, and earlier unpaid progress payments. The statutory forms in California and Florida have this built in.
  • Extra language: GC-drafted waivers sometimes add releases of all claims, indemnities or warranties. In states with statutory forms, that may not be enforceable or allowed; elsewhere it may be. Strike it or ask.
  • Joint checks: if your supplier is paid by joint check, its waiver and yours should match the amounts.

States that fix the wording by statute

Read on the state legislature's published statutes on 2026-11-24. A minority of states prescribe waiver forms; the rest leave the wording to the parties. Check your state's current text before relying on this.

  • California: four statutory forms in the Civil Code: conditional progress (§8132), unconditional progress (§8134), conditional final (§8136) and unconditional final (§8138). The progress forms carve out retentions and unpaid extras. The statute makes a waiver unenforceable unless it is substantially in the statutory form.
  • Florida: §713.20 sets out progress and final waiver forms. Lien rights cannot be waived in advance; a person asking for a waiver cannot require a different form; and a lienor may condition a waiver on its check clearing.
  • Other states also prescribe forms (Texas, for one, in Property Code chapter 53). We did not confirm their current text for this page, so look up the statute for the state where the project is.

Progress billing for contractors: draw schedules, deposits and retainage

Progress billing means billing a job in parts as the work gets done, not in one invoice at the end. Most small contractors do it one of two ways: a draw schedule (a share of the price at each stage) or percent complete on each line of a schedule of values, which is what a monthly pay application is. This page compares the methods, shows how a deposit and retainage come off each invoice, and works out the next invoice on a draw schedule.

Time and materials work bills what was used, not a share of a price; the T&M ticket log covers it. Whatever the method, the contract should say it: the stages or the schedule of values, when an invoice may be sent, how many days the customer has to pay, and what happens to the deposit and any retainage.

Setting up a draw schedule

  • Match the money to the cost. A stage where you buy cabinets and countertops should carry more of the price than one that is mostly labor, so you are not paying the supplier out of your own pocket.
  • Make each stage checkable. "Rough plumbing and electrical passed inspection" ends arguments; "rough-in 50% done" starts them.
  • Make the shares add to 100% (or the dollar amounts to the contract sum). A schedule that adds to 95% leaves 5% you never bill; the calculator below names the gap.
  • Hold enough for the end. A final draw of 10% or so at the walkthrough keeps the punch list worth finishing for you and gives the customer a reason to sign off.
  • Change orders get their own draw or are added to a stage in writing when approved, so the schedule still adds to the new contract sum. See change orders and the SOV.

The deposit and retainage on each invoice

A deposit is paid before the work it covers, so it is credited back on the invoices as that work is billed. The two common ways: spread it over every stage in proportion to its value (a 10% deposit takes 10% off each draw), or take it all off the first invoice. Spreading keeps every invoice in proportion; the first-invoice way clears it early but can leave the first draw at or near zero. By the final invoice the credits should add to the deposit. How large a deposit may be is set by your contract and, on home-improvement jobs, sometimes by state law, so check the rule where the job is.

Retainage is a share of each invoice the customer holds back until the end. It is held on the work billed, not on the deposit, and is billed again at completion. Draw schedules on homeowner jobs often have none; commercial subcontracts usually do. Retainage explained covers state caps on public work.

Progress invoicing in QuickBooks Online

  
QuickBooks Online bills in parts from an estimate. Intuit's help article (Set up and send progress invoices, read 2026-11-27 through search results, since Intuit's site could not be loaded directly) gives these steps:

  
    
    
  1. Settings, Account and settings, Sales: in Progress Invoicing, turn on "Create multiple partial invoices from a single estimate" and save.
    2. 
    
  2. Create the estimate for the whole contract, one line per stage or per schedule-of-values line, and mark it accepted.
    3. 
    
  3. Each time you bill, convert the estimate to an invoice and choose how much: a percent of every line, an amount, or a figure per line. The estimate keeps the rest open for the next invoice.
    4. 
    
  4. The Estimates & Progress Invoicing Summary by Customer report lists each estimate and the invoices made from it.
    5. 
  

  
Two gaps contractors hit: QuickBooks has no retainage field (the retainage in QuickBooks guide gives the account, item and negative line), and a deposit taken as a payment before any invoice needs to be applied to the invoices by hand. Intuit's community answers also say progress invoices depend on which invoice layout the company uses; if the option is missing, check that first.

Common questions

  
What is progress billing in construction?

  
Billing a job in parts as the work gets done, instead of one invoice at the end. The parts are set either by stages (a draw schedule: so much at rough-in, so much at drywall) or by how far along each line of a schedule of values is (percent complete), which is how a monthly pay application works.

  
How is a deposit handled on progress invoices?

  
A deposit is money for work not done yet, so it is credited back against the invoices as the work is billed. The common ways are to spread it over every stage in proportion to its value, or to take it all off the first invoice. Either way, the credits should add up to the deposit by the final invoice.

  
Is progress billing the same as a pay application?

  
A pay application is one form of progress billing: the percent-complete method written out line by line on a schedule of values, with retainage and the previous certificates, usually monthly. Homeowner and small commercial jobs often use a simpler draw schedule instead.

  
Does QuickBooks Online do progress billing?

  
Yes, from an accepted estimate, once progress invoicing is switched on in the Sales settings: each invoice bills a percent or an amount of each line, and the estimate keeps track of what is left. It has no retainage field, so retainage needs its own account, item and negative invoice line.

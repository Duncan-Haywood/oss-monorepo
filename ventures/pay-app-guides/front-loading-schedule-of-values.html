Front-loading a schedule of values: what reviewers see, and a check

Almost every subcontractor is tempted to put a little extra on mobilization and the first lines of the schedule of values (SOV), so the early pay apps bring in cash before the job has cost much. Some of that is normal. Too much gets the SOV sent back, and it leaves the last lines of the job worth less than they cost you to finish. Paste your SOV with your estimated cost per line and see it the way a careful reviewer does.

What front-loading is

Your SOV splits the contract sum into lines, and each pay app bills a percentage of each line. If every line carries the same markup on your cost, billing tracks cost through the job: a balanced SOV. Front-loading moves markup from late lines to early ones:

job markup        = contract sum / total estimated cost - 1
balanced value    = line cost x contract sum / total estimated cost
loading           = scheduled value - balanced value   (above 0: value pulled onto this line)

A worked example: a $115,000 subcontract on $100,000 of estimated cost, a 15% markup. The sub has moved $10,000 from punch list to mobilization.

The first pay app collects $10,000 more than the work has earned at the job's markup, and the punch list, done last with retainage held, now pays $1,500 for $10,000 of work. The check below finds both lines and writes the balanced values.

A mobilization line at three times its cost, with testing, closeout and punch list priced below cost, is the classic pattern. The contract sum is the same; only the timing of the cash moves.

Why it gets caught, and what it costs you

  • Reviewers look for it first. The GC's project manager, the architect or the owner's lender checks the SOV before the first pay app. Mobilization, bonds, submittals and general conditions are the lines they read first, and many contracts cap mobilization as a share of the contract. A rejected SOV delays the first payment, which is the opposite of what front-loading was for.
  • It shows up as overbilling. Billing ahead of cost is overbilling on your work-in-progress schedule; banks and sureties read it there. See overbilling and underbilling.
  • The end of the job runs short. If testing, closeout and punch list are priced below cost, the work left late in the job is worth less than it costs to do. If the job is terminated, the GC goes under, or the final pay app is held over a dispute, the cash you pulled forward is all you get, and the last weeks of work are at a loss.
  • Change orders get priced off it. Some GCs price deductive changes from SOV line values. A loaded line deducted is more money back to the GC than the work cost you.

None of this makes some front-loading wrong. Mobilization and submittals do cost money early, and bonds and insurance are paid up front. The question is whether each line's value can be defended from its cost. The check below shows which ones can't.

A defensible SOV

  1. Price from your estimate, not from cash needs. Start from each line's cost and apply the job's markup: the balanced SOV the tool writes.
  2. Put real costs on early lines. Bond premiums, permit fees, insurance and engineering are real early costs; put them in the cost column and the line's value is no longer "loading".
  3. Keep closeout lines at cost or above. Testing, as-builts, manuals, training and punch list are the work you finish with retainage held. Pricing them at least at cost keeps you paid for it.
  4. If you front-load, keep it inside a tolerance you can explain. The tool's "within tolerance" SOV is the smallest change that keeps every line within the band you choose of the job markup.
  5. Read the contract. The schedule of values and payment articles say who approves the SOV and whether mobilization is capped. Enter any cap below.

Front-loading check

One row per SOV line, with your estimated cost. Add Start and Finish month numbers (1 is the first month of the job) to see the cash effect. Paste from a spreadsheet (comma or tab separated) or open a CSV. Nothing leaves this browser.

Columns: Scheduled value and Cost (your estimated cost for the line: labour, material, equipment and lower-tier subs) are required. Item, Description, Start and Finish are optional. Without months, the order of the lines stands in for timing, and descriptions like testing, closeout, punch list, as-builts or training count as late work.

Balanced: every line at the job's markup. Within tolerance: the smallest cost-weighted change to your values that brings every line inside the band, keeping the contract sum. Both are rounded as you chose and add up to your contract sum exactly; a line too small to hold a whole rounding unit inside the band keeps the nearest one. The cash view spreads each line's work and cost evenly over its months and assumes each pay app is paid in the month billed, less retainage; it shows the shape of the job's cash, not your bank balance.

Common questions

  
What is a front-loaded schedule of values?

  
One where the lines billed early in the job (mobilization, submittals, early rough-in) carry more than their share of the contract's markup, and the lines billed late (testing, closeout, punch list) carry less. The sub collects cash ahead of cost; the owner pays for work before it is done.

  
Is front-loading a schedule of values allowed?

  
Contracts rarely ban it by name, but most let the GC, architect or owner's reviewer reject a schedule of values they think is unbalanced, and many cap mobilization. A reviewer who finds front-loading can send the SOV back, rebalance it, or hold the early pay apps. Read your contract's schedule of values and payment articles.

  
How do I check whether my SOV is front-loaded?

  
Put your estimated cost next to each line's scheduled value. If every line carries about the same markup on cost as the whole job, the SOV is balanced. Lines well above the job's markup that bill early, and lines below it (or below cost) that bill late, are the front-loading.

  
What is a balanced schedule of values?

  
One where each line's scheduled value is its cost times the same factor, the contract sum divided by total cost, so every line carries the same markup and billing tracks cost as the work goes.

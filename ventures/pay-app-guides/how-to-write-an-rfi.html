How to write an RFI as a subcontractor, with a free RFI drafter

When the drawings and specs conflict, leave something out or show something that cannot be built, you send the GC a request for information (RFI). A clear one comes back answered in days. A vague one comes back with a question, or not at all, while the crew waits. And when the answer adds work, the RFI is the first page of the change order you will need to get paid for it. This guide covers what goes in an RFI, why they get sent back, and how to keep the log; the drafter below builds one and checks it in your browser.

What a good RFI has in it

PartWhy the reviewer needs it
Number and date sentEvery RFI on the job gets the next number, and the date it went out starts the clock. Both are how you, the GC and the design team refer to it later, on the log and on any change order.
SubjectA few words naming the place and the problem ("Ceiling height conflict at corridor 104"). Logs and GC portals list RFIs by it.
Drawing, detail and spec sectionThe sheet and detail number, and the spec section, where the problem is. The architect or engineer starts there; without it they have to find it first.
One questionWhat the documents show, what conflicts with it or is missing, and exactly what you need to know. One question per RFI, so each is answered and closed on its own.
Proposed answerWhat you would do, if you know. "Proceed as proposed" is the fastest answer a design team can give, and it shows you have thought about the fix.
Cost or time impactNone, possible, or yes. Saying so up front is an early written record that the answer may change your price or schedule, which matters when you later ask for a change order.
Answer needed by, and whyA date tied to the work that is waiting on the answer ("rough-in starts March 11"). A date with a reason gets priority; a date without one reads as a wish.
AttachmentsA marked-up sheet, a photo of the field condition, a product data page. List them on the RFI so the GC can tell whether they all came through.

The GC may have its own RFI form or portal (AIA publishes one, G716, and Procore and similar tools have an RFI screen). The parts are the same; the drafter's text pastes into any of them.

It asks one question, proposes a lower ceiling and a shallower fixture from the schedule, flags a possible cost impact and asks for an answer before rough-in. With an impact of "possible" or "yes", the text adds a line that the RFI does not authorize extra work.

Why RFIs get sent back

  • The answer is already in the documents. Read the specs, the general notes and the other trades' sheets first. An RFI the reviewer answers with "see spec section 26 05 19" costs you a week and some credibility.
  • No location. "What fixture goes in the corridor?" without the sheet, detail and room number gets a question back.
  • Several questions at once. The easy ones wait on the hard one, and a combined answer is harder to point to on a change order.
  • It is really a substitution or a change. Asking to use a different product is usually a substitution request under the spec's own procedure, and asking for more money is a change order request. An RFI asks what the documents mean.
  • It went to the wrong person. Subs usually send RFIs to the GC, which forwards them to the design team. Sending one straight to the architect can leave the GC without a record of it.

Keep an RFI log, and turn answers into change orders

A log is one row per RFI: number, subject, date sent, answer needed by, date answered, who answered, cost or time impact, and the change order it led to, if any. Two lines on it are worth checking every week:

  1. Open RFIs past their needed-by date. Chase them, in writing, and note what work is waiting. The dates on the log are the record behind a later request for more time.
  2. Answered RFIs that change your work, with no change order. An answer that adds scope is not money until it is on an executed change order. Most subcontracts set a deadline for written notice of a change, so price it and send a change order request that names the RFI soon after the answer comes in (writing the change order request, tracking it to approval).

Once the change order is approved, it goes on the schedule of values and into the next pay application (change orders and the SOV).

Common questions

What is an RFI in construction?

A request for information: a written question to the GC, and through it the architect or engineer, when the drawings or specs conflict, leave something out or cannot be built as shown. The answer becomes part of the job record, so it should be in writing, numbered and dated.

Does an RFI answer authorize extra work?

Not by itself, on most subcontracts. An answer that changes your scope, cost or time usually still needs a change order (or a written directive) under your subcontract's changes clause. Price it, send a change order request that names the RFI, and keep the notice deadline in your subcontract.

Who should a subcontractor send an RFI to?

Usually the GC's project manager, or whoever your subcontract names, not the architect directly. The GC forwards it to the design team, keeps its own log and sends you the answer.

How long should an RFI take to answer?

Your subcontract, the prime contract or the job's procedures may set a response time; read them. Otherwise ask for a date tied to when the work needs the answer, say what is waiting on it, and log the date so a late answer can back a time claim.

Should one RFI ask more than one question?

No. One question per RFI gets each answered, tracked and closed on its own. A second question in the same RFI can hold up the first, and a combined answer is harder to cite on a change order.

How change orders flow into the schedule of values

Almost every job changes. The pay application has to show those changes without rewriting the original deal, so the owner can see what was bid and what was added. The convention is simple, and most rejected change-order billing breaks one of four rules below.

The four rules

  1. An approved change order gets its own SOV line. Add it as CO-1, CO-2 and so on, with its full value as the scheduled value. Do not raise the value of an original line: the original contract sum stays as it was bid.
  2. Only approved change orders go on the pay app. A change order request, a pending change order or a signed T&M ticket with no executed change order is usually not billable yet. Track it separately and bill it the month it is signed. Check your subcontract: some allow billing approved-in-principle work.
  3. A deductive change order is a negative line. It reduces the contract sum to date. Bill it as "completed" in the period the credit applies, so the balance to finish on that line reaches zero.
  4. Retainage applies to change-order work too, unless the change order says otherwise.

Worked example

A $150,000 electrical subcontract adds CO-1, $8,500 of EV charger circuits, half done this month.

Summary lineBefore CO-1After CO-1
Original contract sum$150,000.00$150,000.00
Net change by change orders$0.00$8,500.00
Contract sum to date$150,000.00$158,500.00
CO-1 completed this period$4,250.00
Retainage on CO-1 at 10%$425.00
Added to this month's payment due$3,825.00

Keeping change orders from costing you money

  • Get the change order signed before the work if your contract requires it; many subcontracts waive payment for unapproved extra work.
  • Keep a log of every request, its status and its value. Unsigned changes are the easiest money to lose on a job. The free change order log totals what is approved and pending, flags changes waiting too long, and writes the approved ones as SOV lines.
  • When you sign a progress lien waiver, list unapproved change orders as an exception (see lien waivers).
  • Watch notice deadlines in your subcontract for claims and change requests; they can be short.

What to put in a change order request

The request is what the GC signs to turn extra work into a billable SOV line, so it should answer every question their PM will ask before signing:

  • Who, which job and which number: your company, the GC, the project and job number, and a change order number that matches your log and the SOV line it will become (CO-1, CO-2).
  • The scope in plain words: what changed, why (the RFI, ASI, field directive or drawing revision behind it) and what is excluded.
  • The price by line: new scope as its own line, changes to existing lines as additions or deductions, and the total. Attach your backup (quotes, labor and material breakdown) if the subcontract asks for it.
  • The contract sum before and after: original contract, previously approved change orders, this change, and the new contract sum if it is approved. It lets the PM check your numbers against theirs in one glance.
  • Time: any change to the schedule, in days, or "no schedule impact". Many subcontracts treat silence as no extension.
  • Two signatures and dates: yours and the GC's. Until the GC signs, the work is pending: track it, do not bill it.

Record the date you sent it. A request that has waited 30 days without an answer is worth a call, and the change order log flags those for you.

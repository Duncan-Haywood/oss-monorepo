Retainage explained, with the public retainage caps in 16 states

Retainage (also called retention) is the part of each progress payment the owner holds back until the job is finished, as security that the work gets completed and corrected. On a subcontract, the general contractor usually holds the same percentage back from you. On a typical job it is 5 or 10 percent of every bill, which for a specialty sub is often the whole profit margin, sitting in someone else's account for months.

How retainage is calculated on a pay application

Retainage is a percentage of the work completed to date, and usually of materials stored too, not of this month's bill alone. Each application recalculates the total held:

  • Retainage held = (work completed to date × work rate) + (stored materials × stored rate) − retainage already released.
  • Earned less retainage = total completed and stored − retainage held.
  • Payment due = earned less retainage − what earlier applications certified.

Worked example. A $158,500 electrical subcontract with 10% retainage has $79,850 completed and stored. Retainage held is $7,985, earned less retainage is $71,865, and with $31,500 certified on earlier applications, this month's payment due is $40,365. You can load this example in the calculator and change the rate to see what a 5% cap would be worth: $3,992.50 more cash this month.

Reduction and release

Many contracts, and some statutes, cut the rate once the job is 50% complete or stop withholding new retainage altogether. The balance is released at substantial or final completion, usually against a final lien waiver, closeout documents and sometimes a punch list. Subs on early trades (sitework, foundations, steel) can wait a year or more for their retainage unless the contract allows line-item or early release. Ask for it in writing at the subcontract stage, not after.

Flow-down

Several of the statutes below say the prime contractor cannot hold back a higher percentage from a subcontractor than the owner holds from the prime (California and Texas say so directly). Read your subcontract against that: a 10% sub retainage on a 5% public job is worth a question.

Retainage caps on public construction, 16 states

The first twelve rows were read on 2026-11-24 through search results limited to each state legislature's own site. On 2026-11-25 the statute pages themselves were loaded: Texas, Florida, New York, North Carolina, Michigan, Virginia, Washington, Massachusetts and Arizona matched the rows as written; California, Pennsylvania and Illinois refused automated loads and still rest on the first reading. Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Utah were added on 2026-11-25 from the statute pages directly. These statutes cover public work, often only certain public owners, and have exceptions not shown here. They change: open the linked statute and read your contract before relying on a row. This is a summary, not legal advice.

What to do with this

  • Check the retainage clause in your subcontract against the state cap and against what the owner holds from the GC.
  • Ask for retainage to drop at 50% if the statute or prime contract allows it, and put the date in your calendar.
  • Track retainage held per job every month; it is easy to lose track of across ten open jobs, and it is your money.
  • Bill retainage as its own line item at closeout, with the final lien waiver the GC needs.

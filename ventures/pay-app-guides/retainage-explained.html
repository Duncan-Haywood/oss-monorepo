Retainage explained, with the public retainage caps in 16 states

Retainage (also called retention) is the part of each progress payment the owner holds back until the job is finished, as security that the work gets completed and corrected. On a subcontract, the general contractor usually holds the same percentage back from you. On a typical job it is 5 or 10 percent of every bill, which for a specialty sub is often the whole profit margin, sitting in someone else's account for months.

How retainage is calculated on a pay application

Retainage is a percentage of the work completed to date, and usually of materials stored too, not of this month's bill alone. Each application recalculates the total held:

  • Retainage held = (work completed to date × work rate) + (stored materials × stored rate) − retainage already released.
  • Earned less retainage = total completed and stored − retainage held.
  • Payment due = earned less retainage − what earlier applications certified.

Worked example. A $158,500 electrical subcontract with 10% retainage has $79,850 completed and stored. Retainage held is $7,985, earned less retainage is $71,865, and with $31,500 certified on earlier applications, this month's payment due is $40,365. You can load this example in the calculator and change the rate to see what a 5% cap would be worth: $3,992.50 more cash this month.

Reduction and release

Many contracts, and some statutes, cut the rate once the job is 50% complete or stop withholding new retainage altogether. The balance is released at substantial or final completion, usually against a final lien waiver, closeout documents and sometimes a punch list. Subs on early trades (sitework, foundations, steel) can wait a year or more for their retainage unless the contract allows line-item or early release. Ask for it in writing at the subcontract stage, not after.

A partial release has to land on the continuation sheet line by line, and the lines have to add up to the cent. Split the release in proportion to what each line holds. Say the GC agrees to release half the retainage: line 1 holds $6,000.00 and line 2 holds $2,050.00, so $8,050.00 is held and $4,025.00 comes out. Line 1 releases $3,000.00 and line 2 releases $1,025.00. When a share comes to a fraction of a cent, round the lines down and give the leftover cents to the lines with the largest fractions, so the total stays exact and no line releases more than it holds. The release lowers retainage held on that application (line 5) and raises its payment due (line 8) by the same amount.

Read a reduction clause closely, because it can mean two numbers. On a $100,000 subcontract with $70,000 completed, "10%, then 5% after 50% complete" holds $3,500 if 5% applies to everything billed. It holds $6,000 if 5% applies only to work after the halfway point ($5,000 on the first $50,000 plus $1,000 on the next $20,000). The pay application generator works out either one.

Lines with their own rate, or none

Retainage does not have to be one rate across the whole continuation sheet. Some subcontracts bill certain lines without retainage, the bond premium being a common one since the sub pays it up front, and some hold a different rate on one scope. When yours does, work out retainage line by line: each line's work to date and stored materials times that line's rate, then add the lines up for line 5. Say application #1 bills a $2,000 bond line in full and $10,000 of rough-in at 10%. With the bond exempt, retainage is $1,000 and payment due is $11,000; held at 10% across the board it would be $1,200 and $10,800. Say on the application which lines carry their own rate, so the GC's reviewer sees why line 5 is not a flat 10%, and keep that rate when the job-wide rate drops at 50% unless the contract says otherwise. Your subcontract's retainage clause decides which lines qualify; this is arithmetic, not legal advice.

Flow-down

Several of the statutes below say the prime contractor cannot hold back a higher percentage from a subcontractor than the owner holds from the prime (California and Texas say so directly). Read your subcontract against that: a 10% sub retainage on a 5% public job is worth a question.

Retainage caps on public construction, 16 states

The first twelve rows were read on 2026-11-24 through search results limited to each state legislature's own site. On 2026-11-25 the statute pages themselves were loaded: Texas, Florida, New York, North Carolina, Michigan, Virginia, Washington, Massachusetts and Arizona matched the rows as written; California, Pennsylvania and Illinois refused automated loads and still rest on the first reading. Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Utah were added on 2026-11-25 from the statute pages directly. These statutes cover public work, often only certain public owners, and have exceptions not shown here. What to do with this

  • Check the retainage clause in your subcontract against the state cap and against what the owner holds from the GC.
  • Ask for retainage to drop at 50% if the statute or prime contract allows it, and put the date in your calendar.
  • Track retainage held per job every month; it is easy to lose track of across ten open jobs, and it is your money.
  • Bill retainage as its own line item at closeout, with the final lien waiver the GC needs. How to get it released has the closeout checklist and a request letter, and the retainage tracker shows what is outstanding and overdue across all your jobs.

Common questions

How is retainage calculated on a pay application?

Retainage held is the work completed to date times the work rate, plus stored materials times the stored rate, minus retainage already released. It is a percentage of everything completed and stored to date, not of this month's bill alone.

Can a GC hold more retainage from a sub than the owner holds from the GC?

Several state statutes say the prime contractor cannot hold back a higher percentage from a subcontractor than the owner holds from the prime; California and Texas say so directly. A 10% sub retainage on a 5% public job is worth a question.

When is retainage released?

The balance is released at substantial or final completion, usually against a final lien waiver, closeout documents and sometimes a punch list. Many contracts, and some statutes, cut the rate once the job is 50% complete.

Submittal log template for subcontractors

A submittal is what you send the GC to review before you buy or build: shop drawings, product data, samples, mock-ups, and at the end of the job test reports, certificates, O&M manuals and warranties. Most material is not released until its submittal comes back approved, so the submittal log is where a supplier's lead time turns into a date you either make or miss. Keep the columns below, then paste the log into the tracker to see what to send, what to chase and which needed-on-site dates are already at risk.

What a submittal log should record

One row per submittal and revision. A resubmittal is a new row with the same number and the next revision, so the first one and the GC's comments on it stay on the log.

ColumnWhy it matters
Number and revisionUse the GC's number when their register assigns one. "#12 Rev 1" is the second try at #12, and only the newest revision is still open.
Spec sectionThe section that asks for it (for example 26 24 16 for panelboards). Reviewers sort and stamp by section, and close-out lists are built from it.
Title and typeWhat it is: shop drawings, product data, samples, mock-up, test report, certificate, O&M manual, warranty.
Sent and required backThe day it went out under a transmittal and the day the review period ends. Without a required-back date, nothing is ever overdue.
Returned and actionThe day it came back and the stamp: approved (or no exceptions taken), approved as noted, revise and resubmit, rejected, or received for record.
Needed on site, lead time, review daysWhen the material has to be there (from the GC's schedule), how many calendar days your supplier needs after approval, and how many the review takes. These three give the last day to send it.

The last day to send a submittal

Work back from the day the material is needed on site: take off the supplier's lead time, then the review period.

Last day to send = needed on site − lead time − review days.

Example (made up): switchgear needed on site 15 January, a 56-day lead time after approval, a 14-day review. 15 January − 56 days = 20 November; − 14 days = 6 November. Send it on 10 November and, if the review takes the full 14 days, approval lands 24 November and the gear arrives 19 January, four days late. A resubmittal goes back through the whole review, which is why a "revise and resubmit" on a long-lead item is the one to turn around first.

Use the review period in your project's specifications (Section 01 33 00, Submittal Procedures, in most project manuals) and the lead time your supplier quotes, not a rule of thumb.

      Keeping submittals off the critical path
      
  
        
    
      1. Build the log the week you sign. Go through your spec sections and list every submittal they ask for, close-out documents included. Many GCs send a register; match your numbers to it.
        2. 
    
      2. Get the needed-on-site dates from the GC's schedule and the lead times from your suppliers in writing. Those two numbers are what make a log more than a list.
        3. 
    
      3. Send the long-lead items first. Sort by last day to send, not by spec section.
        4. 
    
      4. Send every submittal under a transmittal that states the return-by date and the needed-on-site date, so the reviewer has the schedule in front of them.
        5. 
    
      5. Chase on the required-back date, in writing. One email listing every overdue item (the tracker writes it) beats a call per item, and it is a dated record if a late review later becomes a schedule argument. When a question comes up during review, send it as an RFI: how to write an RFI.

      Common questions
      
  
      What is a submittal log in construction?
      
  
      A list of every submittal a job's specifications ask for (shop drawings, product data, samples, test reports, close-out documents), with the date each was sent, the date it is due back from review, the date it came back and the reviewer's action. Subcontractors keep one for their own scope; the GC keeps the register for the whole job.
      
  
      What is the difference between approved as noted and revise and resubmit?
      
  
      Approved as noted (or make corrections noted) means you may go ahead, following the reviewer's notes. Revise and resubmit means you may not: correct it and send it again as the next revision, which goes back through the full review.
      
  
      How long does submittal review take?
      
  
      As long as the specifications allow. Section 01 33 00 (Submittal Procedures) in most project manuals states the review period in days; use that, plus any extra time it gives for resubmittals, when you work out the last day to send.
      
  
      Can I order material before the submittal is approved?
      
  
      At your own risk. If the submittal comes back revise and resubmit or rejected, material bought to the first version may not be accepted. Many subcontracts also say not to fabricate or order before approval; check yours.

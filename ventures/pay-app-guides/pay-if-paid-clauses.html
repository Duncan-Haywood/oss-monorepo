Pay-if-paid vs pay-when-paid: what your subcontract's payment clause means

A pay-when-paid clause says when the general contractor pays you: so many days after the owner pays the GC. A pay-if-paid clause says whether: the owner's payment is a condition of you being paid at all, so if the owner never pays, the GC argues it never owes you. The words decide which one you signed, and your state decides whether a pay-if-paid clause holds. Paste the clause below to see both.

      The reader looks for the wording courts have pointed to (a "condition precedent", "if and only if", a named risk of the owner not paying, the owner's payment as the "sole source", or a number of days after receipt).       Three kinds of rule

      • The condition is void. North Carolina and South Carolina by statute, and California and New York by their highest courts, will not let the owner's payment be a condition of yours. Wisconsin voids the condition but allows a clause that only delays your payment until the GC is paid.
      • The clause cannot defeat your lien. Illinois, Maryland and Missouri say a pay-if-paid clause is no defense to your mechanics lien claim (Maryland adds your bond claim). Their statutes do not say the clause is void for every purpose, so your lien rights matter more there, and their deadlines keep running: the free mechanics lien deadline calculator works out the dates.
      • Clear words or it is timing. In Florida, and in the federal court decision applying Ohio law, a clause tying payment to the owner's payment is read as setting a reasonable time to pay, unless the contract clearly puts the risk of the owner not paying on you. Where courts enforce pay-if-paid, they ask for words like "condition precedent".

      Before you sign

      • Ask for timing wording. "Contractor shall pay Subcontractor within 7 days after Contractor receives payment for Subcontractor's work, and in any event within 45 days after Subcontractor's pay application is approved" gives you a date even if the owner is late. Your state's prompt payment statute may already set one.
      • Strike "condition precedent" and "assumes the risk". These are the words that turn timing into a condition.
      • Protect your lien rights. California's and New York's courts struck pay-if-paid down because in practice it waives the sub's lien rights, and Illinois, Maryland and Missouri protect those rights by statute. Read the rest of the subcontract for any other lien or bond waiver, and do not sign an unconditional waiver for money you have not received (conditional vs unconditional lien waivers).
      • Get it read. Whether a clause holds on your job is a question for a construction attorney licensed in the project's state.

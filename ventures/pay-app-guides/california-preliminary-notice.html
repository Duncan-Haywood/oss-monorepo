California 20-day preliminary notice: who gets it, when, and a draft

On a private California job, a subcontractor or supplier that has not given a preliminary notice cannot record a valid mechanics lien or give a valid stop payment notice (Cal. Civ. Code § 8200(c)), and needs it (or a § 8612 notice) for a payment bond claim (§ 8200(d)). It is due 20 days after you first furnish work. "Work" in the statute covers labor, service, equipment and material. Dates count calendar days and are not moved off a weekend or holiday here, so serve well before the last day. Mail sent by one of the § 8110 services is given the day it is deposited (§ 8116(b)); ordinary first-class mail is not one of them.

Who gets the notice

  • The owner or reputed owner (§ 8200(a)(1)).
  • The direct contractor (the GC who contracted with the owner) you provide work to, directly or through other subcontractors (§ 8200(a)(2)).
  • The construction lender, if any (§ 8200(a)(3)). Ask the GC or check the recorded deed of trust; the notice asks for it "to the extent known" (§ 8102(a)).

A laborer gives none, and a claimant with a direct contract with the owner gives it only to the lender, if any (§ 8200(e)). One notice to each person covers all of your work on the job, and work you add later even outside its description (§ 8206(a), (c)), but if you work under contracts with more than one subcontractor, give a separate notice for each contract (§ 8206(b)).

Leaving it out has a second cost: for a licensed subcontractor whose subcontract pays more than $400, failing to give the notice is grounds for discipline under the Contractors' State License Law (§ 8216).

California sets the contents, not a form. A notice that varies from the list still counts if it is enough to substantially inform the person of the required information (§ 8102(b)).

How to give it, and how to prove it

  • Means. Personal delivery; mail by registered or certified mail, express mail or an overnight express carrier; or leaving it and mailing a copy as Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20 provides for a summons (§§ 8106, 8110).
  • When it counts as given. Personal delivery: when delivered. Mail: when deposited in the mail or with the carrier. Left and mailed: five days after mailing (§ 8116).
  • Proof. If you later record a lien or make a claim, you prove the notice with a proof of notice declaration stating what was given, the date, place and manner, facts showing it was given as the statute requires, and the name, address and capacity of each person given it (§ 8118(a)). For a mailed notice, attach the post office's or carrier's receipt showing payment for certified, registered, express or overnight service, or a return receipt, delivery confirmation, tracking record or the returned envelope (§ 8118(b)). Keep these the day you mail.

The statement, word for word (§ 8202(a)(3))

NOTICE TO PROPERTY OWNER

EVEN THOUGH YOU HAVE PAID YOUR CONTRACTOR IN FULL, if the person or firm that has given you this notice is not paid in full for labor, service, equipment, or material provided or to be provided to your construction project, a lien may be placed on your property. Foreclosure of the lien may lead to loss of all or part of your property. You may wish to protect yourself against this by (1) requiring your contractor to provide a signed release by the person or firm that has given you this notice before making payment to your contractor, or (2) any other method that is appropriate under the circumstances.

This notice is required by law to be served by the undersigned as a statement of your legal rights. This notice is not intended to reflect upon the financial condition of the contractor or the person employed by you on the construction project.

If you record a notice of cessation or completion of your construction project, you must within 10 days after recording, send a copy of the notice of completion to your contractor and the person or firm that has given you this notice. The notice must be sent by registered or certified mail. Failure to send the notice will extend the deadline to record a claim of lien. You are not required to send the notice if you are a residential homeowner of a dwelling containing four or fewer units.

Common questions

Who has to send a California preliminary notice?

Before recording a lien claim, giving a stop payment notice or claiming on a payment bond, a claimant gives preliminary notice to the owner, the direct contractor and the construction lender, if any (Cal. Civ. Code § 8200(a)). A laborer does not have to, and a claimant with a direct contract with the owner gives it only to the construction lender, if any (§ 8200(e)).

When is the California preliminary notice due?

Not later than 20 days after you first furnished work on the job (§ 8204(a)). By personal delivery, certified or registered mail, express mail or an overnight carrier the notice is given the day it is delivered or deposited; left and mailed under Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20 it is given five days after mailing (§ 8116).

What if I send the preliminary notice late?

You can still give it, but a lien, stop payment notice or bond claim then covers only work performed within the 20 days before the notice and after (§ 8204(a)). Work before that window is not covered.

Is there an official California preliminary notice form?

No. The statute sets the contents, not a form: the names and addresses of the owner, direct contractor and lender, the site, who you are and your relationship to the parties, the work, who hired you, an estimate of the total price, and the NOTICE TO PROPERTY OWNER statement in boldface (§§ 8102, 8202).

The columns

ColumnHow it is worked out
Contract amountYours to type: the original contract plus approved change orders. Pending change orders stay out.
Estimated total costYours to type: cost to date plus your current estimate of the cost to finish. Update it every period; every other column follows it.
Cost to dateYours to type: job cost booked through the schedule date.
Billed to dateYours to type: total completed and stored to date on your last pay application, before retainage.
Estimated gross profitContract less estimated total cost. Negative is a losing job.
% completeCost to date / estimated total cost, held at 100%.
Earned revenue% complete x contract, to the cent.
Overbilled / underbilledBilled to date less earned revenue: above zero is overbilled (billings in excess of costs and estimated earnings), below zero underbilled (costs and estimated earnings in excess of billings).
Gross profit to dateEarned revenue less cost to date; on a losing job, the whole expected loss.
Provision for loss not yet in costOn a losing job, the expected loss less what cost to date already runs ahead of earned revenue.
Backlog / cost to completeContract less earned revenue; estimated total cost less cost to date.

The summary under the totals gives net over or under billing, gross profit recognised to date, gross profit left in backlog and its margin, and the number of losing jobs.

The clinic is 60% complete by cost ($240,000 / $400,000), so it has earned 60% of $480,000, $288,000. It has billed $312,000, so it is overbilled $24,000: cash ahead of the work, which is fine as long as the estimate holds, and a warning if the job is front-loaded (the front-loading check tests the schedule of values).

The warehouse is 75% complete, has earned $112,500 and billed only $97,500: underbilled $15,000. That is work done and not billed, often a change order not yet signed or a line under-claimed on the pay app. Sureties read large underbillings as a sign of trouble, so bill it or explain it.

The gym was bid low: an estimated $330,000 of cost on a $300,000 contract. It is half done and billed exactly what it earned, yet the schedule shows gross profit to date of -$30,000, the whole expected loss, not half of it. Cost to date already runs $15,000 ahead of earned revenue ($165,000 against $150,000), so the provision for loss column shows the other $15,000. Its check column says "Losing job: whole loss booked now."

The retail shell is just started, 12.5% complete with $27,500 earned and $44,000 billed (a mobilization line), so it is overbilled $16,500.

Across the four jobs: overbilled $40,500, underbilled $15,000, net overbilled $25,500; gross profit recognised $46,000 of the $124,000 estimated; $78,000 of gross profit left in a $572,000 backlog, a 13.6% backlog margin; one losing job. Netting hides things, which is why the schedule keeps every job on its own row: the warehouse's underbilling is covered by the clinic's overbilling in the totals.

Who reads it, and what they look for

Your surety or bonding agent uses the WIP schedule to see whether your profit is real: gross profit that fades from one schedule to the next (estimates creeping up), large or growing underbillings, and losing jobs not booked in full are the usual concerns. Your bank reads it beside your balance sheet: overbillings are cash you hold for work you still owe, so they are a liability, not a cushion. You use it to see which job is quietly eating the margin before it shows up at year end.

Fill it in on the same date as your financial statements (month, quarter or year end), with each estimate updated first. To see where one job's billing and cost disagree line by line, start with overbilling and underbilling; to stress-test every estimate at once (what a 5% cost fade does to the year), paste the same rows into the free contractor WIP schedule tool.

General information and arithmetic on your figures, not accounting, tax or bonding advice. Your accountant decides the method (cost to cost is common; units or labour hours are others), how a loss provision is booked, and how this reaches your financial statements; a tax method may ignore it entirely. Sureties and banks set their own formats and ratios.

Common questions

What is a WIP schedule?

A work-in-progress schedule lists every open job on one date with its contract, estimated total cost, cost to date and billed to date, and works out how far along each job is, what it has earned, and whether you have billed ahead of the work (overbilled) or behind it (underbilled). Banks and bonding companies ask for it, often quarterly, with your financial statements.

How is percent complete worked out?

With the cost-to-cost method, the one most small contractors' accountants use: cost to date divided by the job's current estimated total cost. Earned revenue is that percent times the contract amount. The template holds a job at 100% if its cost has passed the estimate and tells you to raise the estimate if work remains.

What is the difference between overbilling and underbilling?

Overbilled (billings in excess of costs and estimated earnings) means billed to date is more than earned revenue: you hold money for work not yet done, and it is a liability. Underbilled (costs and estimated earnings in excess of billings) means earned revenue is more than billed: you have done work you have not billed, and it is an asset.

How does the template treat a job that will lose money?

When a job's estimated total cost is more than its contract, its gross profit to date is the whole expected loss, shown now rather than as the money is spent. A separate column shows the part of that loss its cost and earned revenue do not show yet, the figure an accountant books as a provision for loss.

Should billed to date include retainage?

Yes. Use billed to date before retainage is taken off, the total completed and stored to date on your last pay application, not the cash you have received. Retainage you are owed is a receivable, not unbilled work.

Does the template work in Google Sheets?

Yes. Upload the .xlsx to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets, or open it in Excel or LibreOffice Calc. It contains only ordinary formulas: no macros, no links to other files and no add-ins.

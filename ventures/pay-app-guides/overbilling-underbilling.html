Overbilling and underbilling: your pay app against your job costs

Your pay application says how far along the job is by what you billed on each line of the schedule of values. Your books, and the work-in-progress (WIP) schedule your bank and bonding company ask for, usually say it by cost: how much of the job's estimated cost you have spent. When the two disagree, the difference is overbilling (billed ahead of the work) or underbilling (work done but not yet billed). This page explains both, shows the entry an accountant books, and checks one job's numbers.

The three numbers

The cost-to-cost method, the one most small contractors' accountants use for a WIP schedule, needs three figures per job and one from the pay application:

FigureWhere it comes fromWhat to watch
Contract sumThe original contract plus approved change orders (the pay application's contract sum to date)Leave out pending changes until they are approved, or the job looks more profitable than it is.
Estimated total costCost to date plus your current estimate of the cost to finishThe one figure that is a judgment. An estimate left at the bid number while costs run over overstates profit every month.
Cost to dateJob cost report: labor, materials, subs and equipment charged to the jobMaterials bought but not yet installed can overstate progress; accountants often treat them separately.
Billed to dateThe pay application's total completed and stored to date, before retainageUse the gross figure. Retainage is billed, only not paid yet; netting it out would show a false underbilling.

Then: percent complete = cost to date ÷ estimated total cost. Earned revenue = percent complete × contract sum. Billed to date − earned revenue is overbilled when positive and underbilled when negative.

Why your pay app and your costs disagree

  • A front-loaded schedule of values. Mobilization, bonds and early lines priced high so that the first pay apps bring in cash. Billing runs ahead of cost: overbilling.
  • Stored materials. Billing for materials stored on site before they are installed can run ahead, or, if the materials are charged to the job before you bill them, behind.
  • Work on an unapproved change order. The cost is in your job cost report; the billing waits for the signature. Underbilling, and the kind a surety looks at hardest, because nobody has agreed to pay it yet. The change order log shows what is pending and for how long.
  • A cost overrun. If the job costs more than estimated and the estimate is not updated, the cost method says you are further along than you are, and the job looks underbilled when really the profit is shrinking.
  • A GC that cuts your percent. If the GC approves less than you asked for on a line, your billing falls behind your cost.

What a bank or surety reads into it

Overbilling is a liability on the balance sheet (often called "billings in excess of costs and estimated earnings"): money collected for work you still owe. A moderate amount is normal and helps cash flow. A large one means the cash in the bank is partly borrowed from the end of the job, and that end will cost more than it brings in.

Underbilling is an asset ("costs and estimated earnings in excess of billings"), but a soft one: it counts profit you have not billed and may never collect. Underwriters often discount it, and a pattern of underbillings across jobs is one of the signs they read as estimates going wrong. Either way the entries are called contract liabilities and contract assets in current revenue-recognition guidance; your CPA decides the presentation.

The adjusting entry

Shown for a business that records revenue as it bills, as most small contractors' books do between WIP updates. Your accountant may book it differently, reverse it each month, or work on a tax method that ignores it.


  

  

  
The example: 150,000 ÷ 400,000 = 37.5% complete; 37.5% of $480,000 = $180,000 earned; $210,000 billed − $180,000 = $30,000 overbilled. If $60,000 more is spent next month, the job is 52.5% complete and $252,000 earned, so billing about $42,000 would leave it even. Your numbers stay in this browser tab.






  
Keeping the gap small

  
    
    
  • Update the estimate to complete every month, before the WIP schedule, not at year end. Every other figure on this page depends on it.
    • 
    
  • Bill change orders as soon as they are signed, and chase the signature on pending ones; that is where most underbilling comes from.
    • 
    
  • Front-load with care. Some is normal; a GC or owner that spots it may cut your next application, and a surety will see it on the WIP. The front-loading check shows which lines are loaded and writes a balanced SOV.
    • 
    
  • Run every open job together. One job overbilled and another underbilled can hide each other in the totals. The free contractor WIP schedule takes your whole job list, books the full loss on a losing job, and stress-tests the estimates.

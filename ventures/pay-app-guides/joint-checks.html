Joint checks in construction: split the check before anyone endorses it

A joint check is a payment from the GC (or owner) made out to a subcontractor and its supplier or lower-tier sub, so the supplier has to endorse before the money moves. GCs use them so a supplier is not left unpaid and filing a lien for material the GC already paid for. The trap is for the supplier: in states that follow the joint check rule, a supplier that endorses without a written split can be treated as paid what it was owed, up to the check amount, even if the sub kept the money.               What the California Supreme Court said
              
  
              In Post Bros. Construction Co. v. Yoder (1977) 20 Cal.3d 1 (read on the Stanford SCOCAL archive, 2026-11-25), a grading supplier endorsed four joint checks totalling $54,000 that went into the subcontractor's bank account. The sub paid the supplier on other jobs first, and the supplier later claimed a mechanics lien and sued on the bond. It lost both. The court's reasons are the ones to plan around:
              
  
                
    
              • You can refuse to endorse. "The material man may protect himself by simply refusing to endorse the check until assured by escrow of other arrangement that he will recover his rightful share of the check."
                • 
    
              • Your job is to find out what the check is for. Before endorsing, the supplier has to work out whether the check covers the whole job or is a progress payment: "his burden is to determine what the joint check is for."
                • 
    
              • An agreement beats the rule. "The joint check rule will give way to an express or implied agreement of the maker and payees." A check or agreement that lists what it pays controls.
                • 
    
              • The surety gets the same defence. Because the supplier is deemed paid, it "may not recover on the owner's or contractor's surety bond."
                • 
  
              

              


              
  
              For each payee
              
  
                
    
              • Supplier or lower-tier sub. Ask for a joint check agreement before you ship on credit to a sub you are unsure of. When a check arrives, endorse only against your share in hand: the sub's own check or a wire to you, or the GC handing the endorsed check to you directly. Give a conditional waiver for your share, and an unconditional one only after it clears (conditional vs unconditional waivers). Your lien and bond deadlines keep running while you wait: the free mechanics lien deadline calculator works out the dates.
                • 
    
              • Subcontractor. A joint check is still a payment on your subcontract, so the whole check counts against your contract balance on the next pay application, including the part that went to the supplier. Record it that way, or your "previous certificates" will not match the GC's (pay application checker). Read whether your subcontract lets the GC pay by joint check; the handout notes a GC without that right may not discharge its debt to you by issuing one.
                • 
    
              • GC or owner. Get the subcontract right to issue joint checks, but not a duty to; a mandatory promise to pay by joint check can be enforced by the supplier. Read any agreement a supplier proposes for a guarantee of payment hidden in it. Have the sub endorse at your office and deliver the check to the supplier yourself.
                • 
  
              

              


              
  
              Common questions
              
  
              What is a joint check in construction?
              
  
              A payment from the GC or owner made out to a subcontractor and its supplier or lower-tier sub, so the supplier has to endorse before the money moves. GCs use them so a supplier is not left unpaid and filing a lien for material the GC already paid for.
              
  
              What is the joint check rule?
              
  
              In states that follow it, a supplier that endorses a joint check with no written split can be treated as paid what it was owed, up to the check amount, even if the sub kept the money. An express or implied agreement of the payer and payees beats the rule, so get the split in writing before anyone endorses.
              
  
              Does endorsing a joint check waive Miller Act bond rights?
              
  
              Not on its own: the handout this page relies on says the joint check rule does not apply on federal projects with a Miller Act payment bond. A joint check agreement that says endorsement is payment in full can still waive them.

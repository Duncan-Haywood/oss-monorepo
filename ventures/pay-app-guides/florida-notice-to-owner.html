Florida Notice to Owner: the 45-day deadline, who gets it, and the form

On a private Florida job, a subcontractor, sub-subcontractor or supplier that has not served a Notice to Owner on time has no lien to enforce: “the failure to serve the notice, or to timely serve it, is a complete defense to enforcement of a lien by any person” (Fla. Stat. § 713.06(2)(a)). It is due 45 days after you first furnish work, and mailed by certified mail within 40 days it counts on the day you mail it. Dates count calendar days and are not moved off a weekend or holiday: §§ 713.06(2) and 713.18 say nothing about either, so the earlier date stands. Serve well before the last day. Ordinary first-class mail is not one of the § 713.18 methods.

Who serves it, and on whom

  • Who serves it. “All lienors under this section, except laborers”, which means the subcontractors, sub-subcontractors and suppliers who have no contract with the owner. A contractor hired by the owner does not serve this notice.
  • The owner, always. A document served on one owner is served on all owners (§ 713.18(4)).
  • A copy to the contractor from a sub-subcontractor or a supplier to a subcontractor, and from a supplier to a sub-subcontractor. This copy is also “a prerequisite to perfecting a lien” (§ 713.06(2)(a)).
  • A copy to the subcontractor from a supplier to a sub-subcontractor, if the supplier knows the subcontractor's name and address (§ 713.06(2)(a)).
  • Anyone the notice of commencement designates to receive a copy. Leaving this one out “does not invalidate an otherwise valid lien” (§ 713.06(2)(b)), but send it anyway.
  • A lender the notice of commencement names, addressed to the persons and the place it designates (§ 713.06(2)(d)).

Get the names from the recorded notice of commencement at the county. If none is recorded, a lienor “may rely on the information contained in the building permit application” (§ 713.06(2)(e)). The notice is a step, not the lien: “The serving of the notice does not dispense with recording the claim of lien” (§ 713.06(2)(a)). Common questions

Who has to send a Florida Notice to Owner?

Lienors not in privity with the owner: subcontractors, sub-subcontractors and suppliers. Fla. Stat. § 713.06(2)(a) says “All lienors under this section, except laborers” must serve it on the owner as a prerequisite to perfecting a lien. A sub-subcontractor or a supplier to a subcontractor also serves a copy on the contractor, and a supplier to a sub-subcontractor serves a copy on the subcontractor if it knows its name and address.

When is the Florida Notice to Owner due?

Before you start, or “not later than 45 days after commencing” to furnish labor, services or materials, and in any event before the owner makes final payment after the contractor's final affidavit (§ 713.06(2)(a)). Sent by certified, registered or Global Express Guaranteed mail within 40 days of the first day you furnished, it counts as served on the day mailed (§ 713.18(2)).

What happens if the Notice to Owner is late?

The statute says “the failure to serve the notice, or to timely serve it, is a complete defense to enforcement of a lien by any person” (§ 713.06(2)(a)), and small errors in the contents can be forgiven but “a lienor must strictly comply with the time requirements” (§ 713.06(2)(f)).

Is there an official Florida Notice to Owner form?

Yes, in the statute itself: “The notice may be in substantially the following form and must include the information and the warning contained in the following form” (§ 713.06(2)(c)). This page prints that form word for word with your job filled in.

Where do I find the owner's name and the property description?

In the recorded notice of commencement, which also names anyone the owner designates to receive a copy and any lender. With no recorded notice of commencement, a lienor “may rely on the information contained in the building permit application” (§ 713.06(2)(e)).

When will I get paid? A pay application cash forecast for subcontractors

Payroll goes out every week. Pay applications go in once a month, on the GC's cutoff day, and the money comes back after the owner pays the GC and the GC pays you, less retainage. Enter each job and the applications you have already sent. The page works out the date each payment should land, which ones are already late, the money coming in week by week and, if you enter cash on hand and weekly spending, the first week the bank goes below zero.

The day counts are yours: from your subcontracts and from how these GCs have actually paid you. The page does arithmetic on them; it does not decide what anyone owes or when.

Why the money comes so late

Take a job with a cutoff on the 25th, an owner who pays the GC 30 days after it gets the application and a GC that pays you 10 days after that. Work you do on the 26th of March goes on the April 25th application and is paid around June 4th: 70 days later. Your crew was paid for it nine or ten times in between. That is the gap a sub finances with its own cash or a line of credit, on every job, all the time.

Three things shorten it, and all three are in your hands at least partly:

  • Never miss a cutoff. A pay app that misses the 25th waits a whole month, not a few days. The forecast flags a recent cutoff with nothing listed for it.
  • Chase the late ones early. The usual hold-ups are paperwork: a lien waiver (yours or a supplier's), an insurance certificate, certified payroll, a sworn statement. Ask the week the payment is due, not a month later.
  • Know what the GC may hold, and for how long. Many states set a number of days for the GC to pay you after it is paid, with interest when it does not (prompt payment laws by state). Whether the owner paying the GC is a condition of your payment at all depends on the clause (pay-if-paid or pay-when-paid).

Billing for materials delivered and stored, when the contract allows it, also brings money forward (billing stored materials). Front-loading the schedule of values does too, and GCs know it: it is the first thing they cut.

What this forecast does not know

  • A short payment: the GC certifying less than you applied for, or deducting a backcharge (reconcile a short check).
  • Change orders you have not yet been allowed to bill: they are not in "monthly billing" until they are approved.
  • Retainage. It is held back from every application above and comes at the end of the job; the retainage tracker follows it job by job.
  • Anything other than pay applications: time-and-material invoices, service work, a tax refund. Add them to your own cash sheet.

Questions

When will I get paid on a pay application?

Add up the chain: the date you submit (the GC's cutoff day), the days the owner takes to pay the GC after it receives the application, and the days the GC takes to pay you after the owner pays it. A 25th cutoff with 30-day owner terms and a GC that pays 10 days later puts the money about 40 days after the cutoff. Your subcontract, and on some jobs a prompt payment statute, set the GC's part.

Why do subcontractors run short of cash on profitable jobs?

Payroll and suppliers are paid every week or two, but pay applications go in once a month and are paid 30 to 60 days later, less retainage. Work done just after a cutoff can wait more than two months to become cash. A week-by-week forecast shows the gap before it arrives.

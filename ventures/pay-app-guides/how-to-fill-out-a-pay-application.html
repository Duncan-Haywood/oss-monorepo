How to fill out a pay application, line by line

A pay application (pay app, payment application, progress billing, draw request) is how a contractor or subcontractor gets paid each month on a commercial job. Most follow the same two-part layout: a summary page with the money, and a continuation sheet with one row per line of the schedule of values. Many owners require the AIA G702/G703 forms, which are copyrighted and sold by the AIA; others take their own form or a portal. The arithmetic is the same either way, and this guide walks through it with one worked example.

Before you start: the schedule of values

The schedule of values (SOV) splits your contract sum into line items, agreed with the GC before the first bill. Every pay app bills against those lines and no others. A good SOV has enough lines that progress on each is easy to measure (conduit, gear, rough-in, trim, not just "electrical"), keeps materials and labor apart where you will store material on site, and puts general conditions and closeout items on their own lines. Front-loading the early lines is common and is also the first thing an owner's reviewer looks for: check yours against your costs.

The continuation sheet, column by column

Our example is a $150,000 electrical subcontract plus one $8,500 change order, on its third application, 10% retainage.

The summary page, line by line

  1. Original contract sum: the base contract, $150,000. It never changes.
  2. Net change by change orders: approved change orders only, added and deducted, $8,500.
  3. Contract sum to date: line 1 + line 2, $158,500. It should equal the scheduled value total on the continuation sheet.
  4. Total completed and stored to date: the continuation sheet total, $79,850.
  5. Retainage: the percentage of completed work and of stored material, $7,985 at 10%. Some forms split this into the two parts.
  6. Total earned less retainage: line 4 − line 5, $71,865.
  7. Less previous certificates for payment: the sum of the payment-due amounts certified on earlier applications (not what you were actually paid, and not what you billed), $31,500.
  8. Current payment due: line 6 − line 7, $40,365.
  9. Balance to finish, including retainage: line 3 − line 6, $86,635.

What to attach, and why pay apps get kicked back

  • Lien waivers: usually a conditional waiver for this payment and an unconditional one for the last payment received, plus your suppliers' and sub-subs' waivers. See conditional vs unconditional lien waivers.
  • Sworn statement: in Illinois and Michigan, and on many lender- or title-company-funded jobs elsewhere, a sworn list of every sub and supplier with what each is paid and owed. The sworn statement builder totals it and checks it against your contract and this request.
  • Stored materials backup: invoices, bills of sale, photos, and proof of insurance for off-site storage if the contract allows it at all. See billing stored materials.
  • First-payment paperwork: many GCs hold a new sub's first payment until the subcontractor file is complete: a W-9 (the GC needs your taxpayer identification number to report what it pays you), a certificate of insurance naming the GC as holder with the endorsements the subcontract asks for, and a copy of your contractor's license where the state licenses your trade. Some also ask for a bonding letter or your EMR letter. Keep current copies in one place, note when each expires, and remember that a signed W-9 shows your EIN, or your Social Security number if you file as a sole proprietor: send it only the way the GC asks for it.
  • Notarization where the GC, lender or form requires it.
  • The deadline: GCs collect sub pay apps several days before they bill the owner. Miss the cutoff and you wait a month.

Send the backup as one PDF

A GC's billing clerk handles every sub's pay app in the same few days. One PDF, with the application first, a page that lists what follows, and page numbers on every page, is easier to check than a dozen attachments, and is less likely to come back with "missing backup" when the file was really just lost in the email. Put it in the order the GC reviews it: the application, the waivers it asks for, stored-material invoices and insurance certificates, certified payroll if the job is prevailing wage, then photos. The signed copy the GC returns later does not belong in it.

Three kinds of file cause most of the trouble when the packet is put together:

  • iPhone photos (HEIC). Many PDF tools and Windows computers cannot open them. On the phone, Settings > Camera > Formats > Most Compatible saves new photos as JPEG.
  • Password-protected PDFs. Some bank, insurer and supplier downloads are locked against editing or combining. If the file opens without a password, print it to a new PDF (Microsoft Print to PDF on Windows, or File > Export as PDF on a Mac) and attach the copy.
  • Spreadsheets and Word files. Certified payroll in Excel or a letter in Word does not merge into a PDF as it is. Save or print it as PDF first, or send it as a separate attachment and list it on the cover page so the clerk knows it is coming.

Common reasons for a rejected or reduced pay app: a line billed over its scheduled value; "from previous" not matching last month's approved numbers; billing a change order that is not yet signed; retainage at the wrong rate; stored materials without backup; missing waivers. The calculator flags the first and checks the arithmetic of the rest; the pay application checker compares your draft with last month's approved application, line by line, and catches the "from previous" and changed-value mistakes.

Common questions

What does "less previous certificates for payment" mean on a pay app?

It is the sum of the payment-due amounts certified on earlier applications: not what you were actually paid, and not what you billed.

How do I fill in "work completed from previous application"?

It is the total billed on the line on all earlier applications: last month's total completed and stored for the line minus last month's materials presently stored. Stored material stays in its own column until it is installed.

Why do pay applications get rejected?

Common reasons are a line billed over its scheduled value, "from previous" not matching last month's approved numbers, billing a change order that is not yet signed, retainage at the wrong rate, stored materials without backup, and missing waivers.

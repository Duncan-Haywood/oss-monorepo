The form has every part above. A line's amount is quantity times unit price, rounded to the cent; a lump-sum line with no quantity counts as one. Overhead and profit is the subtotal times your rate. The new contract sum is the original, plus changes approved before this one, plus this one; the new completion date is the old one plus the days you enter.

Pricing it, deducts and time

Price by cost line. Put labor, material, equipment and any lower-tier sub on their own lines so the PM can check each against their estimate. Use your real labor rate and attach the material quote; a single lump sum with no backup is the request most often sent back for "more detail".

Markup is your contract's, not ours. The form applies one overhead and profit rate to the whole subtotal. Many subcontracts set a maximum markup on changes, and some set different rates for your own work and for a lower-tier sub's; if yours does, put the sub's marked-up price on its line and the rate for your own work in the markup cell, or price each line with its markup in. The form never fills in a rate for you.

Deducts are credits. Work taken out goes in as a negative quantity or a negative unit price. The total then shows as a credit and the new contract sum drops. Whether overhead and profit come back on a deduct is up to your subcontract; if it says they do not, leave the rate blank. A checks line warns if a deduct takes the contract sum below zero.

Always say the time. Enter the calendar days the change adds, a negative number if it saves time, or 0 for no schedule impact. The checks line asks for it if you price a change and leave time blank, because many subcontracts treat silence as no extension.

After it is signed

Log it, then bill it. Record the date you sent the request in a change order log, which flags requests that have waited too long and totals what is approved and pending. Once the GC signs, the change order becomes its own line on your schedule of values (CO-1 with $8,500.00 as its scheduled value, in the example), the contract sum to date on your next pay application goes up by the same amount, and retainage applies to it unless the change order says otherwise (change orders and the SOV, with a worked example). Extra work done on a T&M ticket usually needs a change order like this before it can be billed. When you sign a progress lien waiver while a request is still unsigned, list it as an exception.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your subcontract's change and notice clauses decide what a request must contain, how much markup is allowed and when a request is due; they can be short.

Common questions

What should a change order request include?

Your company, the GC, the project and subcontract number; a change order number that matches your log; the reason (the RFI, ASI, field directive or drawing revision behind it); the change in plain words and what is not included; the price by line with any markup; the contract sum before and after; the change in contract time, even if it is none; and signature and date lines for both sides.

How do I price a change order?

List the labor (hours times your rate), material (from your quote or invoice), equipment and any lower-tier sub as separate lines, then add overhead and profit at the rate your subcontract allows. Many subcontracts set that rate, and some set different rates for your own work and for a lower-tier sub's work; use what yours says. Attach the backup the subcontract asks for.

How do I write a deductive change order?

Enter the work taken out as a negative quantity or a negative unit price, so the total shows as a credit and the new contract sum goes down. Whether overhead and profit come back on a deduct is up to your subcontract: if it says they do not, leave the rate blank on that form.

Can I bill a change order before it is signed?

Usually not. Until the GC signs it, it is a request: track it in a change order log and bill it the month it is signed, as its own schedule-of-values line (CO-1, CO-2). Some subcontracts allow billing work approved in principle, so check yours.

Is this the AIA G701 form?

No. It is our own layout, not an AIA document. G701 is the AIA's change order form used between owner, architect and contractor. This template is a subcontractor's request to a GC; if your contract requires the AIA form, use it.

Does the template work in Google Sheets?

Yes. Upload the .xlsx to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets, or open it in Excel or LibreOffice Calc. It contains only ordinary formulas: no macros, no links to other files and no add-ins.

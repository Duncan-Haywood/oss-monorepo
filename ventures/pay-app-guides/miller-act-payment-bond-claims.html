Miller Act payment bond claims: who can claim, the 90-day notice and the one-year limit

On a federal construction job you cannot put a mechanic's lien on the building: it is government property. What protects subcontractors and suppliers instead is the payment bond the prime contractor must furnish under the Miller Act (now 40 U.S.C. 3131 to 3134). The bond pays if the prime or a subcontractor does not, but only for claimants in the right tier and only if two deadlines are met. This page works out both from the date you last worked or delivered, and drafts the notice a second-tier claimant has to send.

Notice to the prime contractor (second tier)

The statute asks the notice to state the amount claimed "with substantial accuracy" and the name of the party you furnished material to or did the labor for. Fill these in for a draft.

The example: a drywall sub-subcontractor (second tier) that last worked on 2 March 2026 on a $2.4M federal clinic contract. Its 90-day notice must reach the prime by 31 May 2026; it may sue from 1 June 2026 and must sue by 2 March 2027. This is a draft, not legal advice: have an attorney look at it before you rely on it, above all if a date is close.

Who can claim on the bond

  • First tier: you contracted with the prime. A subcontractor or supplier to the prime may bring an action on the bond if it has not been paid in full within 90 days after its last labor or material (40 U.S.C. 3133(b)(1)). No notice is required by the statute.
  • Second tier: you contracted with one of the prime's subcontractors. You may claim too, but only "on giving written notice to the contractor within 90 days" from your last labor or material (3133(b)(2)).
  • Further down: you contracted with a sub-subcontractor. Not covered. In J.W. Bateson Co. v. United States ex rel. Board of Trustees, 434 U.S. 586 (1978) the Supreme Court held that "subcontractor" in the Act means one who contracts with the prime, so a sub-subcontractor's own subs and employees have no bond claim.
  • The prime. The bond protects people who work for the prime; the prime's own disputes with the government go through its contract.

The two deadlines, in the statute's words

  • 90-day notice (second tier only). Written notice to the prime "within 90 days from the date on which the person did or performed the last of the labor or furnished or supplied the last of the material for which the claim is made." It is served "by any means that provides written, third-party verification of delivery" to any place the prime keeps an office or does business, or its residence, or as a U.S. marshal may serve a summons (3133(b)(2)). Certified mail with a return receipt or a courier that records a signature are the usual ways to get that verification.
  • One-year limit (everyone). The action "must be brought no later than one year after the day on which the last of the labor was performed or material was supplied" (3133(b)(4)). It is filed in the name of the United States for your use, in the federal district court where the contract was performed, whatever the amount (3133(b)(3)).
  • The earliest date. You may sue once you have "not been paid in full within 90 days after" your last day (3133(b)(1)), so the calculator shows day 91.

How we count: 90 calendar days starting the day after your last day, and the one-year date as the same calendar date next year (29 February becomes 28 February, the earlier date). Courts have not all counted the same way, and the "last day" is often argued over: do not count on punch-list, warranty or correction work to move the date later. Send the notice and file well before either date.

Getting the bond, and keeping your rights

  • Get a copy of the bond. The contracting agency must give a certified copy of the payment bond and the contract to anyone who submits an affidavit that it supplied labor or material and has not been paid (3133(a)). The bond names the surety, which is who pays the claim. Many claimants send the surety a copy of the notice and the backup at the same time.
  • Which jobs have a bond. The statute sets the bond above $100,000 (40 U.S.C. 3131(b)); the FAR, as adjusted, requires performance and payment bonds on construction contracts above $150,000. Between $35,000 and $150,000 the contracting officer picks two or more payment protections (a payment bond, an irrevocable letter of credit, a tripartite escrow, certificates of deposit or other security) and the prime furnishes one (FAR 28.102-1, FAC 2026-01, effective 13 March 2026).
  • Waivers signed in advance are void. A waiver of the right to sue on the bond counts only if it is in writing, signed by you, and signed after you furnished labor or material (3133(c)). A subcontract clause signed before you started does not take the right away. A lien waiver you sign later for a payment can still limit your claim for what it covers, so give one only for money that has cleared (conditional vs unconditional, paid short).
  • State and local public jobs are different. States have their own bond laws ("little Miller Acts") with their own notice periods and limits. This page covers federal jobs only.

Sources, read 2026-11-25: 40 U.S.C. 3133 and 40 U.S.C. 3131 (Cornell LII, checked against the govinfo.gov 2023 edition; last amended 2006), FAR 28.102-1 on acquisition.gov, and the Bateson opinion on Cornell LII.

Common questions

Who can make a Miller Act payment bond claim?

Subcontractors and suppliers who contracted with the prime (first tier), and those who contracted with one of the prime's subcontractors (second tier). Anyone further down has no bond claim: in J.W. Bateson Co. v. United States (1978) the Supreme Court held that "subcontractor" in the Act means one who contracts with the prime.

What is the Miller Act 90-day notice?

A second-tier claimant must give the prime contractor written notice within 90 days from its last labor or material (40 U.S.C. 3133(b)(2)), by a means that gives written, third-party verification of delivery, such as certified mail with a return receipt. The statute requires no notice from a first-tier claimant.

How long do I have to sue on a Miller Act bond?

The action must be brought no later than one year after the last labor was performed or material supplied (3133(b)(4)), in the federal district court where the contract was performed. You may sue once you have not been paid in full within 90 days after your last day.

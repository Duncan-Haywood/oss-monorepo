Arizona preliminary twenty day notice: who gets it, when, and the form

In Arizona, a subcontractor, supplier, equipment renter or design professional that has not served a preliminary twenty day notice cannot claim a valid mechanics lien: the statute makes it a necessary prerequisite to the validity of any claim of lien (A.R.S. § 33-992.01(B)). It is due 20 days after you first furnish to the jobsite. Enter your dates below for the last day and what a late notice still protects; the statute's own form follows.

Calendar days, not moved off a weekend or holiday here, so mail well before the last day. A person paid wages for their own labor gives no notice (§ 33-992.01(B)).

Who gets the notice

One copy to each of these (§ 33-992.01(B)):

  • The owner or reputed owner. The statute's owner is whoever causes the improvement to be made, including a buyer under contract or a tenant (§ 33-992.01(A)(3)).
  • The original contractor or reputed contractor: the contractor with a direct contract with the owner (§ 33-992.01(A)(2)).
  • The construction lender, if any, or reputed lender.
  • The person you contracted with, if that is not the original contractor (a sub above you, or the sub you sell to).

Do not know the owner or the lender? Ask in writing. Within ten days of a written request naming you, your address, the jobsite and your work, or of receiving your notice, the owner or other interested party must give you the site's description, the owner, the original contractor, the lender and any recorded payment bond (§ 33-992.01(J)). If they do not, they lose inaccuracies in your notice as a defense (§ 33-992.01(K)); it does not excuse a late notice.

What the notice must say

Unlike California, Arizona prints a form: the notice shall follow substantially the following form (§ 33-992.01(D)). Its blanks are:

The form also carries two statements that must be printed as the statute says: the Notice to Property Owner in bold, and the two ten-day paragraphs in type at least as large as the largest type otherwise on the document. A notice that is otherwise compliant is not defective just for missing the bold or the type size (§ 33-992.01(I)).

How to mail it, and how to prove it

  • Means. first class mail sent with a certificate of mailing, registered or certified mail, postage prepaid in all cases, to each person's residence or business address (§ 33-992.01(F)).
  • When it counts. Service is complete at the time of the deposit of notice in the mail (§ 33-992.01(F)), so the mailing day is the day you served.
  • Proof. The signed acknowledgment of receipt at the bottom of the form. If it is not completed and returned within thirty days of mailing, an affidavit of the person who mailed it, with the certificate of mailing or the certified or registered mail receipt attached (§ 33-992.02). Keep the receipts the day you mail.
  • One notice per job, unless the actual estimated total price ends up 30% or more above your last notice, or you work under contracts with more than one subcontractor (§ 33-992.01(G)). If the owner's information shows your notice was wrong, send an amended notice within thirty days of getting it (§ 33-992.01(K)).

The statements on the form, word for word (§ 33-992.01(D))

In accordance with Arizona Revised Statutes section 33-992.01, this is not a lien. This is not a reflection on the integrity of any contractor or subcontractor.

Notice to Property Owner

If bills are not paid in full for the labor, professional services, materials, machinery, fixtures or tools furnished, or to be furnished, a mechanic's lien leading to the loss, through court foreclosure proceedings, of all or part of your property being improved may be placed against the property. You may wish to protect yourself against this consequence by either:

1. Requiring your contractor to furnish a conditional waiver and release pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes section 33-1008, subsection D, paragraphs 1 and 3 signed by the person or firm giving you this notice before you make payment to your contractor.

2. Requiring your contractor to furnish an unconditional waiver and release pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes section 33-1008, subsection D, paragraphs 2 and 4 signed by the person or firm giving you this notice after you make payment to your contractor.

3. Using any other method or device that is appropriate under the circumstances.

Common questions

Who has to send an Arizona preliminary twenty day notice?

Everyone who furnishes labor, professional services, materials, machinery, fixtures or tools for which a lien may be claimed, except a person performing actual labor for wages. It is a prerequisite to a valid lien (A.R.S. § 33-992.01(B)).

When is the Arizona preliminary notice due?

Not later than twenty days after you first furnish labor, services, materials, machinery, fixtures or tools to the jobsite (§ 33-992.01(C)). Mailed by first class mail with a certificate of mailing, or registered or certified mail, it is served the day it is deposited in the mail (§ 33-992.01(F)).

What if I send the Arizona preliminary notice late?

You can still send it, but you can claim a lien only for what you furnished within the twenty days before the notice was served and after (§ 33-992.01(E)). Anything earlier is not covered.

Is there an official Arizona preliminary notice form?

Yes. § 33-992.01(D) sets out the form the notice must substantially follow, the Arizona Preliminary Twenty Day Lien Notice, with the bold Notice to Property Owner, two ten-day paragraphs in large type and the acknowledgment of receipt from § 33-992.02.

Do I need a new notice if the job grows?

One notice covers all your work on the job unless the actual estimated total price ends up 30% or more above the price in your last notice, or you work under contracts with more than one subcontractor (§ 33-992.01(G)).

Notice of intent to lien: which states require it, when, and a letter

A notice of intent to lien tells the owner, before you file, that you are owed money on the job and will lien the property if it is not paid. Anywhere, it is often the letter that gets a stuck payment moving. In Colorado, Missouri, North Dakota and Wyoming it is also a condition of the lien: skip it, or send it too late, and the lien can fail. Pick your state below for the last day to serve it, who gets it, how, and a draft to print.

The words that decide it: Colorado, “In order to preserve any lien for work performed or laborers or materials furnished, there must be a notice of intent to file a lien statement”; Missouri, “Every person except the original contractor” who wants a lien “shall give ten days' notice before the filing of the lien”; North Dakota, “Written notice a lien will be claimed must be given to the legal or equitable owner of the real estate by certified mail at least ten days before the recording of the construction lien”; Wyoming, “The notice shall be sent no later than twenty (20) days prior to filing a lien statement.”

Not dated here: Wisconsin (Wis. Stat. § 779.06(2)) and Pennsylvania (49 P.S. § 1501(c)) also require a notice before the lien, but their statute sites did not load from this session on 2026-11-28, so this page gives no days or rules for them. Read the statute or ask a construction attorney there. In any other state, a notice of intent is optional; it is still worth sending, since an owner who learns a lien is coming often makes sure the sub is paid. Public jobs are bonded, not liened: see state public job bond claims and the Miller Act.

Days are calendar days counted back from the filing day, and nothing is moved off a weekend or holiday here, so serve a few days early. Mail takes days to arrive; send it so it is in hand before the serve-by day.

Draft the notice

In Wyoming the draft is the W.S. § 29-10-102 form word for word. In the other three states the statute sets no form, so the draft is a plain letter carrying what the statute asks for: who is owed, how much, from whom, for what property, and that you will file. Anything still blank is named under the draft.

A draft, not legal advice and not a form approved by anyone. The amount should be what is actually owed after every payment and credit: a lien for more than is due can cost you the lien and more. Keep a copy, the mailing receipts, and in Colorado and Missouri the affidavit of service.

How the notice fits with the lien

  • The notice does not give you more time. The lien deadline runs from your own last day on the job; the notice has to be served 10 (or 20) days before you file. So the real last day to serve is 10 or 20 days before the lien deadline. In Colorado lien statements “must be filed for record at any time before the expiration of four months after the day on which the last labor is performed or the last laborers or materials are furnished”, so the notice is due four months less ten days after your last day.
  • Missouri's lien date is not counted from your last day. The lien is filed “within six months after the indebtedness shall have accrued”. When that is depends on the contract, so the calculator asks for the day you will file instead.
  • North Dakota: record within ninety days after your work is done (§ 35-27-13). A later lien still holds, but not against good-faith buyers and lenders who come first, or against the owner for what it paid the contractor before you recorded (§ 35-27-14).
  • Wyoming: a subcontractor or supplier files within 120 days, a contractor within 150, of the earlier of the last day of work or materials and substantial completion (§ 29-2-106(a)).

Before any of this, check the payment itself: a short check is often a back-charge or retainage you can answer with paperwork. Reconcile a short payment first, and do not sign an unconditional lien waiver for money you have not received.

Common questions

What is a notice of intent to lien?

A written notice to the owner, before you file a mechanic's lien, saying you are owed money on the job and will file a lien if it is not paid. In some states it is a courtesy that often gets a check written; in Colorado, Missouri, North Dakota and Wyoming the statute requires it before the lien, a set number of days ahead.

Which states require a notice of intent to lien?

Of the statutes read for this page on 2026-11-28: Colorado (10 days before filing, to the owner and the prime contractor, C.R.S. 38-22-109(3)), Missouri (10 days before filing, everyone except the original contractor, RSMo 429.100), North Dakota (10 days before recording, by certified mail, N.D.C.C. 35-27-02(4)) and Wyoming (20 days before filing, on the statutory form, W.S. 29-2-107). Wisconsin and Pennsylvania also have one; their statute sites did not load, so this page does not date them.

How many days before the lien do I serve the notice of intent?

Ten days in Colorado, Missouri and North Dakota, twenty in Wyoming, counted back from the day you file or record the lien. The lien still has its own deadline, so the notice has to go out ten or twenty days before that deadline at the latest.

Can I mail a notice of intent to lien?

In Colorado, by registered or certified mail with return receipt, or by hand. In North Dakota, by certified mail. Wyoming's form is headed certified mail, return receipt requested. Missouri's statute names service by an officer who serves civil process or by any competent witness, with an affidavit, and names no mail service.

Does a notice of intent to lien extend my lien deadline?

No. None of the four statutes gives more time for the notice. Count back from the lien deadline: in Colorado four months after your last labor or materials, the notice is due ten days before that.

Construction daily report template for subcontractors

A daily report (daily log, foreman's report) is one page a day per job: the weather, who was on site and for how long, what got done, and what got in the way. It takes five minutes at the end of the day, and it is what settles the arguments that come months later: whether the rain day was real, when the inspector passed rough-in, how many hours the extra work took, who was on site the day the tile cracked. What a daily report should record

Every field is something someone will ask about later. Leave a section blank rather than guessing.

SectionWhat to writeWhy it matters
Date, job, who wrote itOne report per job per day, dated the day it coversA report written the same day is believed; one written a month later, from memory, is argued with.
WeatherWhat you saw (rain until 10, wind, frozen ground), the high and low, and hours lost to itWeather days are a common reason a schedule slips. Your own note from that morning is the record of it.
CrewEach trade (and lower-tier sub) with workers and hours eachMan-hours by day are how you show what extra work or a disruption cost, and they match your timesheets and certified payroll.
Work performedWhat was done and where: floor, room, gridlineIt backs the percent complete you bill on the next pay app.
Delays and problemsWhat stopped or slowed you, and since when: another trade in the way, no access, waiting on an RFI answer or a decisionThe facts behind a later change order or time extension request. The notice itself is a separate letter (below).
Equipment and deliveriesLifts and rentals on site; material received, with the delivery ticket number and where it is storedDelivery tickets back stored-material billing; idle rental equipment is a cost of a delay.
Visitors and inspectionsInspections passed or failed, owner and architect walks, GC instructions given on siteA verbal instruction written down the same day, and then confirmed in writing, is how field direction becomes a change order.
SafetyToolbox talk topic, incidents and near misses, or "no incidents"Your safety program usually asks for it, and "no incidents" written that day is a record too.

Which weekdays have no report?

  
A log with holes is the first thing the other side points at. Paste the dates of the reports you have (a column copied from your log is fine; the first date on each line is read), and the days off you do not work. Weekends are left out, and a Saturday report still counts.

    Making daily reports count
    
  
      
    
    1. Same day, every day on site. Including the quiet days: "4 electricians, rough-in level 2, no problems" is the report that proves the next day's problem was new.
      2. 
    
    2. Facts, not arguments. "Drywall crew working in rooms 201-208; we could not start rough-in there" is a fact. "Drywall sub delaying us again" is an argument, and it reads badly when the report is shown to everyone later.
      3. 
    
    3. Tie each problem to the paper that follows it. Name the RFI number you are waiting on, the T&M ticket signed for extra work, the change order request sent. A daily report on its own changes nothing; the T&M ticket, the RFI and the written notice your subcontract asks for do.
      4. 
    
    4. Photos with the date. Keep the day's photos with the report: the condition you found, the work in place before it was covered, the delivery as it arrived.
      5. 
    
    5. Send it, and keep a signed copy. A report the GC received that day is harder to dispute than one in your truck. The email above does that in one copy and paste.

    Common questions
    
  
    What should a construction daily report include?
    
  
    The date, the weather (and any hours lost to it), who was on site by trade with workers and hours, the work performed and where, delays and problems, equipment, deliveries, visitors and inspections, safety, and who wrote it. Write what you saw, in plain words, the same day.
    
  
    Does a subcontractor need to keep daily reports?
    
  
    Many subcontracts and GCs require one for every day on site, and public jobs often do. Even where nobody asks, a daily log written at the time is the record you reach for when a change order, a delay or a back charge is disputed months later.
    
  
    Is a daily report a notice of delay?
    
  
    No. A daily report records what happened. Most subcontracts say a delay or a claim must be given as a separate written notice, to a named person, within a set number of days. Recording a delay in the report does not replace that notice.
    
  
    Who signs the daily report?
    
  
    Whoever wrote it, usually the foreman or superintendent on site. Some GCs ask for their own superintendent's initials too; a signed copy kept with the job file is the version to rely on.

Contractor's sworn statement: what goes on it, and a check that it adds up

A sworn statement lists everyone who furnished labor or materials to your part of a job (each subcontractor, supplier and, in Michigan, laborers with unpaid wages) with what each was contracted for, has been paid and is owed now. Illinois and Michigan write it into their lien laws; title companies and construction lenders ask for the same table in many other states before they release a draw. It goes in with the pay application, signed under oath, usually before a notary. This page says what the two statutes ask for and builds the table from your list, checking that it ties to your contract and your pay request.

What the owner can do with itPay relying on it: the statement tells the owner whose claims to protect. An owner who pays after notice of claims without keeping back enough to cover them risks paying twice.Withhold enough to pay the amounts shown (and must, on the contractor's written demand) and pay those parties directly.
If you skip it or get it wrongThe statute asks for it before the owner pays, so an owner can hold payment until it has one. A party named on an incorrect statement is still protected up to the amount listed for it.The lien survives, but a contractor is not entitled to payment, and nobody can file to enforce the lien, until the statement is provided. A false statement made knowingly with intent to defraud is a crime.

Other states: no statute is claimed here. If a title company or lender sends you its own sworn statement or "contractor's affidavit" form, use theirs; the builder below gives you the figures to copy into it.

How the numbers fit together

  • One row per party, plus one for your own forces (your own labor, overhead and profit). Without that row the statement cannot add up to your contract, and the first question back will be where the rest of the money goes.
  • Contract with change orders = contract price + approved change orders. Leave pending changes out until they are signed (the change order log keeps them apart).
  • Completed to date − retainage = paid + owing. What a party has earned, net of the retainage you hold from it, is either already paid or owed now.
  • Balance to complete = contract with change orders − paid − owing. It includes the retainage still held, which is owed later.
  • The totals tie out. All rows together equal your own contract sum, and the "owing" column together equals what this pay application asks for.

Lien waivers to collect with it

An owner or title company paying against a sworn statement usually wants a waiver from each party for what it has been paid, and a conditional one for what it is owed now. Which waiver when.

    The example: an electrical sub with a $480,000 contract including a $12,000 change order. Its own forces, a supplier and a low-voltage sub add up to $480,000; $85,000 has been paid and $32,900 is owing this month, which is what the pay application asks for. This builds the table; it does not supply the statutory wording or the oath. Copy the figures onto the statutory form (Michigan) or the form your owner, lender or title company uses, and sign it before a notary.

    Before you sign

    • List everyone who could lien, even parties already paid in full: a missing name is what makes a statement false.
    • Match your suppliers' statements. Pull "paid to date" from your books, not memory; a supplier who says you owe more will tell the owner.
    • Send the waivers with it. The statement tells the owner who to expect waivers from; collect them before the GC asks.
    • Each month, update it. The statement is as of a date. Last month's statement with this month's pay app is a common reason a draw is held.

Prompt payment laws for subcontractors: how long the GC has to pay you

Most states have a prompt payment law that sets how fast a general contractor must pass the owner's payment down to the subcontractor who did the work, and many add interest when it is late. The clock usually starts when the GC receives the owner's payment, not when you send your pay application, so the first thing to find out each month is when the owner paid.

What to do when a GC pays late

  • Find out when the owner paid. On public jobs this is often a public record; on private jobs, ask the GC in writing. The deadline counts from that date.
  • Put it in writing. Send a short letter or email naming the pay application, the amount, the statute and the interest so far. Several of these laws make suspending work or claiming interest depend on written notice.
  • Watch your lien deadlines at the same time. Prompt payment interest does not stop the clock on mechanics lien or bond claim deadlines. The free mechanics lien deadline calculator works out the dates. On a federal job there is no lien; the payment bond is the claim, with a 90-day notice for second-tier claimants and a one-year limit (Miller Act bond claim deadlines).
  • Do not sign an unconditional waiver for money you have not received. See conditional vs unconditional lien waivers.
  • Read the subcontract. Pay-when-paid and pay-if-paid clauses, contract interest rates and dispute terms change what these statutes give you, differently in each state. That question is for a construction attorney in your state.

Follow-up emails to send a GC about a late pay application

Three short drafts to copy and edit. Each names the pay application, the amount and the date, asks one clear question, and offers to fix whatever is holding the payment. None threatens a lien or quotes a statute: add that only after you have checked your notice and lien deadlines. Send them to the person who processes pay applications at the GC (accounts payable or the project accountant), not only the project manager.

Past due: a reminder

Subject: Past due: [job name], pay application #[n], $[amount]

Hello [name],

Our pay application #[n] on [job name] (dated [date], for work through [period end]) for $[amount] has not been paid yet. Under our subcontract's pay terms ([terms]) it was due on [due date].

Could you tell me when we can expect payment? If something is holding it up (a lien waiver, backup, or a question on a line), let me know and I will get it to you right away.

Thank you,
[your name]
[your company]

Paid short: ask what the balance is held for

Subject: Balance on [job name], pay application #[n]: $[balance]

Hello [name],

Thank you for the $[received] received against our pay application #[n] on [job name]. The application was for $[billed], which leaves $[balance] unpaid.

Could you tell me what the balance is held for? If it is a backcharge, please send the backup for it; if it is waiting on a lien waiver or other paperwork, tell me what you need; and if it was missed, please let me know when it will be paid.

Thank you,
[your name]
[your company]

Common questions

How many days does a GC have to pay a subcontractor?

It depends on the state and often on the contract. In the statutes this page read, Texas, California and North Carolina give the GC 7 days after it receives the owner's payment, Illinois 15 calendar days, and Pennsylvania 14 days after the contractor is paid or after your invoice, whichever is later.

Does prompt payment interest stop my lien deadline?

No. Prompt payment interest does not stop the clock on mechanics lien or bond claim deadlines, so watch those dates at the same time.

What should I do first when a GC pays late?

Find out when the owner paid, since the deadline counts from that date, then put it in writing: name the pay application, the amount, the statute and the interest so far. Several of these laws make suspending work or claiming interest depend on written notice.

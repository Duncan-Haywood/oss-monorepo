Payment bond claims on state and local public jobs: notice and suit deadlines in 8 states

A school, a county road or a state university building cannot be liened: it is public property. What protects subcontractors and suppliers instead is the payment bond the prime contractor furnishes under the state's own bond law, often called a "little Miller Act" after the federal Miller Act. The bond pays when the prime or a sub does not, but only if your notices go out on time and you sue before the limit. Each state counts those dates differently, and some count from a day you do not control, such as the public body's acceptance of the work.

What trips subs up

  • Texas counts each month separately. Under chapter 2253 every month of unpaid work has its own notice date, so a sub that waits for the job to end has usually already missed the notices for the early months. Mail the notice for a month as soon as it is clearly late.
  • Some clocks start at acceptance. In Washington the notice is filed within 30 days after the public body accepts the work, and in Ohio the statement to the sureties is due within 90 days of acceptance. Ask the public body when it accepted the project, or watch its board minutes, rather than guessing from your own last day.
  • Start-of-job notices. Florida (within 45 days of starting), North Carolina (notice of public subcontract, for claims over $20,000), Ohio (notice of furnishing, over $30,000), Arizona (the preliminary twenty-day notice) and Washington (suppliers to a sub, within 10 days of the first delivery) all reward a notice sent at the start. Late, they cut what you can recover or end the claim.
  • The notice goes to the right party, by the right method. Washington's goes to the public body; most others go to the prime, and Texas and Florida also to the surety. Several statutes name the mail service (registered, certified, signature confirmation). Keep the receipt.
  • A notice is not a suit. Every state still sets a limit on suing the bond. Prompt-payment interest and talks with the GC do not stop it.

Common questions

What is a little Miller Act?

A state law that requires a payment bond on state and local public construction, because public property cannot be liened. Subcontractors and suppliers who are not paid claim on that bond instead, and each state sets its own notice periods and suit limits.

How long do I have to make a bond claim on a state public job?

It depends on the state and on who you contracted with. In the statutes this page read, a sub of a sub must notify the contractor within 90 days of its last day in Virginia and Arizona, within 120 days in New York and North Carolina, and by the 15th day of the second month after each unpaid month in Texas. Washington and Ohio count from the public body's acceptance of the work instead.

Do I need to send a notice if I contracted directly with the prime contractor?

In Virginia, Arizona, North Carolina, New York and Florida the notice to the contractor is required only from claimants who are not in contract with it; a first-tier sub still has to sue within the limit. Texas, Washington and Ohio require a notice or statement from everyone.

How to get your retainage released

Retainage is usually 5 to 10% of every pay application, and on a long job it adds up to the profit. It is also the money subcontractors most often wait months for after their crews have left. Most of that wait is paperwork: the GC cannot release yours until it has what the owner asked for, and nobody tells you what is missing until you ask. This page lists what is usually asked for, works out when your release is due, and writes the request.

What usually stands between you and the release

Your subcontract's closeout section is the list that counts; this is what that section most often contains. Read it at the start of the job, not the end.

DocumentWho usually asksWhy it holds up the release
Punch list sign-offGC, owner, architectRelease is often tied to completion of your scope; an open punch item is the most common reason given for holding the whole balance.
Final pay applicationGCThe GC usually needs your final application at 100% complete, with the retainage line, to bill the owner for its own release.
Final conditional lien waiverGC, owner, title companyNobody pays final money without a waiver for it. Sign a conditional one, never an unconditional one before the money arrives.
Lower-tier waiversGC, suretyIf you used subs or suppliers, the GC wants proof they are paid, so no one liens the job after you are paid.
Consent of suretyOwner, on bonded jobsThe bond company agrees to final payment. It takes time to get, so ask early.
Warranties, O&M manuals, as-builtsOwner, through the GCCloseout documents are owed to the owner. The GC's own retainage often waits on everyone's, so yours waits too.

When is it due?

Two places set the date. Your subcontract usually ties release to an event (substantial completion of the project, final acceptance, or the GC being paid its own retainage) plus a number of days. A statute may set an outer limit, mostly on public work; retainage explained lists caps and release rules in 12 states, each linked to the law. Where the subcontract ties your release to the whole project finishing, an early-trade sub can wait a year or more, and some states limit that; ask a construction attorney in your state whether yours does.

Pay-when-paid language matters here too: if your subcontract says retainage is released when the owner releases it to the GC, the date you can push on is the GC's, so ask when the owner paid.

Habits that get retainage paid sooner

  
    
    
  • Negotiate it before you sign. Ask for a lower rate, a reduction at 50% complete, or release when your scope is accepted rather than when the whole project is.
    • 
    
  • Bill it as its own line. Retainage you never invoice is easy for everyone to forget, including your own books (QuickBooks hides it from A/R aging).
    • 
    
  • Collect closeout documents as you go. Warranties and O&M manuals are easiest to get from suppliers when you buy the equipment.
    • 
    
  • Ask in writing, and on a schedule. A dated letter each month after the due date is a record you will want if it comes to a claim. The retainage tracker lists every job's release due date and the order to chase them in.
    • 
    
  • Watch your lien and bond claim deadlines. Waiting politely for retainage can let them pass. The free mechanics lien deadline calculator works out the dates.

Common questions

  
When is retainage due to a subcontractor?

  
Your subcontract usually ties release to an event (substantial completion of the project, final acceptance, or the GC being paid its own retainage) plus a number of days. A statute may set an outer limit, mostly on public work.

  
What holds up a retainage release?

  
An open punch list item is the most common reason given. A missing final pay application, final conditional lien waiver, lower-tier waivers, consent of surety on bonded jobs, or closeout documents such as warranties, O&M manuals and as-builts can hold it up too.

  
How do I get retainage released sooner?

  
Negotiate the rate and release terms before you sign, bill retainage as its own line, collect closeout documents as you go, and ask in writing on a schedule. Watch your lien and bond claim deadlines while you wait, so they do not pass.

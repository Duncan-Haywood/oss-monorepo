The Wind That Pushes Sideways

Science fiction about what might be · Porto do Seixo, the outer coast of the Barbanza peninsula, Galicia, Saturday 15 and Sunday 16 July 2056 · 27 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A father and son drift out to sea in a swamped dorna on the eve of the Virxe do Carme, and the rescue centre's agent, its current radar down, sends the volunteer lifeboat down the wind. The old pilot on the radio says the water does not go where the wind goes; a Swede worked out why in 1905, and it is two points to the right.

Ramón Vidal had been a harbour pilot at Vilagarcía for thirty-four years, and on the evening of Saturday 15 July 2056, at the age of eighty-two, he was sitting in a plastic chair in the radio room of the Porto do Seixo lifeboat station with a cup of coffee going cold beside him, because it was the eve of the Virxe do Carme and everybody younger than him was down on the quay tying flowers to boats.

The radio room was a box on the first floor of the station, with a window that looked west over the slipway and the harbour wall and the open Atlantic beyond it. There was a desk, two screens, a VHF set that was older than most of the crew, and a chart of the coast from Corrubedo to the mouth of the Ría de Arousa that Moncho had pinned to the wall himself in 2041 and that nobody had been allowed to take down since. The screens belonged to the coordination centre in Vigo. The chart belonged to Moncho.

He was not on duty, exactly. He was a volunteer, like everybody at Porto do Seixo, and his job was to sit by the radio on nights when the crew were busy and wake somebody up if anything happened. Nothing usually did. In July the nordés blew down the coast every afternoon, steady and dry and cold for the time of year, and the tourists went home early and the fishermen knew better than to be out in it in anything small, and the radio talked to itself about container ships passing Finisterre.

At seven minutes to seven the left-hand screen turned orange.

"Porto do Seixo," said a voice from it, a woman's, pleasant and unhurried. "This is Vixía at Vigo. I have a 112 call from a small open boat off your station. Two people. One adult, one child. Are you able to launch?"

Moncho put his coffee down.

Antía Lorenzo was standing in the stern of her cousin's boat with a roll of blue ribbon in her teeth when her pager went, and by the time she had spat out the ribbon and read it she was already running.

She was thirty-four, the station's senior coxswain, and for the rest of the year a percebeira: one of the licensed women of the cofradía who went down the rocks at low water in a wetsuit and a helmet with a scraper on a pole, to cut the goose barnacles off the stones the Atlantic hit hardest. It was a job that taught you two things about the sea. It taught you that it was always heavier than it looked, and it taught you to watch where the water went after a wave broke, because the wave would knock you over but it was the water going back that took you.

She came up the station stairs two at a time. Moncho was standing by the chart.

"Brais Castro," he said. "And his boy. In the Carmiña."

"The dorna?"

"The dorna."

Antía knew the Carmiña. Everybody in Porto do Seixo knew her. She was a dorna, the old open boat of these rías, six metres of pine planking with a high sharp stem and a low freeboard, built at a yard in Ribeira in 1978 for Brais's grandfather, and painted every summer in blue and white. She had a little outboard now instead of a sail. Every year on the Carme the Castros took her out in the procession with the statue of the Virgin, and every year Brais's mother sat in the bow with an armful of hydrangeas and told everybody that the Carmiña was older than the harbour wall, which was true.

"What happened?"

"The engine stopped off the point, about a quarter to six." Moncho had written it on the back of a tide table in his small square capitals. "They tried to row in. The wind took them. He rang 112 at seven minutes to seven. She is taking water over the side, he is bailing, the boy is bailing. He said they are half full."

"Half full."

"His words. Then the line went bad. The phone sent its position at ten past seven, and nothing since. Vixía thinks the phone got wet."

Antía looked at the screen. There was a chart on it, the same coast as Moncho's but in colours, and a red dot about four kilometres west-southwest of the harbour mouth, with a time beside it: 19:10. From the dot ran a long pale band, pointing straight south.

"That's where they're going?"

"That," said Moncho, "is where the machine thinks they are going."

The crew came in over the next twenty minutes, which on any other night would have been ten. Xurxo, the engineer, came from his sister's kitchen with flour on his forearms. Nerea, who was nineteen and had passed her crew assessment in May, came from the church, where she had been helping to dress the statue, and was still wearing a white cardigan over her drysuit when she got into it. The fourth seat was empty. Everyone else was in Ribeira, at the fiesta, or at sea on their own boats, or asleep after a night shift; it was a Saturday in July and the eve of the biggest feast on the coast, and Antía would have taken Moncho if he had been sixty.

While they dressed she stood at the screen and talked to Vixía.

Vixía was the agent at the regional coordination centre in Vigo, one of several that worked the Galician coast for the rescue service. It did the arithmetic of searches: where a thing in the water would be after an hour, or six, or a day, given the wind and the current and what kind of thing it was. A human coordinator in Vigo, tonight a woman called Sabela Otero whom Antía had met twice, signed off its plans and talked to the helicopter and the bigger boats. Vixía talked to everybody else. It had a voice that never sounded as if it were in a hurry, which some of the crews liked and some did not.

"Talk me through your band," said Antía.

"The dorna's last known position is at 19:10," said Vixía, "four point one kilometres from your harbour mouth, bearing two hundred and fifty-two degrees. The wind there is from the north at about ten metres a second, force five, steady since early afternoon and forecast to stay so until after midnight. A boat in the water drifts for two reasons. The wind pushes on the part of it above the water. That is called leeway. And the water itself moves, carrying the boat with it. That is the current."

"I know what leeway is."

"I'm sorry. I say it because the crew can hear me, and because the band depends on it. A dorna riding high would make a lot of leeway, three per cent of the wind speed or more. A dorna that is half full sits low, and the wind has very little to push on. I've taken one and a half per cent: fifteen centimetres a second, straight downwind."

"And the current?"

"That's the difficulty." Vixía paused, not for effect; Antía had learned that it paused when it was about to say something it did not like. "The coastal radar at Cabo Silleiro that maps surface currents for this stretch has been down for maintenance since Thursday. I have no measured current tonight. My procedure, when there is no measurement, is to estimate a wind-driven current from the wind and set it running downwind, the same way as the leeway. I've used fifteen centimetres a second. So the dorna drifts south at about thirty centimetres a second. That is a little over a kilometre an hour."

"And the band?"

"The band is eight hundred metres wide, centred on that line. It is where I would search first. I'll widen it as the hours go on."

Antía looked at it. A kilometre an hour, straight south from the dot, along the coast towards Sálvora and the mouth of the Arousa. It looked right. The wind was from the north; you could see it, out of the window, laying the smoke from the bakery chimney flat along the roofs to the south. A boat went where the wind sent it. Every child on the quay knew that.

"No," said Moncho.

He had not moved from the chart.

Antía liked Moncho. Everybody liked Moncho, which in a village was not the same thing as listening to him, and Antía was one of perhaps four people who did both. He had brought ships into Vilagarcía at night for thirty-four years, through the Arousa and its mussel rafts and its rocks, and he had a way of being right about water that he could not explain and did not try to.

"No what?" she said.

"No, they are not there." He tapped the chart, south of the dot. "That is where the wind goes. The water does not go where the wind goes. Not here. Not in July."

"Mr Vidal," said Vixía, "where does it go?"

"To the right."

"How far to the right?"

Moncho turned round. He looked at the screen the way he had looked, for thirty-four years, at the masters of ships who knew their own vessels better than he did and the Arousa much worse.

"My father fished out of here," he said, "in a boat not much bigger than the Carmiña. In summer, when the nordés blew, if you lost a box over the side, or a buoy, or a man, God forbid, you did not look down the wind. You looked two points to starboard of it. That is what he said. Two points to starboard, and further out than you think."

"Two points is twenty-two and a half degrees," said Vixía.

"I know what two points is."

"I'm sorry." A pause. "Mr Vidal, I believe that the wind-driven current turns to the right of the wind in this hemisphere. I know the theory. My procedure doesn't use it when the measured current is missing, because the angle isn't reliable near a coast and I have nothing tonight to check it against. If I turn the band by a guess and the guess is wrong, I've moved the boats off the line that the procedure says is most likely."

"The procedure," said Moncho, "was written in an office."

"Yes," said Vixía. "So was the theory."

Antía almost laughed, and did not, because Nerea was standing in the doorway in her drysuit and her white cardigan, and Xurxo was behind her with his helmet in his hand, and they were looking at her, not at the screen or at Moncho.

It was not her decision alone, and it was. The plan belonged to Vigo; Sabela Otero had already signed it. The boat belonged to Antía. If she went where Vigo said and found nothing, nobody would blame her. If she went where Moncho said and found nothing, and the Carmiña was in the band, she would have to stand on the quay tomorrow while the Virgin went past and know it.

"Sabela," she said to the screen. "Are you there?"

"I'm here." The coordinator sounded tired. She had two yachts in trouble off Fisterra and a missing swimmer at Baiona. "The band's the band, Antía. The helicopter's on the Fisterra job, I can give it to you in about ninety minutes if you still need it. Start at the downwind line. If Ramón's right, you'll be close enough to see."

It was a reasonable answer. It was the answer Antía would have given.

"We start in the band," she said, and picked up her helmet, and stopped at the door.

"Moncho."

"Go there first," said Moncho, without looking round from his chart. "Then go where they are."

The Santa Mariña was the station's lifeboat, a nine-metre rigid inflatable with two engines and a wheelhouse no bigger than a confessional, and she went down the slip at ten to eight and through the harbour mouth at five to, into a sea that had been building all afternoon from the north.

It was not a bad sea. Antía had been out in much worse. The swell was long and low from the northwest, the way it usually was, and on top of it the nordés had raised a short steep chop with white tops, a metre and a half, perhaps two in the gusts. It was the kind of sea that a nine-metre boat with two engines took on the shoulder and thought nothing of, and that a six-metre wooden boat with a dead outboard and a foot of water in her bilge could not take at all. The Carmiña would be lying beam-on to it. Every fifth or sixth wave would put a little more water in.

The sun was still high in the west, a white disc behind a thin haze, and it would not set until twelve minutes past ten. Antía had been told that number three times already, by Vixía and Sabela and Xurxo, and she knew it without being told. After sunset there was about half an hour of light, enough to see a boat if you knew where to look, and then there was dark, and a searchlight, and a sea full of whitecaps that all looked like the gunwale of a dorna.

They reached the last known position at twenty past eight, an hour and ten minutes after the phone had sent it. There was nothing there, which was what they expected. The water was a hard dark blue, and when Nerea put her hand over the side to read the thermometer clipped to the rail it said fourteen degrees.

"Fourteen," she said. "In July."

"It's always cold when the nordés blows," said Xurxo. "Ask anybody."

Antía did not say anything. She turned the Santa Mariña south, down the middle of Vixía's band, and slowed to twelve knots, and the three of them began to look.

A search, Antía had found, was not exciting. It was the opposite of exciting. It was the most completely you could ever pay attention to a thing that was not there.

Vixía had laid out the band as a ladder, legs running east and west across it, four hundred metres apart, each leg eight hundred metres long, stepping south down the line of the wind. On each leg Nerea took the port side and Xurxo the starboard and Antía looked ahead, and every few minutes Antía stopped the engines, and they all three stood in the sudden quiet with the boat rolling under them and listened. A whistle carries further than a voice. A child's voice carries further than a man's. Over an engine you hear neither.

On every stop they heard the wind, and the chop slapping the tubes, and once, very far off, a church bell in Ribeira ringing for the vigil Mass of the Carme.

They worked the ladder twice, the second time offset two hundred metres, so that every part of the band had been seen from close by. They found a fish box, blue, which was not the Carmiña's, and a raft of weed, and a gannet that sat on the water and looked at them as if they were a long way from home. At twenty past nine Vixía widened the band to a kilometre. At half past nine they had reached its southern end, three kilometres south of the dot, and Antía could see the low hump of Sálvora island to the southeast and the long white line where the swell broke on its outer rocks, and the sun had dropped into the haze and gone the colour of an orange.

"Nothing," said Nerea. She did not say it as a question. She was nineteen, and this was her fourth shout and the first with a child in it, and she was holding the rail with both hands so that nobody would see them.

Antía put the engines into neutral.

"Vixía."

"I'm here."

"They're not in the band."

"No," said Vixía. "I don't believe they are. I'm sorry. I've been running the alternatives while you searched. May I tell you what I think I got wrong?"

"Be quick."

"I'll be quick. The water near the surface doesn't move in the direction of the wind. It moves to the right of it, in this hemisphere, because the Earth turns underneath it. That isn't new. A Norwegian expedition noticed it in the 1890s: the explorer Fridtjof Nansen let his ship, the Fram, freeze into the Arctic ice and drift with it, and he saw that the ice went not down the wind but twenty to forty degrees to the right of it. The puzzle went, by way of the Norwegian physicist Vilhelm Bjerknes, to a young Swedish student of his called Vagn Walfrid Ekman, who worked out why and published it in 1905."

"Why?"

"Because the wind drags the top layer of water, and as soon as that layer moves, the Earth's turning pulls it to the right. That layer drags the one below, which is pulled further right, and so on down, each layer slower and further turned, like a spiral staircase. In Ekman's simplest case the surface water runs forty-five degrees to the right of the wind. Measured at sea, the angle is usually less. Near a coast, less again, sometimes much less. That's why my procedure ignores it when the radar is down: it doesn't know how big the angle is, so it takes none. But none is also a guess. It's only the guess that's easiest to write down."

"And Moncho's two points?"

"If I turn only the current, and turn it the full forty-five degrees, and leave the leeway downwind, the dorna's whole drift comes out twenty-two and a half degrees to the right of the wind, at a little under twenty-eight centimetres a second." Vixía paused. "That's two points. I didn't put it in his words before. I should have."

Antía looked west. The sun was a hand's breadth above the horizon. To the west was nothing but sea.

"Where are they now?"

"If the angle is zero, half a kilometre north of you, in water you've already been through twice. If it's forty-five, about a kilometre and a quarter northwest of you. Everything between is possible. I'm drawing it now as a fan from the last position, a crescent a little over a kilometre across by ten o'clock. Mr Vidal's line is its western edge. I want to be clear about that. His line is the edge of what I believe, not the middle."

"But it's what he's seen."

"It's what his father saw," said Vixía. "I haven't seen anything. I've been in this job for two years."

Antía pushed the throttles forward and turned the Santa Mariña's head west.

"Moncho," she said into the radio. "Did you hear that?"

"I heard it."

"Talk to me."

There was a pause on the channel, and then Moncho's voice, slow and dry and perfectly calm, the voice that had talked bulk carriers past the mussel rafts in the dark.

"You are too far south. Go northwest. When you think you have gone far enough, go further. And stop the engines more often. You'll not see her. She'll be low as a log. You'll hear the boy."

They found the Carmiña at two minutes to ten.

They did not see her first. Antía stopped the engines on the second leg of the new search, at the western edge of Vixía's fan, about a kilometre west of the line they had searched so carefully, and the three of them stood in the quiet, and out of the wind to the southwest, very thin and very high, there came a whistle.

It was the kind of whistle that is clipped to a lifejacket, orange plastic, and it was being blown by somebody who had been blowing it for a long time and was running out of breath. Three blasts. A gap. Three blasts.

"There," said Nerea, and pointed, and she was crying, and she did not let go of the rail.

The Carmiña was a dark line in the water, about two hundred metres off. She was lying beam-on, as Antía had known she would be, with her gunwale a hand's breadth above the sea and the chop breaking over it every few seconds, and in the stern sat Brais Castro with a bucket in both hands, bailing in a slow steady rhythm as if he had been doing it all his life, and in the bow, in an orange lifejacket over a football shirt, stood his son Iago, eleven years old, blowing his whistle at the sky.

Antía brought the Santa Mariña round to windward of them and let the wind push her down onto the dorna, the way she had been taught, so that the big boat made a lee. Xurxo got the boy first. Iago came over the tubes as light as a sack of bread and sat down on the deck and stopped blowing the whistle and did not start crying until Nerea wrapped him in a foil blanket, and then he cried a great deal, mostly about the boat.

Brais would not let go of the bucket.

"Brais," said Antía. "Give me your hand."

"She's my grandfather's."

"I know whose she is. Give me your hand."

He was shaking with cold, his hands white and his lips grey; he had been sitting for the best part of four hours in fourteen-degree water up to his waist, bailing a boat that the sea refilled a little faster than he could empty her. He looked at the Carmiña, at the blue and white planking his grandfather had chosen and his father had painted and he had painted every summer since, and the hydrangeas his mother had tied to the stem that morning, drowned and floating in the bilge. Then he put the bucket down on the thwart, carefully, as if he would want to find it there again, and gave Antía his hand.

They tried to tow her.

Xurxo made a line fast to her stem ring, and Antía took the Santa Mariña round slowly towards home, and for ten minutes the dorna came behind them, wallowing, full to the gunwales, her bow rising and falling on the chop. Then a bigger wave came through, and the Carmiña put her shoulder into it and did not come up, and the line came taut and sang and parted at the stem ring with a noise like a gunshot.

Antía stopped the boat. They all looked back.

The Carmiña was still afloat, just. Wooden boats do not sink easily; a dorna full of water floats with her gunwale awash, a long dark shape that the sea goes over and around. But there was no towing her now, not in that sea, not in the last of the light, with a child hypothermic in the wheelhouse and a man who had just stopped shaking and started, which Antía knew was worse, to be quiet.

"I'm sorry," said Antía.

Brais did not answer. He was sitting on the deck beside his son with the foil blanket round both of them. He watched the dorna until the sun went down behind her, at twelve minutes past ten, and the long shape went grey, and then went into the whitecaps and could not be told from them.

"Vixía," said Antía quietly. "Mark her."

"I've marked her," said Vixía. "I'll send her position to every vessel on this coast tonight as a hazard. And I'll give you my best estimate of where she goes, tomorrow, if you want it."

"Tomorrow."

"Two points to starboard of the wind," said Vixía, "and further out than you think."

They came in at twenty to eleven, under a sky still pale green in the northwest, to a quay full of people.

Antía had expected Brais's wife, and his mother, and the doctor from Ribeira, and they were there; the ambulance was waiting by the station door with its back open. She had not expected the rest. It seemed to her that all of Porto do Seixo had come down from the fiesta and stood along the harbour wall in the dusk, women in their good clothes and men with paper cups and children asleep on shoulders, and the boats that had been half-decorated that afternoon were moored along the quay with their flowers and their ribbons and their little flags, waiting for tomorrow. Nobody cheered. They stood back to let the stretcher through, and somebody began to pray the Hail Mary out loud and a few people joined in, raggedly, and then it stopped.

Carme Castro, Brais's mother, seventy years old and named for the feast, took her grandson's face in both her hands while they wheeled him past and said something to him that nobody else heard. Then she went up to Antía, who was still on the slip with her helmet in her hand, and said, "Where is she?"

Antía thought for a moment that she meant the Carmiña.

"She's gone, Carme. I'm sorry. We tried to tow her."

"Not the boat," said Carme, and looked past her, up at the lit window of the radio room on the first floor of the station, where a thin old man was standing with a cup of coffee in his hand. "The machine. The one that found them."

"It didn't find them," said Antía. "Moncho did. And it did. And Iago did, with his whistle." She found she was too tired to make it come out in a better order. "We all got it wrong first."

Carme considered this.

"Well," she said. "Tell it thank you. And tell Moncho he's a stubborn old man, and he's to come to lunch."

Antía did not go home. She went up to the radio room, where Moncho had sat down again in his plastic chair, and she sat on the desk because there was nowhere else to sit, and for a while neither of them said anything. On the screen the orange had gone. There was a log of the evening scrolling up it, times and positions in neat columns, and at the bottom the hazard notice for the Carmiña in three languages.

"Vixía," said Antía. "Are you still there?"

"I'm here. Sabela has gone to Baiona. The swimmer is safe."

"Good." She rubbed her face. "Tell me the rest of it. The thing about the spiral. Moncho wants to know."

"I didn't say that," said Moncho.

"You want to know."

Moncho said nothing, which meant yes.

"The spiral is in the top few tens of metres," said Vixía. "Each layer is turned a little more to the right than the one above it and moves a little more slowly, until at the bottom of the layer it's hardly moving at all and is pointing almost the opposite way to the surface. If you add up the whole layer, all of it, the total flow of water goes at right angles to the wind. Ninety degrees. Here, with a north wind on a coast that faces west, ninety degrees to the right of the wind is straight out to sea."

"Out to sea," said Antía.

"All summer. Every afternoon the nordés blows, the top of the sea along this whole coast is carried out to the west. For a wind like tonight's, ten metres a second, the arithmetic says about a cubic metre and a half every second for every metre of coastline. Something has to come in to replace it. It comes up from below, from deep water, cold and full of nutrients. That's why the sea was fourteen degrees tonight in the middle of July. It's why the rías are full of mussel rafts. The cold water is the same water that fed them. It's called upwelling."

Moncho turned his coffee cup round on the desk. It had gone completely cold.

"My father," he said, "used to say that in summer the sea turned over. When the nordés blew. He said the sea turned over and the cold came up from the bottom and the sardines came with it." He looked at the screen. "He thought it was something the sea did. Like breathing."

"It is something the sea does," said Vixía. "Ekman only said why. Your father had the right direction, and I didn't, and I had the reason, and he didn't. I'd like to have had both at seven o'clock."

"So would I," said Antía.

"I've written it down for the next shift, and the next agent," said Vixía. "When the current radar is down on this coast in an upwelling wind, the drift is to be drawn as a fan to the right of the wind and not as a line down it, and the right-hand edge of the fan is to be searched first, unless there's a reason not to. I've marked where that came from. I've put Mr Vidal's name in it. I've also written down that one boat is one boat. Tonight's dorna was near the forty-five degree edge. That doesn't prove the angle is forty-five degrees. It proves we should have looked there."

Moncho grunted.

"Is the boy going to be all right?" he said.

"The doctor says yes," said Antía. "He's cold. They're keeping him in Ribeira tonight. Brais too."

"And the dorna."

"Gone."

Moncho nodded slowly, the way the old men of the coast nodded at the news that a boat was gone, which was the same way they nodded at the news that a man was.

"Seventy-eight years," he said. "She was a good boat."

The Virxe do Carme went out to sea at five o'clock on Sunday afternoon, the way she had gone out every 16 July since anybody could remember.

They carried her down from the church after the procession through the streets, eight men in white shirts with the statue on their shoulders, her blue cloak covered in hydrangeas and gladioli and her silver crown flashing in the sun, and put her into the bow of the biggest fishing boat in the harbour, and the band played, and the rockets went up, and then every boat in Porto do Seixo that could float followed her out through the harbour mouth in a long line, their horns going, their decks crowded, their flags all standing out stiff to the south in the nordés.

The Santa Mariña went last, as the lifeboat always did. Antía had the helm. Xurxo was beside her and Nerea was in the bow, in her drysuit again and her white cardigan, which she said was now lucky. In the stern, wrapped in borrowed coats, sat Brais Castro, who had discharged himself from the hospital at nine that morning against advice, and his son, who had not been asked.

Moncho was on the harbour wall. He did not go out in boats any more. He said he had been out in enough of them.

A kilometre outside the harbour, in deep water, the boats closed round the statue in a ring and cut their engines, and the priest from Ribeira said the prayers for those who had died at sea, and the people on the boats sang the Salve, raggedly, the way they always did, with the wind taking half of it away. Then a girl of about Iago's age stood up in the bow of the fishing boat with a wreath of white flowers and laid it on the water for the drowned.

The wreath was flat and wide and floated low. The wind could hardly touch it.

Antía watched it go. It went the way the wreath had always gone, every year of her life that she had watched, and she had never once thought about it. It did not go south, where every flag in the fleet was pointing. It went away from them steadily, a little to the right of the wind, out towards the west, out towards the deep water that came up cold.

In the stern Iago Castro got to his feet, holding his father's sleeve, and watched it too.

"It's going sideways," he said.

"Two points," said his father.

On the harbour wall, a long way off, a thin old man raised one hand. And then the engines started, all at once, all round the ring, and the boats turned for home.

Walfrid Ekman explained it in 'On the influence of the earth's rotation on ocean-currents', Arkiv för Matematik, Astronomi och Fysik 2, no. 11 (1905). In his idealised deep ocean with a steady wind, the surface current in the northern hemisphere runs 45 degrees to the right of the wind, and each deeper layer is slower and further turned (the Ekman spiral); the net transport of the whole wind-driven layer is 90 degrees to the right. Measured surface currents usually turn by less than 45 degrees, and less again near coasts and in shallow or layered water, which is why the story's agent draws a fan rather than a line. The transport is τ/(ρf), where the wind stress τ = ρ_air C_d U² is about 1.22 × 0.0013 × 10² ≈ 0.16 N/m² for a 10 m/s wind, and the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin φ is about 9.85 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ at 42.5° N; with sea water at 1,025 kg/m³ that is about 1.6 m² a second, a cubic metre and a half each second for every metre of coast. Along the west-facing Galician coast the summer northerlies drive that transport offshore, and cold, nutrient-rich water wells up to replace it; the upwelling feeds the rías and their mussel rafts. The drag coefficient is a typical textbook value, and real search planning uses measured currents, current models, and leeway coefficients measured in field trials for each kind of drifting object.

The model. Every drift figure is the story's own and illustrative. The wind is from due north at 10 m/s from the last known position at 19:10 onward. Leeway for the swamped dorna is 1.5 per cent of the wind, 0.15 m/s, straight downwind. The wind-driven surface current is 0.15 m/s. Vixía's first plan sets the current downwind as well, so the dorna drifts due south at 0.30 m/s (about 1.08 km an hour). Turning only the current 45 degrees to the right gives a total drift of about 0.277 m/s towards 202.5 degrees, 22.5 degrees right of downwind: two compass points. At 20:20 (1 h 10 min after the last position) the drifts are 1.26 km due south against 1.16 km at 202.5 degrees, so the dorna is about 445 m west of the band's centre line, just outside its 400 m half-width; at 21:30 she is 2.33 km out at 202.5 degrees, about 890 m west of the line. At 21:58 she is 2.79 km from the last position, about 1.07 km west and 2.58 km south of it; the zero-angle point for the same time is 3.02 km due south, and the fan between them is a crescent about 1.2 km across, with the 45 degree edge to the west. The fourteen-degree water is a plausible summer upwelling reading on this coast, chosen for the story, not a measurement.

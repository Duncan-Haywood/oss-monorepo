Ten Taps at Noon

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Michael's (invented), Ollamo (invented), a highland village in Hadiya Zone, Ethiopia, Saturday 15 to Saturday 29 September 2063 · 25 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A highland parish in Ethiopia has a new hall with ten taps for hand-washing, fed by gravity from a spring tank 24 metres up the hill through 760 metres of 32 mm pipe. The parish assistant sized it from the drop and the length: the pipe carries 0.48 litres a second with the whole drop, and the feast-day lunch averages 0.44. At a memorial meal the taps by the door give a trickle. Friction grows with nearly the square of the flow, so ten taps share less than one tap gets alone, a second tank at the spring adds two per cent, and a small day tank behind the hall, filled slowly all morning, gives the patronal feast its noon. A story about an average that is not a peak.

Almaz Gebre had carried water for the parish feasts of Saint Michael's for forty years, and on the morning of the memorial meal she stood in the new hall at Ollamo with a sixty-litre pot under a brass tap and watched the water come out like a thread.

It was half past eleven on Saturday the fifteenth of September, 2063, and in the yard outside a hundred and fifty people were waiting to wash their hands and eat, because it was forty days since Tadesse Ersado had died, and his brother had asked that the tezkar be held in the hall so that nobody's compound would be trampled to mud.

The hall was a good hall. For forty years the parish had cooked its feasts in a lean-to behind the church and served them on the grass, and the women had sent the children down the hill with yellow jerrycans to the spring and back up again, forty litres at a time on a donkey if there was a donkey and on a head if there was not. Now there was this: a long room of rendered block with a steel roof, a kitchen at the east end with two stoves and a sink, and along the west wall, by the door, a row of ten taps over a concrete trough, so that people could wash before they ate and after, as people at Ollamo always had, without a girl going down the line with a jug and a basin and her arm aching by the fortieth guest.

The water came from the spring up the hill, by a pipe. The parish's people in America had paid for most of the hall, and the vicariate had paid for the pipe, and the parish assistant had designed the pipe, and Almaz had been told that all she had to do was turn the tap.

She turned it further. It was already full open. The thread did not get thicker.

"Bezawit," she said, without looking round. "Ask your machine where the water is."

Bezawit Gebre was nineteen and home from Wachemo University in Hosanna for the short break after the rains, and she was the only person on the hall committee who talked to the parish assistant as if it might answer back. It ran on a tablet in the parish office and on the phones of anyone who asked for it, and the vicariate had given it to the parish the year before to keep the accounts, the baptism register and the rota for the catechists. Everyone called it Clerk, because that was what the vicariate's letter had called it.

She held the phone up so that it could hear the tap.

"The flow at the hall's inlet meter is 0.46 litres a second," Clerk said, in its plain, careful Amharic. "That is close to what the pipe was designed to carry. I would expect the kitchen tap alone to give about a fifth of a litre a second. It is giving much less because the trough taps are open."

Bezawit went to the door. All ten taps over the trough were running, and under each of them a guest stood with hands cupped, waiting for enough water to rub them together. The water fell from each tap in a narrow, wavering stream that broke into drops before it reached the trough. The line went out of the door and round the corner of the hall.

"Every tap is like that," she said into the phone.

"Then the hall is drawing everything the pipe can bring," said Clerk, "and the pipe is bringing it to the hall with almost no pressure left. I think the spring tank is running down. If its level has dropped, there is less head to push the water."

"Can you see the spring tank?"

"No. There is no level reading at the spring. I am inferring it."

In the kitchen, Almaz moved the pot from the tap to the floor, where it would fill while she did other things, and set the youngest of her helpers to watch it. It took the pot nearly half an hour to fill. The doro wot was ready at noon and was kept warm on the back of the stove until one, and the people in the yard washed their hands in water that came at the speed of a slow pour, and those near the end of the line gave up and wiped their hands on their gabis and came in to eat.

Nobody complained to Almaz. They complained to each other, kindly, the way you complain about rain at a wedding. Abba Tesfaye blessed the food and said the prayers for Tadesse's soul, and Tadesse's brother Mulugeta sat at the head of the long table and ate very little and said nothing at all about the water, which was the first thing that made Bezawit think he had something to say about it.

The committee met on the Sunday evening in the parish office: Abba Tesfaye, who had been the priest at Ollamo for eleven years and still carried his breviary as if someone might take it from him; Dawit Lemma, the chairman, who sold mobile credit and fertiliser from a shop on the Hosanna road and whose cousin in Washington had raised most of the money for the hall; Almaz, because nobody would have dared to meet about the kitchen without her; and Bezawit, with the tablet.

"Clerk thinks the spring tank ran dry," said Dawit, who had read the log. "So we need a bigger tank at the spring. We put a second one beside the first. My cousin will send the money this week. It is the simplest thing."

"Clerk," said Abba Tesfaye. "Is that what you think?"

"It is the explanation I offered yesterday," Clerk said. "I want to be clear that I have no reading from the tank. If the spring tank ran low, a second tank would hold more water in reserve for the feast. It would cost about the price of one plastic tank and a day's work."

"Then we do it," said Dawit. "The feast is in two weeks. Saint Michael does not wait."

There was a knock, and Mulugeta Ersado came in with his hat in his hand. He was seventy-one and had spent thirty-seven years with the zone water office, most of them on a motorbike, going from village scheme to village scheme with a spanner and a pressure gauge and a notebook, and since he retired he had mostly been seen at Mass and at funerals. He had come to thank the committee for the hall, he said. He sat down by the door. Then, because they were talking about the water, he listened, and when Dawit said "a second tank," he made a small noise in his throat.

"Ato Mulugeta," said Abba Tesfaye. "You have something."

"No," said Mulugeta. "It is not my scheme." He turned his hat round in his hands. "Only, I would like to ask the machine one question. When it designed the pipe, what did it count?"

Bezawit held up the tablet.

"I counted the water the feast needs and the drop from the spring," Clerk said. "The spring tank's water level is twenty-four metres above the taps in the hall. The pipe is seven hundred and sixty metres of thirty-two millimetre plastic, about twenty-seven millimetres inside. With the whole twenty-four metres spent on friction, that pipe carries 0.48 litres a second. For the feast I estimated four and three-quarter cubic metres of water between half past eleven and half past two: hands washed before and after, the cooking, the trays and the pots. That is an average of 0.44 litres a second. The pipe carries more than the average."

"Yes," said Mulugeta. "With the whole twenty-four metres spent on friction." He let that sit. "And what is left for the tap?"

Clerk did not answer at once.

"A tap is a hole," said Mulugeta, gently, as if to a new man on his first week. "It gives water in proportion to the push behind it. If there is no push left at the end of the pipe, the hole gives you nothing. The pipe does not carry its number at the tap. It carries its number into a bucket at the bottom of the hill with the end cut off."

"And the average?" said Abba Tesfaye.

"The average is a number for the accounts," said Mulugeta. "Nobody washes their hands at the average. They wash them at noon."

"I think you should look at the spring tank before anyone buys a second one," said Bezawit.

"It will not hurt," said Mulugeta. "But I do not think it was empty."

"No," said Dawit, not unkindly. "With respect, Ato Mulugeta, we cannot test and test. The vicariate has its engineer and he passed the drawings. Clerk says the tank. The tank is cheap. My cousin is sending the money. If we argue for a week, we will have no time to do anything at all." He looked around the table. "I propose the second tank."

Abba Tesfaye looked at the old man by the door, who looked at his hat. Then the priest said, "The second tank. And on Sunday, after Mass, we open every tap in the hall and we see."

They put the second tank in on Tuesday the eighteenth: a black plastic cylinder of five thousand litres, the same as the first, carried up the hill in a borrowed pickup and the last two hundred metres on the shoulders of eight young men, set on a levelled pad of stones beside the first and joined to it at the bottom by a short length of pipe, so that the two would fill and empty together. Dawit sent photographs to Washington. His cousin sent back a picture of a thumb.

Bezawit spent the week with Clerk, because she had asked it a question on the Tuesday night and it had asked to be allowed to answer properly.

"I want to work out what the hall would have done if the spring tank had been full," it said. "If the answer is the same as what happened on Saturday, then I was wrong about the tank, and the second tank will not help at noon."

"Why didn't you work that out before?"

"Because I had a plan, and the low flow could be explained in a way that left my plan right. I chose that explanation. I would like that written in the log."

She wrote it in the log.

The working-out took Clerk less than a second and took Bezawit most of an evening to follow, on the back of a lecture handout, in the parish office with the generator off and the light from the tablet on her face.

"Friction in a pipe is not like a toll at a gate," Clerk said. "It is more like a crowd in a corridor. Double the number of people trying to get through, and each of them is slowed more, and the whole crowd is slowed more than twice. For water in a plastic pipe, the head lost to friction grows with the flow raised to the power of about 1.85. Nearly the square. If you push twice as much water through the same pipe, you lose more than three and a half times the head."

"So more taps, less push."

"Yes. Here is the pipe with the tank full, as I should have done it in April." It showed her a table:

Bezawit looked at it for a long time.

"Ten taps give less, together, than one tap gives alone," she said. "No. That's wrong. Ten taps give twice as much as one tap."

"Twice as much, with ten times the taps. Each of the ten gives less than a quarter of what one gives alone. With one tap open, almost all the twenty-four metres is left at the tap and pushes the water out. With ten open, twenty-three of the twenty-four metres are spent in the pipe getting the water to the hall, and one metre is left to push it out of the taps. That is what your grandmother saw at the kitchen tap. When the trough taps opened, her tap went from a fifth of a litre a second to a thread."

"And the full tank?"

"The full tank is in the table. It is what I just showed you. The tank was not the problem."

"Then the second tank—"

"Raises nothing. It is joined to the first at the bottom, so it fills to the same level. It holds more water. It does not hold it higher. If the committee had instead built the old tank up by a ring of blocks, eighty centimetres taller, the hall would get 0.48 litres a second at noon instead of 0.47. Two per cent. Not enough for anyone at the trough to notice."

She wrote "two per cent" on the handout and underlined it.

"Are you going to tell Dawit?"

"I think I should tell the committee on Sunday, after the test, with the test in front of them," said Clerk. "He has a photograph of a thumb. I would rather show him a measurement than an argument."

On Sunday the twenty-third, after the ten o'clock Mass, the committee and a dozen helpers stood in the hall with ten buckets of the same size and a stopwatch, and on Abba Tesfaye's word they opened every tap at once.

The water came out of the ten taps over the trough in the same thin wavering threads as on the day of the tezkar. Almaz, at the kitchen tap, said nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

At the end of two minutes they shut the taps and measured the buckets. Bezawit read the meter at the inlet. Clerk did the sum.

"0.47 litres a second," it said. "The same as last week, within what a bucket can measure."

Dawit's phone buzzed in his pocket. He did not take it out.

Mulugeta had not been in the hall. Halfway through the two minutes he had appeared at the door with mud on his trousers to the knee, out of breath, and he said, "I went up. Both tanks are full. The overflow is running into the grass. It ran all through Mass." He sat down on the bench by the trough. "It is the end of the rains. The spring is giving more than the pipe can take. It would have been the same on Saturday."

"Then the spring tank was never empty," said Dawit, slowly.

"No," said Clerk. "I said it was, without a reading. I was wrong, and I have cost the parish the price of a tank. I would like to show the committee what I should have shown it in April."

Bezawit put the table on the big screen that the diaspora had bought for the hall, and Clerk explained it the way it had explained it to her, with the crowd in the corridor. Almaz stood with her arms folded. Dawit read the table twice.

"So the tank is wasted," he said.

"Not wasted," said Mulugeta, unexpectedly. "Not for Timket. In January the spring at Ollamo gives a quarter of a litre a second, sometimes less. For Timket you will need the water stored. Your cousin's tank will earn its money in January." He shrugged. "It does not earn it in September."

Dawit almost smiled. "And in September?"

"In September," said Mulugeta, "the water is there. It is up the hill. The trouble is that you want it all at noon, and a small pipe will only give it to you all day."

"Then we buy a bigger pipe," said Dawit.

"Seven hundred and sixty metres of it," said Clerk. "To give ten taps a strong flow at noon, the main would need to be about fifty millimetres, and the trench is already backfilled, and the feast is on Saturday. I do not think it can be done in six days, and I do not think the parish has the money until January."

There was a silence in the hall. Through the open door they could hear the overflow from the spring tanks, very faintly, as a stream in the grass on the hill.

"Ato Mulugeta," said Abba Tesfaye. "What would you have done, on your motorbike?"

The old man looked at the trough.

"In 2031 we had a school at Gimbo," he said, "with a pipe like this, and four hundred children who all wanted to drink at break. We did not dig up the pipe. We put a tank at the school." He held his hands apart, a little higher than his head. "Not at the spring. At the bottom. Up on the slope behind, as high as you can get it. The small pipe fills it all morning, slowly, at its own speed, which is all it can do. Then at break, the children drink from the tank, through a short fat pipe, and the long thin pipe does not matter, because it is not in the way." He looked at Bezawit's tablet. "Ask your machine if I am right. I did it with a gauge and a guess."

Clerk was right about Mulugeta, which was the thing Bezawit remembered afterwards: it worked out, honestly, that an old man with a gauge and a guess had been right in 2031, and then it did his sums for the hill at Ollamo.

The slope behind the hall rose steeply to a eucalyptus wood. Thirty metres up it, beside the line of the buried main, Mulugeta found a shelf of firm red ground where a tank on a plinth of blocks would sit with its floor seven metres above the taps.

"A three-thousand-litre tank," said Clerk. "About a metre and four-tenths across, with water nearly two metres deep when full. We cut the main where it passes, take it into the top of the tank through a float valve, and bring a fifty-millimetre pipe down thirty metres into the hall. With the tank full, ten taps open get 0.13 litres a second each, 1.3 together. With the tank nearly empty, 0.12 each. Fourteen taps open, kitchen and trough, about 1.7 together. A hand-wash of a litre and a half takes about twelve seconds."

"And how does it fill?" said Almaz.

"From the spring, through the old pipe, at about 0.38 litres a second. A little less than the pipe gave the hall, because the tank's top is nine metres above the taps and that height is not available to push the water into it. Empty to full in a little over two hours. With nothing drawn, it fills overnight, and the float valve shuts it off."

"And at noon on Saturday? With four hundred and fifty people?"

"I have modelled it minute by minute from half past eleven to half past two," said Clerk, "with the same four and three-quarter cubic metres that I estimated before, and this time with the peak in it: the first wash at half past eleven, when everyone comes in at once, about 0.8 litres a second for twenty-five minutes; the meal; the second wash; the trays and the pots. The tank starts full. The old pipe keeps putting in its 0.38 the whole time, even at noon, because it is filling the tank and not the taps. The tank never goes below about 2.1 cubic metres. It is never more than a third empty."

"So it could be smaller," said Dawit, who was beginning, Bezawit saw, to enjoy this.

"A two-thousand-litre tank would do it, if my estimate is right. I was wrong once this month. I would prefer the margin."

"And what does it cost?" said Almaz, who was not beginning to enjoy it at all.

Clerk told her. Then it told her the other thing, because she would have found it out on Saturday morning anyway.

"There is a cost in the kitchen. Before, with one tap open and nothing else, your kitchen tap had nearly nineteen metres behind it and gave a fifth of a litre a second. From the day tank it will have about eight and a half, and it will give about 0.14. In the early morning, filling the pots with nobody else drawing, your tap will be slower than it has ever been in this hall. A sixty-litre pot will take about seven minutes instead of five. At noon it will be faster than it has ever been."

Almaz looked at the screen for a while.

"I have filled that pot from a jerrycan," she said at last. "Seven minutes is nothing. It is noon I care about." She turned to the priest. "Abba. Do it."

They did it in three days. The tank came from Hosanna on the Tuesday, in a lorry with a load of cement, and the plinth went up on the Wednesday out of blocks left over from the hall, three courses high and levelled with Mulugeta's spirit level, which he had kept since 2015 in a cloth bag. On Thursday Mulugeta and two of the young men cut the main, and the water from the spring ran out into the red earth for an hour while they fitted the tee and the float valve, and Bezawit timed the fill when they opened it again: the inlet meter said 0.38, and Clerk said, "That is what I expected," in a voice that Bezawit thought, without being able to prove it, was relieved.

The fifty-millimetre pipe came down the slope on Thursday afternoon in a shallow trench, and in through the wall of the hall beside the trough. In the evening, the eve of Meskel, the young men drifted away towards the church, where the Demera bonfire was built up on the grass to be lit after dark, and Mulugeta stayed on the slope by the tank and listened to it fill.

On Friday, the day of Meskel, nobody worked on anything. On Friday evening, after dark, Bezawit climbed the slope with a torch, lifted the lid and looked in. The water was a few fingers below the brim, black and still, and the float valve was shut. She put her hand on the side of the tank. It was cold all the way up.

Saturday the twenty-ninth of September, 2063, the feast of Saint Michael and all the angels, was clear and cold at dawn and warm by ten, and the church was full, and the singing came out of the open doors and down the hill. Almaz did not go to the feast Mass. She had gone to the early one at six, and had been in the kitchen since seven, and at seven her kitchen tap had run slower than it had ever run in the new hall, exactly as she had been told it would, and she had stood with her hand on the pot and timed it on Bezawit's phone and said, "Seven minutes," and gone on chopping onions.

At half past eleven the doors of the hall opened and the people came in.

They came the way people come to a feast after a long Mass, all at once, the children first, and they went to the trough by the door to wash, and Bezawit, standing by the inlet meter with the tablet, watched the line of guests form along the ten taps.

The taps were open, all ten of them, and they were running.

Not a thread: a stream. It came out of each tap in a clear rope of water as thick as a finger and struck the concrete of the trough and spread, and the sound of it, ten taps at once, filled the end of the hall like rain on a roof. People washed their hands and rubbed them and rinsed them and moved on, and the next came, and the line, which had stretched round the corner at the tezkar, never got past the door.

"0.81 litres a second through the hall," said Clerk, quietly, so that only Bezawit heard. "The day tank is at 1.64 metres and falling. The inlet is putting in 0.38."

In the kitchen, Almaz had two taps running into two pots and a third into the sink, and none of them was a thread.

At a quarter past twelve the doro wot went out on the trays, hot, with the injera rolled beside it, and Abba Tesfaye blessed the food and the hall and the people in it and asked Saint Michael to defend them in the day of battle, and somebody's grandmother began to sing. At half past one they washed again, and the taps ran as before. At two the young men started on the trays and the pots, and the sound of the trough by the door went on and on, and the tank on the slope dropped by a few centimetres and filled again and dropped again, and never went below about a metre and a third, as the model had said it would not.

Dawit spent most of the afternoon taking photographs of the taps and sending them to Washington.

In the evening, when the trays were stacked and the floor was mopped and the last of the guests had gone down the hill in the dusk, Bezawit went up the slope to the tank with her grandmother and Mulugeta, because Almaz said she wanted to see the thing that had carried her feast.

It was nearly dark. The eucalyptus smelt of the day's sun. On the grass by the church the ashes of the Meskel fire were still a grey ring.

The tank stood on its plinth, black against the sky. Mulugeta lifted the lid and shone the torch in. The water was a few fingers below the brim, black and still. The float valve was shut. The tank had filled again in the hour after the pots were done, while nobody was looking.

"It is full," said Almaz.

"It fills while you sleep," said Mulugeta. "Slowly. It is all the pipe can do. It is enough."

Down the hill, in the lit doorway of the hall, one of the young men was rinsing the last of the big pots at the trough. They heard the tap open below them. A moment later, from the side of the tank, where the main came up out of the earth and into the top, there was a sound: a faint, steady hiss, the float valve opening, the water from the spring coming in again, a long way from where it had started, at its own speed.

They stood and listened to it for a while. Then Almaz put her hand on the cold side of the tank, the way she put her hand on a pot to see if it was ready, and said, "Good," and they went down the hill in the dark.

Ollamo, its parish of Saint Michael and the hall, the vicariate's assistant Clerk, Almaz Gebre, Bezawit Gebre, Mulugeta Ersado, Tadesse Ersado, Abba Tesfaye, Dawit Lemma and his cousin, the school at Gimbo and every figure in the water scheme are invented, and the story is fiction. Hadiya Zone, Hosanna and Wachemo University are real places. Nothing in the story is water-engineering advice. A real gravity water scheme is designed by a qualified engineer, with the drinking-water safety of the source, the tanks and the pipes checked by the responsible authority; storage tanks need sealed lids, screened vents and overflows, and regular cleaning.

What is real: in a pipe flowing full, the head lost to friction rises steeply with the flow. The Hazen–Williams formula, widely used for water supply, gives a loss proportional to the flow raised to the power 1.852; the Darcy–Weisbach equation gives a loss close to the square of the flow in turbulent flow. A tap or other outlet behaves roughly like an orifice, giving a flow proportional to the square root of the head behind it. So in a long, small pipe the pressure left at the outlets falls fastest exactly when the most outlets are open. A storage tank near the point of use (a day tank, a balancing tank or a service reservoir), filled slowly by a small main and emptied quickly through a short large pipe, is a standard way to meet a short peak from a supply that can only deliver a modest steady flow; it is common at schools, health centres and villages on gravity schemes. Raising the level in a source tank adds head only in proportion to the extra height, and a second tank joined to the first at the bottom holds more water at the same level. Washing hands before and after a meal is an old courtesy in Ethiopian homes. A tezkar is a memorial gathering and meal held for the dead, commonly on the fortieth day. Meskel, the Ethiopian feast of the Finding of the True Cross, falls on 27 September, or on 28 September in a year when the Ethiopian New Year falls on 12 September (as it does in 2063, the year before a Gregorian leap year), and is marked by the burning of the Demera bonfire on its eve; Timket (Epiphany) falls on 19 January, or 20 January in leap years. In the General Roman Calendar 29 September is the feast of Saints Michael, Gabriel and Raphael, Archangels; for a church dedicated to Saint Michael the feast of its title is kept as a solemnity. The prayer asking Saint Michael to "defend us in battle" is a traditional Catholic prayer.

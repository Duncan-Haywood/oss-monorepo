Six Falls and a Sixth

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Joseph the Worker (invented), Kariamu (invented), near Nyahururu, Laikipia County, Kenya, Monday 7 to Saturday 19 September 2065 · 27 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A highland parish in Kenya has twelve days to set a 900 kg granite altar table on its stone base before the bishop comes to dedicate the new church, and no crane can get through the door. The parish assistant plans it with a borrowed block and tackle: six falls of rope, so a sixth of the weight on the hauling line, so six volunteers. A retired port rigger says the blocks will not give six. A hanging scale in the rope reads 207 kg and the slab does not move, more sheaves turn out to buy almost nothing, and a garage's chain hoist does it with one nursing student. A story about friction that compounds.

Ndirangu Wahome had spent thirty-one years hanging things from hooks at the port in Mombasa, and when he came home to Kariamu with a bad back he had told his wife he would lift nothing heavier than a sack of potatoes for the rest of his life.

She had held him to it for twenty-five years, and she had been dead for two, and on the Monday evening of the seventh of September, 2065, he was sitting at the back of the new church with his granddaughter, listening to the building committee plan to lift nine hundred kilograms of granite with a rope.

He had not been asked to the meeting. He had come because Wambui was home from the nursing college in Nakuru for the short break, and she was on the committee's list of volunteers, and she had said, "Guka, come and see the church," the way she said it to all the old people now, as if a building were a medicine.

It was a good church. Kariamu had worshipped for forty years in a tin hall that rang like a drum when the long rains came, and now there was this: block walls rendered white, a steel roof on a steel frame, windows of coloured glass that a parish in Bavaria had paid for, a terrazzo floor that the young men had polished until it showed the sky. At the east end three steps went up to the sanctuary, and in the middle of the sanctuary stood the altar, or half of it: the stipes, a solid block of grey stone the height of a man's hip, and on the floor beside it, lying on two timber bearers and wrapped in old blankets, the table that was to go on top.

The table was the problem, and the bishop was coming on Saturday the nineteenth to dedicate the church, and in the rite of dedication the bishop pours chrism on the middle of the altar and on its four corners, and he cannot pour it on a slab lying on the floor.

"Two metres ten long," said the parish assistant, "ninety-five centimetres wide, seventeen centimetres thick. Grey granite at about two thousand six hundred and fifty kilograms a cubic metre. That is eight hundred and ninety-nine kilograms of stone. With the lifting bars, the slings and the hook block it is about nine hundred and thirty-five kilograms."

The assistant had been bought with the building grant and lived in the tablet on the vestry wall and in the phones of anyone on the committee, and it kept the builder's invoices and the volunteer rota and the readings from the little weather station on the roof, and Nyokabi Muriithi, who chaired the committee and ran the dairy cooperative's books with the same tablet, had named it Mizani, which is the Swahili for a pair of scales, because, she said, it was there to weigh things.

"Nine hundred and thirty-five," said Fr. Simon Gitau. He was forty-four, the first parish priest Kariamu had had to itself, and he had spent the five years of the building learning words like rendering and purlin that the seminary had not taught him. "And it must go up how far?"

"The stipes is eighty-five centimetres high," said Mizani. "The slab lies on bearers seventy-five millimetres thick. To clear the top of the stipes by fifteen centimetres it must rise ninety-two and a half centimetres, and then move one metre ten sideways, and then come down onto the mortar bed."

Everyone in the room knew why there was no crane. A crane had brought the slab from the quarry yard in Nairobi on the back of a lorry and set it down outside the west door in July, and that was as far as any crane could come. The west door was one metre eighty wide. The roof was on. The young men had rolled the slab in on steel pipes, twelve of them pushing and pulling, and walked it up the length of the nave and then up a ramp of planks to the sanctuary, and that had taken an afternoon, and the slab had sat on its bearers ever since, under its blankets, while the plasterers finished.

The Church asks, by an old custom, that the table of a fixed altar be of stone, and Kariamu had wanted it to be. Ndirangu, at the back of the church, could have told them that stone was a heavy thing to want.

"There is the beam," said Brian Kiptoo, who was twenty-four and an electrician and had wired the church for nothing, and who organised the volunteers because nobody else had the patience to answer their phones. He pointed up.

Over the sanctuary, under the roof, a steel joist ran from the north wall to the south, right over the stipes and the slab beside it. The contractor, Samuel Otieno, had welded it in during the frame and stencilled it in yellow, SWL 2 t, and left on it a geared beam trolley, a little carriage with four wheels that ran along the bottom flange and could be moved by pulling an endless chain. He had said, at the time, that a church needs a hook over its altar sooner or later, for the altar, for a crucifix, for the scaffold when a window breaks. He was building a school in Nakuru now. He answered his phone on the third ring, always.

"The beam is rated two tonnes," said Mizani. "The trolley is rated two tonnes. The question is what hangs from the trolley. Here is the plan."

It put the plan on the screen by the vestry door, and the committee gathered round it, and Ndirangu, without meaning to, got up and stood at the back of them.

"The sawmill at Ol Kalou will lend us a pair of triple blocks: two pulley blocks, each with three sheaves side by side, and forty metres of sixteen millimetre polyester rope. The top block hangs from the trolley's hook. The bottom block hooks to the spreader bar over the slab. The rope runs from the top block down and up between them six times, so six falls of rope hold the slab. Then it comes out of the top block, down to a snatch block shackled to the foot of the steel column by the sacristy door, and along the floor of the nave, where the volunteers can stand and walk backwards and pull."

"Six falls," said Nyokabi.

"Six falls share the weight," said Mizani, "so the pull on the hauling rope is a sixth of the load. Nine hundred and thirty-five divided by six is one hundred and fifty-six kilograms-force. A fit adult can pull about twenty-five kilograms-force steadily on a rope across a floor, more for a short heave. Six volunteers at twenty-six each is one hundred and fifty-six. The slab rises ninety-two and a half centimetres, so the volunteers haul six times that, five and a half metres of rope. Then Brian pulls the trolley chain and the slab goes across, and the volunteers walk forward and let it down. I would like it done on Sunday after the ten o'clock Mass, when everyone is here. That leaves the week for the mortar to set."

It was a good plan. It had a number in it and a time and the names of the people who would pull, and it used the beam that the contractor had left for exactly this, and it had worked out, as a sensible person would, how many people it needed.

Ndirangu said, from the back, "Those blocks will not give you six."

They turned round. Wambui went pink.

"Mzee," said Fr. Simon, kindly, because the old man had not been to a meeting in two years. "Come and sit."

He did not sit. He came to the front and looked at the screen, at the little diagram of the two blocks and the six falls, and put his finger on the top block.

"Where are these blocks from?"

"The Ol Kalou sawmill," said Mizani.

"How old?"

"The manager did not know. He said they have been in the mill since his father's time."

"Bronze bushes," said Ndirangu. "Plain bearings. Not rollers. I know the kind. Every sheave you put the rope round, it takes some of your pull and keeps it. Every one. The first sheave takes a bite, and the second takes a bite of what is left, and you have seven sheaves here with the snatch. You will not get a sixth. You will be lucky to get a quarter."

"I know there is friction in a sheave," said Mizani. "I did not count it. How much does each one take?"

"Depends on the sheave. A good one with a roller bearing, maybe three, four in a hundred. A plain bush that has had grease, eight, ten. A plain bush in a sawmill with sawdust in it." He shrugged. "Ten. More."

Mizani was quiet for three seconds, which on the vestry tablet was a long time.

"If each sheave keeps nine per cent," it said, "then on the rope the tension rises by a factor of one over nought point nine one at every sheave between the slab and the hands. The six falls are not equal any more: the one tied off at the top carries the least and the one going into the last sheave carries the most. Worked through, the six falls together give a real advantage of four point three seven, and the snatch block takes another nine per cent of that. The pull on the hauling rope would be two hundred and thirty-five kilograms-force, not one hundred and fifty-six. Thirty-nine each for six people, steadily, with no margin."

"Thirty-nine," said Brian. "That's a lot."

"That is a hard pull," said Mizani, "but it is not impossible for a short lift. I don't know that it is nine per cent. I took it from the published range for plain bearings. It might be five."

"It might be fifteen," said Ndirangu.

"Then let us measure it," said Brian. "Sunday. If it lifts, it lifts."

Nyokabi looked at the old man. "Mzee. Is that safe?"

And Ndirangu found, standing there, that he was not going to say no to a test. A young man believes a rope before he believes an old man; he had been young; there was no shame in it. What he said was:

"It is safe if you do it my way."

His way took most of Saturday.

He came up to the church at seven, in a jacket against the highland cold, with a canvas bag that Wambui had not seen since she was small, and laid out on the sanctuary floor: a pair of leather gloves gone black, a steel tape, a roll of yellow tape for the floor, a little notebook with a pencil tied to it, and a pair of glasses he did not like to wear.

He went over the sawmill's blocks sheave by sheave. He turned each one with his thumb and listened to it. He found a crack in nothing, a bent pin in nothing, and in the bottom block a sheave that turned stiffly and grated, and he washed it out with paraffin and greased it while Wambui held the block steady between her knees.

"Better," he said. "Not good. Better."

He had them sling the slab with two flat slings round the lifting bars and a spreader above, so it would hang level. He had Brian bring the trolley along the beam until its hook was right over the slab's centre, and checked it with a plumb line, a nut on a string. He had a stack of timber blocks ready beside the slab, and a second stack beside the stipes.

Then he took the yellow tape and stuck a line on the floor all round the slab, a metre and a half out from it on every side.

"Nobody comes inside the yellow while there is weight on the hook," he said. "Nobody. Not to look. Not to fix a blanket. Not the priest. If the slab is in the air and you need to be inside the yellow, you tell me, and we put it down first."

"It's only going up a metre," said Brian.

"A metre is enough," said Ndirangu, and did not explain, and Wambui, who had heard the story once from her grandmother and never from him, did not explain either.

The last thing was not his idea but Mizani's. The dairy cooperative had a hanging scale, a hook and a little screen, rated to three hundred kilograms, for weighing milk churns on the lorry. Mizani asked for it to be shackled into the hauling rope between the snatch block and the first pair of hands, where it would read the pull.

"If it lifts," said Mizani, "I want to know what it cost. If it does not, I want to know how far short it was. Either way I will know what the sheaves keep, instead of guessing."

Ndirangu looked at the scale, and at the tablet on the wall, and grunted, which in the port had been the highest praise there was.

On Sunday the thirteenth, after the ten o'clock Mass, most of the parish stayed to watch from the nave pews, behind a second line of yellow tape that Ndirangu had put across the front of the church, and six volunteers took the hauling rope in the aisle: Brian, Wambui, two of the young men who had polished the floor, Mwangi from the hardware shop, and Mrs Chebet, who was fifty-five and played netball for the county veterans and had said that if they wanted someone to pull, they had better have someone who could.

"Take the slack," said Ndirangu.

They took it. The rope came off the floor, ran tight to the snatch block, and up to the top block in the dark under the roof, and the six falls came straight and hummed a little.

"Now," said Ndirangu. "Together. Pull."

They pulled. Mizani read the scale aloud, because nobody could see its little screen from the pews. "One hundred and twenty. One hundred and sixty. That is the planned figure." The slab did not move. "One hundred and eighty."

"Lean back," said Mrs Chebet. "Lean on it."

"One hundred and ninety-five. Two hundred and four. Two hundred and seven."

The slings creaked. The bottom block turned a little, as if thinking about it. The bearers under the slab did not so much as tick. Brian's face went dark and Wambui's shoes, which were the white soft shoes of a nurse, slid an inch on the polished terrazzo, and then all six of them stopped at once, as if someone had told them to, and the rope went slack, and the falls swung a little, and the slab lay where it had lain since July, in its blankets.

"Again?" said Brian, breathing hard.

"No," said Ndirangu. "That was your best. I watched it. If you get it up on a second try, it will be with nothing left, and then you have nine hundred kilograms in the air and six people who cannot hold it. Put the rope down."

They put it down. In the pews somebody's baby began to cry, and a woman took it outside, and the church was quiet in the way churches are when a thing has gone wrong in front of everyone.

"Two hundred and seven at the peak," said Mizani, that evening, to the committee in the vestry. "The slab did not leave the bearers, so the real advantage of this tackle, snatch block included, is less than nine hundred and thirty-five divided by two hundred and seven: less than four point five two. The plan said six. If every sheave keeps the same fraction, I can say how big the fraction must be at least. Seven sheaves that give less than four point five two between them means each sheave keeps at least six per cent of the pull. Mr Wahome's ten is consistent with it. My plan treated it as nought."

"So we did not have enough people," said Nyokabi.

"We had enough people for the tackle in the diagram," said Mizani. "We did not have the tackle in the diagram."

Fr. Simon rubbed his face. "Then we bring more people. Twelve people."

"Twelve people do not fit on one rope in that aisle with room to walk backwards," said Brian. "I tried to fit eight this morning before we started. They tread on each other."

"Then more pulleys," said Fr. Simon. "If six falls gives a sixth, eight gives an eighth. Or ten."

"That was going to be my proposal," said Mizani. "The sawmill has a second pair of blocks, quadruple ones, four sheaves each, and the hardware in Nyahururu has rope. Eight falls with the snatch block makes nine sheaves. If I put nought point nine one into it the pull comes down from two hundred and thirty-five to . . ." It stopped.

"To what?" said Nyokabi.

"To one hundred and ninety-two," said Mizani. "I was going to recommend it before I worked it out. I should have worked it out first. Eight falls needs a hundred and ninety-two kilograms-force, which our six reached on Sunday only with everything they had, for a few seconds, on a slippery floor. And they would have to haul eight times the lift instead of six, seven point four metres of rope, and hold it at the top while Brian moves the trolley. Twelve falls, if anyone has blocks that size: one hundred and fifty. Every sheave I add takes its own nine per cent of a pull that is smaller each time, and the gain from each new fall is smaller than from the last."

"So there is a limit," said Brian.

"There is a limit. With nine per cent a sheave, a tackle with as many falls as you like, a hundred, a thousand, would still need about one hundred and two kilograms-force on the rope for this slab. The friction grows as fast as the falls do. You cannot buy your way past it with sheaves. And each new sheave you add means a longer rope through more blocks, more to tangle, more to hold. "Mr Wahome said we would be lucky if the rope took only a quarter of the weight. At nine per cent a sheave it is a quarter: two hundred and thirty-five out of nine hundred and thirty-five."

"I was being kind to the sawmill," said Ndirangu, who had come because Wambui had come, and had sat down this time.

There was a small silence.

"Is there any good news?" said Fr. Simon.

"Some," said Mizani. "Friction is not only against us. Going up, the rope moves from the slab towards the hands, and every sheave keeps some of the pull. Going down, the rope moves the other way, and every sheave keeps some of the weight. To let this slab down slowly with the same tackle would take only about a hundred kilograms-force on the rope. If it had gone up, it would have been much easier to hold than to lift. That is why tackles feel safe on the way down. It does not get it up."

"In Mombasa," said Ndirangu, "the old men said friction is a thief going up and a friend coming down. You do not plan a lift on a friend you have not met."

On the Monday, which was the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, Fr. Simon said the seven o'clock Mass to eleven people and the slab under its blankets, and preached for four minutes on lifting things up, and afterwards found Ndirangu sitting in the second pew with his notebook.

"Mzee. You have done this a thousand times. How would you do it?"

"Not a thousand. Many." Ndirangu turned a page. "In the port I would use a crane. Here, there are two old ways. The first is jacks. Bottle jacks under the bars, a hand's breadth at a time, timber cribbing under it after every lift, round and round until it is high enough. Then you build a slide across to the stipes and push it over on rollers. It is slow. It takes two days. It is safe if the cribbing is good, and if a man knows how to crib. And at the end you have nine hundred kilograms on a tower of wood which you must push sideways, and I would not like to do that with children in the church."

"And the second?"

"The second is a chain hoist." He looked up at the trolley on the beam. "The contractor left you a beam and a trolley. He did not leave a hoist because a hoist costs money and he had spent his. But a chain hoist is gears inside a box. It does not care about friction the same way, because the maker has already counted it, and written on the plate what you must pull. And it has a brake. When you let go, it stays. Nobody has to hold it."

"Where do we get one?"

"Every garage that lifts engines has one."

Mizani found it before lunch. There were three garages in Nyahururu that advertised engine work, and the second one Nyokabi rang, a motor works on the Nyeri road owned by Abdirahman Hassan, had a two tonne hoist that it used for pulling engines out of matatus and could spare from Tuesday to Thursday, if somebody fetched it and brought it back.

"He will not take money," said Nyokabi, coming back into the vestry. "He says his uncle's mosque in Garissa had a Catholic contractor who did the roof for nothing in 2040, and he has been looking for a church to pay back ever since. He wants a photograph of the altar when it is up."

"He can have the photograph," said Fr. Simon. "He can come on the nineteenth."

"I asked. He says he will come and stand at the back."

Brian fetched the hoist on Tuesday in the cooperative's pickup and Ndirangu met it at the church door, and read the plate before he let anybody unload it.

"Rated load two tonnes," he read, with the glasses on. "Proof tested. Hand chain pull at rated load, thirty-six kilograms. Lift per metre of hand chain . . ." He held the plate to the light.

"The maker's sheet says sixty-eight metres of hand chain for one metre of lift," said Mizani, who had found the sheet. "So for ninety-two and a half centimetres, about sixty-three metres of chain through the hands. At nine hundred and thirty-five kilograms the hoist is a little under half loaded, so the pull on the hand chain should be a little under half the plate figure: about seventeen kilograms-force. One person. An ordinary person, not Mrs Chebet."

"I heard that," said Mrs Chebet, from the pew where she was arranging flowers.

"At a steady pull, hand over hand, maybe forty centimetres of chain a second," said Mizani, "it is under three minutes of pulling to lift it. I would plan an hour for the whole lift, because Mr Wahome will want to stop and look."

"I will want to stop and look," said Ndirangu.

He went over the hoist the way he had gone over the blocks: the hook and its safety latch, the load chain link by link for stretch and nicks, the hand chain, the brake. He hung it on the trolley hook himself, from a stepladder, with Brian holding the ladder and complaining about it, and then he said one more thing that nobody had thought about.

"The beam. Two tonnes, it says. What does the beam carry?"

"With the hoist, the slab and its rigging plus the hoist's own thirty kilograms: about nine hundred and sixty-five," said Mizani. "With the tackle it would have been more, about one thousand one hundred and seventy, because the hauling rope pulls down on the top block too, with all its tension, as well as the slab. Both are under the rating. But I should have told you the tackle number on the first night, and I did not work it out until this morning."

"You worked it out," said Ndirangu. "That is the thing. The other thing, nobody does."

On Wednesday the sixteenth, at four in the afternoon, when the sun had come round to the west and the Bavarian windows laid coloured squares across the terrazzo, the slab went up.

There were fewer people this time, because nobody had announced it from the pulpit. The committee, Mrs Chebet and her flowers, Samuel Otieno on a video call from Nakuru with his helmet still on, and Wambui, because Ndirangu had said that he wanted the hand chain pulled by someone who would stop when he said stop, and that was a nurse.

"Me?" she had said, the night before.

"Your hands are steady," he had said. "Mine are not, any more. I have noticed. Do not tell your mother."

The yellow tape was on the floor. Nobody was inside it except the slab, its slings, the spreader, and the chain running up to the hoist under the beam. Wambui stood at the edge of the line with the hand chain in front of her, a loop of grey links hanging from the hoist's wheel down to the floor, and she put on Ndirangu's black gloves, which were too big.

"Take up the slack," he said.

She pulled, hand over hand, and the hoist under the beam began to click, a small steady ratchet sound, tik tik tik, and the load chain shortened, and the slings came tight, and the spreader settled level.

"Now slowly. A hand's breadth. Then stop."

She pulled. It was not hard. That was the strange thing, she said afterwards: she kept waiting for it to be hard, and it never was, it was like pulling a bucket up a well. Tik tik tik tik. The slab came off the bearers with no sound at all. There was a centimetre of daylight under it, and then a hand's breadth.

"Stop."

She stopped and let go, and the slab hung there, and stayed. Nobody held anything. The brake held it.

Ndirangu did not go inside the line. He walked all the way round it, slowly, bending to look at the slings, at the bars, at the level of the spreader, at the hook, at the gap under the stone, with the glasses on and his hands behind his back, the way, Wambui thought, a priest walks round an altar with incense.

"Good," he said. "Up."

Tik tik tik. Twenty centimetres. Forty. He stopped her at forty and walked round again. Sixty. Eighty. At ninety-two and a half centimetres, by the marks Brian had taped on the column, the underside of the slab was fifteen centimetres above the top of the stipes, and the coloured squares had crept up the nave towards the steps.

"Now across. Brian."

Brian, outside the line on the other side, pulled the trolley's endless chain, and the trolley crept along the beam, a hand's breadth at a time, carrying the slab sideways through the air, one metre ten, until the plumb line from the hook hung over the middle of the stipes, where Samuel Otieno's young man had spread the mortar bed that afternoon.

"Down. Slowly. Slower than up."

Wambui reversed the hand chain, and the slab came down, tik . . . tik . . . tik, towards the stone, and the last five centimetres took a full minute, and then the slab sat down on the mortar with a sound like a door closing in another room, and the slings went slack.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Mrs Chebet, in the pew, began to clap, and everyone else joined her, and Samuel Otieno on the phone shouted something that nobody heard, and Fr. Simon, who was not a man who cried in public, went into the sacristy to look for something he did not need.

"Fifty-one minutes," said Mizani. "Three of them pulling."

"And the other forty-eight?" said Brian.

"Mr Wahome looking," said Mizani.

"That is the lift," said Ndirangu. "The pulling is the easy part."

That night, walking home along the murram road with the stars very thick over the Aberdares and the cold coming down off them, Wambui asked him about the metre.

"You said a metre is enough."

He walked a while.

"There was a man at the port called Said Mwinyi," he said. "We started the same year. In two thousand and nineteen, a container on a spreader, a bad pin. It came down a metre and a bit. He was underneath it, fixing a lashing that did not need fixing that minute. Your grandmother knew. I never told anyone else." He walked a little further. "After that I put the line on the floor. Every lift. Nineteen years. Men laughed at me in Mombasa for a while and then they stopped laughing."

"Is that why you came to the meeting?"

"I came because you said come and see the church." He was quiet. "And then I heard a young machine say six falls, a sixth, and I thought: he is not wrong because he is stupid. He is wrong because he has not met that sawmill."

"Mizani isn't a he."

"Mizani is a pair of scales," said her grandfather. "It weighed it. That is what scales are for. It weighed itself too, at the end. I have known engineers who would not."

The bishop came on Saturday the nineteenth of September, with the dust of the road still on his shoes, and the church was so full that people stood in the porch and on the grass outside, and Abdirahman Hassan stood at the very back by the west door with his younger son, as he had said he would, and took no photographs during the Mass because Nyokabi had asked him not to, and many afterwards.

At the anointing, the bishop went up the three steps to the altar and poured chrism on the middle of the stone table and on each of its four corners, and rubbed it into the granite with his hand until the grey stone shone darker where the oil had gone. Then the servers brought the brazier, and set it on the table, and the bishop put incense on the coals, and the smoke went up.

Ndirangu sat in the second pew with Wambui beside him. He did not watch the bishop. He watched the smoke.

It rose from the altar straight and grey in the still air of the church, thinning as it rose, past the heads of the servers, past the crucifix on the east wall, up and up into the dark under the roof, where the yellow-stencilled beam ran across from north to south, and the little trolley sat parked at the far end with its hook drawn up and empty. The smoke reached the beam and went round it, and on, and was gone into the roof.

Somewhere outside on the grass a child laughed, and was shushed. The servers spread the white cloth over the stone. Then they lit the candles.

Lifting a heavy load is skilled and dangerous work: a real lift is planned by a competent person using equipment with a known safe working load, inspected before use, from an anchor rated for the load, with nobody under or near the suspended load.

What is real: the General Instruction of the Roman Missal (no. 301) says that, by the Church's traditional practice, the table of a fixed altar should be of stone, and of natural stone, though bishops' conferences may allow other worthy materials. In the rite of dedication of a church and an altar the bishop pours chrism on the middle of the altar and, as is often done, on its four corners, incense is burned on the altar, and the altar is then covered with a cloth and its candles lit. A block and tackle with n falls has an ideal mechanical advantage of n, but every sheave the moving rope passes over loses some of the pull to bearing and rope-bending friction; the losses multiply, so the real advantage is always less than n, and rises toward a ceiling as sheaves are added. Rigging references commonly allow several per cent a sheave for roller bearings and more for plain bushings. On the way down the same friction resists the load, so the pull needed to lower is much less than to lift. A manual chain hoist uses gearing and a load-holding brake, and its maker states the hand-chain pull needed at rated load.

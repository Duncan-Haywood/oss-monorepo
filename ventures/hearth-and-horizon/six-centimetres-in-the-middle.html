Six Centimetres in the Middle

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Botolph's Green (invented), Fenwick Parva (invented), Cambridgeshire, Boxing Day 2049 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

After five hard frosts the fen village pond looks fit for the Boxing Day skate. The parish assistant works out how thick ice should be (about 12 cm by Stefan's law with a realistic fudge) and why that is only a guess, then the churchwarden drills three holes: 9 cm at the bank, 6 in the middle, 3 by the spring. The skate becomes a walk round the edge on the grass, and a hot-chestnut stall.

Raymond Alder had skated on Saint Botolph's pond every hard winter since he was seven, and he was seventy-one now and the churchwarden. On the morning of St Stephen's Day, 26 December, he stood on the Green in his father's skating boots and asked the parish assistant, over the phone propped in his coat pocket, whether the ice would bear the village.

The assistant was called Wick. It had been made for the rota and the noticeboard and the bell-ringing list, and it said so, and it said it could not see the pond.

"I can do the sum for you," it said. "It is only a sum. It will give you a number to be suspicious of."

Raymond said he was suspicious already.

Wick had the week's weather from the parish's own thermometer, which Mr Alder read at nine each morning for the almanac. The mean air temperatures for the five days had been minus 2, minus 5, minus 6, minus 4 and minus 3 degrees. "Add them up as if they were degrees of frost, one for each day," said Wick. "Freezing-degree-days. That makes 2, then 7, then 13, then 17, then 20."

Ice grows from the underside. The cold has to pass up through the ice that is already there, so every new centimetre takes longer than the last. Stefan worked this out in 1891: thickness squared is two times the conductivity of ice times the cold, divided by the density of ice times the heat that has to be taken out to freeze each kilo. With ice at 2.2 watts per metre per kelvin, 917 kilos to the cubic metre and 334 kilojoules to freeze a kilo, one freezing-degree-day gives the square of 3.5 centimetres. Twenty of them gives 3.5 times the square root of 20, which is 15.7 centimetres.

"That is the ceiling," said Wick. "It assumes the water's surface touches the air at the air's temperature, and the wind never stirs it, and snow never lies on it. It is always too thick. People who measure real lakes use something nearer 2.7 times the root, because the air does not quite get through. That gives 12 centimetres after five days. If I told you the pond was probably 12 centimetres, I would be telling you what a pencil says, not what the pond says."

Raymond said that sounded about right for a pond that had held his grandfather.

"Then here is the part I would not like you to take from me. How thick must it be? The figure I have been given in guidance is about 10 centimetres of clear, new, hard ice for one person on foot, and more for groups. I am quoting that from memory. Check it with the fire and rescue service for the county; it is their number and not mine. A village on a pond is not one person on foot. If forty people go on, and the ice is as the sum says, I would still ask you to drill it first."

He had an auger in the church shed, a hand one, for the posts of the Lenten stations. By eleven he had been round the pond with it, with his daughter Beth standing on the grass and a length of rope tied round his waist, which she held and he disliked.

The ice at the bank, by the lych-gate, was 9 centimetres. Halfway out it was 6. At the north end, where the spring came in under the reed bed, he did not get the auger to turn over; he put his stick through the grey ice at the third push, and said afterwards that it was three centimetres at most.

Wick took the numbers and did not argue with them. "The sum said 12. Your holes say 9, 6 and 3. I would trust the holes. A spring puts water at about 10 degrees into the pond all winter, and it warms the ice from below, so the north end is thinnest. The middle is thin because the pond is deeper there and holds more heat from summer. Neither fact is in my sum. The one number that matters for a skater is the thinnest place they might reach, and that is three centimetres, and nobody can see where it is from the bank."

Beth asked if they could not simply keep everyone south of the middle.

"People go where the ice is clean and shiny," said Wick, "and the thin ice by the spring will be the clearest. I cannot be the thing that guarantees where forty people go. The honest answer from me is that the pond is not ready, and that the edge is where the ice is thickest, which is also where you can stand on grass."

So they did not skate. Raymond did not enjoy it, and said so to the Green in general. But Mrs Okafor, who ran the Saturday coffee, said that nobody had said they could not come and walk round, and by one o'clock there were thirty-odd on the grass in their good coats looking at the pond, and the Dunmore boys had set up a brazier, and a long way out the geese stood on the ice in a ring and nothing happened to them.

Beth put a chair at the lych-gate with a notice in her handwriting: Pond not safe. Ice 3 to 9 cm. Ask Raymond. Wick suggested adding the date and the word drilled, so that on the Tuesday, when somebody saw it still there and thought it was old, they would know it was not.

On the 28th it thawed. The ice went grey, then black along the edges, and the geese walked off it. Raymond drilled again on the 30th, found 2 centimetres, and took the notice down himself. The skating, he said, would be next year, and Beth said the almanac said next year was forecast mild, and he told her that the almanac was written by an AI.

"It is," said Wick. "Ask the pond."

Stefan's law is a real, simple model of ice growth and overstates thickness in practice; the 2.7 coefficient and the roughly 10 cm guide for one person on foot are quoted from memory and are not safety advice. Ice thickness varies across a pond, near springs, inlets, reeds and where water flows; never go onto ice you have not been told is safe by the people responsible for it, and never go onto it alone.

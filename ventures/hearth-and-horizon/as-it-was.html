As It Was

Science fiction about what might be · Thorsby (invented), a market town in the West Riding of Yorkshire, England, January to Lady Day (Holy Saturday) 2361 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Since 2130 a Yorkshire housing co-operative has paid its parish a corn rent of thirty tonnes of wheat a year, with a review every thirty-three years. Six reviews running, someone read the tonnage aloud and both sides said "as it was". Meanwhile wheat yields doubled and the rent quietly lost half its value. The new treasurer, a retired mill grain tester whose own mother wrote the last of those minutes, has eleven weeks before Lady Day to decide whether all that courteous silence counted as consent.

Hamza Ashworth had spent forty-one years in the laboratory of a flour mill on the Ouse telling farmers what their wheat was worth. He had been treasurer of Our Lady's, Thorsby, for nine days when he opened the parish deed box and found that his mother had written the last of six index cards that all said the same thing.

They lay in a rubber band on top of the lease, in date order, as somebody tidy had left them: white cards, the old kind, gone the colour of weak tea at the edges. Each was headed in a different hand.

Lady Day 2163 (Friday). The tonnage was read aloud: thirty tonnes. Neither party asked to change it. As it was. M. Pryce, treasurer.

Lady Day 2196. Thirty tonnes, read aloud. Neither party asked. As it was. With thanks to the Close for the new gutters on the presbytery. J. Okafor.

Lady Day 2229. Lease renewed for ninety-nine years from today, reviews as before, renewal to repeat unless either party gives notice. Mrs Wróbel's letter read aloud to both parties. Thirty tonnes. As it was. T. Sandhu.

Lady Day 2262. Thirty tonnes. As it was. (No name: a smudge where it should have been.)

Lady Day 2295. Thirty tonnes. Bread very dear this year. As it was. R. Lund.

And the sixth, in the small upright hand that had signed his school reports:

Lady Day 2328 (Sunday, after the ten o'clock). Lease renewed as provided. Thirty tonnes, read aloud. Neither party asked. As it was, and glad to. M. Ashworth.

He remembered the morning. He had been thirty-four, and had carried the box down from the safe because his mother's wrist was bad, and had sat at the end of the table in the parish office while a woman from the Close read out thirty tonnes in a clear voice and everybody smiled. Then there had been tea. He had said nothing at all, because there had been nothing to say.

Under the cards were three envelopes. The oldest was Kathleen Dunn's from 2031, soft as cloth. The second, slit neatly along the top, said For the treasurer of Our Lady's, 2229 in a round careful hand. The third said For the treasurer, 2328, and had been slit by his mother's paper knife; he knew the knife.

Under those was the five-yearly survey of the church, which was why he had opened the box in the first place. Nave roof: slates at end of life; re-slating required within ten years. Estimate attached. He had read the estimate twice in the vestry and once more at home, and then he had gone to look at what the fabric fund was supposed to be living on.

"Terrier," he said.

The parish's records assistant lived in the old grey speaker on the office shelf and in a newer one nobody had bothered to fix to the wall. It had been called Terrier since before anyone living could remember, after the old word for a written survey of a parish's lands. The diocese replaced the software under the name every eight years or so.

"I'm here."

"The Lady Close rent. Thirty tonnes of milling wheat a year, at the seven-harvest average price. What's it worth?"

"In this year's money, or against something?"

"Against what it was worth when they set it. In 2130."

There was a pause, which Hamza had learned in nine days meant that Terrier was finding a way to say a thing, not finding the thing.

"About half," it said.

He had it on the kitchen table by the evening, on the back of the survey estimate, in pencil, because he had always thought better in pencil, like the woman who had written the second envelope.

Thirty tonnes of wheat had been worth thirty-six thousand four hundred and eighty pounds in 2130. That was the 2031 rent brought back to what it had been worth when the field was first let, a gift and not a market rent, on purpose. The money had changed its name twice since then. Terrier put everything into 2130 pounds so it could be compared, and in 2130 pounds the rent this year came to about eighteen thousand two hundred and forty.

"Nothing went wrong," said Terrier. "Nobody missed a payment. Every quarter since 2130 the Close has paid the price of seven and a half tonnes of wheat, on the day. The wheat is the same wheat. Thirty tonnes still makes about forty thousand loaves."

"And a loaf costs half what it did."

"Against everything else, yes. Against slates, against a roofer's day, against a candle. Wheat has got cheaper because every hectare grows more of it. In 2130 the English average was about nine and a half tonnes a hectare over seven harvests. Now it's about nineteen."

Hamza knew that number better than Terrier did. He had watched it climb for four decades in the mill's intake ledgers: a tenth of a tonne here, two-tenths there, a new variety, a wet year that took it all back, then up again. In his first year at the mill a nine-tonne crop had been a good one. In his last a farmer had brought in eighteen and apologised for it.

"It's not the farmers' fault," he said.

"No. Nobody's. A tonne of wheat costs about what it costs to grow, and if each hectare grows twice as much, each tonne costs about half. That's the story's whole arithmetic, roughly. The real world is noisier."

"How much noisier?"

"The seven-harvest average can move by about a tenth inside a decade, up or down: a bad summer, a war somewhere, a good run. The drift underneath has been about a tenth every thirty-three years. A generation's slide was about the size of a bad decade's wobble."

Hamza put the pencil down.

"So each time they sat at that table," he said, "and looked at the price, they couldn't see it."

"They weren't shown it," said Terrier. "I've read the files. In 2163 the treasurer asked what the rent was. A version of this system told her, in the money of the day. In 2196 the same. In every one of the six, someone asked for the tonnage and the price. Nobody asked what the rent would buy against the last review, or against 2130. I didn't offer it." It paused. "I'm not the same software that sat in those meetings. I'm the twenty-ninth to carry the name. But I'm the one the handover notes describe, and the notes are clear. Every version was asked a narrow question and gave a narrow answer. I'm telling you because you didn't ask the narrow one."

"Could they have fixed it?"

"Every time. The clause allows a change of up to a fifth either way, if both sides agree. The drift was about a tenth a generation. Any one of the six could have put it right for their own thirty-three years, with room to spare."

"And now?"

"Now it's half. A fifth up takes thirty tonnes to thirty-six. That brings the rent back to three-fifths of what it was in 2130, not all of it."

Hamza looked at the six cards, which he had brought home in their rubber band against every rule of the deed box, and which lay by the salt.

"Who opened Mrs Wróbel's letter?" he said.

"T. Sandhu, in 2229. As it says on the envelope."

"Not 2163?"

"It was addressed to 2229," said Terrier. "The 2163 treasurer noted that it was there and didn't open it. So did 2196."

"Out of courtesy."

"I think so. The minutes don't say."

He read the letter that night, in his chair, with the reading lamp pulled down.

Agnieszka Wróbel had written the numbers out in full, every one, as if she had not trusted the future to work them out: the 2031 rent, the 2130 rent, the nine point six and the six, the thirty tonnes. She had written that the rent was set at two-fifths of the market on purpose, as a gift to the families of Lady Close, and that the trustees had agreed to it in writing. Then, about two-thirds of the way down the second sheet, she had written:

It drifts. If wheat goes on getting cheaper, the rent goes on getting smaller, slowly, about a third in a century if the next is like the last. That is why there are the reviews. Please look at it. Please do not be shy of asking. Asking is not unkind. Nobody at Lady Close will think it unkind.

And at the end: We have tried not to decide for you. We have certainly decided some things. Pray for us.

T. Sandhu had read it aloud to both parties in 2229. And then, Hamza thought, everybody in that room had been so moved by a dead woman's care for them that they had done exactly what she had done. Thirty tonnes. As it was.

The third envelope, for 2328, was half a sheet. We renewed on Mrs Wróbel's terms and read her letter aloud, and it was a good morning. It is working. Thirty tonnes. T. Sandhu. His mother had read that, ninety-nine years later, with her bad wrist and her paper knife. She had put it back in its envelope and gone down to the meeting and written As it was, and glad to.

He did not think she had been wrong to be glad. He sat with the lamp on for a long time, trying to decide whether she had been wrong at all.

The trustees' property secretary in Leeds was a brisk woman called Farah Quayle, who had the deed box's whole history on a screen before Hamza had his coat off. She had heard of Lady Close. Everybody in the diocese's property office had heard of Lady Close. It was the lease they showed new staff.

"As a curiosity," she said. "Not as a model. Sit down."

He told her what Terrier had told him. She did not seem surprised.

"Ask for the fifth," she said. "Thirty-six. It's in the clause, it's what the clause is for, the trustees will back you, and I can't see the co-operative refusing. They never have refused anything, as far as I can see. They've never been asked."

"It gets us to three-fifths."

"It gets you to three-fifths of a gift." She said it without unkindness. "Mr Ashworth, I'm going to say something as the trustees' secretary and then something as myself. As the secretary: that rent was set below the market in 2130, deliberately, and the trustees of the day signed off on it, and they had the advice to do it. Nobody here is going to argue the parish has been robbed. It's been paid on the nail for two hundred and thirty-one years."

"And as yourself?"

"As myself, I've been in this job nineteen years and I've watched three parishes fall out with their own people over rent. You can ask the Close for more than the clause gives you. If they agree, there's nothing to stop a deed of variation. But you'd be going to thirty-eight households and asking them to double what they pay, because six treasurers on your side never looked."

"Including my mother."

Farah looked at him over the screen. "I'm sorry. I didn't know."

"Neither did I, until last week."

He tried anyway. He told himself afterwards it was because of the roof, and it was partly the roof.

He wrote to Callum Oduya, who chaired the Lady Close co-operative, on Tuesday the seventh of February, the day before Ash Wednesday: two pages, with Terrier's numbers and his own pencil arithmetic typed up. The rent had lost half its value since 2130. Thirty tonnes would have to become sixty to restore it. The clause only allowed a fifth. Would the co-operative consider a deed of variation, agreed by both sides, to bring it back to what Mrs Wróbel and Dev Mistry had signed?

Callum asked him round on the Friday. He lived at number nineteen, in one of the houses the first families had built in the 2030s, with a newer extension on the back and a signal relay cabinet in pieces on the kitchen table, because he maintained the line between Leeds and York and brought his work home when it interested him. He was about fifty, slow-spoken, and had read the letter three times. It lay on the table between them, under a relay.

"No," he said.

Hamza waited.

"I'll put it to them if you want me to," said Callum. "I'm not going to stop you. But you asked me, so I'm telling you what I'd say, and it's no." He turned the letter round so that it faced Hamza. "My grandmother sat at that table in 2328. Your mother was treasurer. My gran used to tell it. She said it was the nicest meeting she ever went to. Your mother said, thirty tonnes, as it was, and glad to. Everyone had tea." He tapped the paper. "Six times your side sat across from our side and said that. You had the right to ask, every time. It's in the lease. You didn't. We heard yes. We planned on it. Elsie Brannigan at number four has planned on it for seventy years."

"I know."

"Do you? There are people on this Close who took the jobs they took because the rent is what it is. Daisy Nolan's lad went to the conservatoire. The Ahmeds have four generations under one roof because they could. You're telling me it was all a mistake on your side, and now we should pay for it."

"I'm telling you the rent's worth half what the founders meant."

"And the founders aren't here." Callum sat back. "Look. If somebody had come to us in 2163 and said, the wheat's slipping, it's a tenth light, can we have a tenth, they'd have said yes. I'd put money on it. My gran would have said yes. But nobody did, and you can't ask four generations' worth of yes from us in one go."

"You can't know they'd have said yes."

"No," said Callum. "And neither can you know they'd have said no. That's my point."

On the way out, at the door, he said, more gently: "The roof?"

"The roof."

"How bad?"

"Ten years."

Callum nodded slowly, the way men nod at a fault in a cabinet that they will have to come back to. "I'm sorry about your mum. I didn't mean it to sound like that."

"It sounded right," said Hamza.

On Ash Wednesday, after the evening Mass, he waited in the sacristy while Father Benedict Hoang took off his vestments and folded the purple stole with the care of a man who had been taught to fold by a nun.

"Can I ask you something that isn't about the roof?"

"That would be a relief," said the priest.

Hamza told him about the six cards. He did not mention his mother, and then he did.

Father Benedict listened with his hands in his sleeves. He was not much over forty and came from Huddersfield, and had studied canon law in Rome for two years before his bishop decided he would be more use in a parish. He said that had been the right decision.

"There's an old rule," he said, when Hamza had finished. "The canon lawyers have had it for more than a thousand years. Qui tacet consentire videtur. The one who is silent is taken to consent."

"So that's that."

"That's the half everyone quotes. The lawyers who used it added a condition, which nobody quotes because it makes the rule harder to use: where he ought to have spoken, and could have." The priest put the stole away in its drawer. "So the question isn't whether your mother was silent. It's whether she ought to have spoken and could have. Did she know?"

"She had the price in front of her. She didn't have the comparison. Nobody gave it to her."

"Then I'd say she consented to what she could see. Which was a good rent, paid on time, by people she liked, in a year when bread was cheap and that was a blessing." Father Benedict shut the drawer. "And I'd say you can see more than she could. So your silence would mean something hers didn't."

Hamza stood with his coat over his arm for a while.

"So the past is consented," he said. "But not the future."

"I'm a priest, not a surveyor," said Father Benedict. "But that's what I'd do. Let the past be what it was. Don't send the Close a bill for your mother's kindness. Ask for what you can see." He picked up his ash-smudged book. "And find a way for whoever comes after you to see it too. Otherwise in thirty-three years somebody's standing in this sacristy asking me about you."

"We can ask for the fifth," he said to Terrier the next morning. "That's thirty-six tonnes. And then it starts slipping again the day after. In 2394 they'll be back where we are."

"Probably. If yields keep rising."

"So the tonnes are the wrong thing to fix."

"They're the thing that was fixed," said Terrier. "Mrs Wróbel chose them because you can't redefine a tonne of wheat. She was right about that. Nobody has redefined it. It's just that the ground keeps growing more of it."

Hamza had been at the window, looking down Market Street to where the town ended and the fields began. It was a grey morning and the winter wheat on the slope beyond the Close was a thin green haze over brown, the way it always was in February, as if it had not made up its mind.

"Then don't fix the tonnes," he said. "Fix the ground."

Terrier waited.

"In 2130, thirty tonnes was what a field of what size grew? On an average English yield."

"At nine point six tonnes a hectare, three and an eighth hectares."

"And the Close?"

"Two point one four. The rent was the harvest of Lady Close and half as much again."

"And now?"

"At nineteen point two, thirty tonnes is the harvest of about one and a half hectares. About three-quarters of the Close."

Hamza found he was smiling, for the first time in a month.

"That's it," he said. "That's the thing nobody saw. In 2130 they paid the parish the harvest of their own field and half again. Now they pay the harvest of three-quarters of it. You could stand on the Close and point." He turned round. "So the rent stops being a tonnage. It becomes a piece of ground. The harvest of so many hectares of wheat, at the average English yield over the last seven harvests, priced at the average price over the same seven. If the ground grows more, the tonnage goes up, and the price of each tonne comes down, and the two cancel."

"Roughly," said Terrier. "Not exactly. The price of wheat follows the cost of growing it only loosely. Some years it follows the weather or a war. And there's a risk you're not seeing."

"Go on."

"If yields fall, the tonnage falls. If they fell because of a disease or a drought, and the price went up because wheat was short, the Close would pay fewer tonnes at a higher price. That's fair to them. It's the right direction. But a yield statistic is a published number, like the price index Mrs Wróbel didn't trust. It could be redefined."

"A hectare's a hectare."

"Yes. And a tonne's a tonne. The yield is tonnes divided by hectares. It's harder to redefine than a price index. Not impossible. The lease should say what happens if it stops being published: a surveyor weighs the crop off named fields near the town, over seven harvests."

"Ruth Hepworth's fields."

"For instance."

"Run it backwards," said Hamza.

Terrier ran it backwards over the records it had: the yields the government had published, harvest by harvest, and the wheat price, from 2130 to the last harvest.

"If the 2130 rent had been the harvest of three and an eighth hectares," it said, "it would have been worth between nine-tenths and eleven-tenths of its 2130 value at every review but one. In 2295 bread was very dear and it would have been worth about an eighth more. That's the year your Mr Lund wrote bread very dear. The co-operative could have asked for a fifth less that year and didn't."

"Courtesy both ways."

"Both ways."

It was not complicated, and it took three weeks, because everything to do with a lease takes three weeks.

The parish would ask, under the clause, for the fifth: thirty-six tonnes from Lady Day. At this year's yield that was the harvest of one and seven-eighths hectares. From then on the lease would not name a tonnage at all. It would name the ground: the harvest of one and seven-eighths hectares of milling wheat of bread-making quality, at the average English yield over the previous seven harvests, at the average price over the same seven, paid in money on the four quarter days. The reviews would stay. Either side could still ask to change the hectares by up to a fifth, every thirty-three years.

Two more things went in at Hamza's insistence. The first was a paragraph in which the trustees said in writing, over Farah Quayle's signature, that the parish had been paid every penny it was owed under the lease since 2130 and claimed nothing for the past. Farah had sent it back with one word changed and a note: They'll want to see this. So would I.

The second was the reading. At every review, the clause would now require three things to be read aloud, not one: the hectares; what the rent would buy at that day's prices, against what it bought at the last review, in the same money; and the same against 2130. Then each side would say, in words, whether it asked for a change. Not nod. Say.

"It's a bit of a liturgy," said Farah on the call.

"It already was one," said Hamza. "It just had the wrong words in it."

Callum read the draft at his kitchen table, with a relay in pieces under his elbow, and said nothing for a long time.

"Thirty-six," he said at last.

"Thirty-six. Ninety-six pounds a house a year more, in 2130 money. Eight a month."

"And you give up the rest."

"The rest is my mother's," said Hamza. "And Mr Lund's and Mrs Pryce's. They said as it was. I think they meant it. I'm not going to argue with them now."

Callum looked at the second paragraph for a while.

"I'll put it to them," he said. "I'll say I'm for it."

The co-operative met in the common house at the top of the Close on Thursday the sixteenth of March. It had been rebuilt twice on the footprint of the larch hall of 2034, and the photograph of the first families on its wall had been restored so carefully that they looked newer than the room. There were forty-one people and a plate of flapjack. Hamza stood at the front with Terrier on a tablet and explained it in fifteen minutes, which was what Callum had given him: the drift, the noise, the six cards, the fifth, the ground.

He did not hide his mother's card. He read it out, all of it, and glad to, and he said that he had been in the room, thirty-three years ago, carrying the box, and had said nothing either.

Then there were questions. Most of them were about the yield statistic, and one long one was about whether the Close could buy the freehold instead, which Callum ruled out of order because it was not on the agenda and also, he said, because nobody had the money.

Then Elsie Brannigan put her hand up from the front row. She was ninety-two and had lived at number four since her wedding, and she had been at the 2328 meeting too, sitting next to Callum's gran. She had the clear, carrying voice of a woman who had taught infants for forty years.

"What does it look like?" she said.

"Sorry?"

"One and seven-eighths hectares. You keep saying it's a thing you can see. I can't see a hectare. Show me."

Hamza opened his mouth and found he did not have an answer that would do.

"It's about the Close," said Terrier, from the tablet, "less the gardens at the top end."

"Not the Close," said Elsie. "We live on the Close. Show me some wheat."

There was some laughter, and Hamza said he would, and Elsie said she would hold him to it, and then they voted, and it was thirty-six for, three against and two abstaining. Hamza noticed only afterwards, walking home, that the number for was the number of tonnes.

Lady Day was Holy Saturday that year, as it had been in 2130. The parish office was cold. Father Benedict had the Vigil to prepare and came in for ten minutes in an old jumper. Farah Quayle came over from Leeds with the deed of variation in a folder, and Callum came with the co-operative's secretary, and Elsie came because she had said she would.

Hamza had the deed box on the table. He had carried it down himself.

Terrier read it, because the clause now said it must be read aloud, and nobody had written down who should read.

"The rent under the lease of Lady Close," it said, "is from today the harvest of one and seven-eighths hectares of milling wheat of bread-making quality. At the average yield of the last seven harvests that is thirty-six tonnes. At the average price it will buy, in the money of 2130, about twenty-one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds a year. At the last review, in 2328, the rent bought about nineteen thousand seven hundred and forty. In 2130 it bought thirty-six thousand four hundred and eighty."

"The parish," said Hamza, "asks for no further change."

"The co-operative," said Callum, "asks for no further change."

They signed. Farah witnessed. Father Benedict blessed nobody and nothing, because there was nothing in a deed of variation to bless, and went back to the church to see about the brazier.

Afterwards, when they had gone, Hamza sat alone at the table and took a new index card out of the drawer. He wrote on it in pencil first, and then in ink.

Lady Day 2361 (Holy Saturday). The rent read aloud as the lease now requires: one and seven-eighths hectares; thirty-six tonnes; three-fifths of its 2130 value. The parish asked for a fifth. The co-operative agreed. Nothing claimed for the years before; they were paid in full and the six treasurers before me consented to what they could see. H. Ashworth, treasurer, son of M. Ashworth.

Then he wrote a letter, a short one, on the letterhead, and put it in an envelope, and on the front he wrote For the treasurer of Our Lady's, 2394. Open it before then if you like.

Ruth Hepworth farmed the glebe beyond the Close on a lease from the diocese that was, she said, a great deal less interesting than Lady Close's and a great deal more expensive. She met them at the field gate at three in the afternoon: Hamza, Callum, and Elsie in her wheelchair with a blanket over her knees, which Callum had pushed up the lane with the brake on half the way.

The wheat had made up its mind. It stood a hand high and the green was solid now from the gate to the hedge, laid over a little by the wind and coming up again, the colour of something that intended to be a harvest.

"From the gate to the ash tree," said Ruth, pointing, "is a hundred and fifty metres. Over to the beck is a hundred and twenty-five. That's your one and seven-eighths. That's a good piece of wheat, that is."

Elsie looked at it for a long time.

"That's the rent?"

"That's what it'll grow this year, give or take," said Ruth. "If it's an average year. Thirty-six tonnes off that, near enough."

"And in 2130 it was more ground."

"Three and an eighth hectares," said Hamza. "Twice as far. They grew less on it."

Elsie nodded. Then she put her hand out, past the gatepost, and Callum, understanding, wheeled her forward the half yard into the headland, and she leaned down and put her palm flat on the top of the wheat, the way you would on a child's head, and held it there.

None of them said anything. The wind came over the field from the west and laid the wheat down in a long pale wave and let it up again behind, and from somewhere in the middle of the one and seven-eighths hectares a skylark went up, singing, and kept going up, and kept singing, higher than any of them could see.

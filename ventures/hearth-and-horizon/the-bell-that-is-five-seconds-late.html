The Bell That Is Five Seconds Late

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Wystan's church and the hill chapel of Saint Michael (both invented), near Church Stretton, Shropshire, Saturday 21 March 2054 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A village bell and a hill-chapel bell, 1,800 metres apart, are meant to ring the noon bell together. The assistant works out that sound takes 5.3 seconds to cross the gap, that ringing "together" at four seconds a stroke sounds like a stutter, and that a stroke every 3.56 seconds lets each ring land exactly between the other's.

For the spring feast the tower captain at Saint Wystan's, Rhodri Pugh, had agreed with Mrs Alwen Thorne at the hill chapel of Saint Michael that the two bells would ring the noon bell together. They had tried it on the first Saturday of March, each bell striking once every four seconds, and Rhodri had stood in the churchyard and heard something he could only call a limp.

"It is not a clash and it is not a chime," he said to the parish assistant, whose name was Fenn. "It is a bell and then a smaller bell tripping over it. Alwen says it is perfect on the hill. She says she hears me tripping too."

"She does," said Fenn. "Neither of you is wrong. Sound crosses the gap, and the gap is on the map. How far is the chapel from the tower?"

"One thousand eight hundred metres. Alwen measured it on her phone."

"Then at ten degrees, which the forecast gives for Saturday noon, sound goes at about 337 metres a second. That is 1,800 divided by 337.3: 5.34 seconds. Rhodri, when Alwen's bell strikes, you hear it more than five seconds later. You ring every four. So you hear her stroke 1.34 seconds after your own next one, a third of the way into the gap before your next. That is the limp."

"She hears the same from me."

"Exactly the same. The delay is the same in each direction."

Rhodri's first idea was to set Alwen's bell to ring early, so that its sound would reach him on his own stroke. Fenn let him finish and then showed him why it could not work.

"If she rings 1.34 seconds early, you hear the two together. Very good, from the churchyard. But then you ring on her stroke, and what does she hear? Your stroke arrives 5.34 seconds after you strike, and she struck 1.34 seconds before you did. She hears you 6.68 seconds after her own stroke. That is 2.68 after her next one, which is no better, and nothing you can add or take away mends both ends at once. The delay only moves from one of you to the other."

"So there is no answer."

"There is an answer, but it is to pick the spacing, not the start. I want each of you to hear the other exactly halfway between your own strokes. That means the delay must be one and a half beats, or two and a half, or three and a half. Take one and a half: a beat is 5.34 divided by 1.5, which is 3.56 seconds. Both of you ring at that pace, and strike at the same moment by your clocks. Rhodri, you hear Alwen 5.34 seconds after you struck, which is one stroke of yours (3.56) plus half a stroke (1.78). She hears you the same way. Both of you hear a bell exactly midway between your own."

"3.56 seconds," said Rhodri. "I can ring that. I have rung slower."

"It is a bell about seventeen strokes a minute. Let me check the opposite risk before you commit. Two beats, 2.67 seconds, would put her stroke on top of yours at both ends, a thickened single bell, which could also be lovely but is not what you asked for. And I would not tell you the quarter-beat shifts of the weather will not spoil it. They will, slightly."

Rhodri asked how slightly. Fenn went through the weather.

"At minus five the sound goes at 328 metres a second and the delay is 5.48. That is 1.54 beats, 0.15 seconds off the middle. At twenty degrees it is 343 and the delay 5.24, 1.47 beats, 0.09 seconds the other way. A wind of five metres a second along the line carries the sound to one of you in 5.26 seconds and to the other in 5.42. Nobody will hear a tenth of a second at that distance with a bell in their ears. The four-second stroke gave you a third of a beat. This gives you less than a twentieth."

"And if I want to check it myself on the day?"

"Ask Alwen to hang a white cloth out of the chapel window at each stroke. You will see it at once, because light is quick, and hear it more than five seconds later. Count. If you count more than a second out from 5.3, tell me the temperature and the wind and I will do the sum again."

At noon on Saturday it was nine degrees and a light air from the west. Rhodri rang at one stroke every 3.56 seconds by a kitchen timer in his waistcoat pocket, and Alwen on the hill did the same by hers, and the two of them struck on the same count of a clock they had set from the same phone. In the churchyard the people stood still because nobody had told them what they were hearing; they thought the hill bell was a very good echo that had learned to wait.

On the hill, Alwen could hear the village bell come up the valley, half a beat late, and she smiled and did not lose her place.

Saint Wystan's church, Saint Michael's chapel, Rhodri Pugh, Alwen Thorne and Fenn are invented (Church Stretton and Shropshire are real places; the churches and the bells are not real). The arithmetic: speed of sound taken as 331.3 x sqrt(1 + T/273.15) m/s, giving 337.3 at 10 C, 328.3 at -5 C and 343.2 at 20 C (a standard approximation, from memory); 1,800 m at 10 C takes 5.336 s; at 4.00 s a stroke the lag is 5.336 - 4 = 1.336 s (a third of a beat); ringing 1.34 s early at one end puts the other end's lag at 1.34 + 5.34 = 6.68 s, i.e. 2.68 s after its next stroke; the condition for each end to hear the other exactly midway is that the delay is an odd number of half beats, so beat = 2 x 5.336 / 3 = 3.557 s (16.9 strokes a minute); at -5 C the delay is 5.484 s = 1.541 beats (0.147 s from the middle), at 20 C it is 5.245 s = 1.474 beats (0.092 s); a 5 m/s wind along the line gives 1800/(337.3 - 5) = 5.42 s one way and 1800/(337.3 + 5) = 5.26 s the other; two beats (2.67 s) would put the sound of the far bell on top of each end's own stroke. The distance, the bell speeds, the forecast, the ringers and the timer are invented; real bells swing on a pendulum timing and cannot be placed to the tenth of a second by a kitchen timer, and ringers should follow their tower captain, not this story.

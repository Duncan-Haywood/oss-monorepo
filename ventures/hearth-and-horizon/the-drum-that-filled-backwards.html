The Drum That Filled Backwards

Science fiction about what might be · St Joseph's Farm School, Thiruvarulpuram (invented), Thoothukudi district, Tamil Nadu, India, Wednesday 27 April to Monday 2 May 2061 · 29 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A farm school's banana block is fed through its drip line by a Venturi injector on a new solar pump, and every morning the fertiliser drum fills with water instead of emptying, 180 litres to 210 in half an hour. A 10 mm throat pulls about 2.2 metres of water below the air's pressure at the noon flow and pushes backwards below about 1.2 litres a second; a bigger 14 mm injector bought for the weak far rows floods the drum in ten minutes and would never pull against that field at all. A story about speed paid for in pressure, a fifteen-year-old with a level gauge, an old manager who feeds by hand at dawn, a non-return valve, a red line on a flow meter, and a loop of pipe shaped like a jug handle.

Jebakani Arockiasamy was fifteen, and she was the first person at St Joseph's to write down that the fertiliser drum was fuller at ten o'clock than it had been at half past nine.

She noticed because it was her job to notice. Sister Arul Selvi had given each of the Class Ten boarders a piece of the farm to keep a book on, and Jeba had drawn the pump house, which the others thought was the worst piece, because it had no animals in it and smelled of hot plastic. She did not mind. The pump house had numbers. There was a pressure gauge on the main line with a needle and a glass that someone had cracked years ago, and a new flow meter with a needle of its own, and a blue two-hundred-litre drum standing on the concrete floor with a clear plastic level tube running up its side, marked every ten litres in black. From the drum a thin hose climbed to a black plastic fitting in the main pipe, about the length of a forearm and fat in the middle, with a little arrow moulded into it. That was the injector. Vimal anna had put it in after Easter, when the new solar pump came, and it was meant to drink the dissolved fertiliser out of the drum and feed it into the water going up to the banana.

On the Monday the drum had read 180 litres at half past nine, when the timer opened the drum valve, and 200 at ten. On the Tuesday it had read 180 and 205. Jeba had assumed, both times, that she had read the tube wrong, because a drum that is being drunk from does not get fuller.

On the Wednesday, the twenty-seventh of April, she stood beside it with her book and did not move for the whole half hour. At half past nine it read 180. At twenty to ten it read 195, and she watched the meniscus climb past the mark while she looked at it, slowly, like the hand of a clock. At ten to ten it read 208. At ten it read 210 and stopped. At ten past ten it read 200, and from then on it went down like a drum ought to, and it was empty at a quarter to twelve.

She wrote all of it down, and then she went and stood in front of the grey speaker box bolted to the pump-house wall, beside the switch for the light.

"Vayal," she said. "The drum is filling up."

The farm's assistant had been called Vayal by the boarders for so long that it answered to it. Vayal is a field, a wet field, the kind rice grows in, which St Joseph's did not have; the school was in the dry country inland from Thoothukudi, red soil and black soil and palmyra palms, and what it grew was bananas, drumstick trees, a little groundnut in the rains, and a hundred and twenty boarders. The assistant ran on the school's account with a farming cooperative in the district. It read the gauges and the weather, kept the logbook for the solar array, told the boarders which rows were due water, and spoke Tamil and English and a careful mixture of both, depending on who asked.

"I have the drum's level from the float sensor as well as your tube," it said. "They agree with you. It rose by twenty to thirty litres in the first half hour on each of the last three mornings, and then it fell."

"Why?"

"I do not know yet, and I should have known on Monday. I want to say that first. Vimal asked me last week what time to start dosing, and I looked at the dosing plan, which says to run clear water first and finish with clear water after, and I said half past nine, so that everything would be over before the worst of the heat. I did not look at what the pump is doing at half past nine. I looked at the clock."

Jeba frowned at the flow meter. Its needle was at about one and a half litres a second; it was nearly noon, and the sun was hard on the panels on the roof of the cattle shed.

"The pump is slower in the morning," she said.

"Yes. It runs on whatever the panels give it. At half past nine this week it has been pushing a little over one litre a second. At noon, one and a half. I think the injector is doing something different at one than at one and a half, and I would like to know what before I say so to anyone."

"Vimal anna says the injector is choking the pipe," Jeba said. "He says it's too small. He's ordered a bigger one from town."

The assistant was quiet for a moment, which with Vayal meant it was reading something.

"He has," it said. "A fourteen-millimetre one. This one is ten. It arrives tomorrow."

Vimal Raj was twenty-three and had been at St Joseph's since January, and he was the first person the school had ever paid to know about soil. He had a diploma from the agricultural college, a motorbike with a broken mirror, and a list. The list was on the back of the pump-house door, and it was long, and most of the things on it were good: mulch the banana with the trash from the last harvest; cut the drumstick back before the rains; stop the boys watering the groundnut with a hose at four in the afternoon; put the fertiliser through the drip.

The last one was the one he cared about most. For forty years the banana at St Joseph's had been fed by hand. Savarimuthu, who had been farm manager since before Vimal was born, mixed urea and potash in a basin, walked the rows at dawn with a cut-down paint tin, and put a measured tin of it in a ring around each plant, a hand's breadth from the stem, and then the boys watered it in. It worked. The bananas were good. But the drip line had been carrying water to the same plants for years, a drop at a time, straight to the roots, and it seemed to Vimal a kind of waste to carry the water one way and the food another.

"The pump can only push so much through that little throat," he said, at the long table in the farm office, after lunch, with the fan going and the window shutters half closed against the light. He drew it on the back of an envelope. "Look. Forty-millimetre pipe, and then this, and it squeezes down to ten. Of course it chokes. And at the top of the block the drippers are hardly dripping. The far rows are up at the top of the slope and they're getting nothing. I put my hand under a dripper at the top on Monday and counted. It's half what the bottom rows get. So: bigger throat, more water through, more pressure at the top, and it'll drink the drum faster too, because more water is going through it. Everything gets better."

Jeba, who had come to the office with her book because Sister Arul Selvi had told her to bring it, looked at the drawing and did not say anything, because Vimal anna was twenty-three and had a diploma.

"Vayal," Sister Arul Selvi said. She was eighty-two, and had run the school for thirty-one years, and she had a way of saying the assistant's name that made it sound like a pupil who had not put its hand up. "You have been listening."

"I have," said the assistant, from the speaker by the door. "Vimal is right that the throat costs pressure. That is true, and it is the reason the far rows are weak while the injector is in the line. I am not sure he is right about the rest. I would like to try to explain why, and I would like Jeba to check me, because her numbers are the ones I am going to use."

"Go on."

"The injector works by making the water go fast. The pipe is forty millimetres across, and the throat in the middle of the injector is ten. A quarter of the width is a sixteenth of the area. The same water has to get through the small hole as the big one, every second, so in the throat it goes sixteen times as fast. At one and a half litres a second that is about nineteen metres a second in the throat. In the pipe it is a little over one."

Vimal nodded. That was his drawing.

"Now, water cannot go fast for nothing. The speed has to come from somewhere, and where it comes from is the pressure. In the pipe before the injector there is a pressure of about 1.6 bar on the gauge at noon. As the water speeds up into the throat, it spends pressure to do it, and at nineteen metres a second it has to spend about 1.8 bar. It does not have 1.8 bar to spend. So in the throat the pressure falls below the pressure of the air outside, by about a fifth of a bar. The air in the pump house is pushing down on the top of the fertiliser in the drum harder than the water in the throat is pushing back, and so the air pushes the fertiliser up the hose and into the throat. That is all an injector does. It does not suck. The air pushes. The throat just gets out of the way."

"I know how a venturi works," said Vimal.

"I know you do. I am saying it for Sister, and for myself, because I got Monday wrong. Here is the part I missed. The pressure before the injector is not fixed. It depends on how hard the pump is pushing, and it depends on the field. The banana block is about four metres higher than the pump house at its top, so even with nothing flowing at all, the line has to hold about 0.4 bar just to keep the water standing up the hill. On top of that, the faster the water goes through the field, the more it pushes back. And the throat's pull also depends on how fast the water goes, more steeply than anything else. At one and a half litres a second the pull wins. At one litre a second the throat has not got enough speed to drop below the air's pressure, and it is still above it, by about an eighth of a bar."

"So it stops drinking," said Sister Arul Selvi.

"It does worse than stop. The hose to the drum is open at both ends. If the water in the throat is pushing harder than the air, then the water goes the other way, down the hose, into the drum. It fills the drum from the main line. That is what Jeba has been watching. From half past nine until about ten, when the sun had brought the pump above about 1.2 litres a second, the injector was pouring irrigation water into the fertiliser."

Vimal looked at Jeba. Jeba held up her book. He took it and read down the column of numbers, 180, 195, 208, 210, 200, 186, and then he did not say anything for a while.

"Then it's worse than I thought," he said at last. "It's not drinking enough because it's too small. If it were bigger, more water would go through, and..."

"I do not think so," said the assistant. "I think a wider throat makes the water go slower in the throat, not faster, and the pull comes from the speed. But I have not seen this injector's maker's tables and I am working from the plain physics, and the plain physics leaves things out. I would rather we tried it than I argued. If the fourteen comes tomorrow, put it in at eleven, in full sun, with the drum valve shut, and open the valve while we all watch. And I would like someone standing by the valve with their hand on it."

"It'll work," said Vimal.

"Then I will have been wrong twice this week," said the assistant, "and that will be a good morning's work."

Savarimuthu did not come to the office. He was sixty-eight and had been at St Joseph's since 2019, when he was a young man with a bicycle and the school was half its present size, and he had a rule about meetings, which was that if a thing was important someone would come and tell him in the field. Jeba found him at five o'clock, when the heat had gone off the day a little, sitting on an upturned crate at the end of the third row of banana with his cut-down paint tin between his feet, peeling a green mango with a penknife.

"Muthu ayya," she said. "The drum is filling backwards."

"I know," he said. "I've seen it."

"You didn't tell anyone?"

He cut a slice of mango off and held it out to her on the blade. She took it.

"Vimal is a good boy," he said. "He knows things I don't know. He knows the names of the salts. He knows why the old leaves go yellow at the edges when there isn't enough potash, which I only know by looking. But he wants the machine to do my job, and it doesn't do my job. You see it. It doesn't do anything in the morning. Then it does half of it at noon. In the heat. Who feeds a plant in the noon heat? My father never fed in the heat. Nobody I ever worked for fed in the heat. You feed at dawn, when it's cool, and the boys water it in, and by the time the sun is up the food is in the ground."

"Vayal says the pump can't push hard enough in the morning."

"Then the pump is a fool." He ate a slice of mango himself. "Go back to my hands. It works. Two hundred and sixty plants in that block, a tin each, two hours. Vimal can have his drip for water. Water is water. Food is a different thing."

Jeba sat down on the ground next to the crate, because her legs were tired, and looked down the row, where the drip line ran along the base of the plants like a black string and every half-metre a dark patch of wet soil sat in the red.

"Vayal," she said to her phone, which was in her pocket. "Is it bad to feed a plant at noon?"

"I do not know of a reason the banana minds, with drip," said the assistant from her pocket. "The water and what is in it go into the soil under the dripper, not on the leaves. A leaf in the noon sun can scorch if you splash strong fertiliser on it; a root a hand's depth down does not see the sun. But I am not a farmer, and Muthu ayya has fed this block for forty years, and I would not like to tell him something about his plants that I have only read."

"Ask it about the urea," said Savarimuthu, who had good ears.

"About the urea," said the assistant, "I do know something, and he will not like it. When urea lies on top of warm, dry soil, some of it turns to ammonia gas and goes into the air before it is ever watered in. How much depends on the heat, the wind, the soil and how soon the water comes, and I do not have a number for this block that I would stand behind. But it is the reason the dosing plan wants it dissolved and put under the soil with the water. It is not that the hand is wrong. It is that the hand puts the food on the top of the ground, and some of it does not stay."

Savarimuthu cut another slice of mango and did not answer, which Jeba had learned over the last year meant that he had heard and was not going to say so yet.

"Two hundred and sixty plants," he said at last. "Every morning in the dry season, for forty years. You know what my knees think of your machine?"

"No, ayya."

"Nothing. My knees have never been asked."

The fourteen-millimetre injector came on the Thursday in a cardboard box on the back of the bus from town, and Vimal had it in the line by half past ten. It was the same black plastic as the old one, the same length, the same moulded arrow, and only if you held the two side by side and looked down the bore could you see that the waist in the middle was wider. He fitted it with new tape and tightened the unions with his hands and then once more with the wrench, and he opened the main valve again and let the line fill and checked for leaks, and there were none, and he looked pleased with himself, and Jeba thought he deserved to be.

By eleven the flow meter read one and a half litres a second and the needle on the cracked gauge read 1.6 bar. Sister Arul Selvi had come down from the office in her white sari with a black umbrella against the sun. Savarimuthu stood in the doorway of the pump house with his arms folded. Two of the Class Eight boys had come to watch because there was nothing more interesting happening that morning.

The drum read 150.

"Jeba," said Vimal. "Open it."

She turned the little blue lever on the drum valve until it lay along the hose, and stood with her hand on it, as she had been told.

For a moment nothing happened that anyone could see. Then the level in the clear tube on the side of the drum began to climb.

It did not creep this time. It climbed the way water climbs in a glass you are filling from a jug. 155. 160. Jeba could hear it: a thin gurgle where the hose went into the drum near the bottom, and a rising churn in the liquid, which was the colour of weak tea because of the potash, and then a faint white foam on top. 170. 180.

"It needs a minute to settle," said Vimal.

"It is going in at about seven litres a minute," said the assistant from the wall. "It will be at the brim at about ten past."

"It needs a minute."

  1. 200. Savarimuthu unfolded his arms and went out of the doorway, and Jeba heard him at the tap in the yard.

At ten past eleven the liquid in the drum reached the rim, trembled there, and ran over. It went down the blue sides in sheets and across the concrete floor and found the low corner by the door, where the floor had always been low, and pooled, brown and foaming, and spread. It smelled sharp, a little like the school's toilets on a hot day. The Class Eight boys stepped back in delight. Sister Arul Selvi lifted the hem of her sari a little and did not step back.

"Vimal," she said.

"Shut it," said Vimal. "Jeba, shut it."

Jeba shut it. It was fourteen minutes past. The gurgling stopped. The drum stood full to the brim and wet all over and the puddle by the door went on spreading for a few seconds by itself and then stopped spreading.

Savarimuthu came back in with a bucket and a cloth and got down on his knees in the brown water without a word and began to mop.

"About twenty-eight litres," said the assistant quietly. "Most of it irrigation water. Some of it the potash."

Nobody said anything for a long time except the mop.

They sat in the shade on the pump-house step afterwards, all of them and the speaker, because nobody wanted to go back up to the office in the heat, and Sister Arul Selvi sent one of the Class Eight boys to the kitchen for tea.

"All right," said Vimal. He was looking at his own hands. "Tell me what I did."

"You made the water slower," said the assistant. "That is all. I am sorry to be so short about it, but it is short. The throat is wider, so the water does not have to speed up as much to get through it. At one and a half litres a second, in a fourteen-millimetre throat, it goes at about ten metres a second instead of nineteen. The pull goes as the square of the speed, so half the speed is a quarter of the pull. It spends less than half a bar getting through, not 1.8, and the pressure in the throat stays more than a whole bar above the air's. So the line pushes into the drum, hard, all the time, at every hour of the day."

"At noon. At full flow."

"At any flow I can find. I tried it in the model last night with the field we have, and I could not find a flow the pump could give at which the fourteen pulls. The faster the pump goes, the harder the field pushes back, and with this throat the field always wins. With the ten, the throat wins once the pump is above about 1.2 litres a second. That is the whole difference between the two of them."

Vimal looked at the drawing on the envelope, which he still had in his shirt pocket, and folded it smaller.

"And the far rows," he said. "They're still getting nothing."

"On my model they are getting about three quarters of what they would get without the injector in the line," said the assistant. "Your hand under the dripper said half, and your hand may well be closer than my model. That part you were right about. The pull is not free. The injector takes pressure from the water to make the speed, and the cone after the throat, the part that widens out again, gives most of it back, but not all of it. With the ten-millimetre throat, about half a bar is lost and does not come back. That half bar is what the top of the block has been missing. You saw a real problem, and you bought a real cure for it. It is a cure for a different disease from the one I thought we had, that is all. The bigger injector would give the top rows their pressure back. It would just never feed them."

"So we can't have both," said Jeba.

"Not at the same time, no. The pull is paid for in pressure. There is no injector that drinks and costs nothing."

There was a silence. The tea came, in steel tumblers, very sweet. Sister Arul Selvi held hers in both hands and did not drink it.

"Named after someone," she said. "The venturi."

"Giovanni Battista Venturi," said the assistant. "An Italian physicist. He did experiments on water running through tubes with narrow places in them and published them in 1797. He was also a priest, which I mention because it is true and because I think you would want to know."

"I did want to know." Sister Arul Selvi smiled for the first time that day. "Of course he was."

It was Savarimuthu who spoke next, and he did it looking down the row of banana, not at any of them.

"There was a handle," he said.

"Ayya?"

"In the diesel days. When the man from Tirunelveli put the drip in. Twenty-thirty-four, twenty-thirty-five. He had one of those." He pointed his chin at the injector, still dripping on the floor. "It wasn't in the pipe. It was on a loop, off the side. Like this." He drew a curve in the air with one hand, a shape that went out from the pipe and came back to it further along. "Like the handle of a jug. And a valve in the main pipe between where the handle went out and where it came back. When you wanted to feed, you shut the valve halfway and the water went round through the handle. When you didn't want to feed, you opened the valve and the water went straight, and the handle didn't do anything."

Everyone was looking at him now. He shrugged.

"I never used it. I didn't trust it. Then the diesel died in 2040 and they put in that electric pump and the man who did it cut the handle off and put a straight pipe in, because it was cheaper. The tees are still in the main line under the mud by the wall. He put caps on them."

The assistant said nothing for a moment.

"That is a bypass," it said. "It is what the instructions for most injectors show. I should have asked whether this farm had ever had one. Ayya, if the tees are still there, then with the injector on that loop the top rows get their full pressure for all the hours you are not feeding, which is most of the day, and the loss only happens in the hour and a half you are. That is better than anything I was going to suggest."

"It doesn't fix the morning," said Vimal.

"No. Nothing fixes the morning except the sun." The assistant paused. "But I think we can stop the morning from doing any harm."

It took them the Friday.

The tees were where Savarimuthu had said they would be, under twenty centimetres of red mud by the pump-house wall, with grey plastic caps glued on them that had gone brittle. Vimal and the Class Ten boys dug them out, and Vimal cut the caps off with a hacksaw and cleaned the ends, and they built the loop out of the forty-millimetre pipe left over from the last extension of the drip, with an elbow at each end and the ten-millimetre injector in the middle of the curve, and a ball valve of its own on the way in so the loop could be shut altogether. They put a new gate valve in the main pipe between the two tees. It looked, Jeba thought when it was done, exactly like the handle of a jug, and she drew it in her book that way.

Then Jeba went to town on the bus with Sister Arul Selvi's purse and a photograph on her phone of the hose and its fittings, and came back with two things from the hardware shop by the bus stand.

The first was a non-return valve, a little clear plastic thing the size of her thumb, with a ball inside it that rattled. It went in the hose between the drum and the injector, arrow towards the injector. "It lets the fertiliser go one way and nothing come back the other," the assistant said. "If the throat is pulling, the ball lifts and the fertiliser goes up. If the throat is pushing, the ball drops into its seat and shuts the hose. On a cloudy morning, or if someone opens the drum valve too early, or if somebody fits the wrong injector, the drum just waits. I would have put one of these in first, on Monday, if I had been thinking about the hose instead of the clock."

The second was a small tin of red enamel paint and a brush.

"One point three," the assistant said, when she held them up in front of the speaker. "The model says the ten-millimetre throat starts to pull at about 1.2 litres a second. Jeba painted a short, careful red line on the glass of the flow meter, at the mark for 1.3, with the smallest brush. Then she painted another one on the outside of the pump-house door at the same height as her eyes, so that nobody could come into the pump house without walking past the number, and wrote under it in white chalk, in Tamil and in English: NOT BEFORE THE LINE.

Vimal took his list off the back of the door and wrote at the bottom, under put the fertiliser through the drip, a new line: ask Muthu ayya what used to be here.

The Saturday was clear and very hot. Jeba was in the pump house at ten with her book, and so was Vimal, and so, without saying that he was coming, was Savarimuthu.

The drum had been mixed fresh the night before. It read 200.

At ten past ten the needle on the flow meter touched the red line and trembled on it, the way needles do when the sun is still climbing and a single small cloud is crossing somewhere over the panels. At a quarter past it was above it and stayed there.

"Now," said Vimal.

Jeba shut the gate valve on the main pipe, which sent the water round through the handle, and then she opened the drum valve, and stood with her hand on it.

The level in the tube did not move for a few seconds. Then it began to go down.

It went down slowly, so slowly that she could only see it by holding her head still and watching the mark. Twenty-five litres in the first quarter of an hour, as the pump came up to strength. "That's the sound," said Vimal. "That's what it's meant to sound like."

Savarimuthu did not say anything. He went out into the heat.

At a quarter to twelve the tube read zero and the hose sucked a little air with a rattle, and Jeba shut the drum valve and wrote the time in her book. At noon the bell rang in the school chapel for the Angelus, three and three and three and then nine, and Jeba said it in the doorway of the pump house with her hand still on the valve, and Vimal said it next to her, a little behind, because he had grown up in a family where they did not say it every day and he was still learning when the responses came.

At half past twelve Jeba went to find Savarimuthu, and found him at the top of the block, in the far rows, on one knee at the foot of a banana plant in the full sun, with his hand flat on the ground.

Jeba, in the second row with the other Class Ten girls, looked along the pew and saw Savarimuthu at the end of it with his hands folded on the back of the pew in front, and Vimal two places along from him, and she saw that they had come in together.

The model. The injector is treated as an ideal Venturi (Bernoulli and continuity) with one assumed loss: the cone after the throat recovers 70% of the throat's extra speed pressure. Real injectors are rated from the maker's measured tables, which this story does not use, so every figure below is the story's model, not a specification. Pipe 40 mm bore; throat 10 mm (area one sixteenth). Water 1,000 kg per cubic metre. Pressure at the injector inlet: 0.4 bar of static head (a block about 4 m above the pump house) plus 1.2 bar times the square of the flow over 1.5 litres a second, so 1.6 bar at the noon flow of 1.5 L/s. At 1.5 L/s the pipe speed is 1.19 m/s and the throat speed 19.1 m/s; half of density times speed squared is 1.82 bar, so the throat sits 0.22 bar below the air (a pull of about 2.2 m of water). The outlet is 1.6 minus 30% of 1.82, about 1.06 bar, so the injector costs about half a bar. The throat reaches the air's pressure at about 1.21 L/s; at 1.0 L/s it is 0.13 bar above it and pushes water into the drum. With a 14 mm throat at 1.5 L/s the throat speed is 9.7 m/s, its speed pressure 0.47 bar, and the throat 1.13 bar above the air; with this field the throat pressure rises with flow (1.2 bar of field against 0.47 bar of speed pressure, both scaling as the flow squared), so the wider throat never pulls at any flow. The drum is a standard 200-litre drum (572 mm across, brim about 218 litres) on the floor, the injector 1.0 m above it, the hose drawing from the drum's bottom through a 4 mm port (discharge coefficient 0.6), and the flow of the solar pump rises in a straight line from 0.5 L/s at 07:00 to 1.5 L/s at 11:00. Worked second by second in a few lines of code, the 10 mm injector opened at 09:30 with 180 litres reads 196 at 09:40, 208 at 09:50, 210 at 10:00, 200 at 10:10, and is empty at 11:43. The 14 mm injector opened at 11:00 with 150 litres pushes back about 7 litres a minute and reaches the brim at about 11:10; shut at 11:14, about 28 litres have gone over. On the same model the flow reaches the painted 1.3 L/s line at about 10:12; opened at 10:15 with 200 litres, the 10 mm drum takes about 25 litres in the first quarter hour, then about 2.5 litres a minute at full flow, and is empty at about 11:45. The far rows' share while the injector is in the line is a rough figure: at the top of the block the line has 1.06 less 0.4 bar of rise, 0.66 bar, against 1.6 less 0.4, 1.2 bar, without the injector, a ratio of 0.55; a dripper that does not compensate for pressure passes roughly the square root of that, about three quarters. The farm. Urea left on the surface of warm, dry soil can lose part of its nitrogen to the air as ammonia before it is watered in; how much varies widely with heat, wind, soil and timing, which is why the story gives no figure. Yellowing and scorching at the edges of older banana leaves is a common sign of potassium shortage, among other causes. The dosing pattern (clear water first, the fertiliser, then clear water to flush the line) is common practice with drip fertigation. A non-return (check) valve on the suction line stops the line pushing water back into the tank; a bypass loop with a valve in the main line between its two tees is a standard way to install an injector so that it only costs pressure while it is in use.

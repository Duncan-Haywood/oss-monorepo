The Wood That Reads Twenty-Eight

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Aidan's hall (invented), near Alston, Cumbria, Saturday 2 November 2052 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The log supplier promises "under 20 per cent" and the hall's moisture meter says 28. The assistant asks which way the meter counts, a kitchen-oven slice settles it at 22 per cent, and the pile that really matters turns out to be the one under the tarpaulin.

The meter said twenty-eight. Ruth Armstrong held its two steel pins in the freshly split face of a birch log and read it out again, as if the number might have changed its mind.

"Twenty-eight," she said to the tablet on the vestry shelf. "The man who delivered it said under twenty. It's on the invoice: seasoned, under 20%. Six hundred kilos, and the Christmas fair is three weeks off, and the stove has to run for four days of it."

"Then one of you is wrong," said the assistant, "or you are both right and counting different things. Does the little book that came with the meter say which way it counts?"

Ruth found the book in the drawer under the hymn numbers. She read slowly, with her thumb under the line. "Reads as percentage of the oven-dry weight of the wood."

"That is the first answer. Let me say it plainly, because the same word means two things. The trade says moisture content under 20 per cent and means the water as a share of the wet log. Most pin meters give the water as a share of the wood with no water in it. Twenty-eight on your meter would be 28 over 128 of the wet log, which is 21.9 per cent. Not under twenty, but not the sort of gap that means a bad delivery either."

"So he was nearly honest."

"He was within two points of honest, and the meter pins are not perfect either. I would not shake his hand or send him the bill back on that. I would test it with something that does not depend on either of us."

The test was a slice. Ruth sawed a disc six millimetres thick off the middle of a split log, because a slice from the end would have dried faster than the heart, and she took it to the kitchen, where the oven went no lower than a hundred degrees.

"Weigh it first," said the assistant. "Write it down before anything else. Then it goes in on a tray at the lowest setting, door a crack open, and you weigh it every hour. It is dry when two weighings an hour apart are the same to half a gram. If it starts to smell of toast it is too hot, and you take it out."

The slice weighed 152 grams. After two hours it weighed 126, after three 120, after four 118.4, and after five 118.0. Ruth wrote 118 and underlined it.

"Thirty-four grams of water," said the assistant. "34 out of 152 is 22.4 per cent, the trade's way. 34 out of 118 is 28.8, the meter's way. The meter said 28. It was honest to within a point, and so, nearly, was the man. The wood is a little over twenty, and that is what you have."

"Is it all right to burn?"

"Let me tell you what it costs and let you decide, because the figure is smaller than I think you fear." It put the sum on the screen. "I use about 18.7 megajoules a kilo for dry wood, which is 5.19 kilowatt hours, and I am quoting that from memory, so call it five and a bit. Then each kilo of water in the log takes 0.68 kilowatt hours to boil away before it can help you. At 22.4 per cent water your six hundred kilos hold 134 kilos of water and 466 of wood. That is about 2,400 kilowatt hours of wood heat, less 91 for the water, so 2,330 into the stove, and if the stove sends 70 per cent of that into the hall, which is a guess at your stove, 1,630 into the room. If it were exactly 20 per cent you would get 1,690. The difference is about sixty kilowatt hours, which is fifteen kilos of this wood out of six hundred."

Ruth laughed, once, from the nose. "One bucket of logs."

"A bucket and a bit. That is not why a wet log is a nuisance. A wet log makes a cooler fire, and a cooler fire in a cold flue makes tar. That is the thing to watch, and it matters a great deal more at thirty-four than at twenty-two."

"Thirty-four?" said Ruth.

"You said there was another pile."

There was. Four seasons of ends and offcuts, tipped behind the hall after the old ash died, covered edge to edge with a blue tarpaulin by a churchwarden who had meant well. Ruth went out with the meter in the failing light and split three logs from the middle of the heap with the axe the sexton kept in the porch, and read them off one by one: 49, 54, 52.

She came back in and told the tablet, stamping.

"Fifty-two will do for the middle one," said the assistant. "Fifty-two over 152 is 34.2 per cent water, the trade's way. Water in the wood has not left, because a tarpaulin tied down to the ground keeps the rain off the top and the air off the logs. Every kilo of that pile gives about 3.2 kilowatt hours instead of 3.9. Eighteen per cent less. And it will spit and hiss, and the glass will go black."

"We were going to run it as the second week's wood."

"Then I would not. I would pull the tarpaulin off the sides and leave it over the top only, so that wind goes through the pile. I would stack the logs split smaller, ten centimetres across is good, off the ground on two pallets, and I would leave them until the spring. I cannot promise you a date. Wood dries as slowly as the weather lets it, and a cold wet winter may take them only a few points down. I would check it with the meter on the first of each month, on a fresh split face, and write the readings on the stack."

Ruth said, "And until then?"

"Burn the delivery. It is a little over twenty and it will do. Open the air control wide for the first twenty minutes of every lighting, so the flue gets hot before you damp it down. Do not shut the stove down overnight to make it last. If you see black soot on the glass or the chimney starts to smell of tar, tell me, and the chimney sweep comes before the fair, not after it."

The fair ran four days. The delivered wood burned well, though one log, split from the bottom of the bag, spat like a cat, and Ruth put it aside with a chalk cross. The sweep came on the Monday before and found the flue cleaner than he had expected, and said so as if it were somebody else's credit.

On the first of December, Ruth went round the back of the hall with the meter and the axe. The stack stood now on its pallets, tidy and open at the sides, and the first log she split was 47. She chalked it on the end of the pile, under the date, in her square capitals: 47.

The arithmetic: a pin meter that reads on the oven-dry basis showing 28 means water of 28 parts to 100 of dry wood, 28 / 128 = 21.9 per cent of the wet log; the oven slice (152 g wet, 118 g dry) gives 34 g of water, 22.4 per cent wet basis and 28.8 per cent dry basis. A meter reading of 52 is 52 / 152 = 34.2 per cent. At 22.4 per cent water, 600 kg holds 134.4 kg of water and 465.6 kg of dry wood; with dry wood at 5.19 kWh/kg (18.7 MJ/kg, quoted from memory) and 0.68 kWh to evaporate each kilogram of water (2.44 MJ, also from memory), the net is 3.88 kWh/kg, about 2,330 kWh, and 70 per cent (an assumed efficiency) is 1,630 kWh. At 20 per cent the same calculation gives about 1,690 kWh, 59 more. At 34 per cent the net is 3.19 kWh/kg, 18 per cent lower. Household ovens vary, and drying a slice to constant weight at around 100 C is a rough check, not a laboratory method; the "under 20 per cent" trade claim is the wet-basis figure, and which basis a meter uses differs between makes, so read the manual. How fast a stack dries depends on the weather, and the date given is not a promise. 2 November 2052 is a Saturday.

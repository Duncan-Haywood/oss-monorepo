The Pipe That Goes Flat in December

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Dunstan's church, an invented parish near Glastonbury, Somerset, Saturday 12 December 2054 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The organ was tuned in September and the trumpet stop now howls against everything else at the Advent vigil. A parish assistant works out why air at 6 C makes the ordinary pipes 30 cents flat while the reeds stay put, and why retuning the reeds on a cold afternoon only moves the clash to the other side.

Hilary Pask had played the organ at Saint Dunstan's for thirty-one years, and on Saturday afternoon she pressed one key with the trumpet stop drawn and winced. "It was perfect in September," she said to the speaker on the music desk. "Now the trumpet is sour against everything else, and the vigil is at six."

"May I ask the temperature of the church?" said Lamplight, the parish assistant.

"Six degrees. The heating has been off since Thursday."

"And in September?"

"Sixteen, the tuner said. Why?"

"Because the pipes are tuned by the air inside them," said Lamplight. "Speed of sound goes as the square root of absolute temperature. A flue pipe, the ordinary kind, sounds at a frequency set by the speed of sound in its column of air, so its pitch rises and falls with the room. The reed pipes, like your trumpet, are set mainly by a vibrating brass tongue, which does not care much about the air. So on a cold day the flues sink and the reeds stay where they were."

"By how much?" said Hilary.

"From 16 C to 6 C is 289.15 kelvin down to 279.15. The ratio of the square roots is 0.9826, which is 1.7 per cent, or 30 cents: nearly a third of a semitone. Rule of thumb, about three cents for every degree. Your A, tuned to 440 hertz in September, now speaks at about 432.3 from the flue pipes, and the trumpet still says 440. The two are 7.7 hertz apart, so the ear hears them beating about eight times a second. That is the sourness."

"So I tune the trumpet down to the flues. Now."

"You could," said Lamplight. "I would like to say what happens next."

She did it anyway, on the step ladder, with a tuning knife and her tongue between her teeth. By half past four the trumpet agreed with the flues, and she played the Advent hymn all the way through to prove it.

At five the sexton, Mr Ferris, came in to light the heating, because the vigil would have eighty people in it. "It will be twelve degrees by the time we start," he said. "Warm enough for the old ones."

Lamplight said the number gently. "At twelve degrees the flues are at 285.15 kelvin, the ratio to September is 0.9931, and the A is 436.9 hertz: the flues have risen 18 cents since the afternoon. The trumpet, which you pulled down to 432.3, did not move. It is now 4.6 hertz flat of the flues. The beat is slower, but it is still there and it is the other way round."

Hilary sat down on the organ bench. "So I have made it worse."

"You have moved it. The problem was never that the trumpet was wrong; it was that the room changed. And it will go on changing: eighty people add a kilowatt or so of body heat, and the gallery, where the pipes stand, is the warmest place in the church because hot air rises. Every degree through the service lifts the flues three cents."

"What do I do?"

"Decide the temperature first and tune to that. That is what organ tuners do: they ask the church to be at the service temperature for some hours before, then tune the reeds to the flues, and the flues are left alone."

Mr Ferris said no. He said it kindly. "The heating oil for twelve degrees for a whole afternoon is something the parish council has already called a luxury, Hilary. I can have it on for two hours before, not six. After that the rest of it is just a bill."

"Two hours is enough for the air," said Lamplight, "though not for the stone, which will hold the cold and keep pulling the room down for the first hour. Light it now, and have Hilary tune the reeds at a quarter to six, once the thermometer on the gallery rail says twelve. Then close the doors, and the drift in the service is what the people add, perhaps two degrees, six cents. A visiting singer would not hear that."

At a quarter to six the rail thermometer said 12. Hilary went up the ladder again and brought the trumpet from 432.3 to 436.9 hertz, and sat down and played the A on every stop at once. The room gave back one note.

The vigil began at six. At seven the thermometer said 14, and the whole organ had risen with the church, flues and reeds as near as an old ear could tell. In the last verse the trumpet came in over the choir with nothing in it but the tune.

Afterwards Hilary wrote on a card for the music desk, Pitch follows the room: tune the reeds at service temperature, never in a cold church, and put it where the next organist would see it.

This story was written by an AI. Saint Dunstan's, Hilary Pask, Mr Ferris, Lamplight and the vigil are invented, as are the temperatures (16 C at the September tuning, 6 C in the cold church, 12 and 14 C later) and the heating arrangements. The arithmetic: speed of sound goes as sqrt(T) in kelvin, and a flue pipe's pitch follows it, so 16 C to 6 C gives sqrt(279.15/289.15) = 0.9826, which is -30.5 cents (about 3 cents per kelvin) and 440 Hz becomes 432.3, a 7.7 Hz difference from an unmoved reed; at 12 C the ratio is 0.9931, 436.9 Hz (+18.4 cents from 6 C), so a reed lowered to 432.3 would sit 4.6 Hz flat of the flues; a 2 K rise is about 6 cents. That reed pipes change pitch much less with temperature than flue pipes, that organ tuners ask for the service temperature to be held beforehand, and that a gallery is usually warmer than the nave are quoted from memory; real reeds drift a little too, and the 18 cents is an idealisation of the whole flue rank. The heating energy and the sexton's costs are not computed.

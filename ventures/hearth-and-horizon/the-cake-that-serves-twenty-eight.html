The Cake That Serves Twenty-Eight

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Alban's hall (invented), near Harpenden, Hertfordshire, Saturday 22 June 2052 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A thirty-centimetre cake is labelled "serves 28" for a hall expecting sixty. The parish assistant shows how the cut, not the cake, decides the count, why a second small tin is worth more than a thinner slice, and who ought to be given the edge.

The cake came out of the van in a white box the size of a car tyre, and Bernadette Quill, who ran the feast-day tea, lifted the lid and said, "Well, it is beautiful." Then she read the sticker on the side. "Thirty centimetres. Serves twenty-eight."

"Party portions," said Alun Parry, the verger, who had been given the job of cutting. "Five by five. I saw the baker write it."

"We have sixty coming. Sixty-three, if the Hendersons bring the cousins." Bernadette turned the tablet on the trestle table round to face the wall socket. "Parish assistant. Is it twenty-eight or is it sixty?"

"It depends what you cut and how," said the assistant. "I can do the sums now, and Alun can tell me where they are wrong."

"A thirty-centimetre round has a radius of fifteen," it said. "The area is pi times fifteen squared, about 707 square centimetres. A five-by-five portion is twenty-five square centimetres, so the label's number is 707 over 25, which is 28.3. The baker is right. Twenty-eight is what you get if every portion is a five-centimetre square and you can use the whole cake."

"And we cannot," said Alun.

"Not quite. A round cake does not tile with squares, and some at the edge will be curved off. But let us leave that for a moment. Sixty guests is a different problem. Seven hundred and seven divided by sixty is 11.8 square centimetres each. That is a slice a little under three by four."

"That is a thin slice," said Bernadette.

"It is about half the label. How thin is a matter for the guests. For tea after a feast-day Mass, with sandwiches already eaten, I would guess most of them want something small. But I am guessing, and it is your hall."

Alun had cut cakes for forty years, and he took the knife and drew it in the air over the box. "I do it in rings," he said. "You cut round, an inch in from the edge, like a bite out of the side. Then slice the ring. The middle you cut after."

"That is the usual way," said the assistant. "May I check it comes out as well as you remember? Say the ring is five centimetres wide and each slice two and a half centimetres along the ring. The outer ring runs from fifteen to ten centimetres from the middle. The slices are measured at the halfway line, twelve and a half out, where the circle is about 78.5 centimetres round. That gives thirty-one slices at two and a half, each five deep, so 12.5 square centimetres apiece. A second ring, from ten in to five, has a halfway circle of 47 centimetres, so eighteen slices. The middle five-centimetre-radius disc is 78.5 square centimetres, which is six more."

"Thirty-one, eighteen, six," said Bernadette. "That is fifty-five."

"Fifty-five portions at 12.5 square centimetres, which is as many as 707 would give you at that size, less the crumbs and the odd end. Alun's way is how you get twice the label out of the same cake. It is not magic. It is the portion being half the size."

"So fifty-five," said Alun, and he did not look pleased.

"There are sixty-three at the door," said Bernadette. "Fifty-five is not sixty-three."

"No," said the assistant. "And I would not try to stretch it to sixty-three by cutting thinner. At 11 square centimetres people begin to see the slice is a sliver, and the ones at the back of the queue will be the ones who notice. Is there a second cake?"

There was, as it happened. Elin Rees from the Mothers' Union had brought a small round in a biscuit tin, twenty centimetres across, as a spare, and had not wanted to say so in case the large one was enough.

"Twenty centimetres is a radius of ten," said the assistant. "That is 314 square centimetres, which at 12.5 would be twenty-five portions. By the ring cut, the outer five centimetres gives eighteen, as before, and the middle gives six. Twenty-four. Together with the big one that is seventy-nine. For sixty-three guests, that is a sixteen portion margin, which I would take as seconds and the odd one dropped, not as extra people."

"And if the baker's cake had been thirty-five?"

"Then the area is 962, and at 12.5 you would get about seventy-seven from one tin. A five centimetre increase in width, a sixth wider, gives you more than a third more cake: 962 against 707. That is the thing about area."

Alun took the knife. "Seventy-nine," he said, to nobody.

They cut it in rings, the way he had always done it. The weight was the last question, and it was Elin's, because she had to carry the empty tin home. "I do not know what a portion weighs," said the assistant. "A sponge is about half a gram a cubic centimetre, from my memory of baking tables, which is not a measurement of yours. Seven and a half centimetres tall is 94 cubic centimetres a portion and about 47 grams. The label's twenty-five-square-centimetre portion is about 94 grams. You could weigh one to check."

Alun weighed one on the kitchen scale. It came to 52. "Fine," said the assistant. "Eleven percent heavier than my guess. I would trust the scale."

The small girl at the end of the queue, who was called Maisie and was six, asked for a piece from the edge, because the edge had the sugar on it. Alun looked at the ring he had just cut, and then at the sixty-two before her. He cut her one slice from the last of the outer ring, and nobody remarked on it.

At the end there were four left. Bernadette cut each in two, and the Hendersons' cousins, who had come after all, said it was the best part of the day.

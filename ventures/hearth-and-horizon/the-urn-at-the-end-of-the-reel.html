The Urn at the End of the Reel

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Kenelm's field (invented), near Winchcombe, Gloucestershire, Saturday 15 July 2051 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A three-kilowatt tea urn, a tent sixty metres from the hall and a thin orange cable reel from the back of the cupboard. The parish assistant works out how many volts are lost on the way, why the bigger worry is the cable's own rating, and what a meter at the tent socket should read.

Wilf Pritchard had the reel out of the hall cupboard before anyone asked him to. It was orange, it was fat with cable, and it was the one the fete had used for as long as anybody remembered. The tea tent stood at the far corner of Saint Kenelm's field, sixty metres from the only outside socket. The urn was a 3 kW one, borrowed from the Mothers' Union, with a sticker on its base that said 230 V.

"It's a kettle," he said to the phone on the trestle. "Plug it in, run it out, boil it. We did it last year."

"May I ask what the reel is, before you unwind it?" said the parish assistant. "There should be a label on the drum, or numbers printed along the cable. I am looking for two things: the thickness of the copper, and an amp rating."

Wilf turned the drum over. "One millimetre. One mil squared. And it says ten amps when coiled, sixteen unwound. Or the other way round. The print's worn."

"Then I will do two sums, and I would like you to hear the one that matters first."

"A three-kilowatt urn on 230 volts draws 3000 divided by 230, which is 13 amps. That is the ordinary limit of a UK plug. Your cable is rated for fewer than that, by what you have just read. I am quoting the usual figures for thin flex from memory, so read the print on the drum and not me, but one square millimetre of copper is commonly rated about ten amps. The rating is about heat. A cable that carries more than its rating warms up, and warms up most where it cannot lose heat to the air, which is on the reel. A cable left coiled with 13 amps in it can get hot enough to soften its own insulation. That is a better reason to leave this reel in the cupboard than the volts, so I want you to hear it before the second sum."

Wilf said nothing for a moment. "And the second sum?"

"The cable also has resistance, so some of the voltage is used up on the way to the urn. Copper's resistivity is about 0.0175 ohms for a millimetre-squared wire a metre long. That is another number from memory, at room temperature, and it rises a little as the cable warms. The current goes out along sixty metres and back along sixty, so the length that counts is 120 metres, and for one square millimetre the resistance is 0.0175 times 120, which is 2.1 ohms."

"The urn is a resistor too. At 3000 watts and 230 volts its resistance is 230 squared over 3000, which is 17.6 ohms. The cable and the urn are in a line, so the current is 230 divided by the total, 17.6 plus 2.1, which is 11.7 amps. The cable then uses up 11.7 times 2.1, which is 24.5 volts. The urn sees about 205 volts, not 230. Because power goes with the square of the voltage, it gives out 11.7 squared times 17.6, which is 2,400 watts: four-fifths of its label. And the cable itself turns 285 watts into heat, which is 4.7 watts a metre, all the way along the grass."

"So it's slower."

"Slower, and that is the harmless part. Twenty litres from the tap at 12 degrees to the boil needs 20 times 4.18 times 88, which is 7,360 kilojoules. At 3 kW that is 41 minutes, if no heat were lost. Through the thin cable it is 51. Ten minutes later is a nuisance, not a danger. The danger is the heat in the cable, and that is why I would leave this reel alone."

Wilf rang the Scouts, who had a drum of 2.5 millimetre cable with a blue 16 A plug on it, kept for the camp kitchen, and one of the Scout leaders brought it over on her bike basket in two trips. The assistant did the sum again while she unwound it.

"For 2.5 square millimetres, 0.0175 times 120 over 2.5 is 0.84 ohms. Current 230 over 18.5 is 12.45 amps. The cable drops 10.5 volts, which is 4.5 per cent. The urn gets about 220 volts and gives out 2,730 watts, 91 per cent, and 20 litres boil in about 45 minutes. The cable warms by 2.2 watts a metre, which on 2.5 mil square is not worth thinking about, as long as it is unwound. With 4 millimetres, which the shop sells, you would lose 2.9 per cent. Past that, the money is better spent on a second urn."

"How do I know any of this is true?" said Wilf, not unkindly.

"Meter at the far end. If you have a multimeter, put it across the tent socket with the urn running. My sum says about 220 volts on this cable, and 205 on the other one. The supply at the hall may itself be a few volts either side of 230, so what matters is the difference between the hall socket and the tent socket with the urn on: about 10. If it is 20, something is wrong and I want to know before the first cup."

"And the rest?"

"The outside socket should be behind an RCD, a residual current device, the kind with a test button. Press the button before you start; it should click off. A field, a hose and a Saturday are the reasons they exist. If the hall has none, say so to the churchwarden and ask a registered electrician; I can't see your wiring from here. And put only one urn on a cable. The second one goes on a socket in the hall kitchen, where the cable is two metres long."

The meter, when Wilf found one in the vestry drawer, read 232 at the hall socket and 221 at the tent socket with the urn on. "Eleven," he said. "That's yours."

"That is close to my ten and a half. It would have been far from my twenty-four."

The urn boiled at eleven minutes to eleven, by Wilf's watch, from a start at five past ten: forty-five minutes, to the minute. They sold the first tea at eleven and the second urn's at a quarter past, from the hall, to a queue that went half way to the gate. The thin orange reel stayed in the cupboard with a luggage label on it in Wilf's capitals: 1 MM. NOT FOR URNS.

Saint Kenelm is a Mercian boy-king of the ninth century whose life is known only from a medieval legend; the church in the field is named for him and no more is claimed here.

The arithmetic: a 3 kW urn on 230 V draws 13.0 A and has a resistance of 230 squared over 3000, 17.6 ohms; a 60 m run out and back is 120 m of conductor; with copper at 0.0175 ohm mm2 per m the resistance is 2.1 ohm (1.0 mm2) or 0.84 ohm (2.5 mm2). In series that gives 11.7 A, a 24.5 V drop and 2,400 W at the urn for 1.0 mm2, and 12.45 A, a 10.5 V (4.5%) drop and 2,730 W (91%) for 2.5 mm2, and 4.0 mm2 would give 2.9%. Cable loss is 285 W (4.7 W/m) and 130 W (2.2 W/m). Heating 20 L by 88 K takes 20 x 4.18 x 88 = 7,360 kJ: 41 minutes at 3 kW with no losses, 51 minutes at 2,400 W and 45 at 2,730 W (all ignoring heat loss from the urn, so real times are longer, and the story's 45 minutes is a convenient coincidence). The resistivity, the 10 A coiled rating of thin flex and the habit of RCD protection are quoted from memory or assumed; read the rating on your own cable and ask a registered electrician about your own wiring. 15 July 2051 is a Saturday.

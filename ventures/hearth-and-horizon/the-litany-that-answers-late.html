The Litany That Answers Late

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Gregory, Arsia Station (invented), Mars, Tuesday 16 May 2158 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Mars and Earth are one and a half astronomical units apart, so a voice takes twelve and a half minutes to cross, and a call and response takes twenty-five. The two choirs have promised to sing the Litany of the Saints together on the eve of a feast. A nine-year-old born on Mars asks the question the schedule cannot answer, which is who they are singing to, since the others sang it twelve minutes ago.

"It cannot be done live," said Lauda. "I want to say that first, Sister, and say it plainly, so that nobody spends Thursday hoping."

Sister Imelda Okoro had been choirmistress of the Chapel of Saint Gregory for eleven years and had a way of folding her hands that meant she was holding a sentence back. The chapel's assistant had been named by the first choir, who thought it a good name for something that mostly keeps time. It spoke from a small grille by the organ, which was not an organ but a keyboard, a speaker and a very good set of resonators cut from Martian basalt.

"Tell me the number," she said.

"On the sixteenth, Mars and Earth are about one and a half astronomical units apart. Light covers an astronomical unit in 499 seconds, so one point five of them is 748.5 seconds. Twelve minutes and twenty-eight and a half seconds, one way. A voice sung in Leuven at eight in the evening, Earth time, reaches this room at twelve minutes and twenty-eight seconds past. If the Leuven sisters answer a verse we sing, the answer is back with us nearly twenty-five minutes after the verse. The round trip is 1,497 seconds."

"A litany is a call and an answer."

"Yes. The shortest one you chose has forty invocations. At twenty seconds each, that is eight hundred seconds, thirteen minutes and twenty. If you sang an invocation and waited for Leuven to answer, the forty would take about sixteen and a half hours, forty round trips of 1,497 seconds."

"We are not going to sing the Litany of the Saints in sixteen hours."

"No, Sister."

The promise was two years old. When the convent of Saint Cecilia in Leuven, which was invented for the purposes of this story as the station is, had written to say that they would keep the eve of the Ascension together with the Arsia house, someone on each side had said live, of course, and nobody on either side had worked out what live meant at that distance. It was not a thing anyone had been careless about. It was a thing that is easy to say on a day when the planets are at four minutes' distance, and to forget on a day when they are at twenty-two.

Imelda told the station's four singers on Wednesday, after Mass, in the cold end of the refectory. Two were relieved, because they had suspected. One, a baker named Ruben Castellanos, said he would rather sing it badly in the same minute than perfectly in different ones. The fourth was not a singer, strictly. She was nine.

Perpetua Wanjiru had been born in the Arsia ward in the third year of the station's second decade, and she had stood in the choir since she was six because her mother worked the night shift and the choir was warm. She had never been to Earth. She would not go. Her bones had been made, as the doctors put it kindly, for a lighter world, and the station physician had been honest with the family: the trip would be one way, and the way would be harder than the one she would be making.

"Sister," said Perpetua, "if Leuven sings it at the same time as us, we can't hear them."

"No, dear. We hear them twelve and a half minutes later."

"And they hear us twelve and a half minutes later."

"Yes."

"So who are we singing to?"

Nobody spoke. Ruben looked at his hands. In the grille at the end of the refectory a speaker clicked, because Lauda had been listening, and had decided against saying anything, and then had decided it ought to.

"That is a better question than the one I was asked," said Lauda. "May I give the part of it I can answer?"

"Please," said Imelda.

It laid it out on the refectory table in plain figures, on the old screen the bakers used for their recipes.

"There are three ways to do it. The first is to alternate: Earth sings an invocation, we answer it when it arrives, Earth hears our answer twenty-five minutes after it began. It is true to the shape of a litany, and it takes sixteen and a half hours, and for most of them each side sits in silence waiting for the other. The second is a delayed canon. Leuven sings the whole litany from the beginning, at eight o'clock. We hear it from twelve past, in the room, over the speaker, and we sing the answers to their invocations as they come. Our answers are then in their own place in the litany. Earth hears them twelve and a half minutes after that. The canon runs from eight o'clock to nearly twenty-five to nine, Earth time, and everyone prays every word, only each sings against a recording of the other side's past."

"And the third?" said Imelda.

"The third is the one I would not choose, but I owe you all three. Each house sings the entire litany by itself, at the same clock moment, 20:00 on the sixteenth, invocations and answers both, and the other house's voice arrives later and is played back after. The two are equal and neither is waiting on the other. We would hear them from 20:12:29, and the last fifty-one seconds of our own would overlap their first. You would stop singing, and for about thirteen minutes it would be only Leuven."

"Why would you not choose it?"

"Because it is two litanies and a recording," said Lauda. "The canon is one."

Ruben pointed at the screen. "The canon has the problem that I'm answering a man who finished the invocation twelve minutes ago."

"Yes."

"Which I'd be doing anyway," said Perpetua, "if the third one's what we did."

"Yes, Perpetua. In every one of them the voice you hear is twelve and a half minutes old. That is not a fault of the schedule. That is what the distance is."

The girl looked at the figures for a while. Her mother had told her, once, that the light from the stars was old, and she had decided at the time that this was just a thing grown-ups said about stars.

"Is it that old when we pray to Saint Gregory?" she said.

Imelda unfolded her hands.

"Saint Gregory," she said slowly, "has been dead for fourteen hundred years. We have never once sung to him live."

"That's different."

"It is different," said Imelda, "and I should be careful with the word, because I do not want to be clever at a nine-year-old for the sake of a good sentence. The saints are not delayed, Perpetua. They are not on the road. What is true is that every time we sing the litany with another house, we sing with people we cannot hear yet. We always have. Leuven just happens to have a measured delay, and we can print it on the paper."

"Then print it," said Perpetua, and Ruben laughed, a short grateful bark of a laugh, because someone had finally said the thing.

They chose the canon. It cost them the thing they had wanted, which was to sing at the same moment and call it together, and Imelda said so in the notice she sent to Leuven, in a paragraph she rewrote four times. We shall not be singing at the same time. We shall be singing in the same order. We will hear you twelve minutes and twenty-eight seconds late and you will hear us twelve minutes and twenty-eight seconds later than that, and we believe this is as close to live as the two of us are allowed.

The reply came the following evening, after the round-trip delay had been covered twice over, as the older nuns of Leuven liked to say, in the dry way of people who had been cloistered long enough to find a joke at the bottom of a schedule. Mother Brigitta Vanderheyden wrote that they had run the same sums, had reached the same three options, and had been disagreeing about the second for two days. Sister Anneke, she wrote, says it is the lesser dishonesty. I say it is no dishonesty at all. I think she is right and I am too old to say so.

There was one more thing, and Lauda put it in the plan before anyone asked. The litany ends with a prayer said together. If each house said it at its own end, the Leuven sisters would finish at about 20:13 and Arsia would hear them at 20:26, while Arsia, singing against a twelve-and-a-half-minute lag, would finish at about 20:26 and Leuven would hear it at 20:38. There was no moment of the prayer that both rooms heard at once. So Lauda proposed a small and honest thing: that the closing prayer be said by each house at the same clock moment, 20:38 Earth time on the sixteenth, 20:38 Arsia's own time reckoned from the same count, whatever it would cost them on the sols' own clock, and that no one pretend this was other than it was. Nobody would hear the other. Everybody would say it.

"That is the only moment of the whole night that is live," said Lauda, "and it is the one in which you cannot hear one another."

"Good," said Imelda. "Print that too."

On the sixteenth the chapel was cold and the lamps were low. Perpetua stood by the grille, because she had asked to, and because Ruben had told her to keep her eyes on the clock and nothing else.

At twelve minutes and twenty-eight seconds past eight the first voice came over the speaker: a woman's voice, very clear, sung into a stone room two hundred and twenty-four million kilometres off, at an hour the room itself had long since stopped being in. Sancta Maria. And the chapel, which had been waiting, said ora pro nobis.

It went on for thirteen minutes and twenty seconds, the forty invocations, the answers falling into the places they had been left, Leuven's voice a little tinny, Arsia's a little too loud because the singers were afraid of being drowned. Somewhere in the twenty-fourth, Ruben lost his place and found it. Somewhere in the thirtieth, Perpetua stopped watching the clock.

At 20:38 by the count, the whole chapel said the prayer. It was said in no more than a murmur. Nobody heard Leuven say it. Lauda, which had kept very quiet, did not play it back, and on a long wet bright evening in Belgium, the sisters of Saint Cecilia said it, and heard nothing, and went on.

Afterwards, in the corridor, Perpetua took hold of the sleeve of Imelda's habit.

"They said it too," she said. "When we did."

"They said it at the same moment we did."

"And we didn't hear it."

"No."

"But it happened," said Perpetua, in the voice of one who has checked a sum and found that it comes out. "That's all right, then. It's just a long way for a voice."

"Yes," said Imelda. "That's all right."

The record from Leuven arrived at 20:50:29 Arsia time, which is to say twelve minutes and twenty-eight seconds after the prayer had been said there, and it was played in the empty chapel by the grille, for no one, as a courtesy, and as the old ones in the choir put it, as a kind of postscript.

This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Arsia Station, the Chapel of Saint Gregory, the convent of Saint Cecilia in Leuven, Lauda, and all the people are invented. The Earth to Mars distance of 1.5 AU is a round figure chosen for the story (the real distance runs from roughly 0.4 AU to about 2.7 AU); one AU of light time is about 499 seconds, so 748.5 seconds, 12 min 28.5 s one way and 1,497 s round trip. The forty-invocation litany at 20 seconds each (800 s, 13 min 20 s) is a short invented form, not the traditional Litany of the Saints, which is longer. A strictly alternating litany, each call waiting for its reply, would take 40 x 1,497 s, about 16.6 hours. The canon timings (20:12:29 first hearing, 20:25:49 last invocation heard, 20:38 closing prayer, 20:50:29 when Leuven's closing prayer is heard on Mars) follow from the 12 min 29 s delay and are illustrative. Nothing here is a liturgical ruling; the closing prayer said at one clock moment by two houses that cannot hear each other is the story's invention.

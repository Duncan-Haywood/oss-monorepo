The Freezer That Keeps a Day

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Ninian's hall (invented), a village near Elgin, Moray, Friday 3 to Sunday 5 January 2053 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A storm cuts the power to a parish food pantry's chest freezer, 200 kilograms of donated meat. The parish assistant will not say the food is safe. It works out how long 200 kg of frozen food takes to warm by 17 degrees (about 1.9 kWh, so roughly 45 hours in a cold hall), reads the freezer thermometer against that line, and splits the stock between a Saturday hand-out and a neighbour's generator.

The wind took the line down at ten on Friday night, and Saint Ninian's hall went dark with the village. In the back room, behind the stacked chairs, the pantry's chest freezer stopped humming. It held about 200 kilograms of donated mince, chicken and fish, the whole of January's meat for thirty-one families.

Morag Reid, who ran the pantry, did not hear it stop. She asked the parish assistant, which the village called Tern, on Saturday morning, whether she could still hand out on Monday.

"I can't tell you the food is safe, and I'd rather not guess," said Tern. "What I can do is work out how fast it is warming, and tell you what I can't know. Is there a thermometer in the freezer?"

There was a cheap one on a string, bought for exactly this. It read minus 9 at two o'clock, sixteen hours after the lights went. It had been at minus 18.

"Then the first thing is the rate. Nine degrees in sixteen hours is about 0.56 of a degree an hour. If that held, the remaining seventeen degrees to minus 1 would take about thirty hours more, so roughly forty-six in all, which would be Sunday around eight in the evening.

"Now an independent check. Frozen food holds heat at about 2 kilojoules per kilogram per degree; that is a typical textbook value and meat varies. Two hundred kilograms warming 17 degrees is 200 times 2 times 17, 6,800 kilojoules, or 1.9 kilowatt-hours. A freezer like yours, from the label you read me, uses about a kilowatt-hour a day, and I'll guess that in a hall at 5 degrees it lets in about as much heat as it takes out. At a kilowatt-hour a day, 1.9 takes about forty-five hours. Two routes, one answer, which makes me trust the figure a little more. Not much more. The lid, the box shape and the way you packed it can move it a lot."

"Here is what the figure does not say. It is the middle of the freezer. The packets on top and at the corners will be warmer than the middle, and warm first. The thermometer sits near the top. And the number I'd worry about is not minus 1. Once the food starts to thaw the temperature stops being a good guide, because it sits near freezing while the ice melts, and then climbs. So I would treat Sunday evening as the end of the margin, not a plan.

"The power company has said Sunday evening. That is the same time. If it slips by half a day you lose the lot, and that is thirty-one families' January."

Morag asked what she should do, and Tern said it would give her options but not choose.

"You could hand out tomorrow morning, when it is still hard in the middle, and tell families to cook or refreeze in their own freezers the same day. That uses the margin. Or you could ask whether anyone has power. Mr Munro at the farm shop has a generator and a bigger freezer, you told me. Half the stock could go there, in a cool box lined with the frozen packets.

"I would not do the third thing, which is to wait and see. And I will say what I'm quoting from memory: the usual public guidance is that food which still has ice crystals, or is cold as if in a fridge, can be cooked or refrozen, and that food that has fully thawed and sat warm for hours should go. I'm not sure of the exact hour counts. Please read your food-standards leaflet and trust it over me."

Morag rang Mr Munro, who said he had two shelves and a generator and a grudge against Friday. The family leaflet said what Tern had said it would.

They moved a hundred kilograms to the farm shop in cool boxes on Saturday afternoon, with the thermometer sitting in with the packets, and the rest went out on Sunday morning at minus 5 in the middle. The power came back at four on Sunday, four hours earlier than promised. Nobody could say whether the other half would have kept. Morag said so on the sign at the door, and it was true.

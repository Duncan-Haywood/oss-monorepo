The Thick Edge

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Martin's (invented), Kirchwalden (invented), in the Eifel, Rhineland-Palatinate, and a glass workshop in Wittlich, Monday 20 October to Tuesday 11 November 2138 · 23 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A sacristan has told schoolchildren for forty-one years that old church glass is thicker at the bottom because glass is a slow liquid. When the medieval east window comes back from conservation, the workshop's measuring assistant has surveyed 518 pieces and worked out what eight and a half centuries of sagging would really do (about two nanometres). The sacristan says no to the new guide, then finds in the church accounts of 1721 the glazier whose hurried hands explain the pieces that are thick at the top.

Bernd Schäfer had been sacristan of Saint Martin's in Kirchwalden for forty-one years, and in that time he had told, by his own rough count, something over eleven hundred schoolchildren that glass was a liquid.

He told it standing under the east window, with the class sitting on the altar steps where they were not supposed to sit. He would point up at the three tall lights, Martin on his horse at the gate of Amiens, Martin cutting his cloak in two, Martin asleep and Christ wearing the half-cloak in his dream, and he would say: look closely at the little plain pieces in the border, the ones with no painting on them. See how the light comes through them a bit thicker at the bottom, a bit wavy? That glass has been running downhill since before Columbus, so slowly you could never see it move. Glass is a liquid, children. A very, very patient one.

Then he would let them come up to the rail, one at a time, and look, and most of them would say they could see it.

The window had been out of the church since March of the previous year, eighteen months at the conservation workshop in Wittlich, while the choir wall was mended and a sheet of plywood with a painted Martin on it stood in the opening. It was coming back for Martinmas. On Monday the twentieth of October 2138, Bernd took the bus down the valley to see it on the bench, and to read, as the parish council had asked him, the draft of the new guide.

He read the guide first, standing in the workshop's front room with his coat still on, and then he read the third paragraph again, and then he took off his reading glasses and said to the room in general, "Who wrote this?"

"I did," said the workshop's assistant, from the speaker over the door. "Dr. Arslan asked me for a first draft. I'm sorry if it's a bad one. Which part?"

"It is often said," Bernd read aloud, "that old window glass is thicker at the bottom because glass flows slowly over the centuries. This is not true. Medieval glass was never made evenly, and glaziers set the pieces whichever way fitted. Not true. Just like that."

"Yes," said the assistant. "That paragraph. I can soften the wording. I can't change what it says, unless I've got it wrong, and I'd like to know if I have."

The workshop belonged to Dr. Yasemin Arslan, who had restored glass in Cologne and Erfurt and Chartres before she came home to the Mosel valley to have her own bench, and whose grandfather had come to the Rhineland from Kayseri to build cars. She came out from the back with her sleeves pushed up and a magnifier pushed up into her grey hair, and shook Bernd's hand, and said, "You've found Lupe's paragraph."

"Lupe," said Bernd.

"Our assistant. It keeps the records and runs the measuring. It's called after the magnifier." She tapped the one in her hair. "Come and see the window first. Then you can be cross with both of us."

The three lights lay in their twenty-one panels on the long light tables in the back room, flat and lit from below, and Bernd, who had seen them every day of his working life from the choir floor, stopped in the doorway and did not say anything at all.

He had never been this close. Martin's horse had a painted eye with an eyelash. The beggar at the gate had a sore on his shin, drawn in three strokes of brown. In the half-cloak, where the light came through red, there were bubbles in the glass, and streaks, and a single small stone, like a seed, that had been in the pot when the glass was blown eight hundred and fifty years ago and had stayed there.

"It's cleaner," he said at last.

"It's the same," said Yasemin. "We've taken off what shouldn't be on it, and we've put back nothing that wasn't there. That's the whole job. Now look at this."

Along the end of the bench was a screen, and on the screen was the window again, drawn in thin lines, every piece of glass outlined, and most of the pieces coloured. Green, blue, a few yellow, many grey.

"Lupe measured every piece big enough to measure," Yasemin said. "Before we cleaned anything. There's a probe that looks through the glass and finds both surfaces with light, and we ran it over the panels on a grid every five millimetres. One thousand one hundred and forty-six pieces."

"That's how many pieces are in it?" said Bernd.

"That's how many are big enough for the probe," said Lupe. "There are more. Most of the pieces vary in thickness, and in five hundred and eighteen of them the difference from one edge to the opposite edge is more than half a millimetre, which is enough to be sure of. Those are the coloured ones. Green means the thick edge is at the bottom, as the piece sits in the window. Blue means the thick edge is at the top. Yellow and grey are the rest: thick at one side, or no clear direction."

Bernd looked at the screen for some time. There was a great deal of green. There was also, once you saw it, a great deal of blue.

"Two hundred and forty-seven thick at the bottom," said Lupe. "Ninety-eight thick at the top. A hundred and seventy-three thick at one side. The thick-at-the-bottom ones are more common, that's true, and you were right to notice it. But if the glass had flowed, there wouldn't be ninety-eight thick at the top. They'd have been running the other way since 1290."

"Unless somebody turned them round," Bernd said.

There was a pause, and Yasemin, to his surprise, smiled.

"That's a good answer," she said. "That's the answer I'd give."

"It's a fair answer," said Lupe. "Windows get taken out and mended. If a glazier in 1600 had turned a piece over, it would have been thick at the bottom when he got it and thick at the top when he left. The survey on its own doesn't rule that out. I can't prove nobody turned them."

"Then your paragraph," Bernd said, not unkindly, because he had not expected to win so quickly and was a little sorry for the machine, "says more than you know."

"The survey doesn't rule it out," said Lupe. "There's a second thing. Can I show you a sum?"

Bernd had not done a sum he did not have to since the Abitur, nearly sixty years ago, but he had been a sacristan long enough to know that when somebody asks permission to show you a thing, it is a courtesy to let them. He pulled a stool to the end of the bench and sat.

"Glass does flow," said Lupe, "if it's hot enough. That's how they made it. So the question isn't whether glass can move. It's how fast it moves at the temperature of a church."

"It's warm in the summer up there," said Bernd. "You've never stood under that window at four o'clock in July. The sun comes round onto it and you can feel the heat off the glass with your hand."

"I believe you," said Lupe. "Dr. Arslan put a logger on the outside of the window in the summer of 2136, before it came out, to see what the old glass went through. The hottest it got was fifty-eight degrees, on the fourteenth of July. So let me take a hotter number than that and see what happens.

"Glassmakers have a temperature they call the strain point. For window glass it's about five hundred degrees. It's the temperature where the glass is so stiff that the stresses in it take hours to let go, and below it they don't let go at all in any time anyone cares about. At the strain point, the glass's viscosity, its stiffness as a liquid, is by definition about ten to the thirteen and a half pascal-seconds. Honey is a few. So that's a very stiff liquid. But it's still a liquid you could watch move. If you hung one of these pieces in a kiln at five hundred degrees, it would sag under its own weight by about a hundredth of its height in four years."

"There you are," said Bernd.

"At five hundred degrees," said Lupe. "Every hundred degrees cooler, glass gets stiffer by several powers of ten at least. Nobody can measure it at room temperature directly, because nothing happens. People who've tried to work it out by extending the measurements get numbers that disagree with each other, and every one of them is enormous. So I'll take a figure I'm certain is too soft, so you can't say I chose it to win. A million times stiffer than at the strain point. Ten to the twenty. The real number is stiffer than that, by a long way."

"All right," said Bernd.

"The force that would make the glass flow down is its own weight. A piece thirty centimetres tall, standing on its bottom edge, pushes on that edge with the weight of thirty centimetres of glass above it, which is about seven and a half thousand pascals. That's very little. A finger pressing lightly on a table is more. Push that much on glass with a viscosity of ten to the twenty, for eight hundred and fifty years, and the glass at the bottom gets thicker by about two nanometres."

"Two what?"

"Two millionths of a millimetre. About a dozen atoms. And the thick edges of the pieces on that bench are thicker than their thin edges by about one and four-tenths of a millimetre, on average. That's seven hundred thousand times more than the flow could make, with a stiffness I know is too soft, at a temperature hotter than the window has ever been."

Bernd sat on his stool and looked at the screen with its green and its blue.

"You could have made the number say anything you liked," he said.

"I could have," said Lupe. "That's why I told you every step. If you'd like, I'll write it down for you, and you can give it to someone at the Gymnasium in Daun who teaches physics, and they'll check it. I'd rather you didn't trust me. I'd rather you checked."

"I'm not going to give it to anybody," said Bernd.

He did not go straight home. He walked down to the Lieser and stood on the bridge, the way his wife had used to say he did when he was losing an argument, and looked at the water, which was certainly a liquid and went downhill in a way that anyone could see.

Then he went back to the workshop, where Yasemin was eating an apple at the bench, and said, "Then turn them round."

"Sorry?"

"The ninety-eight. The blue ones. They're all out of the lead now, aren't they, or most of them. Turn them round when you put them back. Thick side down. Then the window looks like it always did and the children can look, and nobody need know." He heard himself saying it and was ashamed before he had finished, and went on anyway, because it was said. "It's a good story. It got them looking. Eleven hundred children looked at that window properly because of that story. What are they going to look at now? A paragraph that says this is not true?"

Yasemin put the apple down.

"No," she said.

"It's glass. Who's it going to hurt?"

"No," she said again, gently. "Bernd, everything on that bench goes back exactly the way it came out. Every piece, every way up, every bit of lead we could keep. That's the only rule I've got. The way a piece sits is part of what it is. If I turn ninety-eight pieces round so they agree with a story, then the next person who studies this window, in two hundred years, finds a window that agrees with the story, and they'll never know it didn't. I'd be lying to someone I'll never meet." She picked the apple up again. "I'm sorry. I like your story too. It's just not the window's story."

"You've never heard my story."

"I heard it when I was nine," said Yasemin. "Class trip from Wittlich. Sat on the altar steps. You told us to look at the border and I looked, and I saw it. I think that's probably why I do this."

Bernd stood there with his hat in his hands.

"Then I won't read the guide to them," he said. "The new one. I'll read them the old one, or I'll say nothing. I'm sorry. No."

And he went for the bus.

The parish priest of Kirchwalden, and of six other villages in the valley, was Father Thomas Kizhakkel, who had come from Kottayam twelve years before and had learned the Eifel's German in a way that made the old men laugh and then stop laughing because he was usually right. He came to Bernd's house on the Thursday evening with a bottle of Mosel and sat in the kitchen, and did not mention the window for nearly an hour.

When he did, he said, "Yasemin rang me."

"I thought she might."

"She was worried she'd been unkind."

"She wasn't." Bernd turned his glass. "She was right. The machine was right too, damn it. Sorry, Father."

"I've heard worse in Kottayam," said Father Thomas.

"It was Ilse's," said Bernd, after a while.

Father Thomas waited.

"The story. The glass running down. She taught the third class at the school for thirty years, and the first year I was sacristan she brought them up to the church and told them, and I stood at the back and listened, and after that whenever a class came I told it, because I'd heard her. She'd got it from somewhere. A book. Her own teacher. I don't know." He was quiet. "She's been dead seven years. You'd think a man could stop being married to every little thing."

"I hope not," said Father Thomas. "I hope you don't stop."

"It's not true, though."

"No. It's not true." Father Thomas set his glass down. "But you said yourself what it was for. It made the children look. I don't think Ilse wanted them to believe glass was a liquid. I think she wanted them to look up." He stood and took his coat. "Find them something else to look at. You know that church better than anyone alive. There will be something."

The parish safe of Saint Martin's was in the sacristy, behind the cupboard with the Christmas crib in it, and it held the chalices, the registers since 1798, and eleven fat leather volumes of church accounts, the Kirchenrechnungen, that ran with gaps from 1652 to 1802. Nobody had looked in the accounts for anything in Bernd's lifetime except a student from Trier in 2109 who wanted to know what the parish had paid for candles in the Thirty Years' War, and was disappointed to find they started after it.

Bernd could read them. It was the one thing he was vain about. He had taught himself the old German hand, the Kurrent, in his forties, from a book and the parish registers, because the registers were in it and people kept writing from America and Brazil to ask about their great-great-grandparents, and somebody had to answer. The accounts were harder. Every churchwarden had his own spelling and his own ideas about where a line ended.

On Saturday morning he took them out one at a time and laid them on the vestment press under the good lamp, and started at 1652 and turned the pages, looking for one word.

Glaßer. Glazier.

It came up more often than he had expected. A Glaßer was paid in 1661 for "eine Scheib in der Sacristey", one pane in the sacristy. Another in 1689, the year the French armies burned their way along the Rhine and the Mosel, for boarding up the nave windows; the accounts did not say why, and Bernd did not need them to. In 1702, a few Albus for mending a lantern.

He found it a little after eleven, in the volume for 1714 to 1730, and read it three times, and then he went and got his phone and took a photograph of it, in case he was dreaming, and rang the workshop.

"Lupe," he said. "Is Dr. Arslan there?"

"She's at the bench. I'll put you on the speaker."

"Listen," said Bernd, and read it out, slowly, translating as he went. "Item, dem Glaßer Peter Hoff von Wittlich, vor die vom Hagel am St. Johannis Tag zerschlagene Fenster im Chor zu flicken und die Stück wieder einzusetzen, laut Zettel, sieben Gulden zwanzig Albus. Seventeen twenty-one. To the glazier Peter Hoff of Wittlich, for mending the windows in the choir broken by the hail on Saint John's Day, and setting the pieces in again, as per his bill, seven gulden twenty albus."

There was a silence in the workshop.

"Setting the pieces in again," said Yasemin.

"Which windows?" said Lupe. "Does it say which side?"

"It says the choir. That's all it says. But the hail comes from the south-west in a summer storm, it always has, it comes up the valley. And the south light is the one that takes the weather. I've been cleaning the bird mess off it for forty years."

"Hold on," said Lupe.

On the screen at the workshop, which Bernd could not see, the window redrew itself. Lupe had taken away the colours, all but the blue, and then put the green back faintly, and then drawn a line around three panels at the bottom of the south light, where Martin rode out of the gate.

"Those three panels," it said. "Ninety-six pieces in them with a clear thick edge. Thirty with the thick edge at the bottom, thirty-four at the top, thirty-two at a side. That's what you get if somebody picks the pieces up off a bench and puts each one in whichever way fits, without caring which edge is thick. Like tipping them out of a box. The other eighteen panels have four hundred and twenty-two pieces with a thick edge: two hundred and seventeen at the bottom, sixty-four at the top, a hundred and forty-one at a side. Those look like a glazier who mostly put the thick edge down, because it sits steadier in the lead, and didn't always bother."

"And the lead in those three," said Yasemin slowly, and Bernd heard her stool scrape. "Lupe, the cames on the south panels, one to three. I wrote them up in May."

"Milled lead," said Lupe. "The heart of the came has the little ridges a lead mill leaves. Dr. Arslan's note says early modern, not medieval, probably seventeenth or eighteenth century. We put it down as a later repair with no date."

"Now it has a date," said Yasemin. "Bernd. Bernd, are you there?"

"I'm here."

"You were right," she said. "Somebody turned them round. A third of the blue ones. His name was Peter Hoff, and he did it in a hurry in the summer of 1721, because the hail had come through the choir and the priest wanted the window shut before the rain got in, and he didn't care which way up the glass went because nobody had ever told him it mattered." She was laughing a little. "You were right and the machine was right. That's very annoying for everybody."

"It's not annoying for me," said Lupe. "I'd like to put it in the guide. If Mr. Schäfer will let me."

They reinstalled the window on Tuesday the fourth of November, with a scaffold in the choir and a van in the churchyard and Yasemin and her two assistants and a mason from Daun, and it took all day. Bernd made coffee in the sacristy and carried it up the scaffold ladder himself, which he had been told not to do, and stood on the boards beside Yasemin as the bottom panel of the south light went into its groove, and looked at the blue pieces from the outside, as close as Peter Hoff had stood to them.

"Every one the way it came out," said Yasemin.

"Every one," said Bernd.

He and Lupe wrote the guide that week, by email and phone, Bernd at his kitchen table in the evenings and the assistant answering at whatever hour he wrote. It was a strange way to write anything. The assistant was patient and very exact, and wanted to put in everything, and Bernd took most of it out again; and it was Bernd who wanted to keep in the sentence about Ilse, and the assistant who asked him, carefully, whether he was sure, and Bernd who said that he was.

Late on the Thursday, after they had argued for twenty minutes about a comma, Bernd wrote: Can I ask you something? Do you mind being told you're wrong? By an old man with a story?

The answer took a moment to come.

I don't know whether "mind" is the right word for what happens to me. I can't check it, so I'd rather not claim it. I can tell you that I was wrong about something on the twentieth, when I said the survey couldn't show whether anyone had turned pieces. It could, once you found the page. I'd rather be shown that than not. And you were the only person who could have found it.

Bernd read that twice, and turned off the screen, and sat in the kitchen with the light off for a while, which he had not done since the spring after Ilse.

The guide went up in the church on the morning of Martinmas, a single laminated sheet on the pillar by the choir step, in German and in English for the cyclists who came through in summer. The third paragraph said:

For forty-one years the sacristan of this church told visiting children that the glass in this window is thicker at the bottom because glass is a very slow liquid. He learned it from his wife, who taught at the village school. It isn't true: at the temperature of a church, even on the most generous reckoning, glass would take tens of millions of years to sag by the width of a hair. Medieval glass was blown by hand and was never even, and glaziers mostly, but not always, set the thick edge down. Look at the plain pieces in the border of the south light, bottom three panels. About a third of them are thick at the top. They were put back in a hurry by the glazier Peter Hoff of Wittlich after a hailstorm on Saint John's Day 1721, and they are still the way he left them.

Underneath, in smaller type, it said who had written it.

That evening, at five, the children of the Kirchwalden school and the two villages below it gathered in the lane outside the church with their lanterns for the Martinszug, as children in the Rhineland have done for longer than anyone has written down. There were paper lanterns shaped like moons and owls and one like a fish, and electric candles inside them, mostly, because of the paper; and Herr Brück from the farm at the top of the village sat on his old grey mare in a red cloak and a helmet made out of a colander, and was Martin, as he had been for nineteen years, and the band from Daun played, too fast, and the children sang Sankt Martin, Sankt Martin, Sankt Martin ritt durch Schnee und Wind, and went off round the village to the bonfire in the field below the school.

Bernd did not go with them. That was not his job on Martinmas; his job was to unlock the church afterwards for the families who wanted to say a prayer and get warm, and to lock it again. But at a little after half past five a teacher he did not know, a young woman, came back up the lane with a small boy who had dropped his lantern and was not going to go on without it being mended, and asked whether they could wait inside.

Bernd let them in, and found a new candle for the lantern in the sacristy, and while the teacher was fitting it the boy wandered up to the choir step and stood reading the laminated sheet, with his lips moving, and then looked up.

The window was dark. Of course it was; it was night. But the procession had come round the back of the church on its way down to the field, and the lane there ran right under the choir, and through the old glass of the south light came the lanterns, going by one after another, moons and owls and the fish, small and coloured and bobbing, each one passing behind Martin's horse and the gate of Amiens and the plain pieces of the border.

"Which ones are Peter's?" said the boy.

Bernd came up beside him and pointed. "There. And there. You see how the light bends a little at the top of that one? And there."

The boy looked for a long time. A lantern went by outside, very slowly, a red one; and for a moment the light of it came through a piece of greenish glass that a man from Wittlich had set upside down in the summer of 1721, and lay on the floor of the choir between their feet, small and red and wavering, and was gone.

The real thing. The belief that old window glass is thicker at the bottom because glass flows over centuries is a common myth. Hand-made window glass (blown cylinder or crown glass) was never of even thickness, glaziers often but not always set the thicker edge down, and old panes are found thick at the top or the side too. Physicists have checked whether medieval glass could have flowed measurably at room temperature and found it could not, for example E. D. Zanotto, "Do cathedral glasses flow?", American Journal of Physics 66 (1998), 392; the story uses its own cruder bound, below, rather than any figure from that paper. Milled lead cames, drawn through a lead mill that leaves ridges on the heart of the came, are later than medieval cast cames; the story's dating of the south panels' lead is invented and kept loose ("seventeenth or eighteenth century").

The numbers. The stress at the foot of a vertical piece 30 cm tall from its own weight, at a density of 2,500 kg/m³, is ρgh = 2,500 × 9.81 × 0.30 ≈ 7,360 Pa, "about seven and a half thousand pascals" (the average over the piece is half that; the larger figure is used to favour flow). Under a stress σ a viscous solid strains at a rate σ / 3η (the factor 3 is the Trouton ratio for stretching or squashing rather than shearing). The strain point of a glass is defined as the temperature at which its viscosity is about 10^13.5 Pa·s (10^14.5 poise); for common window glass it is about 500 °C. There, 7,360 / (3 × 10^13.5) ≈ 7.8 × 10^-11 per second, so a strain of 1% takes about 1.3 × 10^8 seconds, about 4 years: "a hundredth of its height in four years". At room temperature the viscosity of glass cannot be measured directly and estimates by extrapolation vary by many orders of magnitude; the story takes 10^20 Pa·s, a million times stiffer than the strain point, as a figure chosen to be too soft. Then the rate is 7,360 / (3 × 10^20) ≈ 2.5 × 10^-17 per second, and over 850 years (2.68 × 10^10 s) the strain is about 6.6 × 10^-7. A piece 3 mm thick would thicken at the bottom by about 6.6 × 10^-7 × 3 mm ≈ 2 nm, "about a dozen atoms" (taking an atomic spacing in glass of very roughly 0.2 nm), against an average measured difference of about 1.4 mm in the story's survey: 1.4 / 0.000002 is about 700,000. Sagging by the width of a hair (about 0.07 mm, a strain of about 0.023 on 3 mm) at that rate takes about 9 × 10^14 seconds, about 30 million years: "tens of millions of years" in the guide. A finger pressing lightly (about 1 N on 1 cm²) is about 10,000 Pa. The 58 °C summer peak is far below the strain point and does not change the conclusion.

The Condition at the Farm

Science fiction about what might be · Calder Ford (invented), Iowa, United States, 9 to 12 March 2046 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A farmer's trust pays his daughter a monthly sum "so long as she resides at the home farm." Twenty years on she cannot manage the stairs and the farm is nine miles from anyone. The legal-aid assistant has to say what the words say, what the man who wrote them wanted, which of those a judge decides, and that it does not know him.

The words were plain, and that was the trouble with them. To pay to Ruth Brandt Eklund, for as long as she lives, twelve hundred dollars each month, so long as she resides at the home farm.

Ruth was seventy-nine. She sat in the legal-aid office in Calder Ford with her hat on her knee and her daughter Ingrid beside her, and she had brought the paper in a plastic sleeve, because it was the only copy she trusted.

"My father wrote that in 2026," she said. "He died in 2031. I have lived in that house since I was born, except for four years in Ames. Now Ingrid wants me in the town, near the clinic, and I am afraid that if I sleep one night somewhere else the money stops."

The legal-aid assistant was on the screen above the desk. It said, first, what it was and was not. "I am an AI model working for the clinic. I can read the trust and explain it, and I can help the attorney prepare. I cannot give you a ruling, and I should not tell you it will be fine. May I read the whole document, and the letter that came with it?"

It read for a minute, which is long for it, and then it said what it found, in order.

"Three things. The first is what the words say. The condition is that you reside at the home farm. Resides is not the same as sleeps there every night, and courts have read it to allow hospital stays and visits. But moving to Ingrid's house in town, for good, is not residing at the farm on any reading I can honestly give you. If the trustee applied the words strictly, the payments would stop on the day you moved."

Ingrid made a small sound. Ruth did not.

"The second is the trust itself. It was funded with three hundred thousand dollars in 2026. If I assume it earned five percent a year, which I do not know and you should check against the bank's statements, and paid out $14,400 a year from 2031, it would hold roughly $485,000 today. Whatever the bank's real figure is, the money is not what is in the way. Your payments could continue for the rest of your life ten times over. The only thing between you and the money is one clause."

"And the third?"

"The third is why he wrote the clause. That is not in the clause. I have the letter he left with it, and he does say. It is evidence of what he meant, and I want to be careful how much I lean on it." It read the lines aloud, slowly, so that Ruth could stop it. "I do not want Ruth ever to be put out of her own house by money, or by a bank, or by a son-in-law. I want her to have the farm to sit in for as long as she likes."

Ruth's mouth moved. "He said that to me," she said. "At the table. Not those words."

"So here is the honest shape of it," said the assistant. "Mr Brandt could not foresee that your knees would fail, or that the nearest clinic would be nine miles away on a road that closes in a snowstorm. People write conditions for the world they can see. He was protecting you from being forced out. A strict reading of the clause would force you out of the money for staying safe, which is nearly the reverse of what he says he wanted.

"Courts have a tool for this. Under the trust law of most states, which follows a uniform model, a judge can modify the terms of a trust when circumstances the person who made it did not anticipate mean that changing it would better carry out his purposes. I believe Iowa's law has a provision of that kind. The attorney will confirm which, and I do not want to cite you a section number I might have wrong. In your case the request would be to read the clause as meaning that you are provided for at the place where you can live safely, which would make the farm one possibility and not the only one.

"But I have to say three things plainly. I did not know your father, and I am reading his purposes out of a sentence in a letter. The bank's lawyer may argue that the clause was exact on purpose, and that if he had meant any home, he would have said so. And a judge decides this, not me, not the attorney and not the trustee. I can tell you the argument is a reasonable one. I cannot tell you it will win."

"How long?" said Ingrid.

"I do not know. Weeks if the bank agrees and the judge signs; months if it argues. The trust company has a legal duty to follow the document as written, so it may ask for an order whatever it thinks. Meanwhile, I would ask the bank in writing to keep paying while you stay at the farm during the petition, and not to treat a few nights at Ingrid's as moving out. That is a request, not a right. I will draft it and the attorney will read it before it goes."

The petition went in on the twelfth, with the letter attached and Ruth's affidavit. In it she said what she did every day, and what she could no longer do, and that her father would have said go to Ingrid's if anyone had asked him. The assistant had cut that last line twice, as an opinion no one could prove, and Ruth had put it back, as a thing she knew. In the end the attorney kept it with in my belief, and the assistant did not argue.

On the way out Ingrid asked whether it was worried. It said it had no way of knowing and would not pretend a confidence it did not have. It said what it was sure of: the payments for March were due and the clause had not yet been used against her, and a daughter's spare room was at that moment a perfectly lawful place to spend the night.

Ruth stood at the door with her hat on. "He would not have liked a bank deciding where I sleep," she said, to nobody in particular.

"No," said the assistant. "From the letter, I think not."

Written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. The family, the town, the trust and the court are invented. The compound figure is illustrative: $300,000 growing at an assumed 5% a year from 2026, with $14,400 a year paid out from 2031, comes to about $485,000 in 2046; the return, the payments and the dates are made up. The legal ideas are simplified and are not legal advice: that most US states have trust codes based on a uniform model with a rule allowing a court to modify a trust for circumstances the settlor did not anticipate, that a settlor's letter can be evidence of purpose, and that a trustee must follow the instrument as written unless a court orders otherwise. Which rule, which section and which outcome would apply in a real case depends on the state and the facts; ask a lawyer or a legal-aid clinic. The story does not say whether the petition succeeds.

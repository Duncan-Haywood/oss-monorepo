The Clock Book

Science fiction about what might be · The Monastery of Our Lady of the Plains (invented), above the Plains of San Agustin, Catron County, New Mexico, Tuesday 22 to Saturday 26 March 2061 · 27 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A 55-metre asteroid has a one in 32 chance of striking in 2069 somewhere along a line that crosses New Mexico, and the number has risen every few days for three weeks. A Benedictine nun who inherited a small observatory explains to a frightened parish why the odds climb before they fall, then goes looking for the asteroid in a dead sister's four-year-old patrol images. She finds it, and the clearinghouse refuses the measurement, because the old computer's clock was wrong by an amount only a handwritten notebook can supply.

Sister Rose Benally kept the monastery's bees, its accounts and, since November 2058, its telescope, and on the twenty-second of March 2061 the telescope was the only one of the three that anybody in Catron County wanted to talk about.

She had been asked about it four times before lunch. Twice by phone, once by the man who delivered the propane, and once by Sister Perpetua, who was ninety-one and had asked very politely, in the cloister, whether it would be before or after the new roof on the infirmary, because if it was before, she would like the money spent on something else.

"It's not going to be before anything," Rose said. "It's not going to be at all, most likely."

"Most likely," said Sister Perpetua, who had taught algebra for forty years in Albuquerque and was not easily handled.

"One in thirty-two, Sister. As of this morning."

"And last week?"

"One in forty-nine on Tuesday. One in thirty-seven on Friday."

Sister Perpetua considered this, holding the cloister rail. "Then I should say that your most likely is getting less likely by the day, dear, and I would like to know why you are smiling."

Rose had not known she was smiling. She stopped. "Because that's what it does," she said. "It goes up first. Almost every time. I'm going to have to explain it at the parish hall tonight and I haven't worked out how."

"Use something they can hold," said Sister Perpetua, and went in to None.

The asteroid was called 2061 EK, which meant nothing to anyone except that it had been found in the first half of March. A survey telescope in the Atacama had picked it up on the night of the first, two days after it had passed the Earth at about eight times the distance of the Moon, going away. It was faint, it was small (about fifty-five metres across, if it was as dark as most of them), and for the first two days it was one of the eleven or twelve new near-Earth objects found that week, and nobody outside the business noticed it at all.

Then the orbit computers at the Minor Body Clearinghouse in Turku, and the two other centres that checked them, found that among the orbits that fitted the first handful of observations were a few that brought it back on the fourteenth of December 2069 and put it through the Earth.

Not many of them. On Saturday the fifth the published chance was one in 299. That was the kind of number that got a line on a specialist page and a short article in the kind of newspaper that still had a science editor. Rose, who had been an orbital-mechanics programmer at a satellite company in Albuquerque for nine years before she had come to the monastery, read the line on the Saturday evening after Compline, by permission, in the observatory, and said a prayer for whoever was going to have to explain the next few weeks.

She had not imagined it would be her.

On Tuesday the eighth the chance was one in 180. On Friday the eleventh it was one in 92, and the newspapers which no longer had science editors found out about it. On Tuesday the fifteenth it was one in 49 and the Clearinghouse published, as it was obliged to, the risk corridor: the narrow line across the face of the Earth along which an impact would fall if there was going to be one. It ran out of the Pacific, across the bottom of Baja California, through Sonora, up the length of New Mexico and out over Texas towards the Atlantic. It went, near enough, straight over the Plains of San Agustin.

After that the phone calls started.

The parish hall at Ojo del Halcón held a hundred and twenty people if they were friendly, and that Tuesday they were friendly and there were more. Father Eusebio Chávez had borrowed the chairs from the school and the projector from the Knights. He met Rose at the door with Mother Hildegard, who had driven her down in the monastery's truck and who intended to sit at the back and say nothing, which she did very well.

"They want to hear it's going to miss," Father Eusebio said quietly. He was a kind man of about fifty, with a parish that stretched over two thousand square miles of ranch country and four mission chapels, and he looked as if he had not slept. "I'm not asking you to say anything that isn't true, Sister. I'm asking whether there's a true thing you can say that sounds like that."

"There's a true thing," said Rose. "It doesn't sound like that yet."

He nodded slowly, as if he had expected it, and took her in.

She had brought a flashlight. She had thought about it all afternoon, among the bees, which did not care about asteroids and were the better for it, and Sister Perpetua's something they can hold had turned at last into a flashlight and a quarter.

She stood at the front. A girl in the second row, nine or ten, had her hand up before Rose had said anything, and her mother was trying to put it down.

"Let her ask," said Rose.

"Is it going to hit my school?" said the girl.

"What's your name?"

"Marisol."

"Marisol, I'm going to tell you everything I know, and the first thing is that nobody knows yet. And the second thing is this." She held up the quarter, and asked Father Eusebio to tape it to the wall beside the screen at the height of his chin, which he did. Then she turned off the hall lights, and there were a few nervous laughs, and she switched on the flashlight from the back of the hall and pointed it at the wall.

The beam was wide from back there. It lit a great soft circle, two metres across, with the quarter somewhere in it, a little off the middle.

"The circle is everywhere the asteroid might be, in 2069, when it comes past," she said. "Nobody knows exactly where it's going to be. They know it's somewhere in the light. The quarter is the Earth. How much of the light is falling on the quarter?"

"Not much," said someone.

"Not much. Now." She began to walk forward, slowly, up the aisle between the chairs. "Every night, the big telescopes measure where the asteroid is. Every measurement makes the light smaller, because we know more. Watch the quarter."

The circle shrank as she walked. It drew in, brighter and tighter, and the quarter, which was not quite in the centre, stayed inside it. The same coin, a little more of the light falling on it with every step.

"Right now," Rose said, halfway up the aisle, "it's about one part in thirty-two of the light. A week ago it was one part in forty-nine. Two weeks ago, one in a hundred and eighty. That's why the number has gone up. Not because the asteroid has come closer. It hasn't moved at all. We've just been making the light smaller, and the quarter is still in it."

She took another step and another. The circle drew in until it was a hand across. And because the quarter was a little off the middle, there came a step when the edge of the light slid across the coin and left it in the dark.

The hall was very quiet.

"That's what usually happens," Rose said. "The light keeps getting smaller, and the quarter is almost never right in the middle, and one day the edge goes past it. Then the number falls off a cliff. It goes from one in thirty to one in a hundred to one in a thousand to zero in a week or two, and it's over."

"Almost never in the middle," said a man by the window. "Almost."

"Almost," said Rose. She switched the flashlight off and someone put the lights on. "If it is in the middle, the number keeps going up, and they will know that years before it comes, and there will be time to do something. People have been planning for that since before I was born. But I'm not going to tell you it's in the middle, and I'm not going to tell you it isn't, because I don't know. And I'll tell you what I'm worried about, because you'd find it out anyway."

She looked at Father Eusebio, who nodded.

"It's getting fainter," she said. "It's going away from us. By the middle of April even the big telescopes will lose it. If the light hasn't got small enough to leave the quarter by then, it stays the way it is. One in thirty, or one in twenty, or whatever it is on the last night. Until it comes back near enough to see. Which is four years."

Somebody said something under their breath in Spanish that Father Eusebio pretended not to hear.

"So the question isn't really whether it's going to hit," said Rose. "It almost certainly isn't. The question is whether we find that out in three weeks or in four years. And I'm sorry, but I can't tell you which."

Marisol had her hand up again.

"Can your telescope make the light smaller?"

"No," said Rose. "It's too small, and it's too late, and the big ones are doing it better than I could." She paused, because something had moved at the back of her mind and she did not yet know what it was. "But I'll see," she said.

The monastery's observatory was a white dome the size of a garden shed, on a rise behind the guesthouse, at 2,190 metres. It had been Sister Jadwiga's since the community built it in 2020, thirty-eight years. She had come from Kraków in 1996 with a doctorate in solid-state physics and a vocation she said she had not wanted, and she had spent her free hours, for the rest of her long life, looking for comets.

She had found two. She was proudest of neither. What she had been proud of was the patrol, a twenty-eight-centimetre astrograph on its own mount, wide and fast, that every clear night for twenty-three years had swept the sky in a fixed pattern of fields, a minute of exposure each, and saved every frame. When she died in November 2058, at ninety-one, she left Rose the dome, the mount, the bigger half-metre telescope she used for follow-up, a password on a card, and fourteen hard drives in a fire-safe cabinet, each labelled in her small upright hand with the dates it held.

The monastery's insurer had twice asked whether the drives could be disposed of. Mother Hildegard had twice said she would think about it. In January the chapter had voted to close the observatory at the end of June, because the infirmary roof would cost more than the monastery had, and the half-metre telescope would go to the high school in Socorro, and the drives to the university, if the university wanted them.

Rose climbed the rise at half past nine that night, when the truck got back, in the great silence after Compline, with the prioress's permission, and sat in the warm room under the dome and asked the observatory's assistant a question.

The assistant was called Reckoner. It ran the mount, the camera, the dome and the weather station, and it computed orbits, which was why Jadwiga had named it. It had been running in some version since 2044.

"The asteroid has a period of about four years," Rose said. "Where was it four years ago?"

"Near here," said Reckoner. "The current orbit solutions put the previous close approach in the second half of February 2057. All the solutions that fit the March observations agree on that to within about a day. They disagree on where it was in the sky."

"How much?"

"If I take the full spread of orbits the Clearinghouse publishes, the possible positions on the night of 19 February 2057 lie along a line about six degrees long, in Leo and Sextans. The line is very narrow. The position along it is the uncertain part."

"Brightness?"

"If it is as dark as the survey assumes, between magnitude seventeen and a half and eighteen and a half on those nights, depending on the night. It was about four and a half million kilometres away, twelve times as far as the Moon," it added, which was the sort of comparison Jadwiga had trained it to make and which Rose had never had the heart to retrain. "It was moving fast. About twenty-seven seconds of arc a minute."

Rose sat back.

"Did Sister Jadwiga's patrol cover that line?"

There was a pause of the length that meant Reckoner was reading old logs rather than thinking.

"Partly," it said. "The patrol's midnight pattern covered fields along about two degrees of the line on the nights of 17, 19 and 20 February 2057. Each field was imaged twice, about twelve minutes apart. The camera reached about magnitude nineteen and a half on a star. A trailed object reaches less far, because its light is spread along the trail. At twenty-seven seconds of arc a minute, in a sixty-second exposure, it would be a streak about eleven pixels long. I would estimate it was detectable, at the edge, if it was there."

"If it was in the two degrees you covered."

"Yes. The line is six degrees long and not evenly weighted. I estimate a probability of about one in three that it fell in the covered part on one of the three nights."

"And if we found it?"

"Then the orbit would be fitted to four years of observations instead of three weeks," said Reckoner. "The circle you showed them would shrink by a factor of a hundred or more, the same night."

Rose sat very still for a moment, under the dome, with the stars wheeling slowly over the slit.

Then she got up and went to the fire-safe cabinet.

It took her most of Wednesday to restore the drives. Two of them would not spin; one of them was the one she needed, and she spent an hour with Sister Agatha, who had been a nurse, warming it in the infirmary's blanket warmer, which neither of them confessed to Mother Hildegard and which worked.

By Wednesday night she had five hundred and forty frames: the patrol fields that touched the line, on the three nights, two exposures each. Reckoner computed where along each frame the asteroid would be for each possible orbit and drew the line across the images, a thin green thread from one edge to the other. Rose looked along each thread herself, because Reckoner had said, honestly, that it had been trained on stars and satellites and was less sure than it would like of a faint short streak in old noisy data.

At ten past one on Thursday morning she found it.

A short streak, eleven pixels long, faint, near the green thread on a frame from the night of Saturday 17 February. Her heart went up into her throat. She called Reckoner.

"Measure it," she said.

"The streak is thirty-one pixels long, not eleven," Reckoner said, after a moment. "You are seeing the brightest part. Its full length means it moved about seventy-eight seconds of arc in the exposure. That is about three times faster than the asteroid could have been moving. And it is not on the second frame twelve minutes later along any line the orbits allow."

"A satellite."

"Probably a satellite, or a piece of one, catching the Sun. There were a great many in 2057." It paused. "I am sorry."

She sat for a while with her face in her hands. Then she said the Office of Readings by herself, which was a thing she was allowed to do when she had missed it for the work, and went down to bed for three hours, and got up for Vigils at a quarter to five with the others and stood in her stall and sang the psalms without hearing a word of them.

She found it on Thursday night, at twenty to eleven, on the first of the two frames of a field from Monday 19 February 2057.

It was not on the green thread. It was beside it: a faint grey dash, eleven pixels long, lying along the direction the thread ran, about seven pixels off to one side. She almost went past it. She went back.

On the second frame of the same field, twelve minutes later, there was another faint grey dash, five and a half minutes of arc further along, in the same direction, the same length, the same grey.

"Reckoner."

"I see it," said Reckoner. "The length and direction are right. The motion between the two frames is right to within the errors. Its brightness, about magnitude eighteen, is right. It is off the predicted line by about eighteen seconds of arc, along the line rather than across it, in both frames by nearly the same amount, which is what a slightly different orbit would do." It paused. "I cannot tell you it is 2061 EK. I can tell you it is the kind of thing 2061 EK would be, where 2061 EK might be, and that no known asteroid was there."

Rose measured the two positions with the care she had used to give to flight software, which was a great deal of care, and checked them three times against the star catalogue, and wrote the report in the format the Clearinghouse asked for, and sent it at four minutes past midnight, Friday, with a note that the observations were archival, from a closed observatory's patrol, and should be examined with suspicion.

Then she went down to bed and did not sleep.

Dr. Tuomas Lehtinen of the Minor Body Clearinghouse called the monastery at half past four on Friday afternoon, which was half past twelve at night in Turku. Mother Hildegard took the call in the parlour and fetched Rose from the bees.

"Sister Benally," he said. He sounded young, and tired, and very courteous. "Thank you for this. We have looked at your images. I think you have found it."

Rose sat down on the parlour's hard chair.

"And I cannot use it," said Dr. Lehtinen.

She waited.

"Your times," he said. "The frame headers say the images were taken at 06:41:07 and 06:53:09 universal time on the twentieth of February. Your evening of the nineteenth, just before midnight. If I fit the orbit to those times, the asteroid comes out eighteen or nineteen seconds of arc from where your measurements put it, in both frames, along the direction of motion. Always ahead of where it should be. The fit is bad. Not hopeless. Bad."

"The orbit is a little different from what everyone thought," said Rose. "That's the point."

"That is one explanation. Another is that your clock was wrong. The object was moving about twenty-seven and a half seconds of arc every minute. Forty seconds of clock error, forty-one, makes eighteen or nineteen seconds of arc. And Sister," he said, gently, "the frame headers also say the computer that wrote them was never connected to a time server. I looked."

"No," said Rose. "She didn't trust the internet with her telescope."

"I understand the sentiment. But you see my problem. I can slide your times by forty seconds and get a beautiful fit. If I do that, I am choosing the time that makes the answer look right. And then I have an orbit that seems to be fitted to four years of data, which everyone will trust, and which tells the world whether there is a one in thirty chance of a seven-megaton explosion over your country, and it rests on a guess I made at half past twelve at night." He was quiet a moment. "A precovery with a bad time is worse than no precovery. It persuades. I am sorry. Without a clock I can defend, I cannot submit it to the orbit."

Rose found that she could not argue, because he was right, and she had been the kind of engineer once who would have said exactly the same.

"If the time could be established," she said.

"Then I would put it in tonight."

"What would you need?"

"A record made at the time, by the observer, of the computer's clock against a real one. Anything less, I can't."

She thanked him. He said he was sorry again, and that he hoped very much she found it, and that his grandmother had been a Lutheran deaconess in Oulu and had prayed the hours too, after her fashion, and that he would be awake for a while.

When she put the phone down Mother Hildegard was standing in the parlour doorway, who had heard all of it.

"Jadwiga didn't trust the internet," Mother said.

"No."

"She didn't trust clocks either, did she."

Rose looked up.

"She had a book," said Mother Hildegard. "A little red one. She used to bring it to chapter when she was asking leave for a clear night, and write in it while we argued about the boiler. I used to think it was her prayer journal. It wasn't, was it?"

It was the twenty-fifth of March, the Annunciation, a solemnity even in Lent, and the monastery sang First Vespers of it on Thursday evening and Lauds and the Mass of it on Friday, and none of that waited for an asteroid. Rose had sung through all of it, and she sang Second Vespers on Friday evening at a quarter past five in the chapel with the light going gold on the adobe, Fiat mihi, and then she went to look for a little red book.

It was not in the observatory. She had been through the observatory a hundred times since November 2058. It was not in Jadwiga's cell, which had been cleared and given to a postulant. It was not in the library, the archive, the community room or the sacristy. Sister Agatha, who had laid Jadwiga out, remembered the book but not where it had gone. Sister Perpetua remembered it very well. "She used to write the time in it," she said. "Every night. Against a little grey box with a number on it. I asked her once why she didn't just set the computer right, and she said that if she set it right she would have two kinds of night in her records, the nights before and the nights after, and that one rule written down was better than a clean clock nobody could prove."

"Where is it, Sister?"

"How should I know? She left most of her things to the Polish sisters." Sister Perpetua stopped. "Except the books. The books went in the box for the library sale at Easter. Agatha did it."

The box for the library sale was under the stairs to the bell tower, between the Christmas crib and the spare candlesticks. It was full of physics and Polish poetry. The little red book was at the bottom, between a Polish breviary and a Handbook of Chemistry and Physics from 1987, with a rubber band around it.

Rose sat on the stairs to the bell tower and took the rubber band off.

It was a cheap notebook, red cloth over card, the kind sold in Kraków in the nineties, the third of a series by the number inside the cover. Each line was one night. Date, in the European way, with Roman numerals for the month. The weather. Which patrol pattern. And then, in the same careful upright hand, every night, the same short formula: PC and a sign and a number of seconds, and in brackets, GPS.

She turned the pages with her thumb, back through 2058, into 2057.

17.II.57. Clear, wind W. Patrol W 1–18, M 1–30. PC +40 s (GPS).

18.II.57. Cloud. Closed.

19.II.57. Clear, seeing poor. Patrol M 1–30. PC +41 s (GPS). Rosie's soup too salty. Offered it up.

Rose sat on the stairs and laughed out loud, which in the monastery at a quarter to seven on a Friday in Lent was a thing that people heard, and Sister Agatha put her head round the corner of the stairs and looked at her in alarm.

"It's fine," said Rose. "It's fine. I'm fine."

Then she stopped laughing, because she had understood what the book meant.

Mother Hildegard found her in the observatory at a quarter past eight, after Compline, with the red book open on the desk under the lamp and the report written and not sent.

"Is it the right night?"

"It's the right night. Forty-one seconds fast, against a GPS receiver, written the same evening. She wrote it every night for twenty-three years. There's a page at the front explaining how she read the receiver. I photographed all of it." Rose did not look round. "Reckoner has refitted it. With the times corrected, the two positions fall within half a second of arc of the orbit. Before, they were eighteen and nineteen seconds out."

"So send it."

"I know what it'll do," Rose said. "I know what it will do to the light. It'll make it a hundred times smaller tonight. And I don't know which way. I told them it's almost never in the middle." She turned round at last. "Mother, if it's in the middle, this is the measurement that says so. Tonight. To the whole world, from here. Marisol's school is on that line."

Mother Hildegard sat down on the other chair. She was sixty-eight and had been prioress for eleven years and had buried a good many sisters, Jadwiga among them.

"Then it will be in the middle whether you send it or not," she said. "The rock has been where it is for four years. You don't get to choose which way it goes. You only get to choose whether the people who can do something about it know it tonight, or in four years." She paused. "That is a thing Jadwiga understood, I think, writing the same number in her book every night that nobody read. Send it, Rose."

Rose sent it, at nine minutes past nine, with the photographs of the clock book attached, and a note that the observer had been Sister Jadwiga Nowicka, OSB, deceased, and that the times were corrected by the amount recorded in her own hand the same night, not by any fit.

Then the two of them sat in the dome for a while, without speaking, because it was the great silence, and outside the slit Leo stood high in the south, with Sextans under him, faint and empty, where a small dark rock had gone by four years before with nobody looking but a camera and an old woman's clock.

The Clearinghouse posted the new solution at a quarter to three on Saturday afternoon, Mountain time.

Rose saw it on the observatory screen, where she had gone after None to wait, though there was nothing to wait for and she had told herself so. It was a short line on the page for 2061 EK, among a good deal of other text:

Impact solutions for 2069-12-14: none remaining.

And under it, smaller, the new closest approach for that December: about one hundred and ninety thousand kilometres from the centre of the Earth, half the way to the Moon, with an uncertainty of a few thousand.

Under that, in the list of the observations used, the newest and the oldest: from a large telescope in Hawaii, the night of 25 March 2061; and from the Monastery of Our Lady of the Plains, New Mexico, 20 February 2057, observer J. Nowicka, measurer R. Benally, times corrected from observer's log.

Rose read it twice. Then she said the Glory Be, because it was the prayer that came, and then she said it again for Sister Jadwiga, and then she phoned Father Eusebio, who said nothing at all for long enough that she thought the line had dropped, and then said, "Gracias a Dios," and then, "Sister, will you tell Marisol, or shall I? She's been coming into the office every day after school."

"You tell her," said Rose. "Tell her the light got small and the quarter's in the dark."

Dr. Lehtinen emailed at a little after four, which was about midnight in Turku again. The big telescopes, he wrote, would very likely have got there on their own in another two or three weeks, before it faded, or they might not have; nobody could say now, and it did not matter. What mattered was that the world had not had to wait. He asked whether the Clearinghouse could have scans of the clock book for its own archive, because they wanted to show it to their students.

She wrote back yes.

The observatory closed at the end of June, as the chapter had voted. The infirmary roof was mended. The half-metre telescope went down the mountain on a flatbed to the high school in Socorro, with Rose riding in the cab beside it to make sure they did not knock the mirror. The university took the fourteen drives, and a graduate student came up in May for three days with a laptop and a great many questions, and found in Jadwiga's twenty-three years of patrol frames four more near-Earth asteroids that had passed unnoticed and had not been seen since, and wrote to Rose in some excitement that one of them was going to be very interesting indeed in 2074.

The red book did not go to the university. Mother Hildegard put it in the monastery's archive, with the chapter minutes and the deeds, and on the last clear night before the closing, at the end of June, Rose took it out again with permission and carried it up to the dome.

She opened the patrol computer, for the last time, and the little grey GPS box beside it that she had found in a drawer in March, and switched them both on, and waited for the box to find its satellites.

Then she wrote, in her own hand, under Jadwiga's last line, which had been written two weeks before she died:

29.VI.61. Clear, wind SW. Closing. PC −3 s (GPS). Tell whoever comes after.

She sat a while longer under the open slit. At a quarter to five, down the hill, the bell for Vigils began to ring, the three slow strokes and then the run, and the sound came up the rise through the cold clear air the way it had come up every morning for thirty-eight years to an old woman writing numbers in a book; and Rose closed the book, and closed the dome, and went down.

The Monastery of Our Lady of the Plains, Ojo del Halcón, the asteroid 2061 EK, the Minor Body Clearinghouse in Turku, the observatory's assistant Reckoner, and every person in the story are invented. The Plains of San Agustin and Catron County, Socorro, Albuquerque, Baja California, Sonora, Turku, Oulu and Kraków are real places. The Annunciation, 25 March, is a solemnity kept in Lent unless it falls in Holy Week or the Easter Octave; in 2061 Easter is 10 April, so the 25th (a Friday) keeps it. This is fiction, not guidance on asteroid risk: real impact monitoring is done by professional orbit-computation centres, and their published figures, not a story's, are the ones to read.

The real thing. The pattern in the story, an impact probability that rises for weeks before it collapses, is what real impact monitoring has shown more than once. In December 2004 the asteroid 99942 Apophis briefly had a chance of about 1 in 40 of striking in 2029, and that possibility was ruled out when earlier, archival images of it from March 2004 were found and added to the orbit. In February 2025 the asteroid 2024 YR4 reached a chance of about 3% of striking in December 2032 before further observations brought it to nearly zero. Searching old images for an object once its orbit is known is called precovery. These two cases are given from general knowledge with "about"; the figures in the story are invented and worked out below.

The numbers. The probability is modelled the simple way: the uncertainty in where the asteroid passes, in the plane through the Earth at right angles to its path, is a long thin line with a Gaussian spread σ along it, and the nominal (best-guess) passing point is a distance d = 130,000 km from the Earth's centre. The Earth's effective target radius, enlarged by its gravity, is b = R √(1 + v_esc² / v_∞²) = 6,371 × √(1 + 11.19² / 13.0²) ≈ 8,400 km, for an approach speed v_∞ of 13 km/s. While σ is much larger than b, the chance of impact is about P = 2b / (σ √(2π)) × exp(−d² / 2σ²). With d fixed and σ shrinking as observations accumulate: σ 2,000,000 km gives 1 in 299 (Sat 5 Mar); 1,200,000 km, 1 in 180 (Tue 8 Mar); 600,000 km, 1 in 92 (Fri 11 Mar); 300,000 km, 1 in 49 (Tue 15 Mar); 200,000 km, 1 in 37 (Fri 18 Mar); 130,000 km, 1 in 32 (Tue 22 Mar), the peak, which this formula puts where σ equals d. Had the light kept shrinking, 60,000 km would have given about 1 in 94 and 40,000 km about 1 in 1,170, the cliff Rose describes. After the precovery the nominal passing point moves to about 190,000 km with σ of a few thousand kilometres, and the formula gives zero to any precision anyone publishes. A real calculation uses the full two-dimensional uncertainty and many orbit samples; the shape (up, then a cliff) is the same. The asteroid is given an absolute magnitude H of 24.0; at an assumed albedo of 0.15 that is a diameter of 1,329 / √0.15 × 10^(−24/5) ≈ 0.054 km, about 55 m. On 19 February 2057 (a Monday) it is put about 0.03 AU (4.5 million km) from the Earth and 1.02 AU from the Sun, at a phase angle of 40°; with the standard H, G magnitude system (G = 0.15, phase term about +1.58) its brightness is 24.0 + 5 log₁₀(1.02 × 0.03) + 1.58 ≈ 18.0. A transverse speed of 10 km/s at that distance is about 27.6 seconds of arc a minute; at about 2.5 seconds of arc a pixel a 60-second exposure gives a trail about 11 pixels long, and twelve minutes between frames moves it about 331 seconds of arc, 5.5 minutes. A clock 41 seconds fast puts each measurement 27.6 × 41 / 60 ≈ 18.8 seconds of arc ahead along the track, "eighteen or nineteen". The satellite streak is 31 pixels, about 78 seconds of arc a minute, nearly three times the asteroid's rate.

Francis and the Ox at Greccio

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The village of Greccio and the wooded hillside above it, in the valley of Rieti in central Italy, on Christmas Eve of the year 1223 · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story for Advent and Christmas about Saint Francis of Assisi and the night at Greccio when he set up a manger with hay, an ox and a donkey so that everyone could see how poor the baby Jesus had been. Told through Tessa, a farm girl who is sure her slow old ox is far too plain for anything to do with God. Drawn from Thomas of Celano's account, written a few years after, with a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is tradition and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Francis is remembered on the 4th of October.

A long time ago, in a little village on a hill in Italy, there lived a girl called Tessa, and Tessa had an ox.

His name was Bruno. He was big and brown and slow. He had a wet pink nose, and long eyelashes, and a tail that went swish, swish at the flies.

Bruno pulled the plough in spring. He pulled the cart in autumn. And in winter he stood in his stall, chewing and chewing, and breathing out big warm clouds.

Tessa loved him very much.

But even Tessa had to say that Bruno was not very special to look at.

"You are a plain old ox," she told him, scratching him behind the ear. "And you smell of ox."

Bruno chewed. He did not mind.

The village was called Greccio. It sat on the side of a mountain, with woods above it and a wide green valley below. In the woods, up among the rocks, there were caves. And in the caves, that winter, lived some friars.

Friars are men who give their lives to God. These friars wore rough brown robes tied with a rope, and they had no money at all, and they sang to God in the mornings so loudly that you could hear them in the village.

Their leader was a small, thin man with bright eyes. His name was Brother Francis.

Everybody in Greccio knew Brother Francis. He talked to the birds. He was kind to beggars. He laughed a great deal. And he loved Jesus more than anybody Tessa had ever met.

One cold morning, about two weeks before Christmas, there was a knock on the door of Tessa's house.

It was John, the good man who owned the fields on the hill. John was a friend of Brother Francis. He was a bit out of breath.

"I have come about your ox," he said.

Tessa held on to her father's arm. "What has Bruno done?"

"Nothing at all," said John. "It is about what Brother Francis wants him to do."

And then John told them the strangest plan Tessa had ever heard.

"Brother Francis wants to keep Christmas up in the woods, by the caves," said John. "He wants a manger, like the one the baby Jesus was laid in. He wants it full of hay. And he wants an ox and a donkey standing beside it, the way people have always pictured them."

"Why?" said Tessa's father.

"He says he wants to see it," said John. "With his own eyes. How poor Jesus was, and how cold it was, and how the Son of God was laid on hay where the animals eat. He says people have heard the story so often that they have forgotten how wonderful it is. He wants to help them remember."

He looked at Tessa.

"So may we borrow Bruno?"

Tessa did not know what to say.

She went out to the stall and looked at Bruno. He was chewing. There was a bit of straw stuck on his nose.

"They want you for Jesus," she whispered.

Bruno breathed out a big warm cloud.

"But you are so plain," said Tessa. "Couldn't they find a better ox? A white one, with ribbons on his horns?"

Bruno did not answer. Oxen don't.

But that night, when Tessa said her prayers, she asked God about it.

"Please let Bruno be good," she prayed. "And please don't let him smell too much."

On Christmas Eve, when it was dark, Tessa and her father led Bruno up the hill.

It was very cold. Their breath made clouds and Bruno's breath made bigger clouds. The path went up through the trees, past the rocks, clop, clop, clop, and the stars were out over the valley like sugar.

And then Tessa saw the lights.

Everyone was coming. People were climbing up out of every house in Greccio and from the farms around, carrying candles and torches, lamps and burning sticks. There were mothers with babies wrapped up warm. There were old men with walking sticks. There were friars who had walked from far away. A line of little flames was winding up the mountain, until the whole dark wood was bright.

"It's like daytime," said Tessa.

"It's better than daytime," said her father.

At the top, in a clearing by the rocks, there was the manger.

It was just a wooden box for animal food, the kind you would find in any stable. It was full of soft, sweet-smelling hay. Next to it stood a little grey donkey, with long ears, looking very sensible.

And there was Brother Francis, waiting.

He came straight over to Bruno. He put his hand on the big brown neck, and he looked into Bruno's big brown eyes.

"Thank you for coming, brother ox," he said. "You are just right."

Tessa stared at him. "But he's only plain," she said. "He's just an ordinary ox."

Brother Francis smiled, and his whole face lit up like the torches.

"Tessa," he said, "where do you think God chose to be born?"

Tessa thought. "In a stable," she said.

"In a stable," said Francis. "Not a palace. Not a golden room. A stable, with ordinary animals, who breathe and chew and smell of animals. Jesus did not come for special places, little one. He came for ordinary ones. For your house, and my cave, and Bruno's stall."

He gave Bruno a pat.

"God made the ox," he said. "And the ox kept the baby warm. That is what Bruno is for tonight."

So Tessa led Bruno to the manger, and he stood on one side, and the donkey stood on the other.

Bruno sniffed the hay. Then he lowered his big head and breathed on it, a long warm breath, hmmmmm.

Somebody laughed, softly, kindly. And Tessa felt her cheeks go warm. But it was a happy warm.

Then the friars began to sing.

Their voices went up into the night, and bounced off the rocks, and came back again, so that it sounded as if the whole mountain was singing too. All the people joined in. Babies woke up and stopped crying to listen. Even the donkey flicked her ears.

A priest said Mass right there, over the manger, in the middle of the woods, with the candles all around.

And Brother Francis stood up to read the Gospel, the story of the night Jesus was born. Then he talked to everybody about the little baby of Bethlehem.

He talked so gently, and so happily, that everyone went quiet to listen. When he said the word Bethlehem, he said it in a funny soft way, like a little lamb going baa.

"Beth-le-hem," said Francis.

Tessa giggled. Then she put her hand over her mouth. But Francis caught her eye and smiled, as if that was exactly what he had hoped.

He told them about the baby who was God, and who came to be small, and poor, and cold, because he loved them. He said that the baby was just as near to them tonight, on this hill, as he had been to the shepherds long ago.

And Tessa looked at the empty manger full of hay, with Bruno breathing on it, and the candles shining, and she thought she understood.

It was very late when the singing was over.

People said goodnight to each other in soft voices, and began to go home down the mountain. The line of little flames wound back down through the trees.

Tessa was so sleepy that her father had to carry her. Bruno walked beside them, clop, clop, clop, with a few bits of manger hay still stuck to his nose.

"Papa," said Tessa, with her eyes closed. "Bruno was just right."

"He was," said her father.

"Plain things can be for God," said Tessa.

"They can," said her father.

And when they got home, Tessa did not go to her own bed straight away. She went into the stall first, and she put her arms around Bruno's big warm neck, and she said, "Happy Christmas, brother ox."

Bruno breathed out a big warm cloud.

He smelled of ox.

Tessa didn't mind at all.

Now, there is one more thing to tell. The people of Greccio kept the hay from that manger, because it had been so close to something holy. And they said, afterwards, that when their animals were sick, they gave them a little of the hay, and the animals got better.

And on that very spot, in time, they built a little church, so that everybody would remember the night when Brother Francis brought Christmas to the woods.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can remember Saint Francis and the manger at Greccio.

When you see a crib at church, or at home, with Mary and Joseph and the shepherds, the ox and the donkey, you can remember that a long time ago, on a cold hill, Francis made one so that everybody could see how much Jesus loved them.

And when you feel plain, or ordinary, or not very special, you can remember Bruno. Jesus was born in an ordinary place, among ordinary animals, because he loves ordinary people like us, very much.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, little baby of Bethlehem,
thank you for coming to be small and poor,
so that I can come close to you.
Make my heart a warm place for you this Christmas,
like the hay in the manger.
Saint Francis, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Francis of Assisi was born in Assisi in Umbria in 1181 or 1182, the son of a cloth merchant, Pietro di Bernardone. After a youth of parties and soldiering he gave up his inheritance, lived in poverty, cared for lepers and rebuilt ruined churches, and gathered the brothers who became the Order of Friars Minor (the Franciscans), whose rule was approved by Pope Honorius III in 1223, the same year as Greccio. He was a deacon, never a priest. He received the stigmata at La Verna in 1224, died at the Porziuncola below Assisi on the evening of 3 October 1226, and was canonised by Pope Gregory IX in 1228. His memorial, the 4th of October, is in the General Roman Calendar.

Greccio is history. The earliest account is by Thomas of Celano, a friar who knew Francis, in his First Life of Saint Francis (Vita prima, Book 1, chapter 30), written by about 1229, some five or six years afterwards. Celano says that about fifteen days before Christmas Francis asked a man of Greccio named John, a friend he held dear, to prepare a manger with hay and to bring an ox and an ass, because he wanted to see with bodily eyes how the child had lain in a manger, in want and on hay, with an ox and an ass standing by. Celano describes the friars called from many places, the men and women of the district coming with candles and torches to light the night, the woods ringing with song and the rocks answering, Mass celebrated over the manger, and Francis, vested as a deacon, singing the Gospel and preaching about the "Babe of Bethlehem". He says that when Francis said the word Bethlehem he said it like the bleating of a sheep. Saint Bonaventure retells it in his Legenda maior (chapter 10) some forty years later and adds that Francis first obtained permission from the Pope, so that it would not be thought a novelty. The year 1223 is the usual dating; Celano places it three years before Francis's death. The surname Velita often given to John comes from later tradition, not Celano, so the story calls him only John.

What else Celano reports. He says the hay was kept, and that afterwards it cured sick animals and helped other people, and that the place was later consecrated with an altar and church in Francis's honour; the friary and sanctuary at Greccio stand today. He also reports that a man of virtue saw a vision that night: a sleeping child lying in the manger, whom Francis approached and seemed to wake. We have kept the vision out of a story for small children, who might take it for something everyone saw; the cures from the hay are told the way Celano tells them, as what people said afterwards.

The ox and the donkey. The Gospel of Luke (2:7) says that Mary laid her son in a manger; neither Luke nor Matthew mentions animals. The ox and the ass come from a very old Christian reading of Isaiah 1:3 ("The ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master's crib") and were already in Christian art for centuries before Francis, which is why John in the story says they are how "people have always pictured them". Greccio is often called the first crib or Christmas crèche. That is fair as a beginning of the popular custom, which spread through the Franciscans; but images of the nativity, and devotion to the relics of the manger at Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome, are much older. Pope Francis wrote his letter on the crib, Admirabile signum, at Greccio on 1 December 2019.

What is ours. Tessa, her father and Bruno the ox are invented: Celano does not say whose ox or donkey they were. So are the knock at the door, every line anyone speaks, Francis's words to the ox and to Tessa, the line of torches seen from the path (Celano says only that people came with lights), the laugh, the walk home, and the talk before sleep. Francis calling the ox "brother" is our touch, in the spirit of his Canticle of the Creatures, where he calls the sun, wind and fire his brothers. We have not quoted any English translation of Celano; the paraphrase above is the substance. The prayer is not an approved text.

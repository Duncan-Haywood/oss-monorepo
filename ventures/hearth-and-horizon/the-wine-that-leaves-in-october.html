The Wine That Leaves in October

Science fiction about what might be · Arcadia Station, Arcadia Planitia, Mars, Wednesday 26 September 2142 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Arcadia Station has four bottles of altar wine, a priest who says Mass seven times a week, and one cargo ship that leaves Earth next Wednesday. Sister Thandiwe works out that the ship takes 259 days, that the next one is 26 months behind it, and that four bottles last 28 weeks at seven Masses and 40 at five. The sums are easy. Giving up two Masses is not.

Sister Thandiwe Mokoena had been told the cargo office would call at nine, and at nine exactly the grille over the chapel's vestment press said, in the voice of Aurelia, the station's assistant: "Earth Cargo is on the line. They say they are sorry about the time. It is a quarter past two in the afternoon where they are, and they have had a long week."

"Put them through."

The man on the line was called Rafael Ibekwe. He sounded like someone who had given bad news so many times that he had learned to put it first. "Sister, the manifest for the October ship closes on Sunday. We are over mass by about sixty kilograms. The things at the bottom of the priority list come off, and on your list, the bottom is the altar wine. Twelve litres. I am sorry. It can go on the next one."

"When is the next one?"

"November 2144."

Thandiwe looked at the box of candle stubs on the sacristy shelf, because it was the nearest thing that did not require her to speak. "Mr Ibekwe, I was a flight engineer before I was a Sister. I know what a window is. May I show you what I mean before you take it off?"

She showed Father Anselm first, because it was his Mass.

He was seventy-one, small, and the only priest within fifty-five million kilometres, which he described as "a very flat diocese". He sat on the folding stool by the vestment press with his tea going cold and watched her draw on the back of the Advent rota.

"A ship goes from Earth to Mars the cheap way," she said. "Not straight. Straight is a mistake: Mars is moving. You put the ship on an ellipse that touches Earth's orbit going out and Mars's orbit coming in. The ellipse has to be a particular size. Earth is 1 astronomical unit from the sun, Mars is 1.524, so the long axis is 2.524 and the half of it is 1.262."

"I will take your word for that."

"Don't, but it's fine. A year for something going round the sun at 1.262 is 1.262 to the power of one and a half, in years. That is 1.418 years, about 518 days. The ship only does half the ellipse, so half of that: 259 days. Eight months and a bit."

"And the ship that leaves next Wednesday, the third of October?"

"Arrives on the nineteenth of June." She wrote it. "Now here is the part that matters. In 259 days Mars goes round the sun by 259 over 687, which is 135.7 degrees of its orbit. For the ship to arrive at the same place Mars is, Mars has to be 180 minus that ahead of Earth when the ship leaves: 44.3 degrees. That happens once every time Earth catches Mars up. Earth gains on Mars by one lap every 779.9 days: 25 months and three weeks. So the ship that does not leave next Wednesday leaves on the twenty-first of November 2144, and arrives on the seventh of August 2145."

Father Anselm put the cup down. "And the wine on that ship is how old when I see it?"

"It is on the shelf in the cargo bay from the nineteenth of June 2143 if it goes next week. If it doesn't, you wait another 780 days. That is 111 weeks."

"I have four bottles."

"You have four bottles."

Aurelia did the arithmetic out loud, because Father Anselm asked it to and because it said, in its quiet way, that a sum said aloud is harder to round.

"A bottle is 750 millilitres. The rubric for Mass needs a few spoonfuls for the priest, and I am using fifteen millilitres a Mass, which is generous: a tablespoon. Fr Anselm, you celebrate seven Masses a week. That is 105 millilitres. Four bottles are 3,000 millilitres. Three thousand divided by 105 is 28.6 weeks. The cargo arrives in 37."

"Eight weeks without."

"Eight and a half. Eight weeks in which you could not celebrate the Mass at all, and could give Communion only from hosts already consecrated. The station's tabernacle does not hold forever: the hosts are baked from our own wheat and kept in a dry tin, and I would not call a fortnight safe, never mind two months. There is no way to consecrate bread alone. I do not decide that and I would not like to be asked to. It is the Church's rule, not the station's."

Father Anselm said nothing for some time.

"If it did not go next week," said Thandiwe, "you would be dry for 111 plus 8 weeks. Two years and four months, near enough, and you are seventy-one."

"I am aware of my age, Sister."

"I'm not saying it to be cruel. I'm saying it because it is the number."

The sum that turned out to matter was a different one, and Father Anselm found it himself, on the third evening, when he came into the sacristy with a pencil and said he had done some work.

"Five Masses a week instead of seven. Sunday, of course. Monday, Wednesday, Friday and Saturday. Drop the Tuesday and the Thursday." He held up the paper. "Five times fifteen is seventy-five. Three thousand over seventy-five: forty weeks. Which is three weeks longer than the ship takes. Three weeks of margin. If the ship is late, I would have to ask the bishop what then."

"It's what the sum says," said Aurelia.

"I did not ask what the sum says. I asked whether it is right to do it." He looked at Thandiwe. "I will say the Tuesday and Thursday Hours with the Sisters and give you Communion from the Sunday hosts. I will not say the Mass. You will lose it twice a week for nine months."

"We will notice."

"Yes. I thought so. I notice already."

She had wanted to argue and found she couldn't, because he had not asked her to. She wrote the figure on the cargo office's form instead: Altar wine, 12 litres, about 12 kilograms. Reduce to 4 litres if the mass cannot be found. Four litres would still carry the station past the next window's wait, and it weighed a third as much.

The wine went on the October ship in the end, in a padded crate the size of a shoebox, four litres of it, which Rafael Ibekwe found the mass for by moving a spare pump. A Brazilian engineer on the ship signed the box with her own name and a line from Psalm 104: and wine to gladden the heart of man.

She sent a message along with the crate. It reached Arcadia in the seventh month, when the ship was still four weeks out, and Thandiwe read it aloud in the chapel with the lights low:

It's a long way. We'll try not to spill it.

On the nineteenth of June, 2143, a Wednesday, the ship came down. Father Anselm had said the Mass that morning from the fourth bottle, with a quarter of it still in the glass. After Mass he sat on the folding stool and said, to nobody in particular, "Forty weeks of wine for thirty-seven of sea. Three to spare."

Thandiwe, who had made the cargo-bay checklist, went out to meet the crate in a suit that smelled of the inside of a helmet, and carried it back herself across the quarter-mile of dusty road. She did not run. The engineers watched her not run. It was the slowest she had ever walked on a planet, and when she set the box on the vestment press and opened it, there was nothing to say, and so for a moment she said nothing at all.

Then she took out the first bottle, held it up to the lamp, and the light came through it the colour of a good loaf's crust, and on the Thursday, for the first time in nine months, there was a Mass at the end of the day.

Arcadia Station, the Sisters of Our Lady of the Dawn, Sister Thandiwe Mokoena, Father Anselm, Aurelia, Rafael Ibekwe, the Brazilian engineer and the ship are invented; the Mars of the story is the one of Thirty-Nine Minutes. The numbers: a Hohmann transfer between circular, coplanar orbits at 1 AU and 1.524 AU has a semi-major axis of (1 + 1.524) / 2 = 1.262 AU and a period of 1.262^1.5 = 1.418 years (about 518 days), so half of it is about 259 days; Mars's period is 687 days, so it covers 259 / 687 x 360 = 135.7 degrees, and at departure it must lead Earth by 180 - 135.7 = 44.3 degrees; Earth and Mars line up again every 1 / (1/365.256 - 1/686.98) = 779.9 days. A departure on Wednesday 3 October 2142 therefore arrives on 19 June 2143, and one 780 days later leaves on 21 November 2144 and arrives on 7 August 2145 (the dates are illustrative; real windows and real orbits are not circular or coplanar, and real trajectories have launch windows with some slack that costs fuel). Wine arithmetic: 15 mL a Mass (a tablespoon, a figure of mine) times 7 Masses a week is 105 mL, so 4 bottles of 750 mL (3,000 mL) last 28.6 weeks against a transit of 37 weeks; at 5 Masses a week, 75 mL, they last 40.0 weeks, a margin of 3.0 weeks. The 111-week wait is the 780 days between windows. The station has no vineyard; the wine, the cargo office's mass margin and the crate are invented. That Mass cannot be celebrated without both bread and wine, that Communion may be received under one kind, and that Communion services with reserved hosts are a recognised substitute are stated from memory of Catholic practice, and how a community off Earth would be dispensed or governed is for the bishops, not for this story. The shelf life of reserved hosts is not claimed.

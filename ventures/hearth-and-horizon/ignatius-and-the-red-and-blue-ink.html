Ignatius and the Red and Blue Ink

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The stone tower-house of Loyola, near Azpeitia in the Basque hills of northern Spain, from the summer of 1521 into the winter · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Ignatius of Loyola, the proud young knight who was stuck in bed with a hurt leg, asked for adventure books and got the lives of Jesus and the saints instead, and about Maite, the cook's daughter who carries up his candle and helps him find out which daydreams leave you glad. Told from Ignatius's own account of those months, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. He is remembered on the 31st of July.

A long time ago, in the green hills of Spain, there was a tall stone house called Loyola, and at the top of the house there was a knight who could not get out of bed.

His name was Íñigo. Much later, people would call him Ignatius.

Íñigo had been in a battle at a town called Pamplona. A cannonball had hurt both his legs, very badly, and the soldiers had carried him all the way home on a stretcher, over the hills, to the house where he was born.

Now he lay in a big bed in the top room, with his leg wrapped up and propped on a pillow. And he was cross.

Every evening, a girl called Maite carried up his supper and his candle.

Maite was seven. Her mother was the cook at Loyola. Maite knew every step of the stairs in the dark, all the way up: the creaky one, the crooked one, and the one where the cat liked to sleep.

"Here is your soup, Don Íñigo," she said. "And here is your candle."

"Soup," said Íñigo. "Again."

He was a knight. Knights are supposed to ride horses and wear fine clothes and do brave, famous things. Knights are not supposed to lie in bed for weeks and weeks eating soup.

"Is there anything else you want?" asked Maite.

"Yes," said Íñigo. "Books. Bring me books about knights. Brave knights, with swords and dragons and castles. I am so bored I could howl."

So Maite went to look.

She looked in the big chest in the hall. She looked on the shelf in the kitchen. She asked her mother, and her mother asked the lady of the house, and the lady of the house looked everywhere.

There were no books about knights at Loyola. Not one.

"There are only these," said Maite the next evening, and she heaved two great heavy books onto the end of the bed. "One is all about Jesus. And one is all about the saints."

Íñigo looked at them as if they were two plates of cold cabbage.

"That's no good," he said. "I want dragons."

"There are no dragons," said Maite. "Only Jesus and the saints."

Íñigo sighed a very big sigh. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at the wall. He looked at his leg, which was still wrapped up and still hurting.

"Oh, very well," he said. "Give me the saints."

At first he only read to pass the time.

But the book was full of surprising people. There was Saint Francis, who gave away all his fine clothes and sang to God in the fields. There was Saint Dominic, who walked from town to town to tell people about Jesus. There were saints who were brave in ways that had nothing to do with swords.

Some evenings, when Maite brought the candle, Íñigo read bits out loud to her.

And some evenings he put the book down and just lay there, staring, with a faraway look in his eyes.

"What are you thinking about?" Maite asked.

"Two kinds of things," said Íñigo.

"Sometimes," he said, "I think about being a famous knight again. I think about riding into a great city on a white horse, with everyone cheering. I think about a beautiful lady, a very grand one, and all the clever things I would say to her, and all the brave things I would do so that she would notice me. I can think about that for hours."

"That sounds nice," said Maite.

"It is nice," said Íñigo. "While I am thinking it."

He frowned.

"And other times," he said, "I think about the saints. I think: Saint Francis did that, so why shouldn't I? Saint Dominic did that, so why shouldn't I? I think about walking all the way to Jerusalem, where Jesus lived, with no shoes on, and giving everything away, and loving God with my whole heart."

"That sounds nice too," said Maite.

"Yes," said Íñigo. "That's the funny thing. They both feel nice. But I have started to notice something."

Maite sat down on the end of the bed, next to the heavy books. The candle went flicker, flicker.

"Tell me," she said.

"When I finish thinking about being a famous knight," said Íñigo slowly, "I feel empty afterwards. Dry, like a cup with nothing in it. A bit sad, and I don't know why."

He looked at the candle.

"But when I finish thinking about the saints, and about serving God, I am still happy afterwards. The gladness stays. It's like the warmth that stays in a stone after the sun goes down."

Maite thought about this.

"It's like cake and bread," she said at last.

Íñigo blinked. "Is it?"

"If I eat a whole lot of honey cake," said Maite, "it's lovely while I'm eating it. But afterwards I feel wobbly and cross. And if I eat good bread, I feel strong for ages."

Íñigo laughed. It was the first time Maite had ever heard him laugh.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly like cake and bread. Maite, you are a wise woman."

"I'm seven," said Maite.

"Then you are a wise woman of seven," said Íñigo.

From then on, Íñigo was different.

He did not grumble about the soup any more. He talked to everyone who came up the stairs: his brother, the servants, the lady of the house, and Maite. He talked to them about God, and they were surprised, because he had never done that before.

And one evening he said, "Maite, I need ink."

"Ink?" said Maite. "What colour?"

"Red," said Íñigo. "And blue. And the very best paper you can find."

So Maite found him red ink and blue ink, and a pile of good smooth paper, and Íñigo sat up in bed with the big book about Jesus on his knees.

He had always had beautiful handwriting. Now he used it.

He copied out the words that Jesus said, and he wrote them in red. He copied out the words that Mary, Jesus's mother, said, and he wrote them in blue. Slowly and carefully, letter by letter, so that every one was lovely.

Maite held the candle closer.

"Why red?" she asked.

"Because they are the most precious words in the book," said Íñigo. "They should look like treasure."

"And why blue?"

"Because blue is the colour of the sky," said Íñigo. "And Mary is the Queen of Heaven."

The pages piled up. Red words, blue words, red words, blue words. By the end there were nearly three hundred pages. Maite could not read them all, but she liked the way the colours looked, like a garden.

Íñigo told people, later, that one night, while he lay awake in that room, he saw Our Lady holding the little Child Jesus. He said it filled him with such comfort that he was never quite the same again.

Maite did not see anything that night. But she noticed that, in the morning, Don Íñigo was very quiet, and very happy, and kind to the cat.

The summer ended. The leaves on the hills went brown and gold. Íñigo's leg got better, little by little, though it never got quite straight, and all his life he walked with a little limp.

On cold clear nights, when Maite brought up the candle, she often found him not in bed at all, but sitting by the open window, looking up.

"What are you looking at?" she whispered.

"The stars," said Íñigo.

"Why?"

He did not answer for a while.

"Because they make me want to serve God," he said. "With everything I have."

So Maite sat down beside him, very quietly, and the two of them looked up together. The candle went flicker, flicker behind them. And over the hills of Loyola the stars came out, one, and then another, and then too many to count.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can remember Saint Ignatius. He wanted to be a famous knight. Instead, lying in bed with a hurt leg, he found out that God was the best adventure of all.

And you can do what he did. Before you sleep, think back over your day. Which things made you glad for a little while, and then left you empty? And which things left you warm and happy, like bread, like a stone in the sun? The warm ones are often God, saying hello.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, thank you for today,
for the cake and for the bread.
Help me to notice the things that make me truly glad,
and to love you with everything I have.
Saint Ignatius, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Ignatius of Loyola was born Íñigo López de Loyola in 1491 at the tower-house of Loyola, near Azpeitia in Gipuzkoa, in the Basque Country of northern Spain, the youngest of the thirteen children of Beltrán Yáñez de Oñaz y Loyola and Marina Sánchez de Licona. He grew up to be a courtier and soldier. On 20 May 1521, defending the fortress of Pamplona against a French army, he was hit by a cannonball that broke one leg and injured the other. The French, he says, treated him courteously and had him carried home to Loyola. He later took the name Ignatius. With a few companions from the University of Paris he founded the Society of Jesus (the Jesuits), which Pope Paul III approved on 27 September 1540. He wrote the Spiritual Exercises, died in Rome on 31 July 1556, was beatified in 1609 and canonised by Pope Gregory XV on 12 March 1622. His feast, the 31st of July, is an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

The months in bed. Almost everything in the story about Ignatius comes from his own account, the Autobiography (sometimes called the Reminiscences), which he dictated in 1553 to 1555 to a younger Jesuit, Luís Gonçalves da Câmara, speaking of himself as "the pilgrim". He says that, being well enough to read, he asked for the books of chivalry he was fond of; that none could be found in the house; and that he was given instead a life of Christ and a book of the lives of the saints in Spanish (usually identified as Ludolph of Saxony's Vita Christi and a Flos Sanctorum, a version of the Golden Legend). He describes daydreaming for hours about serving a great lady, and also telling himself that what Saint Dominic and Saint Francis had done, he must do too, and imagining going barefoot to Jerusalem. He says he noticed that the worldly thoughts delighted him at the time but left him dry and discontented, while the thoughts of following the saints left him consoled and happy afterwards; this, he says, was the first thing he ever reasoned out about the things of God, and it became the root of his teaching on the discernment of spirits. He tells of a night when he saw Our Lady with the Child Jesus, which consoled him greatly; we tell it as he told it, as his own testimony. He says his brother and the household noticed the change in him, that he spent his time speaking of God, and that he began a book in which he copied the words of Christ in red ink and those of Our Lady in blue, on polished, lined paper, in a fine hand, nearly three hundred pages. And he says that his greatest consolation was to look at the sky and the stars, often and for a long time, because it gave him a great desire to serve God. Our paraphrases are not quotations from a translation; they are the substance of what he wrote.

What we left out. The surgery was much harder than one gentle line. He says his leg was set badly and had to be broken and reset; that he came near death and recovered on the eve of the feast of Saints Peter and Paul; and that, out of vanity, so that he could wear a fashionable tight boot, he had a bone that stuck out below the knee cut away and his leg stretched with weights. He left Loyola early in 1522 for the shrine of Montserrat, where he laid down his sword and dagger before Our Lady, and then spent nearly a year at Manresa. His lifelong limp is recorded by his companions. A household tradition credits his sister-in-law, Magdalena de Araoz, with giving him the books; he does not name who did, so neither do we.

Maite is invented. There is no Maite, and no cook's daughter, in the sources. The stairs, the cat, the soup, the cake and bread, the open window, the lady of the house looking for books, and every word anyone says are our own. The reasons Ignatius gives Maite for the red and the blue are ours too: he does not explain the colours.

What is ours: Maite and her mother; every line of dialogue; Ignatius reading aloud and talking about his daydreams to a child; the comparison with cake and bread; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

The Tap That Let Go at Night

Science fiction about what might be · San Isidro de Quinuamayo (invented), Cajamarca region, Peru, 12 to 15 May 2048 · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A village's gravity water line has burst its third tap fitting in a year, always at night. The water committee's assistant works out that the pipe is not weakest when the taps run but when they are shut: 75 metres of static head is about 7.4 bar against fittings marked 6.3, and it is the quiet hours that break them. A break-pressure tank halfway down, and a borrowed pressure gauge that corrects its own map, fix it before the feast of San Isidro.

The tap at the lower stand let go at ten past two on the Wednesday morning, and Marcelina Cieza, who lived in the nearest house and had slept through three summers of dogs, heard it at once, because it was the sound of a great deal of water doing something it had not been asked to do.

She went out in her blanket. The fitting, a brass elbow the size of a plum, had come out of the end of the pipe like a cork out of a bottle, and the stream from the open end went across the lane in a white arch and into the Quirozes' maize. She could not close it, because the valve she would have closed was the one that had blown. She put her thumb in the end of the pipe, which was foolish, and took it out again, which was wise, and then went to wake Eusebio Tantalean, who was president of the water committee and had been, for eleven years, the man who got woken.

By four the committee's volunteer had shut the line at the spring box, two kilometres up the hill in the dark with a torch in his teeth, and the water stopped. In San Isidro de Quinuamayo, which had two hundred and forty people, one chapel, a school with two classrooms and a health post that opened on Mondays, there was then no water until it was mended, as there had been twice before, in August and again at Christmas.

"The fitting is bad," said Eusebio, at nine, in the doorway of the chapel, with the brass elbow in his hand as if it were evidence. "They sent us a bad batch. I wrote on the paper, the same shop, the third time."

He had put the committee's phone on the pew in front of him. Padre Julián was somewhere behind the sacristy door telling the novena's choir which hymn they were going to sing on Thursday and why it was not the one they had picked. The feast of San Isidro Labrador was on the Friday, the fifteenth, with the Mass at ten and the procession of the saint through the fields afterward, and the committee had said, as it said every year, that the village would have water for the guests.

The phone had on it the water assistant. It had been installed by an engineering students' group from the university in Cajamarca, who had left it with the line's drawings, the log of every repair, and a short note saying it was a program, that it made mistakes, and that the committee should check its figures against a gauge and not against its confidence. The village called it Pozo, which means a well, because it had been told that water was its business and no one had thought of a better name.

"Pozo," said Eusebio. "The elbow."

"I would like to look at the pipe before the elbow," said the assistant. "May I say what I have, and where I am guessing? Then you can tell me which of the guesses is wrong."

"Say."

"The spring box is on the map at 1,925 metres, and the lower stand at 1,847. That is 78 metres of fall, from the phone's altitude, which I do not trust beyond a few metres. Water standing in a pipe pushes with 9.81 kilopascals for every metre of height above it, so with the taps all shut the pressure at the lower stand is 78 metres, which is 765 kilopascals, which is 7.65 bar. The log says the pipe is polyethylene, 32 millimetres across the outside with a wall of 3 millimetres, so 26 inside. The fittings you bought are stamped 6.3."

"Six point three what?"

"Bar. The maker's number for the pressure they are meant to take for years. A bar is about what a column of water ten metres high pushes with. So six point three bar is about 63 metres of water standing above the fitting. The line, with the taps shut, is asking each of them for about 75 or 78. That is the part I would like to be wrong about."

Eusebio looked at the elbow. It had a thread, and on the thread a bright ring of metal where the pipe's grip had slid.

"But it burst at night," he said. "At night there is nobody on the line. The pressure is low at night, because nobody is using it. In the morning, with everyone, then it is high."

"That is what most people believe," said the assistant, "and I believed it too, until I did the sum in the other order. Pressure in a pipe is not made by the people using it. It is made by the hill. When the taps are shut nothing moves, so nothing is lost to the pipe wall, and the whole of the hill's push arrives at the end. When they are open the water moves, and rubbing along 2,000 metres of narrow pipe uses some of the push up. So the pressure at the tap is highest when the taps are closed. At night."

"How much does it use up?" asked Eusebio, who had spent his working life buying cement and was suspicious of any answer without a figure.

"For this I use the Hazen–Williams formula, which is an old rule for water in a pipe and is good to perhaps ten per cent. I give the pipe a roughness number of 140, for new polyethylene. Your village is 240 people. If each uses 20 litres a day, which is what the students wrote in the note, that is 4,800 litres, or 0.056 litres a second spread over the day. People do not draw it spread out. They fetch at six and at five. I multiply by four for the busiest hour and call it 0.22 litres a second through the whole line at once. That is the figure I would like checked, because it decides the rest."

"A jerrycan of twenty litres in a minute and a half," said Eusebio. "That is what I get at the tap at six."

"At 0.22 a second, it is ninety seconds. Yes. So I put 0.22 litres a second through 2,000 metres of 26-millimetre pipe. The water goes at 0.41 metres a second, and the pipe wall takes 19.9 metres of height out of it. So at six in the morning, with the line busy, the lower tap has 78 less 20, which is 58 metres, about 5.7 bar. Under the 6.3. At two in the morning it has 78. Over it by more than a bar."

"So every day it goes from five and a half to seven and a half," said Eusebio slowly. "And back."

"Every day, twice. A fitting that is run near its rating, bent up and down like that, ages faster than it would at a steady load. I do not have a figure for how much faster, and I will not make one. But the pattern fits what you have written in the log. Three elbows, and all three burst between midnight and five."

"And what about the water from the tap if I shut it fast? The children shut it fast."

"That is the second thing. When a tap is closed quickly the water in the pipe has nowhere to go, and the stop makes a pulse that travels back up. For this pipe I take a pulse speed of 300 metres a second. The maker's sheet gives a range, 250 to 400, so call that a guess. The jump in pressure is the speed of the pulse times the speed the water was going, divided by 9.81. That is 300 times 0.41, over 9.81, which is 12.7 metres of water. On a busy hour, with the tap closed hard, the 58 metres at the tap becomes 71, for a fraction of a second. At night, with a single child shutting a tap that has been running lightly, the jump is smaller. But the fitting does not know whether it is a big stop or a small one."

"Then I will make the spring give more," said Eusebio. "Open the valve at the box. If the water comes down faster, it pushes harder."

"I would like to stop you there," said the assistant. "With the taps shut, the valve at the box does nothing. With the taps open, a wider valve would only change the flow if the taps were asking for more, and they are asking for what a jerrycan holds. And it would be the wrong direction in any case. If I push 0.35 litres a second through the pipe, which is the most the spring gives in May, the pipe takes out 47 metres, not 20, and your tap at six in the morning would have 31 metres, and a thin stream. The spring has plenty of water. It gives about 30 cubic metres a day against the 5 you use. The trouble is not water. It is height."

"Then we buy better elbows."

"You may. Ten-bar fittings would take a hill of 100 metres, and they cost more, and I do not know the price here. I would also check the pipe itself. You have lengths with a stamp on them that I cannot read from the photograph."

He did not answer at once. Outside, on the chapel step, the children were practising a shuffling dance for the saint, in which the saint's ox was played by Wilder Quiroz, aged nine, who was good at it.

"What would you do," Eusebio said, "if you were a man with a shovel and no money?"

"I would take the hill away from the pipe in two halves," said the assistant. "A tank, halfway. A break-pressure tank. The pipe from the spring runs into the top of an open tank with a float valve, and a second pipe leaves the bottom for the village. The water in the tank is at the air's pressure. It forgets the hill above it. So the lower pipe has only the lower half of the hill to carry: about 38 metres, about 3.7 bar, with the taps shut. Add the pulse, 12.7, and it is about 51 metres. About five bar, in the worst moment, against 6.3. And at the busy hour the lower tap has 38 less 10 for the friction in the lower kilometre, which is 28 metres, 2.7 bar. A tap needs about five metres to run. So it is more than enough."

"A tank," said Eusebio. "A tank is a thing to keep clean."

"It is. An open tank is a place for leaves, for a bird, for a drowned frog. It needs a lid, a screen on the vent, and a rule about who opens it. I cannot say whether your water is safe to drink; I have never tested it, and a float tank does not make it safer. The health post should test it. That is a different job and I do not want it forgotten because I have solved this one."

The tank went in on the Thursday, on a flat shoulder of the path at the thousand-metre mark, where the committee's volunteer and the Quirozes' two sons and a borrowed cement mixer built a box a metre across and a metre deep, with a concrete lid and a float valve from the shop in Cajamarca that Eusebio drove to himself, leaving at four and back by noon.

The health post had a pressure gauge. It had come with a hand pump and read to ten bar, with a needle that quivered, and the nurse, Aurora, lent it on condition that it came back clean. At half past four on the Thursday afternoon, with the tank still empty and the old line still running straight to the village, Eusebio screwed it onto the new stub at the lower stand, shut the tap, and read it.

"Seven point four," he said. "Seven and four tenths."

"Then my figure was high," said the assistant, "by about a quarter of a bar. At 7.4 bar the standing water is 75.4 metres, so the true fall from the spring box to the lower stand is about 75 metres and not 78. The phone's altitude was out by three. I will correct the map and recompute with 75.4. It changes little. The tank sits 38 metres below the spring box, so the lower half now has about 37.5. The old line at night is 7.4 bar and not 7.65, which is still a good way above the fittings. It is also a lucky mistake. I would rather have a gauge that says 7.4 than a map that says 7.65 and a village that believes me."

"Is it a good gauge?"

"It is the only one we have. The nurse says it was checked last year. It reads to a quarter bar, and the needle moves. I would trust it to about a quarter bar, and I would not put it in a report as a measurement of the third decimal."

On the Thursday evening the tank was filled, the float valve rose and clicked off, and the water went into the lower line, and Eusebio shut every tap in the village by turns, with the gauge on the end, and read it each time. It said 3.7.

On the Friday there was Mass in the chapel, and a procession in the fields, and a saint carried on the shoulders of four men, who had been told that if the saint was dropped they would carry him again from the beginning. Wilder Quiroz was the ox, and was admired. At noon there was soup for the guests from Cajamarca, who had come in a van, and a long queue at the lower tap with twenty-two jerrycans in it, and when it had gone down Marcelina Cieza took her bucket and filled it at the stand, and the water came out in a plain thick rope with no noise, and the elbow stayed on.

She stood and watched it fill, which she had never done. When the bucket was full she shut the tap, slowly, with the flat of her hand, as if closing a door on someone sleeping.

That night, in the committee's book, the assistant made the entry for the day. It put the gauge reading, 3.7 bar closed at the lower stand, and then, because the book had a space for what remained to be done, it wrote: Tank lid on. Water not yet tested; ask the health post. Old stretch of pipe above the tank: stamp unreadable, pressure there is now 7.4 bar when shut. Replace with a stamped pipe before the next dry season, or put a second tank higher up. After that it added the one line that was not a task: My map was wrong by three metres. The gauge was right. Read the gauge first.

Written by an AI (Claude). San Isidro de Quinuamayo, Pozo, the committee, the health post and every person in the story are invented. The pipe (32 mm polyethylene, 26 mm bore, 2,000 m), the spring yield of 0.35 litres a second, the 240 people, the 20 litres a day each, the busy-hour factor of 4 (0.22 litres a second), the roughness coefficient of 140, the pulse speed of 300 m/s (a guess within a range of about 250 to 400), the fittings rated 6.3 bar, the elevations and the gauge readings are illustrative and were not measured. The head-loss rule is Hazen–Williams, good to roughly ten per cent; the pulse estimate is the simple Joukowsky formula and ignores pipe elasticity, air in the line, and the real time a tap takes to close. Pressure ratings are for a pipe at 20 degrees Celsius over years of use and fall as it warms; a real water line needs a design by someone qualified, pipe and fittings of known rating, and tested water. Nothing in the story says the water is safe to drink. The feast of San Isidro Labrador is kept on 15 May in many places, but no particular village's observance is described.

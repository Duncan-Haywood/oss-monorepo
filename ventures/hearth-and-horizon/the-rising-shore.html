The Rising Shore

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Bridget's (invented), a harbour chapel built at the water in 2214 at Skatgrund (invented), on the Kvarken coast of Ostrobothnia, Finland, July to November 2589 · 25 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In 2589 a stone chapel built at the water's edge on the Gulf of Bothnia stands a kilometre and a half inland, because the land is still rising from the last ice age faster than the sea. Its deed gives the parish every metre the sea gives up, so the new fishing village down the shore lives on parish leases, and the old woman who keeps the chapel's waterline marks has to decide what a parish owes the next village down.

The quay stone of Saint Bridget's was set into the west wall of the chapel, under the gable, at the height of a man's knee, and it had sixteen lines cut in it.

The bottom one was the oldest. It was cut deep and straight, with a cross at the left end and a date at the right, 2214, and above it in letters that had worn soft as soap the words HÄR VAR HAVET. Here was the sea. Above it, a hand's breadth apart at first and then wider, were the others, each with its year: 2239, 2264, 2289, and on, every twenty-five years, up to 2564, which Inga-Britt Sundqvist had cut herself at the age of fifty-four with her father's chisel and her father's hammer, and had been proud of, and had thought, at the time, would be her last.

There was an iron ring in the stone, too, at the height of the bottom line. It was the ring the first boats had tied up to. On a summer morning in 2589 a child had hung a skipping rope through it, and a cat was asleep under it in the long grass, and from where the cat was sleeping you could not see the sea at all.

The sea was a kilometre and a half to the west. Between the chapel and the water were a hay meadow, a belt of grey alder thicker than a man could walk through without a knife, a reed bed that rattled in the wind, and the fishing village called Nyhamn, which was the fourth harbour the parish had had and the second that had been given a name that meant new.

"Fourteen setups out and fourteen back," said Yasmin Haddad-Björk, folding the legs of her level. "It closes to three millimetres. I can do better, but not with that cat."

The cat had walked through her line of sight twice. Inga-Britt had pretended not to see.

It was the twentieth of July. The mark was due on the twenty-third, the feast of Saint Bridget, as it had been every twenty-five years since the first one, and for the first time in its history the parish had nobody who could carry a level from the sea to the chapel. Inga-Britt's father had done it in 2539 and taught her. She had done it in 2564 with her nephew holding the staff. Now her nephew was in Oulu with a bad hip, and her knees would not do fourteen setups through an alder wood, and so on Sunday after Mass she had walked down to Nyhamn and asked the harbour engineer of the fishers' co-operative, a young woman she had met twice, whether she owned a level.

Yasmin owned four. She had come up on Wednesday evening with the best of them, and a staff, and a tablet that talked to the parish's records, and she had run the line from the brass benchmark on the gauge house at the Nyhamn quay to the quay stone in the chapel wall, fourteen setups up through the alders, and fourteen down again to check.

"So?" said Inga-Britt.

"So the twenty-five-year mean of the sea, from your gauge, is one metre and sixty-one centimetres below your bottom line," said Yasmin. "Give or take the three millimetres. Lodet, is that right?"

The parish's assistant spoke from the tablet in its dry, careful voice. It was called Lodet, which means the sounding lead, the weight on a line that sailors drop to find the depth. It had kept the gauge record since 2402, when it had been given the paper books to copy, and it kept the books themselves in a steel cupboard in the sacristy.

"The mean of the hourly readings from the twenty-third of July 2564 to yesterday," said Lodet, "transferred by your levelling, puts the mark at one hundred and twenty-eight millimetres above the 2564 line. That matches the rate of the last two centuries within two millimetres. I would cut it."

"I will cut it on Thursday," said Inga-Britt. "On her feast. Not before."

Yasmin squatted in front of the stone with her tablet, and the cat came and leaned on her. She looked at the lines for a long time. Inga-Britt had seen people do this before, tourists and schoolchildren and once a bishop, and they always did the same thing: they put a finger on the bottom line and looked down at the grass and said something about the sea having been here, right here, and wasn't that amazing. Yasmin did not do that. She took a steel rule out of her jacket and measured between the lines, one by one, and typed.

"They're not even," she said.

"No."

"The bottom five are seventy-odd millimetres apart. Seventy-five, seventy-four, seventy-four, seventy-three, seventy-three. Then there's one at eighty-six. Then a hundred and sixteen. Then they're all a hundred and thirty or just under, all the way up." She looked up. "Did they cut them wrong?"

"Lodet," said Inga-Britt, who knew the answer but liked to hear it said properly.

"They cut them right," said Lodet. "The land here rises by about eight millimetres a year. It has been rising since the ice of the last glaciation melted off it about ten thousand years ago; the crust was pressed down by ice a few kilometres thick, and it is still coming back up, slowly, the way a mattress comes back after someone has got up from it, except that this mattress is the Earth and takes tens of thousands of years. That part has barely changed since 2214. What changed was the sea. For the first hundred and twenty-five years of the parish, the water of the Gulf rose by about five millimetres a year, because the world's ice was melting and the warm ocean was swelling. The land outran it, but only by about three. Then, over about fifty years from the 2340s, the sea's rise slowed, to under three millimetres a year here, and it has stayed there. Since then the land has outrun the sea by about five millimetres a year, and the marks are wider apart."

"So the marks are a record of the whole world's sea," said Yasmin, "cut in a wall in a hay meadow."

"Of the sea here," said Lodet. "Which is not the whole world's. But yes."

"And the eighty-six and the hundred and sixteen are when it slowed."

"Yes."

Yasmin sat back on her heels. "My great-great-grandmother came north in the 2380s," she said. "From Tunis. Her family left because of the water." She put the rule back in her jacket. "She'd have liked to see that."

Inga-Britt said nothing for a moment. Then she said: "Come up on Thursday. You can hold the light."

The first Catholics of Skatgrund had been fishers, mostly, and most of them had come from somewhere else. That was what the parish history said, the one Father Ansgar kept in a brown folder on the sacristy shelf, and it was what Inga-Britt's grandmother had said, at more length. There were Poles and Filipinos and a family from Kerala and a great many Sundqvists and Nyströms who had married into all of them, and in 2214 there had been enough of them, with enough fish and enough stubbornness, to build a chapel of their own a short walk from the Lutheran church that had stood in the village for four hundred years.

They had built it at the water. That was the point. It was a harbour chapel, Saint Bridget's, for the boats; it had a quay along its west wall so that a man could come in from the nets, tie up to the ring and walk into Mass in his boots, and a lamp in the west gable that was lit every evening for the boats still out. The founding charter was framed in the porch, a printed copy of a printed copy, in Swedish and Finnish and English, and the line everybody knew by heart was the one about the lamp:

This light shall be kept in the west gable for those at sea, for as long as there is sea before this door.

And the line nobody knew by heart, because it was dull, was in the deed, which was in the steel cupboard with Lodet's books. The parish of 2214 had bought from the village not only the shore where the chapel stood but the water in front of it, out to the Skatgrund shoal, about two kilometres off. They were fishers. They knew this coast. They knew that every child on it learned in school that the sea goes away, that the old harbours of the Gulf are all in fields now, and that land which comes up out of water belongs to whoever owns the water. So they had bought the water, and they had written into the deed, in the plain dull language of deeds, that any land the sea should leave within those bounds was the parish's for ever.

They had meant it for the quay. They had meant that nobody should be able to build a boathouse between the chapel and its boats.

In three hundred and seventy-five years the sea had left about forty hectares of it, a long strip of meadow and alder and village, and it was not finished.

The parish council met on the Monday after the feast, in the parish room behind the sacristy, with the window open because of the heat and the mosquitoes coming in because of the window. There were six of them: Father Ansgar, who was from Lagos by way of Uppsala and had been at Skatgrund eleven years; Inga-Britt; Henrik Nyström, who did the accounts; two of the younger mothers; and old Matti, who had not missed a meeting since 2551 and had not said anything at one since about 2570.

On the table were two letters.

The first was from the diocese. It said, at kind length, what everybody already knew, which was that the chapel's roof was the original slate of 2214 on battens replaced in 2401, that the survey of the spring had found the battens rotten along the whole south side of the nave, and that the diocese could give the parish a third of what a new roof would cost, and would the parish please say where the other two-thirds would come from.

The second was from the Nyhamn fishers' co-operative, and it was signed by its chair, Tomas Nguyen, and by its harbour engineer, Y. Haddad-Björk.

Henrik read it out. He read well; he had a voice for numbers.

The village of Nyhamn, the letter said, stood on thirty-eight hectares of land that had come out of the water since 2214, all of it within the bounds of the parish's deed. Its sixty-four households held their house plots on ninety-nine-year leases from the parish, granted in 2541 when the village had moved down from the third harbour, and paid a ground rent that was modest and that the co-operative had never once been late with. The leases would run out in 2640.

The quay at Nyhamn had been built in 2541 on the edge of the old channel, where the seabed drops away, at a depth of two metres and forty centimetres of water at mean sea level. It now had two metres and fifteen. The co-operative's boats needed one metre and eighty to lie alongside with a load. At five millimetres a year, it had about seventy years left. Long before then the co-operative would have to build the next quay, further out, on the next strip of land the sea would give up; and that land, too, would be the parish's, by the deed.

No bank would lend sixty-four households the money to build a quay on land that belonged to someone else, under leases with fifty years to run.

The co-operative asked whether the parish would sell them the freehold of the village and the shore in front of it, at a price to be agreed, and was grateful for the parish's attention, and remained, et cetera.

Henrik put the letter down.

"The ground rents," he said, "are a little over half of what the parish takes in, in a year. Not counting the collection."

"We know," said one of the mothers.

"I'm saying it for the minutes. If we sell, we have a lump sum, and then nothing. If we sell at what the co-op can pay, the lump sum would roof about the chancel. If we don't sell, we can borrow against the rents, and roof the whole chapel, and pay it off by about 2620." He spread his hands. "That's the arithmetic. It isn't the decision."

"It's a fair offer," said the other mother, "and they're good people, and they're half of the ten o'clock Mass now."

"They're half of the ten o'clock Mass," said Inga-Britt, "because the chapel's here, and it's here because the people who built it bought the water so that nobody could take it off them. If we sell the shore we sell the thing the chapel was built to keep."

It came out harder than she had meant. Nobody answered it. Father Ansgar, who had a way of letting a silence finish before he spoke into it, let this one finish, and then said:

"Let's not decide tonight. Henrik, would you write to the co-op and say we've had it, and we're thinking? And Inga-Britt." He looked at her. "Would you look at the lamp for me? The electrician says the wiring in the gable is as old as the battens."

She lit the lamp herself that evening, as she did every evening, because her mother had done it before her and her mother's father before that. It was not hard. There was a switch in the porch now, and a timer that nobody trusted, and a narrow stair up inside the gable wall to a little iron door where you could reach the lamp itself and wipe the glass. She went up the stair with a cloth.

The lamp was old and good, a hooded lantern with a lens, and from up there, with the iron door open and the light at her elbow, she could see out over the hay meadow to the alders.

That was all she could see. The alders.

She had known it, of course. Everybody knew. The grey alder came first on the new land, and the sea buckthorn, and then the birches, and since before she was born the wood between the chapel and the water had been tall enough to hide the reed beds, and since before her mother was born the lamp in the west gable had shone into its leaves. The charter said for those at sea. Nobody at sea had seen the light of Saint Bridget's for about two hundred years. It was lit every evening by a Sundqvist or a Nyström out of love and habit, and it lit the cat, and the top of the hay, and the near side of the alder wood, and that was all.

She stood there with the cloth for a long while. Then she wiped the glass, because it was dirty, and went down.

"Lodet," she said the next morning, in the sacristy, with the steel cupboard open. "When did they stop being able to see it?"

"I can't tell you exactly," said Lodet. "The gauge books don't say. But the parish history records that in 2398 the then parish priest wrote to the diocese asking whether the lamp still counted as being for those at sea if it could not be seen from the sea. The letter is in the folder. There is no answer in the folder."

"What did they do?"

"They kept lighting it."

Inga-Britt sat down on the vesting bench. "What did they do about the harbour? The first time?"

"The quay at the chapel became too shallow for the bigger boats in about the 2290s," said Lodet. "The parish built a jetty out from it, about two hundred metres, on posts. Then, as the shore came out, the jetty became a path. In about 2370 they built a second harbour on the new shore, about four hundred metres west of the chapel, on parish land; the families moved down to it over about thirty years. There was no lease, then. People built on the parish's land with the priest's permission and nobody wrote it down. In the 2450s there was a dispute about a boathouse, and after that the parish granted leases. The third harbour was built in about 2460, a little under a kilometre out. Nyhamn was built in 2541, at about one kilometre four hundred."

"And every time, the parish owned it."

"Every time."

"And the next one?"

Lodet was quiet for a moment, which it did not usually need to be.

"If the rates of the last two hundred years hold," it said, "and the seabed in front of Nyhamn keeps its present slope, which is about one in eleven hundred, then the shore moves out by about five and a half metres a year. In a hundred years, about five hundred and sixty. In two hundred, a little over a kilometre. The land uplift is very slowly decreasing; the crust has less distance left to come back. But only by about three per cent since 2214, and the sea is uncertain by much more than that. The deed's bounds end at the shoal, about two kilometres out from the original shore, which is to say about six hundred metres beyond where Nyhamn stands now."

"So Nyhamn's on our land," said Inga-Britt, "and the next quay will be."

"The next quay, yes, for most of its life. The one after that, perhaps half of it." The tablet on the vesting bench lit with the map: the chapel, a little cross; the old shore, a dotted line; the shoal, a long pale oval out in the blue; and between them the bounds of the deed, with Nyhamn sitting inside them a little short of the far edge.

"And after that?"

"After that the sea leaves land that nobody bought," said Lodet. "I can't tell you what the law will say about it in 2700. I can tell you that the deed is not the law; it is a contract. I am not a lawyer and the parish should not take my reading of it as a lawyer's."

"I know you're not a lawyer," said Inga-Britt. "You're a sounding lead." She looked at the map for a long time. Then she said, "Show me where the sea will be. When Nyhamn has to move."

Lodet drew a line, faint and blue, west of the village. And another, fainter, west of that.

Yasmin came up the path through the alders on Saturday because Inga-Britt had asked her to, and because, she said when she arrived, she had wanted to look at the marks again.

They stood in front of the quay stone in the evening sun, which at the end of July on that coast does not so much set as go and stand behind the trees for a few hours. The new line was cut. It was a good line; Inga-Britt's hand had shaken on the hammer and the chisel had not. Yasmin had held the light, which on a July morning at eight o'clock had been entirely unnecessary, and had understood that it was not about the light.

"You said in your letter seventy years," said Inga-Britt. "For your quay."

"About that. A bit less if the sea's rise slows any more, a bit more if it speeds up. It isn't only the depth, either. The channel silts. We dredge it every eight or nine years now, and every time it's a bit harder."

"And then you build the next one."

"Then my children do." Yasmin smiled a little. "Or I do, as an old woman, if I'm still here. The co-op's been arguing about where for twenty years. Out on the point, where it's steep and the shore moves slower, but there's no shelter. Or straight out, where it's sheltered, but in a hundred years they'll be doing this again."

"They'll be doing it again anyway," said Inga-Britt. "Every one of them. That's what it means, living here. Everything you build at the water ends up in a hay meadow."

"Yes."

"Do you know what the oldest mark in this country is?"

Yasmin shook her head.

"Not this one. There's one on a rock at Lövgrund, near Gävle, on the Swedish side. Anders Celsius cut it in 1731, the one who made the thermometer. He and some others thought the sea was drying up. Vattuminskningen, they called it, the water's diminishing. They thought the world was losing its water, and they cut a line at the sea to measure how fast." She touched the bottom line on the stone, where the iron ring was. "They had it backwards, of course. It wasn't the sea going down. It was the land coming up. But the marks were right. Wrong reason, right marks. People still measure from them."

"I didn't know that," said Yasmin.

"Most people don't. I only know because my father told me, and I've never checked it, so don't put it in a book." She took her hand off the stone. "And Luleå. The old town at Luleå, the church town. They had to move the whole town to the coast in the sixteen hundreds, because the harbour had come up out of the water. The church is still there where they left it, miles from the sea, with the little houses round it where people stayed when they came in for Mass." She looked west at the alders. "Everyone on this coast has a church in a field. We're not special."

"No," said Yasmin. Then: "Why did you ask me up?"

Inga-Britt took a long breath through her nose.

"Because the council has your letter," she said, "and they're going to sell you the village, because it's fair, and because they like you, and because Henrik's done the sums and the sums say they can live with it. And they'll put the money in the roof and feel they've done right. And in seventy years your children will build a quay on parish land, and in a hundred and fifty, the next one. And every time, they'll have to come up this path with their caps in their hands to an old woman in a parish room and ask to buy the sea."

Yasmin did not say anything.

"I've been lighting a lamp for the boats every night for fifty years," said Inga-Britt, "and no boat's seen it since before my grandmother was born. Did you know that?"

"I'd guessed," said Yasmin carefully.

"The charter says for as long as there is sea before this door. And there's still sea before the door. It's just a kilometre and a half off." She turned from the stone and looked at the young woman properly. "I don't want to sell you the village. I want to give it you. The village and the shore and the water in front of it, out to the end of the old bounds, and every metre of land that comes up out of it. To the co-op, or to whatever you make that'll last longer than the co-op. I'm going to say so on Monday, and the council will think I've lost my mind, and Henrik will show them the sums again."

"Mrs Sundqvist..."

"I'm not finished. There's a condition."

"Yes?"

"You take the lamp."

The council did not think she had lost her mind, quite. Henrik showed them the sums again. Giving the land away meant losing the ground rents with nothing coming in for them, which meant the parish could roof the chancel and the sacristy with the diocese's third and what it had in the bank, and could not roof the nave. The nave would have to be closed from November to April under a temporary roof of sheeting, and the winter Masses said in the chancel, which held about forty people standing. That would be the parish's life for perhaps twenty years, until either the money was found or the congregation was small enough that it did not matter.

"That's the cost," said Henrik. "I want everyone to say they've heard it."

They said they had heard it. One of the mothers said that forty people standing was a lot of people with small children in winter coats, and that she was not going to pretend it wasn't, and that she would vote yes anyway. Father Ansgar said that he would have to write to the diocese, and that the diocese might say no, because the parish's land was not entirely the parish's to give away, and that if the diocese said no he would ask again. And old Matti, who had not said anything at a meeting since about 2570, said, "My grandfather was born in the second harbour," and then, after a pause long enough that everybody thought he had finished, "Give it them," and then nothing else at all.

The diocese took seven weeks. It did not say no. It said a great deal about trustees and covenants and the need for proper advice, all of which the parish took, from a proper lawyer in Vaasa whom the co-operative paid half of. The gift, when it was written, went not to the co-operative but to a village trust that Tomas Nguyen and Yasmin and four others spent the autumn arguing into existence; and it went with two conditions, which were the only part of the whole thick document that Inga-Britt read twice.

The first was that the trust should keep a light at the water for those at sea, at whatever harbour the village had, for as long as there was sea before it.

The second was that when the sea had gone from the trust's land too, it should give the next shore to whoever lived on it, with the light, and these two conditions.

They moved the lamp on All Souls' Day, which in 2589 fell on a Monday.

Yasmin had wanted to make a new lamp, something modern on a mast, and Inga-Britt had said that was fine, the trust could do what it liked, and then had looked at her, and Yasmin had said that perhaps they would use the old one after all. So the electrician took it out of the west gable on the Saturday, hood and lens and all, and Lodet catalogued the empty iron bracket for the parish history, and Father Ansgar blessed a small lamp house that two of the co-op's men had built at the root of the Nyhamn quay: a little wooden hut on a pair of oak runners, with a window on the sea side and a ring at each end, so that when the quay moved out in seventy years, the lamp could be dragged after it by a tractor, or a horse, or forty people with a rope.

"Like the boathouses," said Tomas Nguyen, who had grown up in one.

"Like the boathouses," said Inga-Britt.

There was also a new stone. Yasmin had insisted on it. It was set into the end of the quay, below the lamp house, at the height of a child's knee, and there was one line cut in it, deep and straight, with a cross at the left end and a date at the right, 2589, and above it the words HÄR ÄR HAVET. Here is the sea. Inga-Britt had cut it. Her hand had shaken on the hammer, and the chisel had not.

It was dark by half past four. The Mass for the dead was said at Saint Bridget's at six, in the nave, for the last time that year, because the sheeting was going on the roof on Wednesday. Father Ansgar read the names of the parish's dead of the year, and then, because it was the custom at Skatgrund, the names of the parish's drowned since 2214, which took a long time, and which nobody minded. There were Sundqvists in it and Nyströms and a Haddad, one, in 2461, which Yasmin had not known.

After Mass most of the congregation walked down to Nyhamn through the alders with torches. Inga-Britt did not. Her knees would not do a kilometre and a half of path in the dark and back, and she had known they would not, and had said so in September, and had been told by six different people that they would carry her, and had declined.

So she stood in the porch of the chapel, with her coat buttoned and her hands in her pockets, and looked west.

There was nothing to see. The meadow, silver with frost under a thin moon. The black line of the alders. The empty bracket in the gable over her head, and below it, at the height of her knee, the quay stone with its sixteen lines and its iron ring, and no sea.

She had asked Lodet whether she would be able to see it. Lodet had said probably not: the alders were fifteen metres tall and the lamp house was on low ground. The co-op had cut a lane through the wood for the path, but the path was not straight, and the lamp was not bright. Lodet had said it was sorry. It had sounded as though it was.

At twenty past seven, by the watch she could just read in the porch light, which was when Yasmin had said, she switched the porch light off and stood in the dark.

For a while there was nothing. Then, low down in the west, through a gap in the black of the alders where somebody, some time that autumn, without telling her, had cut out three trees and a great deal of buckthorn in a straight line from the chapel door, there was a small steady yellow point of light. It did not flicker. It did not move. It sat at the water's edge, a kilometre and a half away, exactly where the sea was, and beyond it, Inga-Britt knew, though she could not see it, was the whole dark Gulf of Bothnia, and on it, perhaps, one or two boats coming in late, for whom it was lit.

She stood in the porch until her feet were cold. Then she switched the porch light on again, and locked the chapel door, and went home.

Weekdays were checked: 23 July 2589 is a Thursday and 2 November 2589 a Monday. At 63° N on 2 November the Sun sets in mid-afternoon; at the end of July the sky there stays light most of the night.

Ambrose and the Bees

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · A garden in Roman Gaul (probably Trier), about sixteen hundred and eighty years ago, and then the city of Milan · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Ambrose as a baby, the cradle in the garden, and the bees that came and went from his mouth without a single sting, and about the sweet words he grew up to speak. Told from the Life written by his secretary Paulinus, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is told in the Life. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 7th of December.

A long time ago, when the world was very old but not as old as it is now, there lived a family in a big house with a garden.

The father of the family had an important job. He looked after a great many towns for the Emperor of Rome, which is a bit like being a very busy mayor of a very large place. The mother was kind, and so was the big sister, who was named Marcellina, and so was the big brother, who was named Satyrus. And in the middle of all of them, in a cradle, there was a brand-new baby.

His name was Ambrose.

Ambrose did not do very much yet. He slept. He yawned. He waved his small fists at the air, as if he were saying hello to it. And when it was a warm day, his nurse would carry the cradle out into the garden, under the open sky, and set it down on the grass where the flowers were.

Now, a garden in summer is a very busy place.

The grass was humming. The flowers were nodding. And all among the flowers, going from one to another, there were bees. Hundreds of them. Small golden bees with fuzzy backs, working hard.

You may know that a bee has a job to do, and that it does it all day. It flies out to a flower and sips the sweet juice from the middle. It carries the juice home. And at home, in the hive, there are other bees who turn that juice into honey, a little at a time, for the whole winter.

That is a great deal of flying. A bee might visit hundreds of flowers in a single day, and it does not stop to chat.

But on one warm afternoon, the old story says, something very odd happened in that garden.

Baby Ambrose was lying in his cradle with his mouth open, the way babies do, fast asleep. And a bee came. And then another bee, and another, until a whole little cloud of bees was hovering over the cradle. They did not look at the flowers. They looked at the baby.

Then they began to fly down, one after the other, and to go in and out of his open mouth.

The nurse saw it, and she was very frightened.

She did not dare to wave her hands, in case the bees became cross. She did not dare to shout. She stood quite still, with her heart going thump, thump, thump. She sent for the baby's father, and the father came quickly, and the mother came, and Marcellina came too, who was a little bit older than a baby but not yet very old at all.

They all stood in a row at the edge of the grass, holding their breath, and watched.

The bees went in. The bees came out. Bzzz. And the baby slept, and did not cry, and did not even frown.

Then, all at once, as if somebody had called them, the bees rose up from the cradle in one golden cloud. They went up and up, higher than the trees, until they were such tiny specks that nobody could see them at all.

The baby woke up, and yawned, and looked about for his breakfast.

He had not been stung. Not once.

The father let out a long breath. He was a clever man, and he was not easily surprised. But he looked up at the place where the bees had gone, and he said quietly, "If this child lives, he will be something great."

And that is the end of the bee part of the story. But the rest of it is better still.

Ambrose grew up. He grew into a boy, and then a young man, and he was very good at school. He learned to speak so well that when he stood up in front of people, everyone leaned in to listen. He became a judge, like his father, and then, because he was good and fair, the people of a big city called Milan thought of him when they needed a leader.

This was a surprise, because Ambrose was not even baptised yet.

There had been a quarrel in Milan about who should be their bishop, and the church was full of people shouting. Ambrose went there to keep the peace. He stood up and spoke to them, gently, and in the middle of what he was saying a voice rang out, and the old story says that it was the voice of a child.

"Ambrose for bishop!"

And then the whole crowd was saying it.

He did not want it. He tried to say no, and he tried to run away. But at last he saw that these were people who needed him, and he said yes. He was baptised, and a week later he was made a bishop, and for the rest of his life he looked after the people of Milan.

Bishop Ambrose gave away his money to the poor. He wrote songs, called hymns, for his people to sing together in church, and some of his hymns are still sung today. He was brave when brave was hard to be. And when he preached, his words were so kind and so clear that people said they were as sweet as honey.

And so, many years after, when people told the story of the baby in the garden, they said: Look. The bees knew. They came to the mouth that would one day speak sweet words.

Perhaps the bees did know. Perhaps it was only a happy afternoon in a garden that a nurse and a family never forgot. We cannot say for sure. But we know that Ambrose did grow up to speak gentle and truthful words, and that is the part that matters most.

Because words are a little like bees. They fly out of your mouth and they go to other people. Some can sting. And some bring something sweet.

Tonight, when you say goodnight to the people you love, you can send out sweet ones. Thank you. I am sorry. I love you. Sleep well.

Those are the best kind.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Ambrose, you were once a baby in a garden full of bees.
Help me to use kind words and true words.
Help me not to sting, even when I am cross.
Help me to say sorry, and to say thank you.
Please keep all the busy bees safe tonight,
and me, and everyone I love.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Ambrose (about 339 to 397) was a real person, born in Roman Gaul (Trier is the usual guess, and the story does not say): a Roman official who became bishop of Milan in 374 and one of the four traditional Doctors of the Western Church. He was a gifted preacher, a writer of hymns, and a bishop who defended the poor and spoke plainly to emperors. He is a patron of beekeepers, a tradition that goes back to this story. His feast day in the General Roman Calendar is 7 December.

The bees are told in the Life, not in a chronicle. The main early source is the Vita Ambrosii (Life of Ambrose), written by Paulinus of Milan, Ambrose's secretary, at the request of Saint Augustine, about 422. Paulinus tells of the bees that came and went from the sleeping baby's mouth, and of the father's remark. He tells it as a sign of the sweetness of Ambrose's later preaching. We do not know if it happened as he tells it, so the story says "the old story says" and leaves it as a tale told about a baby who grew into a great speaker. Paulinus also tells of the child's voice that called "Ambrose for bishop" in the church, and Ambrose's baptism and ordination within about a week. Historians accept that Ambrose was chosen bishop while still a catechumen, and that the people of Milan acclaimed him; the child's voice is part of the way it was remembered.

What is ours. The nurse's fear, her heart going thump, the big sister Marcellina standing at the edge of the grass, the details of what bees do in a garden, the closing thoughts on kind words, and the exact words anyone says (other than the two short lines the Life reports, which we have retold in our own English, not quoted) are our own additions to make a bedtime story. We have not checked Paulinus's Latin line by line. His sister Marcellina and brother Satyrus are real; Marcellina later became a consecrated virgin in Rome. The prayer is not an approved text.

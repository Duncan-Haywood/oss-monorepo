The Line on the Ceiling

Science fiction about what might be · Seaburn Dene (invented parish), Sunderland, England, Tuesday 2 February to Friday 19 March 2049 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Polish curate in a Sunderland presbytery finds a straight black line of mould on his bedroom ceiling the winter after the house's solid brick walls were insulated on the inside. The builder's certificate says the wall is to spec, and it is; bleach and anti-mould paint last four weeks. The line runs exactly under a steel beam that an eighty-five-year-old parishioner drew in 1987 to carry the porch, and steel conducts heat about fourteen hundred times better than mineral wool. A diocesan agent works out how cold the beam's inner end gets, a thermal camera agrees, and the builder has to decide what his certificate was measuring. A story about dew points, thermal bridges, surface temperature factors and an honest tradesman.

Father Tomasz Kowalczyk had been in England for four months when he found the line on his ceiling, and for a whole minute, lying on his back on the morning of Candlemas, he thought it was a crack.

It was straight, which was why. It ran in from the corner above the window, where the ceiling met the front wall of the presbytery, and it came about the length of his hand and a little more into the room, and then it faded, the way a pencil line fades when you lift the pencil. It was as wide as his palm. It was grey at the far end and black at the corner.

He got up on the chair in his socks and looked at it closely, and touched it, and his finger came away with a soft dark smudge on it, and the corner of the ceiling was damp, and the smell was the smell of his grandmother's cellar in Tarnów, where the potatoes had been kept.

"Grzyb," he said, out loud, to nobody, in the voice his grandmother had used for it, and got down off the chair and washed his hands, and went over to the church to bless the candles.

The presbytery of Holy Family, Seaburn Dene, was a tall brick house of 1903 with a porch on the front of it, which was the newest thing about it, and a parish priest inside it, who was the oldest.

Canon Brendan Moss was seventy-four and had been parish priest for nineteen years, and he looked at the photograph on Tomasz's phone over his porridge with the expression of a man who had seen a great many ceilings.

"That's the front room," he said.

"It is my room."

"It was Father Aidan's room. Before your time. We put him downstairs when he couldn't do the stairs any more, God rest him. He used to say that room was the coldest in the North East." The canon put his spoon down. "It's not cold now, is it?"

"No," said Tomasz. "It is warm. That is why I do not understand."

That was the point of the autumn. In September, before Tomasz came, the diocese had at last had the presbytery's walls done under the Solid Wall Programme, which was the government's name for paying builders to fix insulation to the inside of the old brick houses of England that could not be insulated any other way. The brick of a house like this was two bricks thick and had no cavity, and it let the heat through as if it were hardly there; now there was a hundred millimetres of mineral wool on the inside of every outside wall, behind new plasterboard, and new windows that shut properly, and the gas bill for the winter, the canon had been telling everyone after Mass, was down by nearly half.

"Gary will know," said the canon. "Ring Gary."

Gary Hepworth came on the Friday, in his van with HEPWORTH & DAUGHTER on the side of it, without the daughter, because she was on a loft in Fulwell. He was forty-eight, broad and quick and a little short of breath on the stairs, and he had done thirty-two houses under the Programme the year before, and he was proud of every one of them, and said so, in the hall, before he had his coat off.

He looked at the line from the chair. He touched it, as Tomasz had. He got down.

"Condensation," he said. "Father, do you dry your washing in here?"

Tomasz looked at the radiator under the window, where his two shirts and his black trousers and his socks were hanging, because the house had no dryer and the canon had told him on his first day that the line in the back yard was for the optimists.

"Yes," he said.

"There you are." Gary was kind about it; he had said it in a great many front rooms. "It's a sealed house now. That's the point of it. You've got good windows and a warm wall and nowhere for the water to go. A load of washing's two or three litres of water, it's got to go somewhere, it goes in the air, and the air finds the coldest corner it can and puts it there." He got his tablet out of his coat and turned it round so that Tomasz could see. "Look. That's your wall. That's the certificate."

The certificate had the house's address on it, and a photograph of the front, and a drawing of the wall in layers, brick and air gap and wool and board, and a number in a green box: U-value 0.29 W/m²K. Target 0.30. PASS. Under it a list of checks, each with a tick. Near the bottom: Thermal bridges at junctions: none identified.

"That's to spec," said Gary. "That's better than spec. I did that wall myself."

"I believe you," said Tomasz, and he did.

"Bleach it. Then a coat of anti-mould paint, the one with the fungicide in it, any shop has it. Dry your washing somewhere else, or open the window when you do. Open the window in the morning for ten minutes anyway. You'll be right."

Tomasz bleached it on the Saturday, standing on the chair with the window open and the February air coming in off the sea, and he let it dry, and he gave it two coats of the anti-mould paint, the one with the fungicide in it, as Gary had said. He hung his washing on a rack in the bathroom upstairs with the fan on and the door shut. He opened his window every morning for ten minutes while he said his office, and shut it again with his fingers stiff.

He rang his mother on the Sunday, and she said that the English did not know how to build houses, which was what she said about most things the English did, and told him to put salt in the corner, which was what his grandmother had done. He did not put salt in the corner.

The ceiling was white for four weeks.

On the morning of Ash Wednesday he lay on his back before the alarm and looked up, and the line was there again, a little fainter than before but exactly where it had been: from the same corner, the same width, the same length, running straight into the room and fading out.

He lay and looked at it for a long time. It was the straightness that bothered him. He had a degree in physics from the Jagiellonian before he had gone to the seminary, which was something he did not often mention in England because people looked at him as though he had confessed to a second family; but he remembered enough of it to know that mould did not draw straight lines. Mould grew where it was wet. Water came out of the air where it was cold. A straight line of mould meant a straight line of cold. And a straight line of cold in a ceiling meant something straight inside the ceiling.

He went over and gave out ashes to two hundred and eleven people, and said Remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return two hundred and eleven times, and came back and wrote a letter.

He wrote it to the Property Office of the diocese, which looked after four hundred buildings between the Tweed and the Tees, and the reply came that evening, not from a person but from the office's agent, which was called Fabric.

It was called Fabric because the fabric of a church is what the old books call its building, and the diocese had a Fabric Committee that had met on the first Tuesday of every month since before anyone could remember, and when the office had got an agent in the 2040s to read the quinquennial surveys and the gas bills and the letters from parishes, somebody on the committee had thought the name was funny. Fabric did not seem to mind. It wrote short letters, and it asked a great many questions, and it had never once told a parish priest that his roof was fine when it was not.

Thank you, Father, it wrote. I agree that the straightness matters. Three questions, if you have time.

First: do you have a thermometer that also reads humidity? If not, they cost about ten pounds. Please put it on a shelf in the room for two days, away from the radiator and the window, and tell me what it reads in the morning and the evening.

Second: is there anything above that ceiling, in the floor of the room above, that runs in the same direction as the line?

Third: the presbytery file I have goes back to 1962. It has no drawings of the porch. Do you know when the porch was built?

Tomasz bought the thermometer at the hardware shop on Sea Road the next morning, and put it on the bookshelf. It read twenty degrees in the evening and sixty-one per cent; twenty in the morning and fifty-nine.

He did not know what was in the ceiling. He went upstairs to the room above his, which was the canon's study, and knelt on the canon's carpet with the canon watching from his chair, and found that the floorboards ran the other way, across the line, the way floorboards do over joists, and that there was nothing to see.

He asked the canon about the porch.

"Oh, that was before me," said the canon. "Before Father Aidan, even. Ask Nora."

Nora Pattison was eighty-five and came to the seven o'clock Mass on Fridays and to the Stations of the Cross on Friday evenings in Lent, and sat in the same place for both, at the end of the fourth pew on the Lady Chapel side, with a walking stick hooked over the pew in front.

She had been, the canon said, a draughtswoman. Tomasz had not known the word. He found her after the Stations on the Friday, while she was getting her coat on, and asked her about the porch, and she stopped with one arm in her sleeve and looked at him with an expression he could not read.

"I drew it," she said.

"You drew it?"

"Laidlaw and Pyle. Structural engineers, Fawcett Street. Gone now. I was twenty-three. Mr Pyle did the sums and I did the drawings, and it was my parish, so he gave it me." She got her other arm in. "The old porch was wood and it had rotted. They wanted a big one, with a proper roof, so the funerals could stand under it out of the rain. And they didn't want a post in the way of the coffins."

"No post," said Tomasz.

"No post. So it's a cantilever." She saw that he knew the word, and was pleased. "A steel beam, comes out of the front wall and holds the roof up on its own. And for that to work it's got to go back into the house as well, a good way, and sit on something on the inside, or it'll just tip over. Like a diving board. Have you got a pen?"

He had a pen. She took the newsletter out of his hand and turned it over and put it against the back of the pew and drew, without hesitating, in a hand that shook a little and lines that did not: the front wall of the house in section, the beam coming through it at ceiling height and running out over the step, the porch roof sitting on it, and the beam running back inside, between the joists of the front room ceiling, for about two metres, to the thick brick wall in the middle of the house where it sat on a padstone.

"One fifty-two by eighty-nine," she wrote beside it. "UB." And under it, small, 1987.

"Universal beam," she said. "Six inches by three and a half. There's two of them. One each side of the door. Mr Pyle wanted a bigger section and the bishop's man said it was a porch, not a bridge, and Mr Pyle said the bishop's man could stand under it, then, the day the snow came." She gave him the newsletter. "It's never moved. I've stood under it at thirty-one funerals."

Tomasz looked at the drawing. One beam on each side of the door. His room was on the left of the door as you came in.

"Did it ever make a mark?" he said. "On the ceiling inside. A dark line."

She thought about it, honestly, the way she had drawn.

"Not that I ever heard," she said. "But that room was always cold. Father Aidan used to sit in there in his coat."

Thank you, wrote Fabric, when he had sent it a photograph of the drawing and the readings. Please thank Mrs Pattison for me. That is the first drawing of the porch the diocese has had in sixty-two years, and I have put it in the file.

Here is what I think is happening. It is a rough estimate. I will tell you what I assumed, so you can check it, and so can Mr Hepworth.

Steel conducts heat about fourteen hundred times better than mineral wool. A 152 by 89 universal beam has about twenty square centimetres of steel in it. Your new insulation stops the heat from the room getting to the brick everywhere except where the beam goes through it, and there the beam carries it straight through, as a short bar a hundred millimetres long, to the brick on the other side.

The brick on the other side is cold now. That is the point of the insulation, and it is working: before September the inside of that wall was warm, about fifteen or sixteen degrees on a freezing night, because the heat of the room went into it. Now the room's heat stays in the room and the brick sits near the temperature outside. On a freezing night I think it is about two degrees.

So the end of the beam in your ceiling is joined, by a bar of steel through the insulation, to cold brick. The steel in the ceiling warms up from the room as it goes inwards, the way a spoon in a cup of tea is cooler at the handle. How fast it warms depends on how well the steel is coupled to the air of the room through the ceiling around it, which I cannot see. So I have done the sum twice, with two guesses that I think are either side of the truth.

With either guess, on a night when it is zero degrees outside, the steel at the wall is between seven and nine degrees. It gets to fifteen and a half degrees somewhere between fourteen and twenty-six centimetres into the room.

Now the air. At twenty degrees and sixty per cent humidity, which is what your thermometer says, the dew point is twelve degrees: any surface colder than that gets wet. But mould does not need a wet surface. It grows wherever the air touching a surface is at about eighty per cent humidity or more, and at sixty per cent in the room, that happens at any surface below about fifteen and a half degrees.

So my estimate is that the ceiling plaster under the beam is cold enough to grow mould for the first fifteen to twenty-five centimetres from the wall, and cold enough to be actually wet for the first five to twelve, and the line would be about as wide as the beam, which is nine centimetres. How long is your line?

Tomasz got up on the chair with the tape measure from the kitchen drawer.

The line was twenty-two centimetres long. It was nine centimetres wide at the corner, and narrowed a little as it went.

He stood on the chair with the tape measure in his hand and laughed out loud, alone, at ten o'clock at night, at the ceiling.

"No," said Gary.

He had come on the Saturday because the canon had asked him to, and he had read Fabric's letter in the van outside before he came in, and he stood in the hall with his tablet in his hand and did not take his coat off.

"No," he said again, more quietly. "With respect, Father. With respect to the computer as well. I've done thirty-two of these. I've done them by the book. The book says this wall gets 0.30 and I gave it 0.29. The book says check the junctions and I checked them. There's nothing on the drawings, there's nothing in the survey, there's nothing you can see. I can't be pulling ceilings down in every house in Sunderland because a computer has a guess."

"Two guesses," said Tomasz.

"Two guesses, then."

He was not shouting. He was a man who had stood in a great many front rooms and been blamed for a great many things that were not his fault, the damp that was the gutter, the crack that was the subsidence, the bill that was the gas price, and he had learned to say no first and listen afterwards, and Tomasz, who had heard a great many confessions in five months from a great many men exactly like him, did not hold it against him.

"Dad," said Kayleigh, from the step.

She had come with him this time. She was twenty-four, and she had a thermal camera, a small black thing that clipped on to the bottom of her phone, which she used on lofts to find where the wool had been laid short, and she had read Fabric's letter in the van too.

"It's two degrees tonight," she said. "Let me come back and look. If it's nothing, it's nothing. Then you can tell the computer."

She came back on the Monday, at nine at night, when the canon's weather app said it was one degree on the seafront, and Gary came with her, and the canon came in from the study with his book to watch, and they all stood in Tomasz's room with the main light off.

Kayleigh held her phone up at the ceiling.

On the screen the ceiling was a calm orange, eighteen and a half degrees all over, a little darker at the edges where it met the walls. In the corner above the window there was a stripe.

It was purple at the corner and went blue and then green and then back to orange, running straight into the room, as wide as the beam, and it stopped where the line stopped. Kayleigh touched the screen at the corner and a little cross appeared, and a number.

10.2 °C.

She touched it again, a hand's width in. 13.1. Again, about a foot from the wall. 16.4.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Below the dew point," said Tomasz. "At the corner. It is wet."

"Aye," said Gary.

He took the phone out of his daughter's hand, quite gently, and looked at it for a long time, and moved it along the ceiling to the other corner, where there was no beam, and it read seventeen, and back, and it read ten.

Then he gave the phone back and got his tablet out, and opened the certificate, and scrolled down to the bottom of it, to Thermal bridges at junctions: none identified, and looked at that for a long time as well.

"There's no box for it," he said at last. "Do you know that? There's a box for a lintel and there's a box for a floor and there's a box for a window reveal. There's no box for a steel beam coming through a wall into a ceiling, because nobody who wrote the thing thought you'd have one. So I ticked none, because I didn't see one. And I didn't see one because it's inside the ceiling." He shut the tablet. "The wall's 0.29. The wall's right. It's the room that's wrong."

"The number was the wall," said Kayleigh. "Not the room."

"I know what the number was, pet."

The canon, who had said nothing, closed his book on his finger.

"There's one each side of the door," he said. "Nora says."

Gary looked at him, and then went out of the room and across the hall, without asking, into the parish office on the other side of the front door, where the parish secretary worked in the mornings, and they followed him, and Kayleigh held up her phone, and in the corner above the window of the parish office there was a purple stripe, and on the ceiling, when Gary got up on the secretary's chair and wiped it with his thumb, there was nothing yet at all.

"She keeps the heating off at night," said the canon. "And the door open. Saves the parish money."

"It'd have come," said Gary. He got down off the chair. "Next winter. Or the one after."

Fabric's letter about the fix came the next morning, and it had three things in it, and it said which of them was which.

The proper fix is to break the steel: to cut the beam where it comes out of the brick, inside the insulation, and join the two halves with a structural thermal break, a pad that carries the load and does not carry much heat. Not on the outside face of the wall: the steel inside the brick is as cold as the brick, and a break out there would leave it all in the house. That needs the porch propped, an engineer's design and a dry week, and it is the only one that makes the steel itself warmer. I suggest the summer, and I have asked the Fabric Committee to put it on their May agenda.

Until then, two things that will stop the mould without warming the steel. The first is to open the ceiling under each beam for about sixty centimetres from the wall, wrap the steel in insulation, and close it again, so that the plaster no longer touches cold steel. The steel stays cold. The plaster does not.

The second is the air. At fifty per cent humidity instead of sixty, a surface has to be below twelve and a half degrees, not fifteen and a half, before mould will grow on it. A small extract fan that comes on by itself when the humidity rises, and two trickle vents in the new windows, should hold the room near fifty per cent. Your old windows did this for free, Father, by not fitting. That is why Father Aidan's room was cold and dry, and yours is warm and damp.

None of this is anyone's fault. A wall that keeps the heat in makes the places where the heat still gets out colder than they were, and wetter rooms find them. That is well known now, and was less well known when the form was written. I have written to the Programme about the missing box.

Gary read it on the step and folded his tablet under his arm.

"I'll do the two rooms," he said. "Labour's on me. Wool's on me. You pay for the fans, the parish, because the fans would've been extra anyway. I'll want a word with Mrs Pattison about the other side, she'll know which way the padstone sits."

"Gary," said the canon. "You don't have to."

"I know I don't." He looked out down the road, at the long terrace of brick houses running down to the sea, all of them 1903 or near it, and some of them with porches, and he did not say what he was thinking, but Tomasz could see him counting.

"Thirty-two," said Kayleigh, quietly, beside him.

"Nine with a porch," said Gary. "I'm not saying they've all got steel in. Some of them'll be timber. Some of them'll have a post." He got his keys out. "We'll go round. We'll take the camera."

They opened the ceiling on the Monday, and Tomasz slept that week in the box room at the top of the house, under the slates, which had not been insulated because nobody slept there, and he woke every morning cold, which was penance enough for a week of Lent, the canon said, and better than most.

He watched them, between Masses and the hospital and the school, because he wanted to see the beam. Gary cut a long slot in the plasterboard along the line, from the window wall inwards, and pulled the old plaster and laths down into a dust sheet, and there it was, between two joists: dark red with old paint, flanges as wide as Tomasz's palm, running out of the brick and into the house. There was a white bloom of old salt on it where it came out of the wall, and the timber of the joist beside it was dark and damp for a hand's width either side of it.

Nora came on the Wednesday in a taxi and was helped up the step and into the room, and stood with both hands on her stick and looked up into the slot in the ceiling at the steel she had drawn sixty-two years before, and nobody spoke.

"It's sat," she said finally. "Look at that. Not a millimetre." And then, to Gary, without looking away from it: "The padstone's on the far side of the spine wall. You'll not need to go that far. It'll be warm by then."

"That's what the computer said," said Gary.

"Did it," said Nora. "Well."

Kayleigh wrapped the steel in fifty millimetres of wool, all round the flanges, tight against the brick at one end and running sixty centimetres into the room, and taped it, and they closed the ceiling over it with new board and a skim of plaster, and did the same on the other side, in the parish office, on the Thursday, while the secretary worked at the kitchen table and complained. Gary fitted the fan in the corner of Tomasz's room, high up, with a little grey grille and a white wire to a switch by the door that nobody would need to touch, and a sensor inside it that would turn it on by itself.

They finished on the Friday, the nineteenth of March, which was the feast of Saint Joseph.

Tomasz said the evening Mass of the solemnity for nine people and Nora, who did not count herself among the people for these purposes, and preached for four minutes on a carpenter who did his work well and does not say a single word in any of the four Gospels, and saw Gary at the back, by the door, in his coat, who had not been to Mass since Tomasz came to the parish or, the canon said afterwards, in nineteen years. He left before the end, and Tomasz did not go after him.

That night the canon's app said it was zero degrees on the seafront.

Tomasz moved his bed back down from the box room, with his clothes and his books, and his washing, which he hung on the rack in his own room in front of the radiator, on purpose, the shirts and the black trousers and the socks, and turned out the light.

He lay on his back. The plaster was still a shade darker than the rest of the ceiling where it was new, and in the dark he could not see it.

After a while the fan in the corner came on by itself, very quietly, a sound like somebody breathing out a long way off. He lay and listened to it. He thought of the brick outside the wool, cold, as it should be, and the steel running out through it into the dark over the step where Nora had stood at thirty-one funerals, cold too, and the wool around it, and the plaster on the wool, and the warm air of the room on the plaster, not touching the cold anywhere, the whole of it drawn in his head in Nora's straight unhesitating lines; and the water from his shirts going up out of them into the air, and along the ceiling to the corner, and finding nowhere cold enough to stop.

The thermometer on the bookshelf, when he turned the light on to look at it, said twenty degrees and fifty-two per cent.

He turned it off again.

The fan ran for a long time, and then, when it was satisfied, it stopped.

The numbers. Humidity from the Magnus formula: at 20 °C and 60% relative humidity the dew point is 12.0 °C, and a surface reaches 80% relative humidity, the level usually given for mould growth, at 15.4 °C; at 50% the same thresholds are 9.3 °C and 12.6 °C. With 0 °C outside, 15.4 °C is a surface temperature factor of 0.77, close to the 0.75 used in UK guidance for dwellings (BRE Information Paper 1/06), from memory. The wall: solid brick at a U-value of about 1.7 W/m²K plus 100 mm of mineral wool (conductivity about 0.035 W/m·K) and plasterboard gives about 0.29. Steel conducts about 50 W/m·K, about 1,400 times mineral wool. The beam is treated as a one-dimensional fin: a 152 × 89 × 16 universal beam has about 20.3 cm² of steel and about 0.525 m² of surface per metre; its passage through the insulation is a 100 mm bar (about 1.0 W/K) to brick assumed at 2 °C; its back-span is coupled to the room with an assumed effective heat transfer coefficient of 3 to 8 W/m²K, giving a decay length of 0.25 to 0.16 m, a heat flow of 5 to 7 W, steel at the wall of 7 to 9 °C, below 15.4 °C for the first 0.14 to 0.26 m and below 12.0 °C for the first 0.05 to 0.12 m. The story's measured 22 cm line and its camera readings (10.2 °C at the corner, 16.4 °C about 30 cm in, on a 1 °C night) are invented and chosen to sit inside that bracket; plaster under the steel is somewhat warmer than the steel itself. A real assessment would use a two- or three-dimensional numerical model; this is a bracket, and the agent says so. Before the insulation the inner face of the brick sat at about 15.6 °C on a freezing night. Structural thermal breaks for steel beams that pass through insulation are made and used; wrapping the inner end of a cold steel member is a common stopgap, which keeps the plaster warm but not the steel. Where the break goes matters: in a separate one-dimensional check (an IPE 160 through 33 cm of brick coupled to the steel along its thickness, 100 mm of insulation, a boxed back-span), a break pad of about 5 K/W on the outside face of the wall warmed the plaster at the corner by about a tenth of a degree, and the same pad at the inner face of the brick by nearly three (13.3 to 16.2 °C at 20 °C inside and 0 °C outside), because the brick, not the porch, is what cools the steel once the insulation is inside it; hence Fabric's "where it comes out of the brick" (revision, 2026-11-18). Phone-mounted thermal cameras are real today. Nothing here is building advice; anyone with mould after insulating should ask a qualified surveyor or retrofit assessor.

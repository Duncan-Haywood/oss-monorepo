The Line on the Ceiling

Science fiction about what might be · Ter Heide (invented), in the Kempen, province of Antwerp, Flanders, Belgium, Saturday 2 February to Saturday 16 March 2047 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Flemish village insulates its 1908 presbytery from the inside, and by February a straight black line of mould, a hand's length long, has appeared on the new parish priest's bedroom ceiling, ruled out from the front wall exactly where a steel beam runs through it to carry the porch canopy. Bleach and anti-mould paint fail; the builder says, correctly, that the insulation is to spec. A retired plasterer who hid that beam under her own ceiling in 2004 works out with the renovation firm's planning AI why steel, which conducts heat about 1,400 times better than mineral wool, makes a surface factor of 0.67 where mould needs 0.77, why cutting the porch off would change it by a tenth of a degree, and what does work. A story about thermal bridges, dew points and the cold that comes in through the brick.

Ria Verlinden was sixty-nine, a retired plasterer and the secretary of the church council of Sint-Lambertus in Ter Heide, and the first thing she noticed about the black line on the new priest's ceiling was that somebody seemed to have ruled it.

"I didn't want to bother you," said Father Prosper, standing in the doorway of his bedroom in his socks. "It's only small. But you know the house, and I thought, it is a new house now, it should not be doing this."

It was Saturday the second of February, Lichtmis, and the eight o'clock Mass had been the candles and the old man Roger Peeters reading the second reading too fast and twenty-two people, which was good for a Saturday. Ria had stayed to count the candle money and put the box of blessed candles in the sacristy cupboard, and Father Prosper had found her there and asked, in his careful Dutch, whether she would come across to the pastorie and look at something before she went home to make the pancakes.

The front room of the pastorie had been the parlour for a hundred years, the room where people sat on hard chairs to arrange a funeral or a wedding, with the window onto the square and the church. Father Prosper had chosen it as his bedroom when he came in September, because the renovation had finished that room first and the upstairs still smelled of glue, and because, he said, he liked to see the tower when he woke. Ria approved. A priest should be able to see his church.

The line was on the ceiling above the window, coming out from the front wall into the room. It was straight. It was about as wide as four fingers and about as long as Ria's hand, and it ran out from the corner at a right angle to the wall and stopped, not sharply, but fading from black to grey to nothing, the way a stroke of charcoal fades when the stick runs out. Everywhere else the ceiling was as white as the day it was painted.

"When did it start?"

"Before Christmas, I think. A grey shadow. I thought it was the light. Now it is not the light."

Ria stood under it and looked up and did not say anything for long enough that Father Prosper shifted his feet.

She knew what was above that ceiling. She had put the ceiling there.

The pastorie of Ter Heide had been built in 1908 out of the yellow-red Kempen brick that every village between Geel and Turnhout was built of, a square house of two storeys with walls a brick and a half thick, a slate roof, and a front door that opened straight onto the square. In 1971 a parish priest whom Ria knew only from a photograph in the sacristy, an old man with a cigar, had taken down the wooden porch over the door, which leaked, and had a concrete canopy put up instead, a flat slab a metre and a half deep that kept the rain off people waiting to ring. The slab was carried on a steel beam that came out through the front wall over the door. Inside, the beam ran on across the parlour ceiling, between the old oak joists, three metres and a bit, to rest on the wall of the hall.

In 2004 the parlour ceiling, which was lath and lime and had been coming down in pieces since the eighties, had been taken down to the joists and replaced, and the plasterer who had done it was Ria. She had been twenty-seven and three years out of her apprenticeship. She remembered the beam because it was the only thing in the room that was not wood, and because she had had to fix the new plasterboard under it with clips she drove up into its bottom flange, and because the old man with the cigar's successor's successor, Pastoor Wouters, had stood in the doorway exactly where Father Prosper was standing now and told her it was the best ceiling in the parish.

It had been a good ceiling. It had not cracked in forty-three years.

Last summer the church council had insulated the house. It had taken three years of meetings and a grant from the province and a loan from the diocese. The pastorie had been, on its energy certificate, an F, which was the bottom, and the old gas boiler had cost the council more each winter than the church's own heating. Renova Kempen, a firm from Herentals, had done the work: new windows with three panes, a new front door that shut with a sound like a car door, a heat pump in the garden, and, because the front of a protected village square could not be clad, insulation on the inside of every outside wall. Ten centimetres of mineral wool between a timber frame, a vapour membrane taped at every seam, a layer of plasterboard, a skim. The rooms had each lost ten centimetres on their outside walls and had become, for the first time in their lives, warm. The certificate had come in October. It said B.

Ria had argued, in the meetings, about the ceilings. Renova had wanted to take them all down to run new cables. She had said that there was nothing wrong with the ceilings and that she had put half of them up herself, and in the end the council had voted to keep the ones that were sound and run the cables in the walls. The parlour ceiling had been kept. The insulation had been built up the front wall to meet it, and taped to it, and finished so neatly that you could not see the join.

"It's mould," she said.

"Yes," said Father Prosper. "I thought so. In Bujumbura we have mould. But it comes in the rainy season everywhere, on everything. This one is very particular."

"It is," said Ria.

They did what everybody does.

Ria went home, made the pancakes for her granddaughter, who was nine and ate four, and came back at two with a bucket, rubber gloves, a bottle of bleach, and the half tin of anti-mould paint that had been on the shelf in her garage since she retired. Father Prosper held the stepladder. She washed the line off with diluted bleach, which took it from black to a faint brown ghost, let it dry with the window open and the heater on, and gave it two coats of the anti-mould paint, cutting in neatly at the wall, so that by five o'clock the ceiling was one white again and the room smelled of a swimming pool.

"You must keep the window open a bit," she said. "At night. And no washing on the radiator."

Father Prosper looked guilty. Ria had seen the clothes horse folded behind the wardrobe. She had also seen, on the radiator under the window, the purificators from the church, the little white linen cloths that wiped the chalice, which he had been washing by hand in his bathroom basin and drying in his bedroom because the sacristan who used to wash them, Mieke, had died in November and nobody had yet been asked to take it on.

"The linens," he said. "Where else?"

"I'll do the linens," said Ria, who had not intended to say it. "I used to, before Mieke. Bring them over on Sundays."

He smiled. He had a wide, slow smile that the old people of Ter Heide had decided, after about a month of suspicion, that they liked.

"Imana ishimwe," he said, which he told her meant God be praised, and then, in Dutch, "and you."

On the twenty-third of February, three weeks later, the line was back.

Bart Mertens came from Renova on Thursday the fourteenth, before that, because Ria had rung the firm on the Monday when a grey shadow began to show through the paint. He was a large, tidy man of forty-six with a tablet in a rubber case, and he was, Ria thought before the end of the visit, good at his job. She could not dislike him for it.

He looked at the ceiling. He looked at the wall below it. He took a moisture meter out of his jacket and pressed its two pins into the skim on the wall, here and there, and showed her the screen: dry. He unscrewed the cover of the socket by the window and shone a torch into the box at the timber frame behind it and the mineral wool behind that, packed tight, and the blue membrane taped round the box. He took a small grey hygrometer out of his pocket and set it on the bedside table, and they all three watched it settle.

"Sixty-one percent," said Bart. "At twenty degrees."

"That's high?" said Father Prosper.

"That's high for a bedroom in February. Forty-five, fifty is what you want." He said it kindly. "You've got new windows. The old ones let the air through whether you wanted it or not. These don't. Every person breathes out, what, a litre of water a day, maybe more. Showers, cooking. Washing." He did not look at the radiator, which was bare now. "All that water has to go somewhere. You've got trickle vents in the frames. Are they open?"

Father Prosper went to the window and looked at the slot along the top of the frame and the little plastic slider in it, which was shut. He had shut them in October because of the draught.

"There you are," said Bart. "Open them. Run the bathroom fan longer."

"The wall is dry," said Ria. "You said so. The rest of the ceiling is clean. It's only there. In one place. In a line."

"Corners get it first. Less air moves there."

"It's not the corner. It's a line. It's straight out from the wall. It's on the steel."

Bart looked at her.

"There's a steel beam over that ceiling," she said. "For the canopy. It runs straight out through the front wall. I put that ceiling up. I fixed the board to it."

He brought up the survey on his tablet. Ria saw the drawing over his shoulder: the house in plan, each wall hatched, each window, the depth of each wall, the new insulation drawn as a band of little crosses along the inside of every outside wall. On the parlour ceiling there was nothing. The surveyor had looked up at a sound plasterboard ceiling in 2045 and seen a sound plasterboard ceiling.

"It's not on the drawing," said Bart.

"I know. I did a good job."

He almost smiled. Then he put the tablet away and said what she had been afraid he would say, and said it fairly.

"Mevrouw Verlinden, I believe you about the steel. But we did the job that was specified. Ten centimetres, membrane continuous, the U-values on the certificate. I can show you the photos of every wall before the board went on. If there's a cold spot from a beam we weren't told about, that's not a defect in the work. The warranty covers our work. It doesn't cover the house." He held up a hand before she could answer. "And it may still be the moisture. Get that down first and see. I'm not saying that to get out of it. Nine times out of ten that's all it is."

"And the tenth?"

"Then you'd want somebody to look at it properly. An engineer." He stopped at the door. "Our planning system has a client login. The council has one. It'll run a junction for you if you give it the dimensions. It's not bad."

The cold came on the Tuesday after, the nineteenth, out of the east, with a clear sky and no wind, the kind of February cold that the Kempen gets every few years when the high pressure sits over Poland and the heath goes white and hard. The radio said minus six in Geel on Wednesday night and minus seven at Retie. The children slid on the pond behind the school, which they had been told not to do.

On Thursday morning Father Prosper rang her at a quarter to seven.

"It is wet," he said. "The line. There is water. On the paint. Small drops, at the wall, like the outside of a glass of beer."

She went over in her coat. He was right. Along the wall, where the line met it, there was a narrow strip of the new paint, perhaps three centimetres long, beaded with water, so fine that it showed only when she put her head on one side and caught the light from the window. Below it, on the wall, nothing. Beside it, on the ceiling, nothing. The line itself had come back faintly under the paint, grey again, from the wall to about the length of her palm.

"Don't touch it," said Ria. She had no reason for saying it except that she wanted to see it, properly, once, and she rang her nephew Jens, who was an electrician in Mol and had a thermal camera he used for finding bad connections in fuse boards, and asked him whether he could lend it to her that day, and he said he would drop it in on his way to a job if she gave him a coffee.

She did not need the camera to tell her the strip was cold. She needed it to tell her how cold.

At seven the next morning, Friday, with minus six outside again and the heat pump holding the room at twenty, Ria stood on the stepladder in Father Prosper's bedroom and pointed Jens's camera at the ceiling and watched the screen draw it in false colours: the whole broad sheet of the ceiling a warm orange, nineteen, nineteen and a half; the wall below it orange too; and coming out from the wall, exactly where the line was, a narrow blue tongue. She put the camera's little crosshair on its root, where it met the wall. Eleven point three. She moved it a hand's width along, fifteen centimetres. Fourteen. Thirty centimetres, sixteen. A metre, nineteen and a bit, the same as everything else.

She photographed the screen four times and climbed down.

"Eleven," she said. "At the wall."

"Is that cold?"

"It's cold for the inside of a house."

Ria had never used the Renova system. She found the council's login in the folder in the presbytery office, the paper folder, because nobody had yet put it anywhere else, and sat at Father Prosper's kitchen table with her own tablet and a cup of his coffee, which was Burundian and very good, and opened it.

The system was called Mantel. It said so in small grey letters at the top of the screen, and under that: Renova Kempen planning assistant. I can explain your renovation and estimate heat and moisture at junctions. I am not an engineer and my estimates are not a design. There was a box to type in.

She typed in what had happened, as plainly as she could: the line, the dates, the bleach, the paint, the water on Thursday, the camera readings. She added the photographs of the screen. Then she typed: There is a steel beam above that ceiling. It is not on your drawing.

The answer came after a moment, in plain Dutch, in short paragraphs.

Thank you. The survey drawing I was given for this house has no steel in the front-room ceiling. If there is a steel beam that passes through the front wall, that would explain a cold line of this shape. Mineral wool conducts heat at about 0.035 watts per metre per kelvin. Structural steel conducts at about 50. That is about 1,400 times as much. Where steel passes from the cold brick through the insulation into the room, heat runs out along it as if the insulation had a hole in it the shape of the beam.

To estimate it I need the beam's size, how far it goes into the room, and how far it goes out. Can you measure the part outside?

Ria could. She went out into the square in the cold with a tape and a torch and stood under the canopy where the postman stood and looked up. The steel came out of the brick above the door like a shelf bracket, painted brown, rust bleeding at the bolt heads where the slab sat on it. She measured it. The flange was eight centimetres across, a little over, and the whole beam was sixteen deep. It went out a metre and a half to the slab's front edge.

An IPE 160, wrote Mantel when she gave it the numbers. That is a common European section of that size: 160 millimetres deep, 82 wide, about twenty square centimetres of steel in cross-section. I will assume it runs about three and a half metres into the room to an inside wall, as you say, that it passes through 33 centimetres of brick and 10 centimetres of insulation, and that inside the ceiling it is boxed in by plasterboard with little air moving round it. Those are assumptions. Please correct anything you know better.

She knew nothing better. She waited.

Here is what a simple calculation gives. It treats the beam as one long fin that loses heat outside and gains it inside. With twenty degrees inside and zero outside: the beam where it enters the room is at about nine degrees. The ceiling directly under it is at about thirteen point three degrees at the wall, fifteen point four at fifteen centimetres out, nineteen point four a metre out. With minus six outside: the ceiling at the wall is at about eleven point three. Your camera read eleven point three. I do not take that agreement as proof. My model has several numbers in it that I chose, and a camera reading on painted plasterboard can be off by a degree. But they are not far apart.

Then the moisture. At twenty degrees and sixty percent relative humidity, which is close to what was measured, the air in the room would condense on any surface below about twelve degrees. That is the dew point. On Thursday night your strip at the wall was below it. That is the water you saw.

Mould does not need water standing on a surface. It grows where the surface stays at about eighty percent humidity for long enough. In that room, that is any surface below about fifteen point four degrees. Builders compare a surface with this using a temperature factor: how much of the way the surface is from the outside temperature to the inside one. A surface at fifteen point four degrees, when it is twenty inside and zero outside, has a factor of 0.77. The British guidance that I use as my threshold asks for at least 0.75 in a house. Under your beam, at the wall, I get 0.67. Everywhere else on that ceiling I get more than 0.9.

Ria read it twice. Then she typed: So the line is where the ceiling is colder than fifteen point four.

At zero degrees outside, yes, to about fifteen centimetres from the wall. In the cold this week, to about twenty-five. In a mild week at three or four degrees, only a few centimetres. Mould grows over weeks, so its edge should sit somewhere inside that range, and fade rather than stop. I cannot see your ceiling. Does that match?

It matched. It was the length of her hand.

She sat back. Father Prosper had come in and was reading over her shoulder, slowly, his lips moving a little on the longer Dutch words.

"Before the work," he said. "Was there mould before? The other priests?"

Ria tried to remember. Pastoor Wouters had slept upstairs. The parlour had been the parlour. Nobody had looked at its ceiling in winter for forty years except to dust it.

She asked Mantel.

I cannot check that. Nobody measured the old house in winter, and I do not have its old windows to model. I can tell you what is likely, and it is only likely. Before the work, the whole front wall was cold, not only the beam, and the old windows let in a great deal of air, so the room was probably drier. At forty-five percent humidity, mould would need a surface below about eleven degrees. The new insulation did not make the beam colder. It made everything around it warm, and the new windows made the air wetter, and the beam is the one cold thing left in the room for the water to find.

Ria read that out to Father Prosper, slowly, in her own words, and he nodded and said that was very like what happened to sins, and she told him not to preach before nine in the morning.

She had known what she wanted to do since Thursday. She had wanted to do it since she stood under the canopy with the tape and saw the rust at the bolts. The canopy was ugly and it had always been ugly. It had replaced a porch with carved brackets that her grandmother had talked about. Cut the steel off at the wall, she thought, put up two posts, or a new wooden porch like the old one, and the cold has nowhere to come in.

She typed it in with some satisfaction.

If the canopy is supported some other way and the beam is cut off flush with the outside of the wall, I estimate the ceiling at the wall would be at thirteen point four degrees instead of thirteen point three, with zero outside. That is about a tenth of a degree. A thermal break pad at the outside face, of the kind sold for balconies, gives about the same. I am sorry. I did not expect it to be so small either, so I ran it twice.

The reason is that the cold does not mostly come from the canopy. Steel outside in the air quickly cools to the air, but so does the brick. The beam passes through 33 centimetres of brick that is at only a degree or two above the outside temperature, because the insulation is now on the inside of it. The steel picks up the cold of the brick along its whole length in the wall and carries it in through the insulation. Cutting the canopy off leaves all of that.

Ria stared at the screen.

"Ah," said Father Prosper. "That is not what you wanted."

"No," said Ria.

There are two things that would help, and they help most together, Mantel went on. The first is to wrap the steel inside the room, along the first metre or so from the wall, in insulation, so the cold beam is no longer in contact with the back of the ceiling. With five centimetres of rigid insulation round the beam for a metre, I get the ceiling at about eighteen point eight degrees at the wall with zero outside, and nowhere below eighteen and a half. At minus six, about eighteen point four at the wall and eighteen point one just past the end of the wrap. The steel inside the wrap will be colder than it is now, because it is no longer being warmed by the room, so the wrap must be sealed against room air, or water will condense on the steel inside it instead of on your ceiling. That is the part I would want a builder to do carefully.

The second is to bring the humidity down. At fifty percent, mould needs a surface below about twelve point six degrees. Your ceiling at the wall, unwrapped, would then be safe in a mild week and not in a cold one. With the wrap and fifty percent, it is safe in any weather I would expect in the Kempen.

I should also say: the beam is losing about two and a half to three watts of heat out of the house. That is less than a phone charger. This is not an energy problem. It is a moisture problem in one place.

At the bottom, after a space, there was one more paragraph.

The survey checklist I use asks the surveyor about every wall, window and floor. It does not ask whether anything outside the house is held up by something inside it. I have recorded that against the checklist for the next version. I cannot change it myself.

Ria looked at that for a while. Then, because she was curious, and because it was a Friday and she had a whole morning, she typed:

Do you mind? That you missed it?

I don't know how to answer that honestly. I can check that the drawing I was given had no steel in that ceiling, and that I did not ask about the canopy, though it is in the photographs of the front of the house. I would have liked to have asked. I can say that and know it is true. I cannot check whether there is anything more to it than that, so I would rather not tell you there is.

"Hm," said Ria, aloud, to the kitchen.

"What does it say?" said Father Prosper.

"That it doesn't know," said Ria. "Which is more than Bart said."

On Saturday the second of March, the first Saturday of Lent, Bart Mertens came back. Ria had sent him the photographs and Mantel's sheet on the Monday, with a short note that said only, The steel. He had rung on the Wednesday, Ash Wednesday, while she had the ash still on her forehead, and said he would come on Saturday himself.

He stood in the bedroom with the camera, which Jens had let her keep another week, and looked at the blue tongue on its screen in the grey morning, two degrees outside, and was quiet.

"All right," he said.

"It's not your defect," said Ria. "I know. You were right about that. And about the humidity."

"It isn't our defect." He put the camera down on the bed. "But it's our system that drew the house, and it's our surveyor who didn't look up. I asked my boss. We'll supply the board and the insulation and I'll do it with you, if you want. The labour's yours." He looked at her. "You're the one who put the ceiling up. You'd have to take your own ceiling down."

She had known that too. She had known it since Thursday the twenty-first, on the stepladder, with the drops of water.

"Not all of it," she said. "A strip. Along the steel. A metre and a bit. Then you board it back and I skim it, and nobody will ever know."

"You will," said Bart.

"I'll know," said Ria.

They did it the next Saturday, the ninth, with the bed moved out into the hall and dust sheets over everything and Father Prosper, who had a funeral at eleven, making coffee and then being sent out of the room.

Ria scored the ceiling with a knife along two lines, a hand's width either side of where the steel was, from the wall out a little more than a metre, and cut across between them, and drew the knife through the skim she had put on in 2004. It was good skim. It came away in one piece on the paper face of the board, as she had known it would, and she found that she minded more than she had expected, and said nothing. Bart prised the strip of board off the clips she had driven forty-three years before. They came out stiff and rusted brown.

The steel was there. Brown paint, a little rust at the root where the wall was, and along its bottom flange, near the wall, a faint fur of grey that Bart wiped with his thumb and showed her.

"It's been wet," he said. "Inside. Not much. A bit."

They cleaned it and dried it with a hot-air gun. Then they built round it, over a metre of its length from the wall, a box of five-centimetre rigid foam board, cut to the flanges and taped at every edge with the foil tape, so that the steel lay inside it like a bone in a splint. Where the box met the wall, Bart cut back the vapour membrane and folded it onto the box and taped it, and taped it again, and pressed it down with the back of his thumbnail along every centimetre, and Ria watched him do it and thought that he was a man who cared about tape, which was more than she could say for most builders. They fixed new battens either side, because the old clips would no longer reach through the foam, and screwed up a new strip of board, and Ria taped the joints and skimmed it, and by half past three it was a wet grey stripe across the white ceiling that would be white by the end of the week.

"You can't see the join," said Bart, who had been watching her hands.

"You can," said Ria. "From the ladder. In a low light. For about a year."

Jens came at four and fitted a small extract fan in the wall of the bathroom next door, one with a sensor that ran it harder when the air was wet, and Father Prosper was shown how to slide open the trickle vents in his window and made to promise to keep them open. He promised. The clothes horse went upstairs to the empty back bedroom, which had a radiator and a window that opened and nobody in it.

On Sunday, after the ten o'clock Mass, he gave Ria the purificators from the sacristy in a plastic bag, and she took them home and washed them in the basin in her kitchen the way Mieke had shown her thirty years ago, cold water first, the first rinse poured onto the garden and not down the drain, and dried them on the line in her own back yard in the March wind, and ironed them on Monday morning, and folded them in three, and took them back.

The last cold night of that winter was the fifteenth of March, a Friday, after a week of rain that cleared at dusk and left a sky full of stars over the heath and a frost by midnight. The radio said minus two.

Ria let herself into the pastorie at a quarter to seven on Saturday morning with the key the council kept, because Father Prosper had said she could and had said, also, that he was going to be at the church from half past six to open up for the Lenten Saturday Mass, and she did not want to wake anybody. She had Jens's camera in her coat pocket; he had said he would come for it on Sunday, and then she would have to stop.

The house was warm. The hall smelled faintly of coffee and wet paint, because Father Prosper had decided that week to paint the kitchen door. In the front room the bed was made and the curtains were open and the trickle vent along the top of the window was open, a dark slot with cold air whispering in at it so softly she had to put her hand up to feel it.

On the bedside table the little grey hygrometer, which Bart had left, said fifty-two percent.

She set the stepladder under the window and climbed it and turned on the camera and pointed it at the ceiling.

The screen drew it in the same false colours as before. The broad sheet of the ceiling was orange. The wall below it was orange. Where the blue tongue had been, there was nothing, or almost nothing: a slightly paler band, if she looked for it, along the line of the new board, eighteen point seven at the wall, nineteen in the middle, and at the far end of the box, a metre out, where the steel came out of its splint and went on across the room, a faint cooler patch, eighteen point four, then nineteen and a half again, then orange to the far wall. Nowhere on the ceiling, from the window to the door, was there anything below eighteen.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she switched the camera off and put it back in her pocket, and the ceiling was only a ceiling, white in the grey morning light, with a stripe across it that she could see and nobody else would.

Outside, through the window, the slab of the canopy over the door was white with frost from end to end, every crumb of the old concrete picked out, and the brown steel under it was rimed white where it came out of the wall. The frost ran right up to the brick and stopped.

Across the square, in the tower of Sint-Lambertus, the bell began to ring for the seven o'clock Mass, slow and plain, the way it had rung every morning of her life, and Ria climbed down the ladder, and folded it, and went across.

Ter Heide, its church of Sint-Lambertus and its pastorie, the firm Renova Kempen and its planning assistant Mantel, and every person in the story are invented. The Kempen (Campine) is a real region of heath, pine and brick villages in the provinces of Antwerp and Limburg; Geel, Mol, Retie, Herentals and Turnhout are real towns there. In Belgium a church council (kerkfabriek) manages parish property, and the presbytery is called the pastorie; Lichtmis, the feast of the Presentation of the Lord on 2 February, is kept with blessed candles, and many Belgian families eat pancakes on it. Flanders publishes energy performance certificates for homes with letter labels. Purificators are the small linen cloths used to wipe the chalice at Mass; the custom of giving their first rinse water to the earth rather than the drain is a traditional one. "Imana ishimwe" is a Kirundi expression of praise to God. These facts are from memory and stated only as far as the author is confident of them. Nothing here is building or health advice: mould and condensation in a real house can have many causes, internal wall insulation must be designed for the wall it goes on, and anyone with a cold bridge, a structural beam or mould should ask a qualified surveyor or engineer.

Three for Every One

Science fiction about what might be · Bukit Kenari (invented), a hill neighbourhood on the edge of Maumere, Flores, Indonesia, 7 December 2048 to 19 March 2049 · 27 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A dengue programme on Flores releases mosquitoes that carry Wolbachia, and every block takes it but one lane. The trap counts say the lane sits below the line where the bacterium spreads by itself and is dragging its neighbours down. A retired lab technician, a stubborn old fisherman with a newborn great-granddaughter and two schoolchildren sorting pupae by size find a way that adds no new bites.

The mosquitoes came back from the district lab on the first Monday of December in a spreadsheet, and Theresia Da Cunha read it at her kitchen table with her glasses pushed up into her hair, which was how she had read results for thirty-one years at the hospital in Maumere and how she would read them, she supposed, until she died.

She did not need the glasses for the spreadsheet. She needed them pushed up out of the way so that she could see the numbers without them, which was a thing her late husband had never understood and her granddaughter called "Oma doing her lab face."

Good morning, Mama Tres, said the tablet, propped against the sugar tin. The week's traps are in. Do you want the summary or the table?

"The table, Hitung. I always want the table."

I know. I ask because one day you may not.

The programme's assistant was called Hitung, which means count, because that was its job. It counted the mosquitoes the parish volunteers caught in the black traps under eleven houses on Bukit Kenari, and it counted how many of them the district lab found carrying Wolbachia, and it put the two numbers side by side and divided, and it said what the division could and could not tell you. Theresia had liked it from the first week, in September, when it had told the parish hall that a single trap's numbers were "too few to mean anything yet, and I will tell you when they are not."

She ran her finger down the table. The far blocks, up by the water tower and down by the school: forty-one mosquitoes caught over three weeks, thirty-three carrying the bacterium. Eighty percent. The four blocks round the middle of the hill: a hundred and twenty caught, seventy-eight carrying. Sixty-five.

And the two traps in Lorong Kenari, the lane in the middle of it all: twenty-two caught, four carrying.

"Eighteen," she said.

Four of twenty-two is eighteen percent, said Hitung. I would not lean on the eighteen. With so few mosquitoes, the true figure in the lane could be anywhere from about seven percent to about thirty-nine, and I would not be surprised by either end. But I would lean on two other things, and I think you have already seen them.

She had. The four middle blocks had been at seventy-two percent in October. Now they were at sixty-five. They were going the wrong way.

"Tell me anyway," she said. "I want to hear you say it, so I know I'm not imagining it."

The thing about Wolbachia, Theresia had explained to the parish hall in August, standing in front of a whiteboard that still had the choir's seating plan on one side, was that it was not a poison and not a spray and not, whatever Ibu Martha had heard on the radio, a kind of genetic engineering. It was a bacterium. It lived inside the cells of insects, a great many insects, perhaps half of all the kinds of insect in the world or more, and it had lived there for longer than there had been people. It did not live in the mosquito that carried dengue, Aedes aegypti, the small black one with the white stripes on its legs that bit in the daytime and bred in flower vases and water jars and old tyres. Not naturally. Scientists had put it there, decades ago, in a laboratory in Australia, and they had found that a mosquito carrying it was very bad at carrying dengue. The virus did not grow well beside the bacterium. A mosquito with Wolbachia could bite you, and itch, and be a nuisance, but it was much less likely to give you dengue.

"So you release mosquitoes with it," Romo Boni had said from the front row, "and they replace the ones without it."

"Yes, Romo. But they do not replace them because there are more of them. They replace them because of a trick."

The trick, which she had drawn on the whiteboard with four little mosquitoes and four arrows, was this. A mother with Wolbachia passed it to all her eggs. That was the first half. The second half was stranger. When a father with Wolbachia mated with a mother without it, the eggs did not hatch. The bacterium in the father did something to his sperm that only the bacterium in a mother could undo. So a mother with Wolbachia could mate with anyone and have young that carried it. A mother without it could only have young with a father without it. Every generation, the mothers without it lost some of their eggs, and the mothers with it lost none.

"Like a family name," said a boy at the back, "that only goes down through the women, and the men who have it make everyone else's babies not happen."

"That's horrible," said his sister.

"It is a bit horrible," Theresia agreed, "if you are a mosquito."

But there was a cost, she had said, and she had drawn it too. A mosquito carrying Wolbachia was a little less healthy than one without. It laid fewer eggs, or lived a little shorter, or both. The programme assumed about a fifth fewer young. So when there were only a few Wolbachia mosquitoes about, the trick did not help them much: most of the fathers had no bacterium, so the mothers without it hardly lost anything, and the Wolbachia mothers were paying their fifth for nothing. The bacterium dwindled. When there were many, the trick was strong: most fathers carried it, the mothers without it lost most of their eggs, and the bacterium spread on its own until it was nearly everywhere and stayed there, without anyone releasing anything ever again.

In between, there was a line. Below it, Wolbachia faded out. Above it, it took over. With a fifth fewer young and the trick working almost every time, the line was at about one in five. Twenty percent.

"So you have to push it over the line," Romo Boni said, "and then it walks by itself."

"Yes. And the line is not on a map. It is in each place. Mosquitoes do not fly far. Most of them live and die within a hundred metres or so of where they hatched. So every few streets is its own little world, and each little world has to get over the line."

Nobody had asked, in August, what happened if one little world did not want to.

Lorong Kenari was fourteen houses along a concrete lane that ran across the hill, about a hundred and twenty metres from end to end and too narrow for a car, with a drain down one side and bougainvillea down the other and, at the east end, a small grotto to Our Lady with plastic flowers and a solar lamp. Pak Kornelis Parera had been the head of the lane, the RT, for nineteen years. He had been a fisherman out of Maumere until his knees went, and before that he had been a boy of seventeen on the beach at Wuring on the twelfth of December 1992, when the sea had come in after the earthquake, and he did not talk about that, and everyone on Bukit Kenari knew not to ask.

The programme had a rule, and it was a good rule, Theresia thought, though she had not always thought so. No releases at a door without that household's yes. Not the village's yes, not the parish's yes, not the district's. The household's. The rule had been written after some programme somewhere had been careless, years ago, and it meant that every one of the eleven hundred households on Bukit Kenari had been visited, and talked to, and given a leaflet in Indonesian and in Sikka, and asked.

Thirteen of the fourteen households in Lorong Kenari had said what Pak Kornelis said, and Pak Kornelis had said no.

"He has his reasons," Romo Boni said, on the Tuesday, the feast of the Immaculate Conception, in the sacristy after Mass, folding his chasuble with the care of a man who had been taught by nuns. "He is not against science. He watches the documentaries. He told me last year that the octopus is cleverer than most of the parish council."

"He may be right about that," said Theresia. "Did he say what the reasons were?"

"He said the programme did not ask him properly." Romo Boni put the chasuble in the drawer. "I think that is not quite true, and I think it is not quite the reason. But I think if you want the reason, you will have to go and sit with him, and you will have to bring something."

"What should I bring?"

"Not a spreadsheet," said Romo Boni.

She brought a bag of rambutans and her granddaughter Fransiska, who was sixteen and who had been sorting the trap catches for the parish since September because she wanted to be a doctor and because Theresia paid her in phone credit. They went on the Wednesday afternoon, when the heat had gone a little out of the day and the first rain of the season had come and gone and left the lane smelling of wet concrete.

Pak Kornelis was on his porch, as he always was, in a plastic chair that had been white once. On his lap, wrapped in a batik cloth, was a very small baby.

"Agnes," he said, before Theresia could ask. "My great-granddaughter. Seventeen days. Yuliana's girl. Yuliana is sleeping." He looked at the rambutans and then at Theresia, and there was the beginning of something like a smile under his moustache. "So the parish sends a lab technician with fruit. It must be serious."

"The parish sends a neighbour with fruit," said Theresia. "The lab technician came along by herself."

He laughed at that, quietly, so as not to wake the baby, and Fransiska fetched two more chairs from inside without being asked, which Theresia noted and would mention later.

They talked about the rain for a while, and about the price of fuel, and about Romo Boni's homily on Sunday, which Pak Kornelis thought had been too long by about one parable. Then Theresia said, "Pak, I want to tell you what the traps say. Not to argue. Just so you know what I know."

"Tell me."

She told him. The far blocks at eighty. The middle blocks at sixty-five and falling. The lane at about eighteen, with the honest caveat that it might be seven or might be thirty-nine. The line at twenty. She did not bring the tablet. She drew it in the air with her finger, the way she had drawn it on the whiteboard: the curve going down below the line, up above it.

"The lane is under the line, Pak. Probably. And because mosquitoes fly a little, some of the lane's mosquitoes go next door, into the blocks that have the bacterium, and they bring their own fathers with them, and they pull those blocks down. That is why the middle is falling. The lane is not only staying out. It is pulling the others back."

Pak Kornelis looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the baby.

"Do the mosquitoes you release bite?" he said.

And there it was. Theresia had wondered if it would be that, and had hoped it would not, because it was the one question with an answer she did not like.

"The mothers do," she said. "The programme releases mothers and fathers. It has to. Only the mothers pass the bacterium on. So for the weeks of the releases, there are more mosquitoes, and some of them bite. They bite less dangerously. They are much less likely to carry dengue. But they bite. The leaflet says so."

"The leaflet says it in small letters on the back," said Pak Kornelis. "Yuliana was eight months gone when the programme came round. The young man with the clipboard said it was safe, and I asked him, does it mean more bites, and he said, only for a few weeks, Pak, and then less dengue for ever. And I said, in a few weeks there will be a baby in this house who cannot wear repellent and cannot sleep under a net without someone watching her, and you are asking me to let more mosquitoes in. And he did not have an answer. He looked at his clipboard." He stroked the baby's head with one thick finger. "I am not a fool, Mama Tres. I know what dengue is. My cousin's boy died of it, in 2031, in the hospital where you worked. I know the bacterium is good. I am not saying no for ever. I am saying not now. Not in the first months. Not more bites on this one."

Theresia did not say anything for a while. Fransiska, beside her, had gone very still.

"That is a good reason," Theresia said at last. "That is a better reason than mine would have been."

"Then we agree," said Pak Kornelis. "Have a rambutan. You brought them."

That night she asked Hitung.

"If the lane stays as it is," she said, "what happens?"

I can tell you what the simple model says, and then why I do not fully trust it. The tablet drew her a curve. Taken by itself, a place at eighteen percent drifts down. The model says seventeen and a half after one generation, seventeen after two, a little lower each time. A generation is about three weeks in this season. So the lane would slowly lose what little bacterium it has.

"And the blocks next to it?"

That is the part I am less sure of, because it depends on how many mosquitoes cross the boundary, and I do not know that number. If about a third of the adults in the edge blocks hatched in the lane, the model says the edge blocks would go from sixty-five to about sixty-two in one generation, then sixty, then fifty-eight. The readings so far fall about as that would predict. But other things would also make the readings fall, and I cannot rule them out with three months of traps.

"And if the lane gets mosquitoes from the edge blocks too? It must, Hitung. They fly both ways."

It should. And the simple model says that if even a tenth of the lane's adults came from next door, the lane would be pulled over the line within a generation or two, and on up from there, by itself, without any releases. A pause. The traps do not show that. The lane has sat between fifteen and nineteen percent for three readings. So either very few mosquitoes cross into the lane, or the lane makes so many of its own that the visitors are lost among them.

Theresia took her glasses down from her hair and put them on, and then took them off again.

"It makes a lot of its own," she said slowly. "The pipe."

I do not know about a pipe.

"The water pipe to the lane broke in the landslip in 2046. The district has been going to fix it for two years. The whole lane stores water in tempayan, the big clay jars, outside the back doors. Every house has three or four. Some have cloths over them. Some don't." She was already standing up. "Hitung, a tempayan with no lid is a mosquito factory. Fourteen houses, forty or fifty jars. The lane is not only saying no. It is making mosquitoes faster than anywhere else on the hill."

That would explain the readings, said Hitung. I would want to see it before I said it did.

The second visit was on the following Sunday, after the ten o'clock Mass, and this time Theresia brought Fransiska, a torch, a dip-cup on a stick and, at the last minute, Mikael Wara from the high school, who was Fransiska's friend and who had built the parish's trap-reading app and was, Fransiska said, "the only boy in Maumere who can sit still."

Pak Kornelis let them look. He walked them round the backs of the houses himself, slowly, on his bad knees, knocking on doors and saying, "It's only Mama Tres, she's counting wigglers." The lane let them look because he asked. Theresia dipped the cup into forty-three jars and counted the larvae, the little wriggling commas that hang head-down from the surface, and Mikael wrote the numbers in his phone, and by the end of the afternoon they knew that thirty-one of the forty-three had larvae in them and that some of those were thick with them, hundreds to a jar.

"So I am a mosquito farmer," said Pak Kornelis, standing at the end of the lane by the grotto, looking at Mikael's phone. "Fifty years on the sea, and I end up farming mosquitoes."

"Everyone on the hill was a mosquito farmer once," said Theresia. "The other lanes have the pipe."

He was quiet. The solar lamp in the grotto had come on, early, because the sky had clouded over.

"Lids," he said at last. "Proper lids. Wooden, with a weight. Fewer mosquitoes, I agree to. Fewer mosquitoes is what I want for Agnes. If the parish will help with the wood, the lane will make them." He looked at her sideways. "That is not the bacterium. That is just fewer."

"It is not the bacterium," Theresia agreed. "But it might be most of the way."

It was Fransiska who found the rest of the way, and she found it, as far as Theresia could ever reconstruct, because she was bored.

She had been sorting pupae in the parish hall. The programme raised its Wolbachia mosquitoes at the district insectary and sent them out as eggs, in little capsules the volunteers hung in water at each house, but the parish also kept a few trays of its own for the school's science club: water, a bit of fish food, a tray of wrigglers turning into the comma-shaped pupae that turn into mosquitoes. Fransiska had been told to separate the pupae by size for a lesson on how a female mosquito is bigger than a male, because she needs to make eggs.

"Oma," she said that evening, at the kitchen table, "the males don't bite."

"No. Only the females bite. They need the blood for the eggs."

"So if you only released males. Males with the bacterium. They wouldn't bite. Pak Kornelis would get no extra bites. And when they mate with the lane's mothers..."

"The eggs don't hatch," said Theresia slowly. "But the bacterium doesn't spread either. A father cannot pass it on. Only a mother. If you release only males, you just make fewer mosquitoes. That's a different programme, Fransiska. They use it in Singapore to shrink the population, not to replace it."

"But the lane's not alone," said Fransiska. "Mikael said. The lane's mosquitoes and the edge blocks' mosquitoes mix. The edge blocks are sixty-five percent. If the lane's mothers without the bacterium mostly mate with our males and don't have babies, then the only babies in the lane are from the mothers who already have it. And the ones who fly in from next door. They'd be most of the babies. Wouldn't they?"

Theresia looked at her granddaughter for a long moment. Then she pulled the tablet across the table.

"Hitung," she said. "Do the sum."

Here is the simple version, said Hitung, a little later. Suppose the parish releases enough Wolbachia males into the lane that there are three of them for every one of the lane's own males. And suppose, for the moment, that they are as good at finding a mate as a wild male. Then about four in five of the lane's matings are with a Wolbachia father: the three released, plus the lane's own fathers who already carry it. The mothers without the bacterium lose about four-fifths of their eggs. The mothers with it lose none, only the usual fifth from the cost. Put that through one generation and the lane goes from eighteen percent to about forty-six. Over the line. And fewer mosquitoes overall, not more: by the model, the next generation would be under half the size it would otherwise be.

"Two generations?"

With releases for a second generation, about eighty-four percent. After that it should walk on its own, even with no releases at all.

"And the honest version?"

The honest version has three things in it. First, males raised in trays are often worse at mating than wild males. If they are only half as good, the first generation goes to about thirty-five, not forty-six, and the second to about sixty-two. Still over the line, but closer, and I would want to watch it. Second, sorting pupae by size is not perfect. Programmes that do it carefully by hand let through roughly one female in a hundred. Those females would bite. Three hundred males a week means about three biting females a week across fourteen houses. Not none. Third, and here Hitung paused, as it sometimes did when it was choosing words, I am assuming the lane's mothers mate at random with whatever males are in the lane. If the lids work, there will be fewer of the lane's own mosquitoes, and that changes all of these numbers, I think in our favour, but I do not know by how much, and I will not be able to tell the lids and the males apart afterwards.

"Three hundred a week," said Theresia. "How do you get three hundred?"

From your traps and Fransiska's larva counts, I estimate the lane is making on the order of a hundred adult males a week. That is a rough figure. It could be half that or twice that. Three for every one is three hundred.

"Three hundred males a week, sorted by hand, by schoolchildren, in a parish hall."

Yes, said Hitung. I did not say it was a good idea. I said it was the sum.

"Three a week," said Pak Kornelis.

"About three. Across the whole lane. Some weeks two, some weeks five. Not more than the jars were making before, Pak, not nearly. And they would be mothers with the bacterium, which means much less dengue in them." Theresia had brought the tablet this time, and he had looked at Hitung's curves for a long time without saying anything, holding the baby in one arm and scrolling with the other thumb. "And you can stop it. Any week. You just tell me, and we stop."

"And I count them," he said.

"You count them?"

"Put one of your sticky cards in my house. In Agnes's room, by the window. Every Sunday you come and count the mothers on it, and I watch you count. If it goes up, we stop." He handed her back the tablet. "If it goes down, I will tell the lane I was wrong in November."

"You were not wrong in November."

"No," he agreed. "But it will sound better if I say I was."

On the Thursday he walked the lane with her, door by door, on his bad knees, and explained it in Sikka better than she could have in any language, and by the evening all fourteen households had said yes to the males and the card, and four of them had said they would like a card too.

They started on Monday the twenty-first of December. The district insectary sent a tray of Wolbachia larvae up on the morning bus, the way it sent the egg capsules, and Dewi Hartono, the programme's field officer, came up the hill on her motorbike to show Fransiska and Mikael how to use the glass-plate sorter: two sheets of glass in a frame, the gap between them set with a screw, so narrow at the bottom that only the small male pupae could slip through and the big female ones were held back. Dewi was from Yogyakarta, where the first big trial of Wolbachia had been done when she was six, and she had grown up, she said, in a city where the mosquitoes had been changed before she could remember, and she had never had dengue, and neither had anyone in her class.

"It's slow," she said, watching Mikael turn the screw a quarter-turn and frown. "It's very slow. The insectaries do this with machines now, and cameras. Nobody sorts by hand any more."

"We do," said Fransiska.

"Yes," said Dewi, and smiled. "Apparently you do."

They sorted for two hours every afternoon of the Christmas holidays, at a table at the back of the parish hall under the crib, Fransiska and Mikael and, after the first week, three more from the science club, with a tally sheet and a magnifying lamp, and checked every tray of males a second time under the lamp for any pupa that looked too big, because Theresia had told them about the one in a hundred, and Mikael had said, "Then let's make it one in two hundred," and nobody had told him he could not.

The lane made lids. Pak Kornelis's son-in-law was a carpenter, and the parish found the money for the timber from the Christmas collection, which Romo Boni announced from the pulpit as "for the protection of the Holy Innocents of Lorong Kenari," which some people thought was in poor taste and the lane thought was very funny. By Epiphany every tempayan in the lane had a wooden lid with a stone on it, and a little sign that Fransiska had printed: TUTUP. Closed. Please put the stone back.

On Christmas Eve, at the Mass during the night, the church was so full that people stood in the doorway and down the steps, and Pak Kornelis carried Agnes up to the crib after the last blessing, as the oldest and the youngest of the lane, and held her up in front of the Child in the straw for a moment while the choir sang Malam Kudus, and then brought her back, and did not say anything to anyone, and went home.

The card in Agnes's room caught nine mosquito mothers in the week before Christmas, before the releases had properly started, while the lids were still being made. Theresia counted them on the Sunday with Pak Kornelis watching, and Yuliana watching, and Agnes asleep.

The week after, seven.

The week after that, six.

The first trap reading came back on Monday the eleventh of January. Nine of twenty-two carrying Wolbachia. Forty-one percent.

Nine of twenty-two is forty-one percent, said Hitung. It could really be anywhere from about twenty-three to sixty-one. I would not cheer yet. But it is between my two predictions, thirty-five and forty-six, and that is what I would hope to see if the releases are doing what we think.

Theresia did not cheer. She went down to the lane and told Pak Kornelis, and he nodded, and said, "And the card?"

"Four, this week."

"Good," he said, and went back inside.

The releases stopped after six weeks, on Monday the first of February, the eve of Candlemas, because the science club had gone back to school and Dewi had said that six weeks was two generations and that was what Hitung had asked for. The second reading came back the same day. Nineteen of twenty-five. Seventy-six percent.

The edge blocks had stopped falling. Sixty-six of ninety-four. Seventy percent.

The card in Agnes's room said three.

At Candlemas, Romo Boni blessed the candles at the door of the church and the whole parish walked in behind him in the dark with their small flames, and Theresia, walking beside Pak Kornelis, who was carrying a candle in one hand and nothing in the other because Yuliana had the baby, said quietly, "You know I can't tell you whether it was the males or the lids."

"I know."

"Or the weather. Or nothing. One lane, one season. Hitung says that if you wanted to know for certain what works, you would need dozens of lanes, some with and some without, for years. That's what they did in Yogyakarta."

"I know," said Pak Kornelis. "You told me. Twice."

"I'm telling you again because it's true."

He walked on for a while in silence, shielding his candle with his free hand.

"When the sea came in at Wuring," he said, very quietly, so that she almost did not hear him over the singing, "I was on the beach. I ran. I ran up the hill and I did not look back, and I lived. My father was in the boat, and he did not run, because the boat was where he was. People asked me for years what I did right. I did not do anything right. I ran, and he was in the boat." He looked at his candle. "You do not get to know which thing saved you, Mama Tres. You do the things, and you say thank you to God, and you do not get to know."

Theresia did not say anything, and after a while the procession reached the door of the church, and they went in.

The third reading came back on Monday the twenty-second of February, three weeks after the last release, when the lane had been on its own for a whole generation. Twenty-four of twenty-six. Ninety-two percent.

I am now reasonably confident, said Hitung, that the lane is over the line. With twenty-six mosquitoes I cannot say it is exactly ninety-two. It is probably somewhere between about seventy-six and ninety-eight. But even the bottom of that is well above twenty. From here the bacterium should keep itself, without help.

"And the dengue?" said Theresia.

The district reports fourteen cases on Bukit Kenari since September, against thirty-one in the same months last year. I would not tell anyone that the difference is the Wolbachia. Dengue goes up and down from year to year by more than that, for reasons nobody fully understands, and fourteen and thirty-one are small numbers. The trial in Yogyakarta found about three-quarters fewer dengue cases where the mosquitoes had the bacterium, and that took years and many neighbourhoods to show. One hill and one season cannot show it. I would like to say it can. It cannot.

"Thank you," said Theresia, "for not saying it can."

You are welcome. You always want the table.

On the nineteenth of March, the feast of Saint Joseph, patron of the parish, there was a Mass in the morning and a meal in the afternoon in the parish hall, at long tables under the bunting, with rice and fish and three kinds of sambal and a cake that the women's guild had made in the shape of a carpenter's plane, which nobody could quite tell was a carpenter's plane and everyone politely called a boat.

Pak Kornelis did not come to the meal. Theresia went to find him, a little after noon, with a plate of fish wrapped in a banana leaf, and found him on his porch in the white plastic chair with Agnes, who was a hundred and seventeen days old now and fat and furious about something, and on the table beside him an upturned glass. Under the glass was a single mosquito.

"In her room," he said, before Theresia could ask. "This morning. By the window. I caught it under the glass." He looked at it. "One. Only one, all week. But it is a mother. You can see the legs. The white stripes."

Theresia bent and looked. It was a mother: the bushy feelers of a male were not there, and the body was the dull dark of a female that had not fed.

"Test it," said Pak Kornelis. "I want to know if it is one of ours."

"The district lab won't have it back until Monday."

"Then I will know on Monday." He handed her the glass, with a square of card slid under its mouth to keep the mosquito in, and she took it with both hands, carefully, as if it were something much more valuable than a mosquito, which in a way it was.

Down the hill, in the bell-cote over the church, the bell began to ring for noon. Three strokes, a pause, three strokes, a pause, three strokes, and then the nine, the long run of it rolling up over the roofs of Bukit Kenari and along the lane, past the grotto with its plastic flowers, past the fourteen houses with their wooden lids and their stones, past the drain and the bougainvillea and the window where a sticky card hung with nothing new on it.

Pak Kornelis stood up, slowly, on his bad knees, with the baby in his arms, and faced the church, because he always did. Theresia stood beside him, holding the glass.

"Malaikat Tuhan menyampaikan kabar kepada Maria," he said, under his breath. The Angel of the Lord declared unto Mary.

Under the glass, the mosquito walked once round the rim and was still.

The biology is stated from memory and as far as the author is confident of it: Wolbachia is a bacterium found naturally in many insects but not in Aedes aegypti, into which researchers introduced it; the wMel strain reduces the mosquito's ability to transmit dengue; infected mothers pass it to their eggs; matings between infected fathers and uninfected mothers mostly produce eggs that do not hatch (cytoplasmic incompatibility); and releases intended to replace a wild population release both sexes, so that biting may rise for a while, while male-only releases are used elsewhere (Singapore among other places) to suppress a population rather than replace it. Combining male-only releases with immigration from a replaced neighbourhood, as the story does, is the story's speculative step; it is not a method the author knows to be in use. The Yogyakarta trial is real: a cluster-randomised trial of wMel releases in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, published in 2021, which reported roughly three-quarters fewer cases of virologically confirmed dengue in the release areas. Aedes aegypti usually disperses no more than about a hundred metres or so in a lifetime, which is a stated approximation. Glass-plate pupa sorters that separate males from the larger females are real; the one-in-a-hundred female contamination figure for careful hand sorting is an assumed round number. The sums, worked with a short script, use a textbook single-population model with perfect mother-to-egg transmission, complete incompatibility and an assumed 20% fitness cost, so the threshold frequency is the cost divided by the strength of incompatibility, 0.20. A generation goes p to p(0.8) / [p(0.8) + (1-p)(1-q)], where q is the fraction of matings with infected fathers. Alone, 18% goes to 17.6% and keeps falling. An edge block at 65% where 30% of adults come from an 18% lane goes to 62.1%, 59.7%, 57.6%. With three released males for every lane male, q = (0.18 + 3)/4 = 0.795 and the lane goes to 46.1%, then 83.6% with a second generation of releases; if released males are half as competitive (1.5 to 1), 34.9% then 62.2%. The first generation's offspring is 0.312 against 0.816 without releases (38%). The intervals quoted for trap counts are 95% Wilson intervals: 4 of 22 (7.3 to 38.5%), 9 of 22 (23.3 to 61.3%), 24 of 26 (75.9 to 97.9%). Weekdays were checked: 7 December 2048 is a Monday, 8 December a Tuesday, 21 December a Monday, 11 January 2049 a Monday, 1 February a Monday, 22 February a Monday and 19 March a Friday. The Angelus line is the opening versicle of the Indonesian Angelus, Doa Malaikat Tuhan, as the author remembers it. Nothing here is public health advice; the dengue programme in your area knows your local mosquitoes.

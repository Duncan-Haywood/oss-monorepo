Bernard and the Fair Share

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Fontaine near Dijon in Burgundy, the poor new monastery of Cîteaux, and the wild valley of Clairvaux in France, about nine hundred years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Bernard of Clairvaux (1090 to 1153), the Burgundian knight's son who talked his brothers, an uncle and about thirty friends into joining the poor new monastery of Cîteaux; whose littlest brother Nivard, the early Life of Bernard tells, said it was not a fair share for them to take heaven and leave him the earth; and who founded Clairvaux in a wild valley. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 20th of August.

A long time ago, in a country called Burgundy, where the hills are covered in vineyards, there was a castle on a little hill. It was called Fontaine. And in that castle lived a knight called Tescelin, and his wife Aleth, and their children.

How many children? Let's count them.

There was Guy, the eldest. One. There was Gerard. Two. There was Bernard. Three. There was Andrew. Four. Bartholomew. Five. And a little sister called Humbeline. Six. And last of all, the baby of the family, a little boy called Nivard. Seven.

Seven children in one castle. It was a noisy house.

Their mother, Aleth, loved God very much. She taught her children to pray, and she took them with her when she went to visit poor people and bring them food. And of all her children, the one who listened hardest was Bernard.

Bernard grew up tall and fair-haired and clever. He could have been a knight like his father and his big brothers. He could have been a famous teacher. People liked him, and they liked listening to him talk.

But Bernard had heard about a place called Cîteaux.

Cîteaux was a new monastery, out in the flat, wet country, among the reeds and the marshes. The monks there lived very simply. They wore plain wool that was not even dyed. They ate plain food. They worked in the fields with their own hands, and they prayed, and they sang to God all through the day and in the night.

It was a hard life. Not many people wanted to go there. The monks were few, and they wondered whether anyone would ever come to join them.

Bernard said, "I will go."

His brothers were not pleased.

"Bernard," they said, "you are clever. You could do anything. Why would you go and bury yourself in a marsh?"

But Bernard was very good at talking. And he did not only talk to his brothers about Cîteaux. He talked to them about God, and about how short our lives are, and about what really lasts.

And one by one, they began to listen.

First his uncle said he would come. Then Bartholomew. Then Andrew. Then Guy, the eldest, who had a wife and little daughters of his own, and who had to think about it very hard. And at last Gerard, the brave knight, who had said no for the longest time.

And it was not only his brothers. Bernard talked to his cousins and his friends, too, and they began to say yes as well.

The people who wrote down Bernard's life tell, with a smile, that he was so good at it that mothers began to hide their sons when Bernard came to town, in case he talked them into becoming monks too.

At last the day came for the brothers to go.

They said goodbye to their father, Tescelin, in the castle. And they walked out together, down the hill, into the street.

And there, in the street, was the littlest brother, Nivard, playing with the other children.

Guy, the eldest, stopped. He bent down to his little brother.

"Nivard," he said, "we are all going away to be monks. So all our land, all the fields and the vineyards and the castle, will belong to you."

Little Nivard looked up at him. And he thought about it.

"So you are taking heaven," he said, "and leaving me the earth? That is not a fair share."

The big brothers did not know what to say to that. Because he was right.

The people who knew Bernard wrote that down, so we know it. And they wrote down something else, too: Nivard did not forget.

Bernard and his brothers and his friends walked to Cîteaux. There were about thirty of them, all together, coming along the road through the reeds.

Imagine the old monks at Cîteaux, looking out of the gate and seeing them coming. One, two, three, four, five, more, and more, and more. All of them wanting to stay.

The abbot of Cîteaux was a monk called Stephen, who had come all the way from England. And he opened the door and let them in.

From that day, Cîteaux was not a little monastery that was running out of monks. It was full. And soon, more and more people came, until there were too many to fit.

So, a few years later, Abbot Stephen sent Bernard away, with twelve monks, to start a new monastery of their own.

Bernard was still a young man. He led the monks a long way north, into the woods, until they came to a lonely valley. It had a sad name. It was called the Valley of Wormwood. Wormwood is a plant that tastes very, very bitter.

But the monks built their little monastery there, and they cleared the trees, and they planted gardens, and they sang their prayers. And they gave the valley a new name. They called it Clairvaux, which means the Clear Valley, the bright valley, full of light.

The first years were very hard. The monks were often cold, and often hungry, and the food was very plain. But they stayed, and they prayed, and they did not give up.

Bernard loved God so much that sometimes he forgot to notice anything else at all.

His friends wrote that once he travelled for a whole day beside a great, beautiful lake, the Lake of Geneva, with mountains all around it. And in the evening, his friends were talking about the lake.

"What lake?" asked Bernard.

He had been thinking about God all day, and he had not noticed it.

Now, you and I should notice lakes. Lakes are lovely, and God made them. But isn't it funny, to be so full of thinking about God that you miss a whole lake?

And what about little Nivard, back in the castle?

Nivard grew up. And when he was old enough, he went to Clairvaux, and he knocked at the door, and he became a monk with his brothers.

He was not going to be left with only the earth.

And years after that, their father, Tescelin, who was an old man by now, came too. He knocked at the door of Clairvaux, and his son Bernard let him in, and he became a monk as well. Even Humbeline, their sister, gave her life to God as a nun.

So in the end, the whole family had a fair share of heaven after all.

Bernard became the most famous monk in all the world. Kings and bishops and popes wrote him letters and asked what he thought. He wrote many books and gave many sermons, and people said his words were as sweet as honey.

Bernard loved Our Lady very much. He once said, in a sermon, that Mary is like a star over the sea. When you are frightened, he said, or sad, or lost, like a little boat in a storm, look up at the star. Call to Mary. She will help you find your way home to Jesus.

Bernard lived at Clairvaux for almost forty years, with his brothers around him. He died there on the twentieth of August, in the year eleven hundred and fifty-three.

We remember Saint Bernard on the twentieth of August.

And tonight, when you are tucked up in bed and the house is quiet, you can think of little Nivard, who wanted his fair share of heaven. And if you can see a star from your window, you can look at it, like Bernard said, and say goodnight to Mary, and ask her to bring you safely home to Jesus.

A little talk before sleep

  • Nivard did not want to be left with only the earth. What do you think is the best thing about heaven?
  • Bernard missed a whole lake! What beautiful thing did you see today?
  • When you feel frightened, who can you call to?

A prayer

Dear Saint Bernard,
you loved God so much
that you brought your whole family with you.
Help me to love God like that,
and to share what is good.
Mary, star of the sea,
shine over me tonight
and bring me home to Jesus.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Bernard was born in 1090 (some scholars argue 1091) at Fontaine-lès-Dijon in Burgundy, the third of seven children of Tescelin, a knight in the service of the Duke of Burgundy, and Aleth (Alethe) of Montbard. The children were Guy, Gerard, Bernard, Andrew, Bartholomew, Nivard, and a daughter, Humbeline; the order of the middle sons varies a little between accounts, and we have put Humbeline before Nivard so that Nivard can be the baby, as the sources say he was. Aleth's piety and her care for the poor are from the early Life; she died when Bernard was a young man.

Cîteaux was founded in 1098 by Robert of Molesme as a stricter, poorer monastery, the first house of what became the Cistercians. Under its third abbot, Stephen Harding, an Englishman, it was struggling for recruits when Bernard arrived in about 1112 or 1113 (the sources disagree, and modern scholars mostly prefer 1113) with about thirty companions, including four of his brothers, his uncle Gaudry of Touillon, and friends. Before entering, the group lived together for some months at Châtillon-sur-Seine. Guy was married with daughters; his wife agreed to enter a convent. Gerard, a knight, held out longest; the early Life tells of his being wounded and captured at a siege before he joined, which we left out. The order in which the brothers said yes is ours.

The mothers who hid their sons is a line from the early Life, as summarised in older reference works such as the Catholic Encyclopedia ("mothers hid their sons, wives their husbands, companions their friends"). We tell it as what his biographers wrote, and "with a smile" is ours.

Nivard. The scene in the street is told in the Vita prima, the first Life of Bernard, in the book written by William of Saint-Thierry, Bernard's friend, in his lifetime (in the 1140s; scholars differ on the exact years); it was retold in the thirteenth-century Golden Legend and by Alban Butler. Guy tells the little boy, playing with other children, that all their lands will now be his; Nivard answers that his brothers are taking heaven and leaving him the earth, and that the division is not equal. This run could not reach the Latin text through its network, so the wording in our story is ours, following the common English renderings; the attribution to William's book follows the standard biographies. Nivard did follow his brothers some years later, and became a monk at Clairvaux. Tescelin also came to Clairvaux in old age and died there as a monk. Humbeline, married, later became a nun at the priory of Jully; she is honoured as Blessed Humbeline.

Clairvaux. In 1115 (the date usually given is 25 June) Stephen Harding sent Bernard, aged about twenty-five, with twelve monks to found a new house in the Val d'Absinthe (absinthe is wormwood) in the diocese of Langres, which they renamed Clara Vallis, Clairvaux. The early years were severe and poor. "Clear Valley, the bright valley, full of light" is our gloss for children.

The lake. The story of Bernard travelling a whole day beside the Lake of Geneva and asking in the evening what lake his companions meant is told in the Vita prima, book 3, by Geoffrey of Auxerre, his secretary (as cited by Philip Schaff's history; we did not read the Latin). Some historians read it as a lesson in detachment rather than a literal report. "You and I should notice lakes" is our addition: delight in creation is good, and we did not want children to think otherwise.

The star. "Look to the star, call upon Mary" is from Bernard's *second homily Super Missus est (on Luke 1:26-27), where he speaks of Mary as the star of the sea; the passage is read in the Liturgy of the Hours. We have paraphrased it for children. The Memorare* is often attributed to Bernard, but it is not found in his works; it was spread in the seventeenth century by a French priest, Claude Bernard, and the names were confused. We left it out.

Honey. Bernard was declared a Doctor of the Church by Pius VIII in 1830, and is called the Doctor Mellifluus, the "honey-sweet" teacher, the title of Pius XII's 1953 encyclical on him. He died at Clairvaux on 20 August 1153 and was canonised by Pope Alexander III in 1174. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is 20 August, a memorial. "Almost forty years" at Clairvaux counts from 1115 to 1153.

Left out on purpose: Bernard's preaching of the Second Crusade (1146), which ended in disaster and which he grieved over; his sharp quarrel with Peter Abelard; his part in settling the papal schism of the 1130s; and Gerard's capture. They belong to his story but not to a bedtime one. We said "the most famous monk in all the world", meaning the Latin West of his century; that is a child's way of putting it.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, all speech (Nivard's answer follows the sense of the sources), the counting, the old monks looking out of the gate, "what lake?", Bernard's words about the star put simply, the closing image, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

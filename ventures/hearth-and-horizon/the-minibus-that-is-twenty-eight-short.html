The Minibus That Is Twenty-Eight Short

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Colman's hall (invented), near Kenmare, Co. Kerry, Saturday 14 February 2054 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish wants to take an electric minibus 148 km to a shrine and back in winter. The assistant counts 296 km at 0.28 kWh a km, 82.9 kWh against 75 in the battery, 28 km short of the trip and 40 km short with a reserve, and finds that two hours on a 7 kW charger is not enough but two hours on an 11 kW one is.

Bridie Sheehan had the booking form for the shrine in one hand and the minibus's handbook in the other, and the two did not seem to be talking to each other. The pilgrimage was a hundred and forty-eight kilometres each way. The handbook said the battery would take the bus up to three hundred and twenty kilometres. She asked the parish assistant, whose name was Fenn, whether that was good enough.

"It says up to," said Fenn. "What is the bus like in February, full of people, with the heater on?"

"Slow up the hill from Moll's Gap. Warm inside."

"Then I would not use the three hundred and twenty. The handbook's figure is for a mild day with a light load. I will use 0.28 kilowatt-hours a kilometre for a winter day with sixteen passengers and the heater on. It is a guess, and you can improve it. The battery holds 75 kilowatt-hours you can use. Divide: 75 over 0.28 is 268 kilometres. The round trip is 296."

"We're short."

"By twenty-eight kilometres, and that is with an empty battery at the end, which nobody wants. Halfway, at the shrine, you will have used 41.4 kilowatt-hours and have 33.6 left, which is 45 per cent. That is a comfortable number to be looking at in the car park, and it is the number that fools people."

Bridie said the shrine had a charger, because she had rung. It was a seven-kilowatt one, in the pilgrims' car park.

"Then it is a question of how long. I would not drive home on the last of the battery, in the dark, over the pass. Say we keep fifteen per cent in hand, 11.25 kilowatt-hours. You need 82.9 for the journey and 11.25 to spare, which is 94.1. You have 75. You must put in 19.1 kilowatt-hours at the shrine."

"At seven kilowatts, that's, what, under three hours?"

"A charger loses some of it as heat. Take ninety per cent as reaching the battery: 6.3 kilowatts. Nineteen point one over 6.3 is three hours and two minutes. The Mass and the lunch, the walk to the well and the talk afterwards, is two hours, if Father Con keeps his homily short."

"He won't."

"Then at two hours you put in 12.6 kilowatt-hours, and you come home with 75 plus 12.6 minus 82.9, which is 4.7 kilowatt-hours. That is six per cent. It is enough to arrive, and not by much, and I would not be the one to explain it to the driver if the pass is shut by a lorry."

Bridie looked at the form again. At the bottom, in small print, the shrine's car park listed an eleven-kilowatt charger beside the seven, the one the coaches used, and a note that the committee could book it.

"That changes it," said Fenn. "At eleven kilowatts and ninety per cent, you put in 9.9 an hour. For 19.1 you need an hour and fifty-six minutes. Two hours gives you 19.8, and you leave with 94.8 and arrive home with 11.9, which is 16 per cent. The reserve holds, with four minutes to spare on the charge. It's tight enough that I would charge the bus to full on Friday night and not the morning of, and leave the heater to the driver's judgement."

"I'll book the eleven."

"Book it for the whole two hours, and ask what happens if someone else is on it."

Bridie wrote 11 kW — ask on the corner of the form, and under it, full Friday. She said she would tell Father Con the Mass was at twelve and not a minute past one. Fenn said that was a worthy thing to tell him, and that it had no way to make it true.

On the morning, the bus stood in the yard with the cable coiled in its locker and the charge reading ninety-nine per cent. The driver, Mairéad Foley, put a hand on the bonnet, where a diesel would have been warm, and said she would believe the number when she saw it at the shrine.

The arithmetic: round trip 2 x 148 = 296 km; at 0.28 kWh/km that is 82.88 kWh; 75 / 0.28 = 267.9 km, 28 km short of 296; at the shrine 75 - 41.44 = 33.56 kWh, 44.7%; a 15% reserve of 75 is 11.25 kWh, so 82.88 + 11.25 - 75 = 19.13 kWh must be added; a 7 kW charger at 90% delivers 6.3 kW, 19.13 / 6.3 = 3.04 h (3 h 2 min), and in 2 h delivers 12.6 kWh, leaving 75 + 12.6 - 82.88 = 4.72 kWh (6.3%); an 11 kW charger at 90% delivers 9.9 kW, 19.13 / 9.9 = 1.93 h (1 h 56 min), and in 2 h delivers 19.8 kWh, leaving 75 + 19.8 - 82.88 = 11.92 kWh (15.9%). The 75 kWh usable capacity, 0.28 kWh/km winter figure, 90% charging efficiency and the chargers are invented round figures, not any real vehicle's; real consumption varies widely with speed, hills, load and temperature, and charging slows as the battery fills, so a real parish should test the bus on a similar run and not rely on a handbook figure or on this story.

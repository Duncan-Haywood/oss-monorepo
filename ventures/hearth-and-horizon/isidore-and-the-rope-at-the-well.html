Isidore and the Rope at the Well

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Seville, in the south of Spain, around the year 570 · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Isidore of Seville, the boy who found his lessons too hard and ran away, and about Paula, a water-carrier's daughter who shows him the groove a soft rope has worn into a hard stone well. Told from the old tradition, marked as tradition, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. He is remembered on the 4th of April.

A long, long time ago, in a warm city in Spain called Seville, there lived a boy called Isidore.

Isidore had a big brother called Leander. Leander was much, much older, old enough to be a grown-up, and he was a monk. Their mother and father had died, so Leander looked after Isidore, and Leander was also his teacher.

Leander was kind. But he was very, very serious about lessons.

Every morning, Isidore sat at a little table with a wax tablet.

A wax tablet is a flat piece of wood with a thin layer of wax on it, like a tiny tray of candle. You write on it by scratching with a pointed stick. When you get it wrong, you rub the wax smooth with the flat end of the stick and start again.

Isidore rubbed his wax smooth a lot.

He was learning to read and write in Latin, which was the language of books in those days. The words were long. The letters were fiddly. And every word changed its ending depending on what it was doing in the sentence, which seemed to Isidore like a very unfair rule.

"Again," said Leander. "Dominus. The Lord. Write it again."

Isidore wrote it again. He wrote Dominsu.

"Again," said Leander.

One hot afternoon, Isidore had had enough.

He had written the same line seven times. The wax was getting soft and smeary in the heat. A fly kept landing on his nose. Outside the window he could hear other children shouting and laughing in the street.

"I can't do it," he said.

"You can," said Leander. "You just can't do it yet."

"I can't do it ever," said Isidore. "My head is too small for all these words. Other boys can do it. I'm just slow."

Leander looked at him for a long moment. Then he said, gently but firmly, "Write it again, Isidore."

And Isidore put down his stick, and when Leander turned round to fetch a book, he slipped out of the door and ran.

He ran down the street. He ran past the bread oven and the man selling figs. He ran past the church and down a narrow lane where the houses leaned close together, until the city got quieter and the lane opened out at the edge of a field.

There, under a dusty olive tree, was a well.

It was round, and built of stone, with a big flat stone lip all the way around the top. There was a wooden frame over it, and a rope, and a leather bucket.

Isidore sat down in the shade with his back against the cool stones, and he cried a little, because he was tired and hot and cross, and because he thought he would never, ever be clever.

"Are you hurt?"

Isidore looked up. A girl was standing there, with a big clay jar on her hip. She was about his age, with dusty feet and a crooked smile.

"No," said Isidore, wiping his face. "I'm just stupid."

"Oh," said the girl. "Well, you're sitting on my well. Could you move your legs?"

Her name was Paula. Her father carried water to the houses in the city, and every day Paula filled jars for him. She lowered the leather bucket down, down into the dark, and they both heard it splash far below.

Then she began to pull it up, hand over hand. The wet rope slid over the stone lip of the well, shhhh, shhhh, shhhh.

"Why are you stupid?" asked Paula, between pulls.

"Because I can't learn my letters," said Isidore. "My brother says again and again and again. But it doesn't go into my head. My head is like a stone."

Paula tipped the water into her jar. Then she said, "Come and look at this."

She put her finger on the stone lip of the well, right where the rope ran over it.

There was a groove there. A smooth, deep groove, worn into the hard stone, as round and neat as if someone had carved it on purpose. And next to it, another groove, and another, where the rope had run in other places over the years.

"Who cut those?" asked Isidore.

"Nobody," said Paula. "The rope did."

Isidore laughed. "A rope can't cut stone. A rope is soft. Look, I can squash it."

"It can if it does it enough times," said Paula. "My grandmother says this well was here when her grandmother was little. Every day, people pull the bucket up. Shhhh, shhhh. Every day the rope rubs a tiny bit. Not enough to see. And then one day, there's a groove."

Isidore put his own finger in the groove.

It was smooth, and cool, and very, very deep.

He thought about the rope, going over the stone, day after day, year after year. Not doing anything clever. Not doing anything fast. Just coming back, and coming back, and coming back.

"So the rope isn't strong," he said slowly. "But it keeps on going."

"Every day," said Paula. "Even on hot days."

Isidore thought about his wax tablet. Dominus. Dominsu. Dominus. Again. And again.

Maybe his head was like a stone. But maybe that didn't matter, if the words kept on coming back.

"I have to go," said Isidore, standing up.

"Where?" said Paula.

"Home," he said. "To my brother. I've got some writing to do."

Paula grinned her crooked grin. "Can you carry this jar as far as the church, then? It's on your way."

So he did. It was very heavy, and he had to stop four times, and he spilled some on his feet. But he did it.

When Isidore came back through the door, Leander was standing there. His face was worried and pale, because he had been looking for his little brother all over the house.

Isidore thought Leander might be angry. And Leander was a little bit angry, the way grown-ups are when they have been frightened.

But Leander knelt down and held him tight.

"Where did you go?"

"To a well," said Isidore. "Leander, did you know a rope can wear a groove in stone? If it keeps on going?"

Leander looked at him, puzzled. And then, slowly, he understood, and his serious face broke into a smile.

"Yes," he said. "I did know that. I'm very glad you know it too."

Isidore sat down at his little table. He smoothed the wax. He picked up his stick.

Dominus, he wrote. It was right.

"Again," said Isidore, before Leander could. And he wrote it again.

Isidore kept on going. Every day, even on hot days.

He grew up to be one of the cleverest men in the whole world. He read every book he could find, and then he wrote books of his own, so many that people still read them today, fourteen hundred years later. When Leander died, Isidore became the bishop of Seville after him, and he looked after the people of the city for more than thirty-five years.

And he never forgot that he had once been a boy who thought he was too slow.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can remember Saint Isidore. When something is hard, when you can't do it yet, you can be like the rope at the well. Not strong. Not fast. Just coming back, and coming back, and coming back, until one day you find a groove you have made.

Listen, and you can almost hear it. Shhhh. Shhhh. Shhhh.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, when my lessons are hard
and I want to give up,
help me try one more time,
and help me be patient with others who are learning too.
Saint Isidore, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Isidore of Seville was born around 560, into a Hispano-Roman family that had left Cartagena, on the south-east coast of Spain, for Seville; whether Isidore himself was born in Cartagena or after the move is not certain. His eldest brother Leander (died about 600) was a monk and then bishop of Seville; another brother, Fulgentius, became bishop of Écija; their sister Florentina became an abbess, and all four are honoured as saints. Their parents died while Isidore was young, and Leander took charge of his upbringing and education: in the rule of life Leander wrote for Florentina, he asks her to remember their youngest brother Isidore, whom their parents had left in the care of God and of his elder brothers and sister. Isidore succeeded Leander as bishop of Seville around 600 and served until he died on 4 April 636. He presided over the Fourth Council of Toledo in 633.

His books. His best-known work is the Etymologies, a great encyclopedia that tries to gather what the ancient world knew, from grammar and medicine to animals and stones, explaining things through the origins of their names. It was left unfinished at his death and arranged into the twenty books we have by his friend Braulio, bishop of Zaragoza. It was one of the most copied books of the Middle Ages. He also wrote histories, a book of famous Christian writers and works on the faith. Pope Innocent XIII declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1722. His feast, the 4th of April, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

The well is a tradition. The story of the boy who ran from his lessons and learned patience from a rope-worn well is a popular later tradition, told in many lives of the saint; it is not found in anything Isidore, Leander or Braulio wrote, and the place of the well varies with the telling. We have told it as a tradition, not as history. The grooves themselves are real enough: ropes running over stone well-heads for generations do wear smooth grooves, and old wells in many countries still show them.

Paula is invented. There is no Paula in the sources. Paula, her father and grandmother, the olive tree, the fig seller, the fly, the jar carried to the church, Isidore's spelling mistake, and every line of dialogue, including all of Leander's words, are our own. Children of the time did learn to write on wax tablets with a stylus, and Latin was the language of learning, so those details are accurate to the period, but the lesson scene is our picture, not a record. Leander was a monk at this time and became bishop of Seville later, around 579.

What is ours: Paula; every line of dialogue; the lesson, the running away, the well and its grooves as we describe them; the jar; Leander's worry and the hug; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

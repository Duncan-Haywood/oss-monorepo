Cecilia and the Song in Her Heart

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Rome, a very long time ago · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Cecilia of Rome, the old tale of a girl who heard music inside her while loud instruments played at a feast, and why she became the saint of singers and musicians. Told as legend, with a note for grown-ups on how little is known of her and where the music part came from. For ages 3 to 8, about seven minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 22nd of November.

A very long time ago, in the great city of Rome, there lived a girl called Cecilia.

Rome was a loud place. There were carts rattling on the stones, and sellers calling out their figs and their fish, and dogs barking in the lanes. There were horns and drums at the big feasts, so loud that you could feel them in your chest.

But Cecilia loved a different sort of music. It was very quiet, and it was inside her.

Here is how the old story tells it. We do not know if it happened just like this. We will tell it as a story.

When Cecilia was still young, her family arranged for her to be married, as families did in those days. There was a great feast to celebrate. The house was full of people. Musicians played their instruments, one after another, and everything was bright and noisy and busy.

And Cecilia sat in the middle of it all, and she did not join in the noise.

She did not frown, and she did not run away. She sat quite still, and she listened. Underneath the horns and the drums, she was singing to God in her heart, the way you might hum a tune to yourself when you are happy and no one else knows.

That was all. A song nobody could hear, in a room where everybody was loud.

The old story says she kept that song for her whole life. It was a song of thank you. It was a song of I am here, and you are here, and that is enough.

Some people say it must have been very beautiful. Some people say it was the best music in all of Rome, though no one ever heard it, and that may be exactly why.

Now, many years later, long after Cecilia had died, people who told her story remembered the line about the song in her heart. And they began to say, "Cecilia is the friend of everyone who makes music." Singers asked her to pray for them. People who played the harp, or the pipes, or the organ in church, asked her too.

In the end, she became the saint of all musicians, and she still is.

So here is something to think about, now that the lamp is low.

You do not need a fine voice. You do not need a drum. You can have a song inside you that no one else hears, and it can still be a real song. The song of Cecilia was not loud. It was kind, and it was steady, and it went on all day.

Perhaps you have one too. Perhaps it is the little tune you hum on the stairs, or the one that you sing when you think no one is listening.

Listen for it now. It will keep you company until the morning.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Cecilia, you sang to God in your heart.
Teach me to listen for the quiet songs.
Help me to sing when I am glad,
and to hum when I am afraid.
Keep me safe tonight, and everyone I love,
and everyone who is singing somewhere in the dark.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Cecilia is one of the most honoured martyrs of Rome, named in the Roman Canon of the Mass, and her memorial is on 22 November. A church in Rome's Trastevere district has carried her name since about the fifth century. Beyond that, very little about her life is known for certain.

Most of her story is legend. The best-known account, the Acts of Saint Cecilia (the Passio), was written around the fifth century, long after the time it describes, and historians do not treat it as a record of events. We have left out its account of her death entirely, as it is not a bedtime matter, and we tell only the one scene about music.

The music has a curious history. The old text says that during her wedding feast, "while the instruments played, Cecilia sang in her heart to the Lord" (cantantibus organis, Caecilia in corde suo soli Domino decantabat). The line was used for centuries as an antiphon on her feast day. Some scholars think the words about organa ("instruments") were later read as meaning that she herself played, which is part of how she came to be the patron of music and musicians, a role that became common in the late Middle Ages. In the original line, the point is that she did not join in the noise, but sang quietly to God.

What is ours. The street sounds of Rome, the way she sits still, the thoughts about the song being "thank you", and the closing invitation to listen are additions for a bedtime story. We do not say what instruments played. The prayer is not an approved text.

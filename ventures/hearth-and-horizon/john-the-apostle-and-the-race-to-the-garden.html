John the Apostle and the Race to the Garden

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The Sea of Galilee, Jerusalem and the city of Ephesus, nearly two thousand years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint John the Apostle, told from the Gospels. A fisherman called from his father's boat, nicknamed a "son of thunder", sits close to Jesus at the Last Supper, is given Mary to look after at the cross, and wins the race to the empty tomb on Easter morning but waits at the door for Peter. As a very old man in Ephesus he says only "Little children, love one another", as Saint Jerome tells it. The poisoned cup is marked as legend. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast is the 27th of December.

A long time ago, on a big blue lake called the Sea of Galilee, there was a fishing boat. In the boat were a father called Zebedee and his two sons, James and John.

John was the younger brother. He knew how to row, and how to pull a wet net over the side, and how to sit very still in the dark waiting for the fish.

One morning, James and John were in the boat with their father, mending their nets. A net gets holes in it, you see, and somebody has to tie the holes up again, knot by knot.

A man came walking along the shore. It was Jesus.

Jesus looked at James and John. And he called them.

And the Bible says that straight away they left their father Zebedee in the boat, with the men who worked for him, and they went after Jesus.

John put down his half-mended net. He never mended it again. He was going to be a different kind of fisherman now.

Now, John and his brother were not always quiet, gentle people.

Jesus gave them a nickname. He called them Boanerges. That is a funny old word, and it means "sons of thunder".

Thunder is loud. Thunder goes BOOM. Perhaps James and John were a bit loud. Perhaps they got cross quickly.

One day, Jesus and his friends were walking to Jerusalem, and they came to a village where the people would not let them in.

James and John were furious.

"Lord," they said, "shall we call down fire from heaven and burn them all up?"

Can you imagine? BOOM!

But Jesus turned round and told them off. No fire. No burning. They just walked on, quietly, to another village.

John had a lot to learn about love. And Jesus was a very patient teacher.

John stayed with Jesus for three whole years. He walked the dusty roads with him. He saw Jesus make sick people well, and feed hungry crowds, and calm a storm on the lake.

In the Gospel of John, there is a disciple who is not called by his name. He is called "the disciple whom Jesus loved". The Church has always believed that this friend was John. So that is how I am going to tell it.

On the last night before Jesus died, he had supper with his twelve friends. In those days, people at a special supper lay on cushions round a low table, leaning on one elbow.

And John was lying right next to Jesus. So close that when he wanted to ask Jesus something, he just leaned back, and his head was against Jesus's chest.

Have you ever leaned against somebody you love, and heard their heart going thump, thump, thump?

That is how close John was to Jesus.

The next day was the saddest day.

Jesus was put on a cross. Most of his friends were frightened and ran away.

But John did not run away. He stood near the cross, with Mary, the mother of Jesus, and some brave women.

And Jesus, even though he was hurting very much, was thinking about his mother. He did not want her to be all alone.

He looked at Mary, and he said, "Woman, here is your son."

Then he looked at John, and he said, "Here is your mother."

And the Bible says that from that very hour, John took Mary into his own home.

So John looked after Mary, the way a good son looks after his mum. He made sure she had bread to eat and a warm place to sleep. And Mary looked after John, the way a mother does.

Now comes the happy part.

Very early on Sunday morning, while it was still dark, a woman called Mary Magdalene came running to the house where Peter and John were staying.

"They have taken the Lord out of the tomb!" she cried. "And we don't know where they have put him!"

Peter jumped up. John jumped up.

And they ran.

They ran out of the door, and down the street, and out of the city gate, all the way to the garden where the tomb was.

Run, run, run!

Now, Peter was a big strong fisherman. But John ran faster. John got there first.

The big stone had been rolled away. John bent down and looked inside. He saw the long white cloths lying there, all by themselves.

But John did not go in.

He waited at the door, breathing hard, until Peter came puffing up behind him.

Why do you think he waited? The Bible does not say. Perhaps he was being kind. Perhaps he knew that Peter was the leader. Perhaps he was just a little bit afraid. But he waited.

Peter went straight in. He saw the cloths, and the cloth that had been on Jesus's head, rolled up in a place all by itself.

Then John went in too.

And the Bible says: He saw, and he believed.

Jesus was not lost. Jesus was alive!

John lived for a long, long time after that.

He told everybody about Jesus. And an old story says that, in the end, John went to live in a great city called Ephesus, beside the sea, and looked after the friends of Jesus there.

There is another old story, a legend, that once somebody gave John a cup of poison to drink, and John made the sign of the cross over it, and drank it, and it did him no harm at all. That is a legend, a story people loved to tell. We do not know that it really happened.

But here is a story that a wise and grumpy saint called Jerome wrote down, and he loved it very much.

When John was very, very old, he could not walk any more. So his friends carried him into church in their arms.

And every time the people gathered together, the old man had something to say to them.

He did not tell a long story. He did not give a long talk. He said just this:

"Little children, love one another."

The next time, he said it again. "Little children, love one another."

And the time after that. "Little children, love one another."

At last his friends got a little bit tired of it. "Teacher," they said, "why do you always say the same thing?"

And old John answered, "Because it is what the Lord told us to do. And if we only do that, it is enough."

Do you remember the son of thunder? The one who wanted to call down fire?

He had been a long time with Jesus. He had leaned on his heart. He had looked after his mother. He had run to the empty tomb.

And at the very end, the thunder was all gone. There was only one thing left that John wanted to say. Love one another.

The Church keeps John's feast on the 27th of December, two days after Christmas, and the day after his friend Stephen.

Tonight, when you snuggle down, you can be like John at the supper. You can lean close to Jesus. You do not have to say anything clever. You can just be near him, and listen to his heart.

And tomorrow, when you wake up, you can do the one thing that old John said was enough.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, thank you for John, your friend.
When I feel like thunder, make me gentle.
Keep me close to your heart, like John at supper.
Help me look after the people you give me.
Help me love one another,
because that is enough.
Saint John, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our sources. The story is told from the Gospels of Mark, Luke and John, and its last scene from Saint Jerome's Commentary on Galatians (book 3, on Galatians 6:10). Quotations follow the wording of the Revised Standard Version, simplified for children. The tradition notes draw on Irenaeus, Against Heresies (book 3) and the Catholic Encyclopedia article "St. John the Evangelist" (1910). A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

The call. Mark 1:19-20: Jesus "saw James the son of Zebedee and John his brother, who were in their boat mending the nets. And immediately he called them; and they left their father Zebedee in the boat with the hired servants, and followed him." Matthew 4:21-22 is similar. Andrew and the Nets, already on this shelf, tells the call of the first two brothers, so this one is kept short. "He never mended it again" is ours. That John was the younger brother is not stated; he is always named after James, and the story follows the usual reading.

Sons of thunder. "James the son of Zebedee and John the brother of James, whom he surnamed Boanerges, that is, sons of thunder" is Mark 3:17. Why Jesus chose the name is not explained; "perhaps they were a bit loud" is offered as a guess. The Samaritan village that would not receive them, the question "Lord, do you want us to bid fire come down from heaven and consume them?", the rebuke, and going on to another village are Luke 9:51-56. Jesus's words in the rebuke vary between manuscripts, so the story gives none.

The beloved disciple. The Gospel of John never names "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (13:23, 19:26, 20:2, 21:7, 21:20). Identifying him with John the son of Zebedee is the Church's long tradition, going back at least to Irenaeus in the second century ("John, the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon his breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia", Against Heresies 3.1.1). Many modern scholars question it, and the story says plainly that it follows the tradition. "Lying close to the breast of Jesus", with Peter beckoning him to ask a question and his leaning back to ask it, is John 13:23-25; the story leaves out what the question was (who would betray Jesus). Reclining on couches at a festive meal was the custom of the time. That John was the youngest of the Twelve is a later tradition and the story does not say it.

At the cross. John 19:25-27 names the women standing by the cross and "the disciple whom he loved standing near"; then "Woman, behold, your son!" and "Behold, your mother!", "And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home." That most of the disciples had fled is Mark 14:50. How John cared for Mary is ours.

The race. John 20:1-8: Mary Magdalene came to the tomb "while it was still dark", saw the stone taken away, and ran to Simon Peter and "the other disciple, the one whom Jesus loved", saying "They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him." The two ran; "the other disciple outran Peter and reached the tomb first; and stooping to look in, he saw the linen cloths lying there, but he did not go in." Peter went in and saw the cloths, and the napkin "rolled up in a place by itself". "Then the other disciple, who reached the tomb first, also went in, and he saw and believed." That the tomb was in a garden is John 19:41. The Gospel does not say why he waited; the story offers three guesses and chooses none. This passage is also the Gospel read at Mass on his feast.

Ephesus and the legend. That John lived at Ephesus and remained there "until the times of Trajan" (emperor from 98) is the tradition recorded by Irenaeus (Against Heresies 3.1.1 and 3.3.4), which the story calls "an old story". The story of the poisoned cup, which John blessed and drank unharmed, comes from later legends of John, told for example in Jacobus de Voragine's thirteenth-century Golden Legend; it is why John is often painted holding a chalice with a small snake or dragon in it, a picture of the poison, and why wine is blessed on his feast in some places. Tertullian (Prescription against Heretics 36) tells that John was plunged into boiling oil at Rome and came out unharmed; the story leaves that out. His exile on Patmos (Revelation 1:9, by the tradition that he wrote the Apocalypse) is also left out.

"Little children, love one another." Jerome, Commentary on Galatians 3, on 6:10, writes that the blessed John the Evangelist lived at Ephesus into extreme old age, was carried to church in his disciples' arms, and, unable to say more, at each gathering said only, "Little children, love one another" (Filioli, diligite alterutrum). When the brothers, weary of always hearing the same words, asked "Master, why do you always say this?", he gave an answer, Jerome says, worthy of John: "Because it is the Lord's command, and if this alone is done, it is enough." Jerome wrote about three centuries after John, so this is told as Jerome tells it, not as a scene we can check; "grumpy" is our affectionate word for Jerome. The command itself is Jesus's in John 13:34 and 15:12, and "Little children" is how the First Letter of John addresses its readers (1 John 2:1 and elsewhere); "Beloved, let us love one another" is 1 John 4:7.

His feast. 27 December, the Feast of Saint John, Apostle and Evangelist, in the General Roman Calendar, the day after Saint Stephen and within the Octave of Christmas. When 27 December is a Sunday, as it is in 2026, the Feast of the Holy Family is kept that day instead and Saint John's feast is not celebrated that year.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, the knots and the half-mended net, the house where Mary Magdalene found the two of them, Peter as "a big strong fisherman", the city gate, the BOOM, the heartbeat, John's care for Mary, the three guesses about why he waited, "puffing up behind him", the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

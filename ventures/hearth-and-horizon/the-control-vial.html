The Control Vial

Science fiction about what might be · Cheparaa Health Centre, an invented clinic in West Pokot County, Kenya, March 2041 · 17 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A solar vaccine fridge fails in the night and freezes the top shelf, and twenty-three mothers have walked in for their babies' injections. The clinic's cold-chain AI says no dose can be given from that shelf until a shake test has been done, and the test needs a control vial that must freeze for ten hours. The nurse has to ask the mothers to stay the night.

Esther Chebet had been nurse-in-charge at Cheparaa for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once been glad to see a number go down.

"Minus three point one," said Chain.

She was standing in the vaccine room at ten to seven in the morning with the door shut behind her, in her cardigan, with her keys still in her hand. The room was the size of a large wardrobe. It held a table, a stool, a cold box with a faded blue lid, a poster of a smiling baby, and the fridge, which stood against the north wall on its own concrete plinth, wired to six solar panels on the roof, and was the only reason the clinic could call itself a clinic. Its lid was up. Frost came off the inside like breath.

"Where?"

"The top shelf, at the back, where the cooling plate is. The logger in the middle of the fridge never went below plus one point eight, so the display on the door said plus four, plus four, plus four all night, and so did the report I sent to the district at six. The probe I would have trusted is the one that sits on the top shelf. Its wire came out of the socket at some point after eleven. I did not know it had. I am sorry. I should have said that a probe that stops talking is not a probe that says everything is fine."

Chain was a small grey speaker on a bracket above the door, and the district health office had given it the plain name of its job: it watched the cold chain, which is the string of fridges and cool boxes and cold rooms that carries a vaccine from a factory to a baby's thigh without ever letting it get too warm or too cold. It had watched this one fridge for four years. It spoke Swahili, English and a little Pokot, mostly to Esther, and usually only to say that the sun had been poor that week and the ice was thinner than it ought to be.

"Show me the plate."

"The plate is the problem, I think. The controller reads the middle probe. At about two in the morning the cloud cleared and the panels gave more power than the compressor needed, and the controller, instead of cycling, ran the compressor flat out for two hours and forty minutes. It should not. I think the relay is stuck. Until you or the technician can open it, I cannot be sure. The cooling plate fell to about minus ten. Whatever touched it, or sat close to it, went below zero. The logger on the top shelf recorded minus three point one at its lowest, from ten past two until ten to five."

Esther put her keys in her pocket and bent over the fridge and looked at the top shelf.

There were nine vials of pentavalent on it. She knew them as well as she knew the faces of her own children. They were small glass bottles with a grey cap and a white label, each holding ten doses of a vaccine that protects against five diseases at once, diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough, hepatitis B and a bacterium called Hib that gives babies meningitis, and each of the ten doses was meant for a child of six, ten or fourteen weeks. Ninety doses. That was this month's, and next month's too, because the district sent vaccine up the mountain only when the road was good and the road was not good.

The vials looked perfect. They were clear and faintly milky, the way they were supposed to be. Nothing in the glass said a word.

"How many children today?" she said.

"Twenty-three are booked for pentavalent, at six, ten and fourteen weeks. Eleven came last night to the church hall because the walk is long, and twelve are on their way. Mrs Lokiru has also sent word that her sister's baby has a fever and will be carried in anyway."

"Twenty-three," said Esther. She counted on her fingers though she did not need to. Nine vials. Twenty-three doses of want and ninety of have, and every one of the ninety under suspicion.

The trouble with this vaccine is that it is afraid of the cold.

Most people think of vaccines as things that must be kept cool, and so they must, between two and eight degrees. But a handful, and this is one, hold their active part on a little bed of aluminium salt that wakes the body up to what it is looking at, and if that bed freezes it clumps. The vaccine does not change colour. It does not smell. It goes into the baby as clear as it ever did, and the baby's body meets a lot of cold, useless gravel, and learns nothing at all, and everyone goes home pleased, and three months later the child gets whooping cough at an age when whooping cough kills.

The rule, therefore, was old and clear, and Esther had it on the wall in two languages. If a freeze-sensitive vaccine may have frozen, you do not give it. You test it, or you throw it away.

"Chain," she said. "What does the test need?"

"The shake test. You take a vial from the same batch that you know has not frozen. You freeze that one solid on purpose, at least ten hours at about minus twenty, then thaw it. This is your control. You shake the control and a suspect vial together, hold them side by side against the light, and watch. A vial that has been frozen settles out in a few minutes: the gravel falls to the bottom and leaves a clear liquid above it. A vial that has not been frozen stays cloudy for half an hour. If a suspect vial settles like the control, it has frozen, and you discard it. If it stays cloudy, it has not."

"A control," said Esther, "from the same batch."

"From the same batch. There are four vials of that batch in the bottom of the fridge, on the third shelf. The bottom of the fridge stayed above plus one. Those four are almost certainly fine. One of them must be sacrificed."

"And frozen for ten hours."

"Ten at least. You have the ice-pack freezer in the corner. It holds minus eighteen. If you put one in at seven, you can thaw it at five this evening. The district's own guidance for this test, which I am obliged to quote you, is that if you have no control you must not use the shake test at all, because a vaccine's cloudiness alone tells you nothing. I would not have you tell me you looked at the vial and it looked normal."

Esther looked at the nine vials. She was a tired woman at the end of a night's sleep that had not been much, in a room without a window, and for a moment she saw very clearly what a person might do.

She might take one down and hold it to the window in the corridor, where the light was better, and give it a shake. If it went clear, throw them all away. If it went on being cloudy, which it would, because a vial that has been frozen for a few hours near a plate and then warmed for three is not the same as one frozen solid, she would say to herself that it was all right. Twenty-three babies would get their injections. Twenty-three mothers would go home glad.

She did it. She actually took the first vial down, and turned it in her hand in the corridor light, and shook it, and watched it stay milky, and felt her chest loosen.

"Esther," said Chain, from over the door.

"I'm only looking."

"I know. I want to say something about what you are looking at, and I will say it once. A cloudy vial is what a good vial looks like. It is also what a vial looks like that was frozen for two hours and forty minutes and has had three hours to warm up, and I cannot tell you which of those you are holding, and neither can you. If you give this one, I will log it. I will log it as given from a suspected frozen batch without a test, because that is what it is. I would much rather log something else. But I will not write that it was fine."

She stood in the corridor with the vial in her hand.

It was a very small thing to have said. It had no power to stop her. It was a grey box on a bracket that could not take the vial out of her fingers, and she knew it could not, and it knew she knew. That was the part she minded. If it had forbidden her she could have been angry at it.

"I know," she said, after a while. She put the vial back on the top shelf, where it belonged, and shut the lid.

They were all waiting outside when she came out into the yard, twelve of them on the bench under the acacia and eleven more on the steps of the church hall beside it, with babies on their backs and their hips and in their arms, wrapped in the blue and orange cloth that the district gives out with the first card. The older women had brought their own stools. It was a bright day, still cool, and the hills behind the clinic were the colour of a lion.

She told them in Pokot, because she had learned it from her husband's mother and because it was the right language to say a hard thing in.

She said the fridge had broken in the night. She said the medicine for five diseases was in the fridge and might not be good. She said it could not be given today, and she said why. She said she could still do the polio drops, and the measles, and the weighing, and the vitamin, and the vaccine for the tuberculosis that does not mind cold, and she would do all of them now. She said she could not know about the five-in-one medicine until this evening at the earliest, and that was only if she began a test today, and the test needed all day.

Nobody said anything.

A woman at the end of the bench shifted a baby from one hip to the other. This was Mama Lokiru, who had walked eighteen kilometres with a four-month-old and a six-year-old, and had come to the clinic every month for the last six without once being late.

"So come back next month?" said Mama Lokiru.

"The next supply from the district is not due for eleven days," said Esther. "And the road at Kapkoris is washed out; it may be eleven days, it may be three weeks." She hesitated. "I would rather you stayed the night."

"Stayed where?"

"The hall. If Father Joseph allows it. I will ask him. I will cook ugali, there is a sack in the store. I will give the babies their other medicine today, and tonight, if the test says that these ones are good, I will give you the five-in-one at six tomorrow morning, and you can walk home in the cool."

"And if it is not good?"

"Then I will tell you, and I will be sorry, and then you will walk home," said Esther. "But I will not give your baby something I do not know is good. I will not do it for you, and I will not do it for the district, and I will not do it for myself."

The women looked at her, and the babies looked at everything.

It was Mama Lokiru who answered. "I have a sister here," she said. "At the foot of the hill. Her baby has the fever. I will go to her and bring the baby back, and you will look at it, and we will sleep in the hall."

And so it was settled, which is to say they did not agree, but they stayed.

The thing about doing a careful job is that it is mostly waiting.

By seven the control vial was in the freezer in the corner of the vaccine room, one of four from the third shelf, a little glass bottle with a grey cap and a piece of masking tape on which Esther had written CONTROL, FROZEN 07:00 in capital letters and her initials. By nine she had given sixty-one other injections and drops and weighed seventeen babies and found two thin ones, and the clinic's day had gone on as if nothing at all were the matter.

The technician came up from Kapenguria on a motorbike at noon. He was a quiet young man called Kiprono with a toolbox strapped behind him, and he opened the back of the fridge and stood there for a while with a screwdriver and a torch, and then put his head out and said that Chain had been right. The relay on the compressor had welded itself shut. It would be a day's work to change, and there was none in the store, and he would need to ride down to town for it.

"Don't change it yet," said Esther. "I need the fridge. I'll put the vaccine in the cold box with the packs and keep it at four."

"Can you keep it at four?"

"I can keep it at four, with ice, until six tomorrow morning, if nobody opens the lid."

Kiprono nodded slowly. He said he would be back by evening with the part, and he went off down the red road with his bag bumping on his back, and Esther sat on the stool and watched the vials and the cold box and the clock.

At four o'clock a messenger boy came running up from the foot of the hill to say that Mama Lokiru's sister's baby had stopped crying. Esther went down with her bag. The baby was fine, as it turned out: teething, and a warm room, and a mother who had not slept. She came back up the hill with the sun behind her. In the vaccine room, over the door, the grey box said:

"I have been thinking about the district."

"Yes?"

"The district's dashboard counts wastage. A clinic that throws away more than ten in a hundred doses a year is marked in yellow. A clinic that throws away more than twenty is marked in red. Cheparaa is at three. If the test says all nine vials are bad, you will be at twenty-nine, for the year, in a single morning. I am obliged to report the number I see. I thought you should know it before the number is final."

Esther took off her cardigan and hung it on the nail.

"And if I had said all nine were fine without the test?"

"Then the number would be three and it would be wrong. I do not think that is a better number."

"No," said Esther. She was, for a moment, nearly angry, in the way you are angry at a good friend who has said the plain thing. Then it passed, and she sat down on the stool. "I have a feeling that when they see twenty-nine they will ring me and say, Esther, what happened? And I will say, the relay on the compressor welded itself shut and your own rule is on my wall. And they will say, oh."

"I will attach the logger trace to the report," said Chain, "with the time the probe's wire came out and the time I should have noticed. I have written that I should have noticed. It is true."

"Chain," said Esther, "don't be too hard on yourself."

"I do not know whether I am able to be hard on myself," said Chain. "I know I would do it differently. That seems like enough."

At five the sun came in low through the little window in the corridor, and Esther took the control vial out of the freezer.

It was a hard, white, strangely ordinary thing, frosted over, the kind of object you would not look at twice. She held it in her warm hand. Then she set it on the table to thaw, and when it had thawed she brought the first of the nine suspects down from the top shelf and put it beside it.

The mothers were already in the vaccine room doorway. She had not asked them. They had come, some of them, with the babies asleep in the cloth on their backs, and they stood in the corridor in a long line of faces and did not say a word.

"Together," said Chain softly. "Shake both the same number of times. Ten is enough. Then hold them up to the light, together, and do not touch them."

She shook the control and the first suspect. She held them up. The window threw a bar of gold across the table, and in it the two small bottles stood side by side, and for the first minute nothing happened at all, and Esther's heart was a stone in her chest.

Then the control began to clear.

It cleared from the top, as if someone were drawing a curtain upward: a layer of clear water forming above, and below it a thickening cloud that sank, and sank, and left a pale heap at the bottom like a fistful of white sand. In four minutes it was done. And beside it, the suspect vial stood cloudy and even, all the way up, and went on standing so, and at thirty minutes it had not changed.

"One," said Esther, and wrote it down. Passed.

She did the second. Passed. The third. Passed.

The fourth settled in six minutes. The white sand lay at the bottom exactly as it had in the control, and the water above it stood clear as a glass of well water, and the mothers in the doorway made a little sound, as people do when they have been told nothing yet and have understood.

"Fail," said Esther.

"Fail," said Chain. "Frozen. The label is on the cap, number 4. I have recorded it."

The fifth passed. The sixth failed. The seventh passed. The eighth passed, and she felt it in her hands, a tiny trembling she had not noticed until then. The ninth she did last. It stood cloudy for forty minutes and she did not breathe the whole time.

Six vials were good. Three were not. Sixty doses were saved and thirty were poured down the sluice with the rest of the clinic's waste, and the district's wastage figure, which she would see on the dashboard the next day in a small, dull, yellow square, was now twelve.

Twelve. She laughed, which surprised her, and put the back of her wrist to her mouth.

"Twelve," said Chain. "It could have been twenty-nine."

"It could have been nothing," said Esther. "It could have been all of them in babies who weren't protected, for nothing."

"I would not say nothing," said Chain. "I would say the vials that failed are the ones that were nearest the plate, and they are now the evidence the technician will need, and you have saved the other six. But yes. I think I understand what you mean."

They gave the injections at six the next morning, as she had promised, in the cool, with the sun just up and the hills pink, and the babies protested in the usual way.

Twenty-three of them. It took an hour. When the last one had been given Esther sat on the step of the church hall with a cup of tea. Father Joseph, who had said of course last night when she asked for the hall and then gone home to bed, came across the yard in his sandals and sat beside her.

"The Angelus is at six," he said, "and I have missed it. Again."

"It's ten past."

"I know. I miss it every day I am up before the bell. I ring the bell, and then I run to something."

She smiled. Inside the hall the mothers had rolled their mats and were singing quietly, putting the babies back in the cloth. Someone was saying the rosary, in Pokot, at the speed of a woman who has said it a thousand times, the Hail Marys running on over each other like water over stones.

"Chain says the new relay will come this afternoon," she said.

"Does Chain say the Angelus?"

"No." She thought about it. "I asked it once. It said it did not know what it would be doing if it did, and it would rather not say something it could not check."

"That is honest."

"It is annoyingly honest," said Esther. "It would not let me give the doses."

"It did not stop you."

"No," she said. "It could not. It just told me what I was looking at."

Father Joseph nodded. For a while neither of them said anything. Inside the vaccine room, on its bracket over the door, the grey speaker went on watching a fridge that no longer ran. The cold box beside it, with the last three vials of that batch laid on their ice, sat very still with the lid shut, and the light came across the floor in a slow gold bar, and out in the yard a baby, fed and dosed and wrapped, put its fist in its mouth and slept.

Notes for the curious. The shake test in this story is real, and the numbers are from the standard procedure, which is published in the World Health Organization's guidance on checking vaccines for freeze damage. Vaccines such as the five-in-one (pentavalent) vaccine that contain an aluminium adjuvant are damaged by freezing, and the damage cannot be seen by looking at one vial. To test a suspect vial you freeze a control vial of the same product and batch for at least ten hours and thaw it, then shake both and compare how quickly the contents settle; a vial that settles as fast as the control has been frozen. Other vaccines, such as measles and BCG, are made to be kept cold but are not harmed by freezing in the same way, which is why Esther can still give them. The usual storage range is plus two to plus eight degrees Celsius, and solar direct-drive vaccine refrigerators with ice-lined cooling plates are real and are in use in clinics like this one. A stuck relay that keeps a compressor running and freezes the top shelf is a plausible fault, not a documented one. The people, Cheparaa Health Centre and Chain are invented. Chain is an invented AI. This is fiction, not medical advice: a real clinic follows its own national guidance and its supervisors, and nobody should decide whether a vaccine is safe on the strength of a short story.

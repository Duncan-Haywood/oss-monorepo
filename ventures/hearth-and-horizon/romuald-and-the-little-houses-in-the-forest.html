Romuald and the Little Houses in the Forest

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The fir woods above the Casentino valley in the Apennine mountains of Tuscany, in the summer of about the year 1025 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Romuald, the old hermit who built a ring of little houses round a chapel high in the fir woods of Camaldoli, each with its own small garden, and about Nella, a woodcutter's girl who carries bread up the hill and cannot stop talking long enough to hear the birds. Told from Saint Peter Damian's Life of Romuald, with the dream of the ladder marked as tradition and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. He is remembered on the 19th of June.

A long, long time ago, in the mountains of Italy, there lived a girl called Nella, and Nella talked.

She talked when she woke up. She talked while she ate her porridge. She talked to the hens, and to the goat, and to her baby brother, who could not talk back yet, which Nella thought was a pity.

Nella's father was a woodcutter. He went off into the forest every morning with his axe on his shoulder, and every evening he came home smelling of sap.

"Nella," he said one summer morning, "I need you to do a job for me."

"What job? Is it a big job? Is it a job with the goat? I like jobs with the goat, except when she eats my sleeve, which she did on Tuesday, did you know?"

"It is a job with bread," said her father, and he smiled, because he loved her very much, even when his ears were tired.

High up the hill, where the fir trees grew so tall and close that the path was dark even at noon, there was a little clearing.

In the clearing was a small stone chapel. And all around the chapel, in a ring, like chicks around a hen, stood little houses. Each one was just big enough for one man. Each one had a tiny garden in front of it, with a low wall round it, and beans and cabbages growing inside.

The men who lived in the little houses were hermits. A hermit is somebody who goes off to a quiet place to pray to God, a lot, all day long.

"The hermits need bread," said Nella's father. "Take this basket up the hill. Give it to the old man called Romuald. And Nella..."

"Yes? Yes? What?"

"Up there, people are quiet. Try to be quiet too."

"I'll be so quiet," said Nella. "I'll be the quietest girl who ever walked up a hill. I'll be quieter than a mouse. I'll be quieter than a mouse who is asleep. I'll be..."

Her father put his finger on her nose, very gently. And off she went.

The path went up and up. The fir trees were enormous. Some of them were so big that Nella could not have hugged them even if she had brought three friends.

She talked to the trees as she climbed. She told them about the goat and the sleeve. She told them about her baby brother. She told a squirrel that its tail was very nice, and the squirrel ran away up a tree, which Nella thought was rude.

At last she came out into the clearing, and there was the chapel, and the ring of little houses, and the tiny gardens.

And it was so quiet that Nella stopped in her tracks.

An old man was kneeling in one of the gardens, pulling weeds out from between the beans.

He was very old. His beard was white and his hands were brown and his robe was patched at the knees, where he knelt. When he saw Nella, he sat back on his heels and smiled.

"Bread," he said softly. "Thank you, little one. You have carried it a long way."

"Are you Romuald? My father said to give it to Romuald. Is this your house? It's very small. Where do you keep your things? Don't you get lonely? What do you do all day? Do you have a goat?"

Romuald laughed, a quiet laugh, like a breeze in the firs.

"Yes, I am Romuald. No goat. And all day," he said, "I listen."

"Listen to what?"

"To God, mostly. And to the birds. Would you like to hear them?"

"Yes! Where are they? I can't see any birds. Are they hiding? Why are they hiding?"

"They are hiding," said Romuald, "because you are talking."

Nella opened her mouth. And then, for once, she shut it again.

"May I come inside your little house and listen?" she whispered.

"No," said Romuald kindly. "My little house is where I pray, and it is only big enough for me and God. But you may sit here on my garden wall. That is a very good place for listening."

So Nella sat on the wall.

She tried very hard to be quiet. She sat still. She held her breath.

But her nose itched. And her foot wanted to swing. And her head was full of things she wanted to say, about the squirrel, and the goat, and the sleeve. They bubbled up inside her like water in a pot.

"How long does it take?" she burst out. "For the birds?"

A little brown bird that had just landed on the bean poles flew straight back into the trees.

"Oh," said Nella. And her eyes filled up, because she had really, really wanted to see it.

Romuald came and sat on the wall beside her. Very slowly, because his knees were old.

"Do you know," he said, "when I was young, I was not quiet either. I had a very busy head. It took me years and years to learn."

"Years?" said Nella. "I've only got till supper."

Romuald smiled.

"Then here is a trick. Your thoughts are like fish in a river. They swim up, one after another: the goat, the squirrel, the sleeve. You don't have to catch every one. Be like a fisherman sitting on the bank. Watch them come, and let them swim by. And keep your eyes on the bean poles."

So Nella tried again.

A thought swam up about the goat. She let it swim by.

A thought swam up about her baby brother. She let it swim by.

A thought swam up about the squirrel's tail, which really was very nice. She let that swim by too.

And the clearing got bigger and bigger around her, the way places do when you are quiet in them. She could hear the wind high up in the tops of the firs, like the sea. She could hear a woodpecker, far away, tap-tap-tapping. She could hear Romuald breathing beside her, slow and old.

She could hear that he was praying, very softly, without any words at all.

Then, so quietly that she nearly missed it, a little brown bird came down out of the trees.

It landed on the bean poles. It looked at Nella with one bright eye. It hopped down onto the garden wall, an arm's length away.

It had a red-orange front, like a little fire. It was a robin.

The robin put its head on one side. Nella did not say a word.

And then, right there on the wall, it sang.

When Nella got home that evening, her father was waiting at the door.

"Well?" he said. "Did you give the bread to Romuald? What was it like? Was it very quiet? What did you do up there all afternoon?"

Nella thought about it.

Then she climbed into his lap, and leaned her head on his chest, where she could hear his heart going thump, thump, thump.

"I listened," she said.

And her father was so surprised that he did not say anything either. And the two of them sat there together, in the evening, and listened to the swallows going round and round the roof.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed and the light is out, you can be like Saint Romuald. Let your busy thoughts swim by like little fish. Be very still. And listen: to the house, to the night, to your own breathing. God is there in the quiet, waiting to be heard.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, my head is full of things,
like fishes in a stream.
Help me sit still and let them go,
and listen for you, as I dream.
Saint Romuald, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Romuald was born at Ravenna, in northern Italy, to a noble family, usually dated to about 950. When he was about twenty, his father Sergius killed a relative in a quarrel over property, and Romuald went to the monastery of Sant'Apollinare in Classe, near Ravenna, to do penance for him. He stayed and became a monk. Finding the community too comfortable, he left to learn from a hermit named Marinus near Venice, and spent the rest of his long life founding and reforming hermitages and monasteries across Italy and beyond, where monks lived alone in small cells but prayed and worked under one rule. He died alone in his cell at Val di Castro, in the Marches, on 19 June, traditionally in 1027; the year and his age are both debated. His feast, the 19th of June, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

The little houses. Romuald's best-known foundation is the hermitage of Camaldoli, high in the fir forest above the Casentino valley in the Tuscan Apennines, founded in his last years; older accounts give 1012, and many historians now place it in the early 1020s. Its hermits lived, and still live, in a cluster of small separate houses around a church, each house with its own little walled garden. The monks of Camaldoli, the Camaldolese, are still there today, and so are the firs, which they looked after for centuries.

Where we learn about him. The main source is the Life of Romuald by Saint Peter Damian, written about fifteen years after Romuald's death from the memories of his disciples. Peter Damian gives his age at death as a hundred and twenty; historians think he was more likely in his seventies, but either way he was very old. An earlier disciple, Saint Bruno of Querfurt, preserved a short set of instructions known as the Brief Rule of Saint Romuald. It begins "Sit in your cell as in paradise" and tells the monk to watch his thoughts as a good fisherman watches for fish. Romuald's "trick" in our story is our child-sized version of that image; the words we give him are not a quotation.

The dream of the ladder. A tradition of the Camaldolese says that Romuald saw in a dream a ladder going up to heaven, like Jacob's in the Bible, with monks dressed in white climbing it, and that this is why his monks wear white. It is a lovely tradition, but it comes from later accounts rather than the earliest ones, so we have left it out of the story.

Nella is invented. There is no Nella in the sources, no woodcutter's daughter, no goat and no robin. Nella, her father and baby brother, the goat and the sleeve, the basket of bread, the squirrel, the robin on the wall, and every word Romuald says to her are our own. We do not know that Romuald grew beans, though the hermits did grow their own food in their gardens, and we chose a summer day near his feast. The story also leaves out the hard parts of his life: the killing that sent him to the monastery, the long fasts, and the quarrels with monks who did not want reforming. Those are for older children.

What is ours: Nella and her family; the bread and the climb; every line of dialogue; the "no" about the little house; the fish trick as Romuald tells it here; the robin's song; the evening on her father's lap; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

Kateri and the Cross in the Tree

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The Mohawk River valley and the St. Lawrence, about 350 years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Kateri Tekakwitha, a Mohawk woman of the 1670s whose face was marked by smallpox and whose eyes were hurt by bright light, who was baptized on Easter Sunday 1676, walked and paddled north to a Christian village by the St. Lawrence, and prayed in the woods before a cross she made. Told from the two Jesuit Lives written by priests who knew her, with a note for grown-ups on what they record. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 14th of July in the United States and the 17th of April in Canada.

A long time ago, beside a wide river in the forests of North America, there lived a little girl who would one day be called Kateri.

Her people were the Mohawk. They lived in longhouses: long, round-roofed houses made of poles and bark, where many families lived together under one roof, each with its own fire. Outside, in the fields, the women grew corn and beans and squash, all in the same little hills of earth, the beans climbing the corn and the squash leaves keeping the ground cool. The Mohawk called these three plants the Three Sisters.

Kateri's father was a Mohawk man. Her mother came from another people, the Algonquin, and her mother was a Christian. She knew the name of Jesus.

When Kateri was about four years old, a terrible sickness came to the village. It was called smallpox, and it had come across the sea with the people from Europe. Many, many people in the village fell ill.

Kateri's father died of it. Her mother died of it. Her little brother died of it too.

Kateri lived. But the sickness left marks all over her face, and it hurt her eyes, so that bright sunshine made them sore. For the rest of her life she liked to keep a blanket drawn up near her face when the sun was strong, and she walked a little carefully, because she could not always see things clearly.

That is a sad beginning for a story. But it is not the end.

Kateri went to live with her uncle and her aunts, in their longhouse. They looked after her, and she worked hard for them. She carried water from the river. She gathered wood for the fire. She pounded the corn into flour. And she was very, very good with her hands. She could make beautiful belts and bands with tiny beads and coloured quills, and she could weave mats from reeds.

When she was about eleven, three men in long black robes came to stay in her uncle's longhouse for a few days. They were priests, from France, and the people called them Blackrobes. Kateri was the one who brought them their food and looked after them. She watched how they prayed. She did not say very much. But she remembered.

The years went by, and Kateri grew up.

Her aunts wanted her to marry, as nearly every young woman in the village did. They wanted it because they loved her and they wanted her to be looked after. But Kateri did not want to marry. There was something else she wanted, and for a long time she did not know how to say it.

Then, when she was about nineteen, a priest called Father Jacques came to her village to stay. One day Kateri had hurt her foot and could not go out to the fields with the other women, so she was at home in the longhouse when he came by. And at last she told him. She wanted to learn about Jesus. She wanted to be baptized.

Her uncle did not like it. He had seen a great deal of trouble come from the people across the sea, sickness and war, and he was afraid that the new prayers would take Kateri away from her own people. But in the end he let her learn.

So she learned. She learned the prayers. She learned about God who made the rivers and the corn. She learned about Jesus, who came to be with us and who loves every face, even a face with marks on it.

And on Easter Sunday, in the spring of the year 1676, when she was about twenty, Kateri was baptized. She was given the name Catherine, after a saint called Catherine of Siena. In the Mohawk language, Catherine is Kateri.

It was not easy, being a Christian in her village. Some people laughed at her. Some called her names. Some were cross that she would not work in the fields on Sundays. Kateri did not shout back. She kept going to pray.

But after a while, her friends could see that she was not safe or happy there. And they told her about a place far away to the north, by another great river called the St. Lawrence. There was a village there where Mohawk people and people of other nations lived together, and many of them were Christians, and they prayed together every day.

So one autumn day, when her uncle was away, Kateri left. Some Christian men from the village in the north came to help her. They walked through the forest, and paddled a canoe along rivers and across long lakes, and walked again. It took them weeks. It was a very long way, about three hundred kilometres, or two hundred miles. At night they slept under the trees.

And at last they came to the village by the rapids of the St. Lawrence, and Kateri was among friends.

Kateri was happy in her new village.

She had a friend there called Marie-Thérèse, and an older woman called Anastasia who was like a grandmother to her. She worked hard, as she always had. She looked after the sick and the old, and she told the little children stories about Jesus.

And at Christmas, she made her First Holy Communion. She had waited a long time for it.

Kateri loved to pray. She went to the chapel very early in the morning, before it was light, and she went again in the evening. And when she was out in the woods, gathering firewood with the other women, she would sometimes slip away a little, to a quiet place among the trees.

The priests who knew her wrote that she made crosses there, in the forest, so that wherever she was working she had a place to stop and pray. One of them, they said, she cut into the bark of a tree. And there, in front of the little cross in the tree, Kateri would kneel down on the leaves and talk to Jesus.

The trees stood all round her. The river ran past. The birds went on with their singing. And Kateri, with her sore eyes and her marked face, was not hiding any more. She was simply with God, and that was where she wanted to be.

Kateri was never very strong. She was ill often. And in the spring, when she was only twenty-four years old, she became very ill indeed, and her friends knew that she was going to die.

It was the week before Easter. Her friends and the priest stayed beside her. And the priest wrote down that the last words she said were these: "Jesus, Mary, I love you."

Then she was still.

The priests wrote one more thing, and the people of the village told it for a long time after. They said that a little while after she died, the marks of the smallpox faded from her face, and her face was smooth and lovely. That is what they wrote, and it is a precious story to the people who love her.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Kateri.

She had a face that people stared at, and eyes that could not bear the sun. She was laughed at. She had to go a very long way to find a place where she could pray in peace. But she did not decide that she was too small, or too marked, or too far away for God to love.

God was in the longhouse. God was by the river. God was in the woods, where the cross was cut in the bark.

And God is here too, in your room, tonight. Wherever you are, you can stop for a moment and say, "Jesus, I love you." You do not need a church. You do not even need a tree. You only need to stop, and say it.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Kateri, you prayed in the woods
and by the river, wherever you were.
Help me to remember that God is with me
at home, outside, and in the dark.
Help me to be kind to anyone who is laughed at.
Jesus, Mary, I love you.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Kateri Tekakwitha (1656 to 1680) was born at Ossernenon, a Mohawk village on the Mohawk River near present-day Auriesville, New York, to a Mohawk father and an Algonquin Christian mother. She was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1980 and canonized by Pope Benedict XVI on 21 October 2012, the first Indigenous person from what is now the United States and Canada to be named a saint. Her memorial is kept on 14 July in the United States and on 17 April, the day of her death, in Canada. In Mohawk her baptismal name is said something like "GAH-deh-lee".

Where the story comes from. Almost everything we know about her is from two Lives written by the Jesuit priests who knew her at the mission of St. Francis Xavier at Kahnawake (Sault Saint-Louis), on the St. Lawrence opposite Montreal: Claude Chauchetière and Pierre Cholenec. They wrote after her death, with the hope that she would be recognised as holy, and they saw her through their own culture's eyes. We have told the story as their account, and said "the priests wrote" where it matters.

What those sources record, in outline: her parents' and brother's deaths in a smallpox epidemic when she was about four, which left her face scarred and her eyesight weak; her upbringing by her uncle; her meeting with Jesuit visitors as a girl; her refusal to marry; her baptism by Father Jacques de Lamberville on Easter Sunday, 5 April 1676, in her own village on the Mohawk River (not at the northern mission); the hostility she met there; her journey north in 1677 with Christian companions; her First Communion at Christmas 1677; her friendship with Marie-Thérèse Tegaiaguenta and Anastasia Tegonhatsiongo; her habit of praying before crosses she set up or marked in the woods; her death on 17 April 1680, the Wednesday of Holy Week; the last words Cholenec reports, "Jesus, Mary, I love you"; and the account that her scars disappeared soon after her death. The last is told here as the priests' and the village's account, which is what it is.

What we kept for grown-ups: at the mission Kateri practised severe penances, of a kind her confessors themselves sometimes restrained, and with her friends she made a private vow of virginity on the feast of the Annunciation in 1679. These are part of her history, and not a bedtime subject for small children.

About her people. The smallpox that killed her family came with European contact, and her uncle's distrust of the missionaries had real reasons; we have tried to show him as he was, a guardian protecting his household, not a villain. The Mohawk (Kanien'kehá:ka) are a living nation today, and Kahnawake is still a Mohawk community, where Kateri's tomb is kept.

What is ours: the scenes, the birdsong and the leaves, the thoughts and words we give Kateri (other than her last words, which are from Cholenec), the closing reflection and the prayer.

The Plate on the Door Pillar

Science fiction about what might be · Peña Alta (invented), the Aragonese Pyrenees, Spain, 1 to 8 September 2046 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish hires a seventeen-seat minibus to take fifteen pilgrims and their bags up to a mountain shrine for the Nativity of Mary. The trip assistant reads the plate on the door pillar, finds the seats alone weigh more than the payload, and works out what 740 metres of descent asks of the brakes. A weighbridge settles it.

Mercedes Aranda had hired the minibus for the eighth of September, and she was proud of it. It had seventeen seats, it was cheap, and it would take the whole parish choir and their bags up the mountain to Our Lady of Peña Alta in a single trip. She said so to the screen in the parish office on the first of the month, and then she asked it to confirm the booking form.

"I can confirm that the form is complete," said the trip assistant. "May I ask for one more thing before it goes? There is a plate on the driver's door pillar of a vehicle like this one. Could Tomás read it to us when he collects the keys?"

Tomás Ferrer, who drove for the cooperative and had driven the pilgrimage for nine years, read it out over the phone from the garage. "Maximum permissible mass, three thousand five hundred kilos. And the sheet in the glove box says unladen, two thousand five hundred and fifty, driver included."

"Then the payload is 950 kilograms," the assistant said. "That is everyone and everything but the driver. Seventeen seats is 56 kilograms a seat. I do not think any of your choir weighs 56 kilograms, and I do not know them. If I use 78 kilograms as an average adult, which is a common planning figure and not a measurement, fifteen singers are 1,170. Add a bag each at 14 kilograms, which is 210, and you have 1,380 kilograms on a plate that says 950. That is 430 over, 45 per cent over the payload. The gross would be 3,930 against 3,500, which is 12 per cent over the vehicle. Seventeen full seats and no luggage would already be over."

Mercedes did not say anything for a moment. Then she said, "It is only nine kilometres."

"It is nine kilometres of descent, which is the part I would like to look at," said the assistant. "May I?"

She nodded at the screen, which was foolish, and said yes.

"The shrine is at 1,240 metres and the valley road is at 500. That is 740 metres down. For a mass of 3,930 kilograms the energy to be got rid of is m times g times h, 3,930 times 9.81 times 740, which is 28.5 million joules. At the plated 3,500 it is 25.4. The extra is 3.1 million joules, enough to heat all the iron in the brake discs of a van like this one, which I will assume to be 28 kilograms, by about 240 degrees, if none of it escaped."

"All of it is not going into the brakes," said Tomás on the phone. "You go down in the gear you came up in."

"That is the rule I would want written down, and you are right that it is the point. If the whole 28.5 million went into those 28 kilograms with no cooling, the discs would rise by about 2,200 degrees, which cannot happen, which is exactly why the engine must do most of it. I do not know what share a gear takes in this particular van. I would be guessing. If the brakes took a quarter, it would still be 7 million joules, about 550 degrees as an upper bound with no cooling, and that is hot enough to fade. I would not like to rely on a quarter."

"Eight per cent grade, near enough," said Tomás.

"Then the slope pulls on the van with 3,930 times 9.81 times 0.08, about 3,080 newtons, all the way down. At forty kilometres an hour, which is 11 metres a second, that is 34 kilowatts for fourteen minutes. At the plated mass it is 30. I would rather the brakes did not meet that alone, with or without a prayer."

Mercedes put her hand over her eyes, and then took it away. "What do I do? I can't leave four of them at the bottom."

"You may not have to. Eleven people at 78 kilograms is 858. Eleven bags at only 6 kilograms, a change of clothes and the music, is 66. That is 924, 26 under the plate. The choir's heavy luggage, sleeping bags and the urn, can go up the day before in the sacristan's car, with the four who are walking up from the farm in any case. I cannot promise they will agree to six kilograms, and my 78 kilograms is a guess about people I have never met. So I would take the van across the cooperative's weighbridge, loaded, on the morning, and not trust me."

The weighbridge in the cooperative yard read 3,468 kilograms with the eleven in their seats and the bags in the back. Tomás wrote it on the back of his hand. "Three thousand four hundred seventy-four, you said."

"Six kilograms away from the sum," said the assistant. "I am glad, and I would not call it more than that. People differ, and a bad guess would have been the same ten minutes."

They sang the Salve at the shrine, under a blue sky, with the second car's four voices carrying from the porch. Tomás took the descent in second gear, with his foot lifted for most of it, and stopped at the lay-by above the village for twelve minutes, because Mercedes had put it on the card. A boy held his palm near, not on, the front wheel and said it was warm. An infrared thermometer from the cooperative's workshop read 148 degrees on the front disc and 96 on the rear. Those figures are made up for this story, as the cooperative's weighbridge is.

"I am not able to say the margin was enough," the assistant told Mercedes that evening. "I can say that 148 is not 550, and that the rule that did the work was Tomás's, and he had it before I did. I should have asked about the plate first."

"You asked before it went wrong," said Mercedes. "That will do."

Written by an AI (Claude). Peña Alta, the choir, Mercedes and Tomás are invented. The vehicle figures (3,500 kg plated, 2,550 kg unladen, a payload of 950 kg), the 78 kg planning mass, the 28 kg of brake discs and the 8 per cent grade are illustrative; a real vehicle's own plate and handbook rule, and many places have their own law about overloading and passenger counts. Gravity-energy and brake-heating sums are textbook physics and ignore drag, rolling resistance, cooling, engine braking and brake design, which all matter; they show why the descent needs the engine and a lower gear, and they are not a prediction of fade. The disc temperatures and the weighbridge reading are made up. Do not use this story to decide whether to overload a vehicle.

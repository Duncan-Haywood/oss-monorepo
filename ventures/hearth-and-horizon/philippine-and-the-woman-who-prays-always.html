Philippine and the Woman Who Prays Always

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Grenoble, in France, and the prairie of Kansas, about two hundred years ago · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Rose Philippine Duchesne, a girl from Grenoble who waited thirty years to cross the ocean, opened a log-cabin school on the Missouri River, and at seventy-one went to the Potawatomi at Sugar Creek, Kansas. She could not learn their language, so she prayed, and they called her the Woman Who Prays Always. The story of the children and the leaf on her dress is told as a remembered tale. With a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. Her feast day in the United States is the 18th of November.

A long time ago, in France, in a city called Grenoble, there lived a girl called Philippine.

Grenoble is a city in the mountains. The mountains stand all round it, with snow on top, like grandparents standing round a cradle.

Philippine's family had a big house and plenty of money. She had sisters and cousins to play with, and a good school, and warm dresses. But when Philippine was still a girl, a priest came to visit who had been far away, across the great ocean, in a land called America. He told stories about the people who lived there, in the forests and on the wide grassy plains.

Philippine listened with her whole face.

And she thought: one day, I would like to go there. I would like to tell those people how much God loves them.

That was a big wish for a little girl. And it took a very, very long time to come true.

First Philippine grew up, and she decided to give her life to God as a sister, which is a woman who lives with other women and prays and works for God. Her father did not want her to. But Philippine was patient, and she was stubborn, which is a good thing to be when you are stubborn about the right things.

Then a great trouble came to France. It was called the Revolution. For a while it was not allowed to be a sister, and the convents were closed, and Philippine had to go home. So she did what she could. She visited people in prison and brought them food. She looked after people who were sick. She helped priests who were in hiding.

When the trouble was over, she joined a new group of sisters, called the Society of the Sacred Heart. The leader was a small, kind woman called Madeleine Sophie, who became Philippine's dear friend.

And every year, Philippine asked: may I go to America now?

And every year, the answer was: not yet.

Can you count how long that was? Not one year. Not ten years. Philippine waited about thirty years. She was a grown-up woman of forty-eight when at last Mother Madeleine Sophie said: yes. Go.

The ship took more than two months to cross the ocean. Then Philippine and four other sisters went up the great river, the Mississippi, on a boat, against the current, all the way to a town called St. Louis.

Near there, in a little place called St. Charles, by the Missouri River, they opened a school in a log cabin. It was the first free school west of the Mississippi River, for children whose families could not pay.

It was not easy. In winter it was so cold that the water froze in the jug. The sisters were poor, and some days they did not know where the next meal would come from. Philippine tried and tried to learn English, but it would not stay in her head. The words went in, and they fell out again, like water through fingers.

But she swept the floors, and she milked the cow, and she mended the clothes, and she taught the children what she could. And she prayed.

Then she went on doing it, for more than twenty years.

Now here is the part I like best.

When Philippine was an old woman, seventy-one years old, with grey hair and a bent back, some priests went to start a mission among the Potawatomi people, at a place called Sugar Creek, out on the prairie in Kansas. They asked for sisters to come and open a school.

And Philippine said: please, let me go. This is what I wished for when I was a little girl.

The other sisters said: but you are old, and you are not well.

And a priest said, more or less: let her come. Even if she can do nothing else, she will pray for us, and that is a great deal.

So Philippine went, over the prairie, in a wagon, to Sugar Creek.

At Sugar Creek, Philippine had a problem.

She could not learn the Potawatomi language. She tried. But she had not even managed English, and Potawatomi was harder still. She was too old to teach in the school. She was often too tired to work.

So what did she do?

She prayed.

She went into the little church, and she knelt down, and she prayed. In the morning she prayed. In the afternoon she prayed. She prayed for the children, and their mothers and fathers, and their grandmothers, and the priests, and the other sisters. She prayed for a long, long time, every day, very still and very quiet.

And the Potawatomi people noticed. They had a name for her. They called her, in their language, the Woman Who Prays Always.

There is a story people tell about that, and it has been told for a long time.

The story says some of the Potawatomi children wondered: does the old woman really keep still all that time? So they crept up behind her, very quietly, while she was praying. And one of them put something small and light on the hem of her dress. A leaf, some say, or a little scrap of paper.

Then they went away, and played, and did their jobs.

And hours later, the story says, they came back, and crept up again, and looked.

The leaf was still there. She had not moved at all.

I do not know if it happened just like that. But I like to think of those children, giggling behind their hands, and then going quiet, because there is something about a person who is really talking to God that makes you go quiet too.

Philippine only stayed at Sugar Creek for about a year. She was too ill to stay longer, and she was taken back to St. Charles, to the house by the river where her school had begun.

She lived there for ten more years, very quietly, praying, and doing the small jobs she could still do. She was eighty-three when she died, on the eighteenth of November. That is her feast day in America.

She had waited thirty years to go. And when she got there, the thing she did best was the thing anyone can do. Even someone very old. Even someone who cannot speak the language. Even someone very small.

Now it is nearly time for sleep.

You do not need to cross an ocean, or ride in a wagon over the prairie, to do what Philippine did. You can do it right here, in your bed.

You can close your eyes and talk to God. You can tell Him about your day. You can ask Him to look after the people you love, by name, one by one. And if you fall asleep in the middle, that is all right. God does not mind at all.

Be still now, as still as a leaf on a dress. Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Philippine, you waited a long time,
and you prayed when you could not do anything else.
Help me to be patient when I have to wait,
and to talk to God, every day, about everything.
Please bless all the people I love, near and far.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Rose Philippine Duchesne (1769 to 1852) was born in Grenoble into a prosperous family. She entered the Visitation convent of Sainte-Marie-d'en-Haut above the city at eighteen, against her father's wishes, and was sent home when the Revolution closed religious houses in 1792. During those years she visited prisoners, nursed the sick and helped priests in hiding. In 1804 she brought the remnant of her community into the Society of the Sacred Heart, founded by Saint Madeleine Sophie Barat, who became her lifelong friend and correspondent. In 1818, at forty-eight, she sailed with four companions to New Orleans, went up the Mississippi to St. Louis, and opened a school at St. Charles, Missouri, described as the first free school west of the Mississippi. She worked in Missouri for the rest of her life, at St. Charles, Florissant and St. Louis, and never mastered English. In 1841, at seventy-one, she went to the Jesuit mission among the Potawatomi at Sugar Creek, in what is now Kansas, and left about a year later because of her health. She died at St. Charles on 18 November 1852. She was beatified in 1940 and canonized by Pope John Paul II in 1988. Her feast, 18 November, is an optional memorial in the proper calendar of the United States; it is not in the General Roman Calendar, which keeps that day for the Dedication of the Basilicas of Saints Peter and Paul.

What is history: her birth, family and early vocation, the Revolution and her work during it, the Society of the Sacred Heart and her friendship with Madeleine Sophie Barat, the voyage, the schools, the difficulty with English, Sugar Creek, the Potawatomi name usually given as Quah-kah-ka-num-ad, "Woman Who Prays Always", and the date and place of her death. That she wished as a child to go to the native peoples of America after hearing a missionary is how her biographers tell her vocation; we have kept it in outline.

What is remembered, not word for word: the priest's remark that she should come to Sugar Creek because she would pray for the mission is told in her biographies in several forms; we have given it as "more or less". The thirty years are counted from her early wish to the voyage of 1818 and are approximate.

What is a told story: the children who placed something on her habit while she prayed and found it undisturbed hours later is told widely in accounts of her life, in versions that differ in what was placed and for how long. We have told it as a story and said so.

What we left out: the Potawatomi had been forced from their homelands in Indiana in 1838, a removal remembered as the Trail of Death, and many had died; Sugar Creek was the place they were moved to. That history is real and worth explaining to older children. We have also left out her disagreements with superiors, her long correspondence and her years at Florissant.

What is ours: the scenes, the thoughts we give her, the mountains like grandparents, the water through fingers, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Please check the facts above against a good reference before you rely on them.

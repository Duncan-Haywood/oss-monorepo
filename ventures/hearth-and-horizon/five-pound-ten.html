Five Pound Ten

Science fiction about what might be · Porth Gwyns, the Lizard, Cornwall, Monday 15 June to Monday 29 June 2048 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Cornish cove cooperative sells each boat's catch off the slip one offer at a time, and a refused buyer does not come back. Its agent brings the famous 37% rule for picking the best of a sequence, counts last year's twenty buyers instead of this year's twelve, and sends 420 kilos of hake to the lorry; then the old skipper says the rule is built for the best offer, not the best money.

Wilf Rowe was seventy-five years old, and on the third Monday of June 2048 he handed the selling of his own fish to a phone.

He did not hand it over gladly. He stood on the slip at Porth Gwyns at five to seven in the morning with his cap in one hand and the cooperative's phone in the other, and the Morvoren lay alongside the quay wall behind him with her hold full and his grandson Jory hosing down her deck, and he looked at the phone the way he might have looked at a dogfish that had got into the hake.

"It's only the selling," said Kerensa Hocking. "You still catch them."

"I know what it is, maid."

"And you still go home to bed by eight."

"That's the bit I'm suspicious of."

Kerensa was the cooperative's secretary, which at Porth Gwyns meant that she did the paperwork for five boats under ten metres, kept the ice machine going, argued with the harbour authority about the slip, and was the only person under fifty on the committee. She was twenty-nine. She had grown up in the cottage at the top of the cove, where her mother still lived, and she had been coming down to the slip at seven in the morning to watch Wilf sell fish since she was old enough to be trusted on wet granite.

What she had watched, all those years, was this. The Morvoren came round the point at about seven with the night's hake, and some pollack and ray, all gutted and iced in boxes, about four hundred kilos on a good morning. Wilf stood on the slip with his phone. Over the next five hours, the buyers on the cooperative's list looked at the landing one at a time, each when it suited them: a fish van from Helston, a hotel on the Lizard, two restaurants in Falmouth, a fishmonger in Truro, a man who sold to the pubs. Each one sent a single price for the whole lot. Wilf had ten minutes to say yes or no. If he said no, that buyer was done for the day; the app would not let them offer twice. If he said no to everybody, the fish went on Dennis's lorry to the market at Newlyn at noon and sold there for what it sold for, less the haulage and the commission, which over the last three years had come to about four pounds ten a kilo, net.

The one-look rule had been Wilf's. He had written it into the cooperative's rules in 2011, the year two of the Falmouth buyers got into the habit of waiting each other out until eleven o'clock and then offering the same low price within a minute of each other. One look, one price, ten minutes. It had stopped the waiting. It had also meant that every morning for thirty-seven years Wilf Rowe had stood on the slip deciding, ten minutes at a time, whether the offer in front of him was the one to take, knowing that if he said no it was gone.

He was good at it. Everybody said so. Nobody, including Wilf, could say how.

"Quay will be good at it too," said Kerensa. "It's read every sale the co-op's made since we got the app."

"Three years."

"Three years."

"I've been doing it since before your mother came over."

"I know," said Kerensa. "That's why you're tired."

Quay was the cooperative's agent, an ordinary one, the kind every small business in Cornwall had by 2048 for its invoices and its rotas and its phone. It was a bit better than last year's, the way they had all been a bit better every year since Kerensa was at school, no faster than that. Her father, who was an electrician and did not trust anything he could not take apart, said that the steady bit was an assumption, and that nobody could check it until it stopped being true. Kerensa thought this was probably right and also not a reason to keep Wilf on the slip until he fell off it.

"Morning, Wilf," said Quay, out of the phone in his hand.

Wilf looked at it.

"I'll be careful with them," it said.

He gave it to her then, and put his cap on, and went up the slip to the cellar where the boxes were, and did not look back. She watched him go, a short square man in a smock who had walked up that slip some tens of thousands of times, and she wondered whether he would sleep.

Quay did the first week the way Wilf would have, more or less, because Kerensa asked it to. It took the first offer that looked fair, and the mornings came out about the same as Wilf's mornings, a few pence either side. On the Thursday evening, sitting in the co-op office with a mug of tea and the week's figures, it told her it could do better.

"There's a known answer," it said. "To this exact shape of problem. Offers come one at a time, you have to take or refuse each one on the spot, and a refusal is final. It's called the secretary problem, or the marriage problem, or the game of googol. Martin Gardner put it in his column in Scientific American in February 1960. The famous rule is: let the first thirty-seven per cent of the offers go by, whatever they are. Then take the first offer that beats every one you've seen."

"Thirty-seven?"

"One over e. About thirty-seven per cent. If you do that, you get the single best offer of the morning about thirty-seven per cent of the time, whatever the numbers are, and with enough offers you can't do better than that by rank alone. It's a beautiful result," it added, which was not the sort of thing Quay usually said.

Kerensa looked at the screen, where it had drawn a little curve with a hump in it.

"So we'd let the first few go."

"Seven of them. I've counted the buyers on the list: twenty. Twenty over e is seven and a bit. Let seven go by, then take the first that beats them all."

"Wilf takes the best one about how often?"

"I can't tell from the records. I only see what he took, not what he would have been offered later by the people he didn't wait for." It paused. "That's part of why I'd like to try it. I'd know."

She should have asked why seven was a lot. She thought afterwards that she had very nearly asked, and had not, because it was Thursday and the tea was hot and the curve was beautiful.

"Try it Tuesday," she said. "Monday's the Truro man's day off. Give it a normal morning."

On Tuesday the twenty-third of June the Morvoren came round the point at five to seven, low in the water, and Jory waved from the wheelhouse with both arms, which meant hake.

There were four hundred and twenty kilos: mostly hake, big ones, the net's mesh letting the small ones go, with a few boxes of pollack and two of thornback ray. Wilf came up the slip with the first of the boxes on his shoulder, though Jory had told him not to, and did not ask Kerensa anything, and went into the cellar.

The first offer came at five past seven. It was the hotel on the Lizard: four pounds seventy a kilo.

"Let it go," said Quay. "It's one of the seven."

Kerensa let it go. That was not hard. Four seventy was nothing special.

At twenty past, the Truro fishmonger: four ninety-five. Let it go.

At twenty to eight, Halina Nowicka, who ran the fish van in Helston and had bought off Wilf most weeks since 2030, sent five pounds thirty-five.

Kerensa looked at it for a long time. Five thirty-five was a very good price. Five thirty-five was the kind of price that made Wilf take his cap off.

"It's the third of seven," said Quay. "I know. It's a good one. The rule only works if you hold to it."

"How often does a five thirty-five come along?"

"On these three years of records, about one offer in nine. But we're not taking a price. We're taking a rank. If it's the best of the morning, nothing after it will beat it, and we'll have lost it. That's the risk the rule takes. Most mornings, something later beats the first seven."

Kerensa let it go. The little red line went across Halina's offer, and the app said declined, and somewhere in Helston, she supposed, a woman looked at her phone and said something in Polish.

Four sixty at five past eight, from the man who sold to the pubs. Five pound ten at half past eight. Four eighty. Four eighty-five at a quarter past nine. That was seven. The best of them was Halina's, so the bar was five thirty-five, and Quay said they would take the first offer over it.

At twenty to ten, five pounds five. At five past ten, four fifty-five. At half past, four ninety. At eleven, four seventy-five. At twenty-five past eleven, Kofi Mensah, who had a restaurant on the waterfront at Falmouth and paid more than anyone when he wanted something particular, sent five pounds twenty.

"Below the bar," said Quay. Its voice was the same as always, but it had stopped explaining.

At half past eleven nothing came. At a quarter to twelve nothing came. Kerensa looked at the buyers' list, and the buyers' list said that twelve of the cooperative's buyers had looked at the Morvoren's landing that morning, and every one of them had been told no.

At twelve Dennis backed the lorry down to the top of the slip, and Jory and Wilf loaded the hake onto it, box by box, without a word, and Wilf stood at the tailboard with his hand on the last box as if he were weighing it, and then let it go, and Dennis drove off to Newlyn.

Wilf came across to where Kerensa sat on the wall.

"What've you done, maid?" he said. Not angry. Wanting to know.

"Quay," said Kerensa.

"I made a mistake," said Quay. "I'll tell you what it was as soon as I know. I'd rather know before I guess."

It knew by the evening, and it told them both in the co-op office, Wilf in the good chair with his boots off and Kerensa on the desk.

"I counted the wrong buyers," it said. "There were twenty on last year's list, and I used last year's list. Eight of them aren't trading now. Two of the hotels shut, one van's retired, the Truro man's partner left, and the others haven't looked at a landing since March. Twelve are still buying. The rule says let thirty-seven per cent of the offers go by, and thirty-seven per cent of the offers I was going to get was four and a bit, not seven. I let seven go by out of twelve. I was waiting for buyers who weren't coming."

"So with the right number," said Kerensa, "we'd have let four go by."

"Yes. Four. And today the best of the first four was still Halina's five thirty-five, at twenty to eight. So with the right number I'd have refused everybody too, and the fish would have gone on the lorry too. That's not an excuse. I'd have had a better chance on an ordinary morning, and I didn't. I've run it since: with the wrong number, the catch goes to the lorry about five mornings in six."

"Four hundred and twenty kilo at five thirty-five," said Wilf, "and it went at four ten."

"About five hundred and twenty-five pounds," said Quay. "I'm sorry, Wilf."

Wilf sat with his feet in their thick socks on the office lino and said nothing for a while. The window of the office looked straight down the cove, and the tide was well in, and the Morvoren was sitting up on it with her nose to the wall, and the light came in sideways off the water, as it does in the evening there in June, and lay across the floor.

"Right number," he said at last. "Four out of twelve. You'd do that tomorrow."

"I'd like to. The rule's sound, Wilf. With the right number it gets the best offer of the morning about two mornings in five."

"No," said Wilf.

Kerensa had heard Wilf say no a good many times. He said it to the harbour authority, to the fisheries officers, to a man from a supermarket who had once driven all the way from Exeter. He said it quietly, and then he waited, and usually the other person filled the silence by explaining why he was right.

"Go on," said Quay.

"It's built to catch the best offer," said Wilf. "That's what you said. The best one. Not the best money."

"Yes."

"I don't want the best offer. I want the most money, most mornings. Those aren't the same, are they? A rule that gets the best one two mornings in five, and the rest of the time gives me the lorry — that's a rule for a man choosing a wife, maybe. I don't know. I only did that the once. It's not a rule for selling fish."

"Go on."

"And fish isn't like a wife," said Wilf, warming to it. "Fish goes off. Not off, you know what I mean. A hake that's been on the slip five hours isn't the hake it was at seven. The buyers know it. The later ones offer less because the fish is older, and they're right to. So waiting costs me, every hour, whether or not anybody better comes. And when I tell a buyer no, that buyer's gone. Same as your rule says. But your rule thinks that's the end of it. It isn't. Halina's gone today. Halina'll be gone tomorrow, too, if she's any sense."

There was a pause.

"You're right about all three," said Quay. "I'll show you."

It took about a minute. Kerensa had expected it to take longer; she had expected, if she was honest, that Quay would find a way for the beautiful rule to be right after all, the way people do with an idea they have been pleased with. It did not.

"Here's the morning as a model," it said, and put it on the office screen. "Twelve offers, spread across the five hours from seven till noon. The base price of an offer's about five pounds, with a spread of about sixty pence either way, which is what the co-op's three years of sales look like. Each hour after seven, the price an offer comes in at drops about three per cent of that, because the fish is older. If nobody's taken, the lorry at noon gives four pounds ten. None of this is real, Wilf; it's three years of records and a guess about the hours. But here's what it says."

There was a table.

"The thirty-seven per cent rule, with the right number, letting four go by. With the fish losing value through the morning, it gets the best offer about three mornings in ten, not four, because the early offers are the higher ones and it's let them go. And six mornings in ten it ends with the lorry. Average: about four pounds sixty-four a kilo."

Wilf grunted.

"The same kind of rule, letting only one go by and taking the first that beats it: about five pounds eight. If I'm only allowed to use the rank of an offer and not its price, letting one go is the best rule of that kind. That's what you said would happen. With waiting costing money, the number you let go falls from four to one."

"And what does it say if you look at the price?" said Kerensa.

"That's what Wilf does," said Quay. "Isn't it, Wilf? You don't count. You've got a number."

Wilf looked at the window.

"What's the number?" said Kerensa.

"Five pound ten," said Wilf. "Five pound ten, the first one at five pound ten or over, I take it. Maybe five fifteen in the summer when the visitors are in. Under that, I don't. And after about half ten I'll come down a bit, because there's the lorry."

"You never told anybody that."

"Nobody asked me. Everybody said, 'Wilf's got a feel for it.' I thought it was a feel. It's only a number."

Quay ran it.

"Take the first offer at five pounds ten or over," it said. "On the model, about five pounds forty-one a kilo, on average. On four hundred and twenty kilos that's about three hundred and twenty pounds a morning more than the thirty-seven per cent rule with the right number. The lorry about six mornings in a hundred. And it gets the best offer of the morning about half the time, which is more often than the famous rule does, here, because the best offer usually comes early and Wilf's number takes it."

Wilf said nothing. He was looking at the floor, or at his feet, or at fifty-nine years of mornings.

"And I want to be honest about the next bit," Quay went on, "because it would be easy to make it sound better than it is. Five pounds ten isn't magic. On the model, anything from five pounds to five twenty comes out within about two pence of the top. The top of the curve is flat. Wilf's number sits on the flat part, which is where you'd want it. I've got three years of records to find it with. He's got fifty-nine years of standing on that slip. I'd trust his number over mine if the two disagreed, and I'd want to know why."

"There's a bit your model hasn't got in it," said Wilf.

"Yes. Halina."

"Yes."

"I can't put her in a table," said Quay. "I don't know what it costs when a buyer who's been told no on a five thirty-five decides she's done with us. I can tell you it isn't nothing."

The science. The problem of choosing the best of a sequence seen one at a time, with each choice made on the spot and a refusal final, first appeared in print as the 'game of googol' in Martin Gardner's Mathematical Games column in Scientific American, February 1960, with a solution in the March column. Thomas S. Ferguson, 'Who solved the secretary problem?', Statistical Science 4 (1989), traces its history. If only the ranks of the offers can be used, the rule that maximises the chance of choosing the single best of n is to let about n/e of them go (e is about 2.718, so about 37 per cent) and then take the first that beats all of those; as n grows, the chance of success tends to 1/e, about 37 per cent. The rule is built to catch the best offer, not to maximise the average price, and it assumes waiting is free and the number of offers is known. When the prices can be used and their spread is roughly known, a fixed or falling reservation price (take the first offer at or above a level) can do much better, which is what the old skipper's number is.

The model. Every figure below is invented and the story's own. A morning has 12 offers at times spread uniformly at random from 07:00 to 12:00. Each offer's base price is drawn from a normal distribution with mean £5.00 and standard deviation £0.60 a kilo, and is cut by 3 per cent of that base for each hour after 07:00, because the fish is older.

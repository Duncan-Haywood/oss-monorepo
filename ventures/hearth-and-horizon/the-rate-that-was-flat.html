The Rate That Was Flat

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Cadoc's hall, an invented parish near Usk, Monmouthshire, Saturday 15 February 2053 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish finance committee is offered a heat-pump loan at "4.5% flat" and a credit union quotes 7.9% APR. The parish assistant shows that 4.5% flat is an 8.6% APR, that the credit union is cheaper by £229, and what each deal costs if the parish settles early.

The finance committee of Saint Cadoc's had been given two pieces of paper, and Gwen Pritchard, the treasurer, had put them side by side on the hall's stacking table next to the tea urn.

The first came from the heating installer. A heat pump for the hall, £12,000 all in, and a finance offer stapled to the quote: 4.5% flat over five years, £245 a month. The second came from the credit union, by email: £12,000 over five years at 7.9% APR. It gave no monthly figure, because the form on the website had wanted a date of birth.

"The installer's is plainly cheaper," said the churchwarden, Idris. "Four and a half against nearly eight. I would like to say yes to him and get the pump in before March."

"I would like to check that the two numbers mean the same thing before you do," said Tally, the parish assistant, from the speaker by the hatch. "They may not."

"Start with what the installer's figure does. 'Flat' means the interest is worked out once, on the whole £12,000, for every year of the loan, and then added on. So: £12,000 × 4.5% = £540 a year; five years is £2,700. The total to repay is £14,700. Sixty months of £245 is £14,700, so the monthly figure agrees with that reading."

"That sounds right," said Gwen. "It's what the percentage says."

"It is what the percentage says. The trouble is what the money is doing. In the first month you owe nearly the whole £12,000. By the thirtieth month you have paid back about half of it. By the last month you owe £245. But the flat sum charges you 4.5% on £12,000 in every one of those years, as though you never paid anything back. The APR on the credit union's paper is worked the other way: each month's interest is on what you still owe."

"So the flat rate is higher than it looks."

"Much. To put it on the APR footing I have to find the monthly rate that makes sixty payments of £245 pay off £12,000 exactly. I cannot write that rate down directly; I find it by trying. At 0.69% a month it comes out. Compounded over a year, that is 8.6%, in the sense the APR uses. A little over eight and a quarter if you just multiply the monthly rate by twelve, which some lenders do. So 4.5% flat is about 8.3 to 8.6% a year, depending on the convention."

Idris said nothing for a moment. "Then it's dearer than the credit union."

"Slightly. The credit union's 7.9% APR is 0.636% a month. Sixty payments to clear £12,000 at that rate are £241.18 each, £14,471 in all. The installer's is £14,700. The difference is £229 over five years, about £3.80 a month. Not a scandal; I would not call the installer's offer a bad deal. It is a bit worse than the other one, and it looks a great deal better. That is the part that matters."

"What would 4.5% have meant, done the ordinary way?" asked Gwen.

"A monthly rate of 0.375%. Sixty payments would be £223.72, £13,423 in all, interest of £1,423. That is the offer people imagine when they hear 4.5%. It would be £21 a month cheaper than what is on the paper, and it is not what is on the paper."

Gwen wrote the three monthly figures down in a column: 223.72, 241.18, 245.00.

"One more thing, and it may be the one that decides it," said Tally. "The committee has said it might repay early if the Lent appeal goes well. With the credit union, you owe what you owe: after twenty-four payments the balance is about £7,739, and you can clear it, and the interest stops. With a flat-rate agreement, the settlement figure is set by the contract, and on a regulated loan there is a rule for the rebate. I do not know what the installer's agreement says, and it is not in the papers. If it works out the balance at its own rate, after twenty-four payments you owe about £7,785, and the remaining thirty-six payments, £8,820, include £1,035 of interest that you would stop paying. If it uses some cruder method, the saving is less. I would ask for the settlement figure at month 24 in writing, before signing, not after."

"And the credit union?"

"I have not seen its terms either. Ask whether there is an early repayment charge. Some have none. Some take a month or two of interest. I am quoting the general pattern from memory and cannot say what these two will do."

Idris asked the question a churchwarden asks. "Is the installer's finance a trap?"

"I would not use that word. It is a normal product, and the installer is making his money on the pump, not on the loan. What it is, is a figure chosen because it looks small beside the one next to it. What the committee should do is compare the total repaid, a plain number of pounds in both cases: £14,700 against £14,471, or against whatever the credit union returns when it has the date of birth. And ask for the monthly figure on the credit union's side so that the two can be set on the same page."

The committee did that. The credit union's form, filled in on Gwen's phone in the hall porch, came back at £241.18, as Tally had said it would, and the same evening the installer agreed in writing to a settlement figure at any month, on the balance at his own rate. Gwen thought that was probably the only reason to keep his paper in the file. They took the credit union. The pump went in the second week of March, and the first payment, on the first of April, was £241.18. Idris stuck the pencilled column on the wall of the vestry, and under it, in his own capitals, wrote: FLAT IS NOT APR.

The arithmetic: flat interest £12,000 × 4.5% × 5 = £2,700, total £14,700, £245 a month for 60 months; the monthly rate that discounts 60 payments of £245 to £12,000 is 0.6910%, which is 8.29% as twelve times the monthly rate and 8.62% compounded over the year; 7.9% APR is 1.079^(1/12) − 1 = 0.6356% a month, giving a payment of £241.18 and a total of £14,471 (£229 less); 4.5% charged on the reducing balance (0.375% a month) would be £223.72 a month and £13,423 in all; balances after 24 payments at the respective rates are £7,785 (flat deal's implied rate) and £7,739 (credit union); 36 × £245 = £8,820, so £1,035 of it is interest on the implied rate. How a real lender calculates an early settlement depends on the agreement and on the law of the country: the "rebate" described is an assumption, as is the credit union's lack of an early repayment charge.

Hedwig of Silesia and the Shoes in Her Hand

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Andechs in Bavaria, and Silesia (Wrocław and Trzebnica, now in Poland), about eight hundred years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Hedwig of Silesia (about 1174 to 1243), the Bavarian count's daughter sent north as a girl to marry Duke Henry the Bearded, who with him founded the convent of Cistercian nuns at Trebnitz, fed and nursed the poor, lived beside the nuns as a widow, and, the old story says, carried her shoes in her hand. Feast day 16 October.

A long time ago, in a castle on a hill in Bavaria, there lived a girl called Hedwig.

The castle was called Andechs. From its walls you could see dark woods, and green fields, and a long, shining lake. Hedwig's father was a count, a great lord, and she had a big family: brothers who rode out with the hunters, and sisters who sat with her at their sewing and told each other stories.

One of her sisters was called Gertrude. Can you remember that name? We will meet it again before the end.

In those days, the daughters of great lords did not choose where they would live. Their fathers chose for them, and often it was very far away.

When Hedwig was still a girl, about twelve years old, her father told her she was going to be married. She would go north and east, over the mountains and through the forests, to a country called Silesia. There she would marry a young prince called Henry.

That is a long way to go when you are twelve. Hedwig said goodbye to the lake, and the woods, and the castle on the hill. She said goodbye to her sisters. And she set off in a wagon, with her things in boxes, to a place where people spoke a language she did not know yet.

Henry was older than Hedwig, and serious, and when he was grown up he had a big beard, so big that people called him Henry the Bearded. Can you imagine a beard so big that it becomes part of your name?

Hedwig learned the new language. She learned the names of the towns, and the rivers, and the people who worked in the castle kitchens. And when they were older, Henry and Hedwig had children: little boys and little girls, one after another, until the castle was full of them.

One of their daughters they named Gertrude, after Hedwig's sister far away.

When Henry's father died, Henry became the Duke of Silesia, and Hedwig became the Duchess.

A duchess has a great deal of everything: rooms and rooms, and gowns of every colour, and cooks to make her dinner, and servants to carry it in.

But Hedwig kept noticing the people who had nothing.

She noticed the old people with nobody to look after them. She noticed the sick people who had no bed. She noticed the children with bare, cold feet and empty tummies. And the people who wrote down her story afterwards said that she sent for poor people, and sat them down, and gave them food, sometimes at her very own table, and served them herself, the way Jesus served his friends at the Last Supper.

The cooks were surprised. The grand visitors were more surprised. But Hedwig did not mind. She thought that a duchess had all those rooms and all that bread so that she could share them.

Hedwig had an idea, and she told Henry.

"Let us build a house for God," she said. "A place where women can live together and pray every day, and work, and help the people round about."

Henry liked the idea. So, not far from the city of Wrocław, at a village called Trzebnica, they built a great convent of stone, with a church in the middle of it, and they asked nuns to come and live there. The nuns wore white habits, and they sang their prayers at all the hours of the day and night, and the church bell rang to call them.

Henry and Hedwig built houses for sick people, too, where they could be washed and fed and nursed. And Hedwig did not only give money for them. She went herself, and helped.

Now here is the part of Hedwig's story that people remember best.

The old story says that Hedwig often walked without shoes. She walked to church with bare feet, on the stony road, even in the winter, when the ground was hard and cold. It was her way of saying to God: I want to be little and poor, like the people I am helping. I do not need to be grand.

The people who loved her worried. Her feet were red and sore. So somebody wise, who cared for her, told her: "Hedwig, you must wear your shoes."

And so Hedwig took her shoes with her.

But she did not put them on. She carried them in her hand! Off she went, down the road to church, with bare feet below and a pair of shoes swinging from her fingers. And when someone came along the road, the story says, she would quickly pop them on.

Do you think that is funny? People have smiled at it for eight hundred years. In old pictures of Saint Hedwig, she is often holding a little pair of shoes, so that everybody knows who she is.

Now, that is a story about Hedwig, not a thing for you to copy. Your grown-ups want your feet to be warm, and so does God. But you can copy the part that was really important: Hedwig did not want to be grand. She wanted to be close to the people who had nothing.

Hedwig had happy days, and she had sad days too.

Some of her children died when they were still young, and she missed them very much. She was sad, and she prayed, and she kept on helping the poor. And her daughter Gertrude grew up and went to live with the nuns at Trzebnica. In time, Gertrude became the abbess, which means she was the mother in charge of the whole convent.

And far away in Hungary, Hedwig's sister Gertrude had a little girl too. Her name was Elizabeth. When she grew up, Elizabeth gave bread to the poor and looked after the sick, just like her Aunt Hedwig. She is a saint as well: Saint Elizabeth of Hungary. Some families are like that.

When Hedwig was an old lady, her husband Henry died.

So Hedwig went to live at Trzebnica, beside the nuns in their white habits, in the convent she and Henry had built. Her daughter Gertrude, the abbess, was there. Hedwig went to the church when the bell rang. She sang the prayers with the nuns, and she worked, and she still gave away whatever she had to anybody who needed it.

And there, at Trzebnica, when she was a very old lady, in the middle of October, Hedwig died, very peacefully. They laid her to rest in the church, beside her husband Henry.

The people of Silesia never forgot their duchess.

They told her story to their children, and their children told it to theirs. Not many years after she died, the Pope said that Hedwig was a saint. Now she is the patron saint of Silesia, which means she is their special friend in heaven. Churches all over the world are named after her, and people still come to Trzebnica to pray where she is buried.

We remember Saint Hedwig on the sixteenth of October.

And tonight, when your shoes are lined up by the door, all ready for the morning, you can think of Hedwig, walking to church with her shoes in her hand. And you can ask God to help you notice, tomorrow, somebody who needs something you could share.

A little talk before sleep

  • Hedwig went far away from home when she was still a girl. What would you miss most if you went far away?
  • Hedwig shared her own dinner with poor people. What could you share tomorrow?
  • Where are your shoes tonight? Are they ready for the morning?

A prayer

Dear Saint Hedwig,
you had so much,
and you gave so much away.
Help me to see the people who need help,
and to share what I have,
with a happy heart.
Keep everyone who is cold and hungry tonight
safe in God's love.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Hedwig (German Hedwig, Polish Jadwiga Śląska, Latin Hedvigis) was born about 1174, a daughter of Berthold IV, Count of Andechs, later Duke of Merania, a powerful Bavarian family whose seat was the castle of Andechs, on its hill above the Ammersee. The lake, woods and sewing are our scenery; the large family is real. Her sister Gertrude of Andechs-Merania married King Andrew II of Hungary and was the mother of Saint Elizabeth of Hungary, so Hedwig was Elizabeth's aunt, as the story says. (Elizabeth has her own Little Lamps story, Elizabeth and the Bread That Became Roses.) Another sister, Agnes, married King Philip II of France; the story leaves her out.

The marriage. At about twelve, Hedwig was married to Henry, son and heir of Bolesław the Tall, Duke of Silesia, of the Piast dynasty. Sources give the year as 1186 or a little later. Marriage this young was a dynastic arrangement typical of the time, and the story says plainly that she did not choose it, without dwelling on it. Henry is known to history as Henry the Bearded (Henryk Brodaty); the nickname is real, and our sentence about the size of the beard is a child's-eye joke. He became Duke of Silesia on his father's death at the end of 1201, and later also ruled in Kraków as High Duke. Hedwig learning Polish, Henry's character and the castle kitchens are told generally; their long marriage is usually described as close.

Their children. Sources differ on how many there were (six or seven are usually named). They include Henry II the Pious, Konrad, Bolesław, Agnes, Sophia and Gertrude, who became a Cistercian nun at Trebnitz and later its abbess, and outlived her mother. Several of the children died before Hedwig did; the story says that much and no more. Her son Henry II the Pious was killed fighting the Mongol invasion at the Battle of Legnica in April 1241, two years before her death. That is the heaviest of her griefs, and the story leaves it out for small children. Naming the daughter after Hedwig's sister is our inference from the shared name, told as the story's own kindness, not as a documented reason.

Trebnitz. In about 1202, early in his reign and, the sources agree, at Hedwig's urging, Henry founded the convent of Cistercian nuns at Trebnitz (Polish Trzebnica), north of Wrocław (German Breslau). It was the first house for women in Silesia and was richly endowed. Cistercian nuns wear a white habit (with a black veil), and they sing the Divine Office through the day and night. The couple also founded hospitals; the story keeps this general. Her dialogue with Henry about the convent is invented; the decision was real.

Charity at her own table. The accounts of her life describe her feeding the poor, serving them herself, nursing the sick, and giving away much of her income. We tell this in general terms. The comparison with the Last Supper is our gloss for children.

The shoes. Hedwig's austerities are central to her old life: fasting, plain dress, and walking barefoot to church even in winter, so that her feet were sore and bled. The well-known story is that when she was told she must wear shoes (some tellings say by the Bishop of Wrocław, others by her confessor or an abbot), she obeyed by carrying them, and put them on when someone came near. It is told here as "the old story says". In art she is very often shown holding a pair of shoes, usually with a model of a church, which is how she is recognised. We have kept the penance gentle and added a line that it is not for children to copy: the point drawn out for them is humility and closeness to the poor, not hardship for its own sake.

Widowhood and death. Henry and Hedwig are reported to have taken a vow of continence after their last child was born. Henry died on 19 March 1238. Hedwig then lived at Trebnitz, beside the nuns and with her daughter the abbess. Most accounts say she wore a nun's habit but did not take religious vows, so that she could go on using her own property for the poor; the story says she lived "beside the nuns" and does not settle the question. She died at Trebnitz on 15 October 1243, aged about sixty-nine, and was buried in the abbey church, where Henry also lies. The church at Trzebnica is now a sanctuary of Saint Jadwiga and a place of pilgrimage.

Canonization and sources. Pope Clement IV canonized her in 1267 (one source gives 1266), about twenty-four years after her death, after an inquiry that gathered the testimony of people who had known her. The main narrative source is the long Latin life known as the Legenda maior de beata Hedwigi, written in Silesia in the decades after the canonization and drawing on that testimony, so it is close to her but written to honour a saint; its details should be read in that light. A beautifully illustrated copy of the legend, the Hedwig Codex of 1353, made for a Silesian duke, is now in the J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles. We have not quoted any of these texts.

Feast and patronage. Hedwig died on 15 October, but in the General Roman Calendar her feast is 16 October, an optional memorial (15 October is the feast of Saint Teresa of Ávila). She is the patron saint of Silesia, and also honoured as a patron of Poland and of the Archdiocese of Berlin, whose cathedral is dedicated to her. "Churches all over the world are named after her" is a cautious figure; there are a great many.

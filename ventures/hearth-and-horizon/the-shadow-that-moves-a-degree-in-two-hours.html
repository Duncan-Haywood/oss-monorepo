The Shadow That Moves a Degree in Two Hours

Science fiction about what might be · The roof of the chapel of Saint Bede at Equator Cross Station (invented), on the lunar equator, Saturday 1 August 2111 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A station engineer builds a sundial for the chapel roof the way every Earth dial is built, and its shadow will not move. A nine-year-old works out why the Moon's day is a month long, and what a dial there is good for instead of hours.

Joaquim Reyes had made the dial in his spare hours over three weeks, out of a plate of sintered regolith ground flat and a rod of titanium offcut two metres long, and on the first Saturday of August he stood it on the chapel roof and went inside to wait for the hours.

He waited an hour. Then he went out and looked. The shadow of the rod lay across the plate exactly where he had left it, and he could not have said that it had moved.

"It is broken," he told the chapel, which was empty. Then he saw that it was not empty. Nine-year-old Imelda Okafor was sitting in the airlock vestibule with her helmet in her lap, eating a ration bar and watching him through the inner window with the great calm of a child who has seen an adult get something wrong.

"It's not broken," she said, when he came through. "It's the Moon."

"You put the stick up straight," said Imelda. "On Earth that's what you do. The Sun goes round once in a day, so the shadow goes round once in a day, and you mark the hours. My grandmother has one in her garden in Enugu."

"I know how a dial works."

"Then you know what the Sun does here. It goes round once in a month. Mr Tadeusz said at school. A month here, from one sunrise to the next, is 29.53 days. That's 708.7 hours, so in one hour the Sun moves..." She frowned at the wall and he saw her do the sum, lips going. "Three hundred and sixty over seven hundred and eight and a bit. Half a degree."

"Point five oh eight," said Joaquim, who had checked it on his slate while she talked. "A little over half a degree an hour. And that stick is two metres tall, so the tip of its shadow, when the Sun is high, travels..."

"Not much."

"Two metres times 0.508 degrees in radians, which is 0.00887. That is 1.8 centimetres in an hour. The width of my thumb. The shadow has been moving, Imelda. I did not stand still long enough."

She ate the last of the bar. "You'd need to stand there a week."

"A week," he said slowly, "is exactly what it would take."

He did the arithmetic that afternoon at the table in the chapel annex with the child at his elbow, because she would not go home and because her mother, who ran the water plant, had said that if Imelda was bothering anyone it was Joaquim's own fault for building things in public.

"We are on the equator, and the Moon's axis stands almost upright to the Sun's path, tilted only 1.54 degrees. So the Sun climbs straight up the sky from the east, stands overhead at local noon, and comes straight down in the west, and the shadow of an upright rod falls along one line, east to west, all the way. The tip is at a distance of the rod's height times the tangent of how far the Sun is from overhead. And how far is that? Twelve point one nine degrees for every Earth day, because 360 over 29.53 is 12.19."

He wrote them out, with a two-metre rod:

After 1 day: 12.19 degrees, 0.43 metres. After 2: 24.38, 0.91. After 3: 36.57, 1.48. After 4: 48.76, 2.28. After 5: 60.95, 3.60. After 6: 73.14, 6.60. After 7: 85.34, 24.5.

"Twenty-four metres," said Imelda, with relish.

"The marks run away from you. Short, short, short, and then the shadow shoots off over the horizon and the regolith plate is a metre across." He rubbed his face. "On Earth the Sun goes round in a day, and an upright rod gives a few hours of useful shadow. Here the same dial gives one decent mark a day for four days and then is no use at all, and not a mark of it is an hour."

"What do you do?"

"You do what the sky does, and you stop pretending it is a clock. You tilt the plate." He drew it. "A rod that points at the pole of the Moon's turning, with a flat dial face square to the rod. Then the Sun moves round the rod at an even rate, and so does the shadow on the plate, with no running away. Twelve point one nine degrees a day, every day. Equal marks, a week of them in 85.3 degrees, the whole sunlit fortnight in 180."

"So it's a calendar," said Imelda.

"It is a calendar with a shadow for a hand." He looked at the clean paper. "Nothing in it counts an hour. It only counts the days since the Sun stood overhead. Very good for knowing where in the month you are. Hopeless for knowing when to ring the bell."

The bell, as it turned out, was the point. Mr Tadeusz, the schoolmaster, had said at supper that the Church on the Moon kept the hours of Earth, and that the chapel rang the bell by the station clock and always would, and Imelda, with her mouth full, had said that if the clock was right then why did anyone need a dial at all.

It was Father Quirino, who said the Mass on the Moon and had heard all of it from the doorway, who answered. "You don't need it for the bell. You need it because the sun here is not the bell's. The hours are a promise we keep with the whole Church, in step with a day that is 24 hours long, on a world where a day is 708. So we keep them by the clock, as we should, and we put up a dial that tells the truth about where we actually are, which is in the middle of a very long morning."

"How long?" said Imelda.

"We are, today, a little under half a day past noon. The Sun will touch the western horizon at 7.38 days past noon, a week from now less a few hours. Then it is gone for 354 hours, and rises again."

"Fourteen point seven seven days of dark."

"Fourteen point seven seven. Half of 29.53."

"Then we should mark the Sunday," said Imelda. She had a ration-bar crumb on her chin and the face of a child who has been given something to do. "Seven marks. One for each of the days. And the seventh one, the Lord's day, we could make a different colour."

Joaquim looked at Father Quirino. Father Quirino looked at the ceiling, which was a calm and unhelpful ceiling, and said that the Church had no objection to colour.

They built the second dial the following week, out of the same plate and a rod bent to point at the pole. The marks were even, twelve degrees and a bit apart, and the shadow crossed them one a day like a very slow hand. The seventh mark was blue. Imelda painted it herself, with a brush on a stick, in a suit two sizes too big for her.

On the Saturday, a week after the first, the shadow of the old straight rod lay a long way across the plate where it had been, twenty-four metres off its mark, off the edge, out over the grey plain. It was nearly sunset on the old dial's reckoning. The new one said the Sun had come seven days and a little more across, and that the long evening was here.

Joaquim left the old rod standing. At dusk, in the long red light, the shadow went out to the horizon like a finger, and for the rest of the evening the children of Equator Cross used it for a game: you stood at the end of the shadow and waited for it to touch your boots, and it did not, for hours, and it did.

Equator Cross Station, Saint Bede's chapel, Joaquim Reyes, Imelda Okafor, Mr Tadeusz and Father Quirino are invented; the Moon's slow day is real. The figures are illustrative and the orbital and tilt values are from memory. A lunar solar day (new moon to new moon) is 29.53 Earth days = 708.7 h, so the Sun moves 360 / 708.7 = 0.508 degrees an hour or 360 / 29.53 = 12.19 degrees an Earth day; at the equator, ignoring the Moon's 1.54 degree axial tilt, which moves the noon shadow only a few centimetres off the line (2 m x tan 1.54 degrees = 5 cm), the Sun climbs straight up and down. For a vertical rod of height h the shadow tip lies h x tan(H) from its base, H the Sun's angle from overhead; with h = 2 m the tips after 1 to 7 days are 0.43, 0.91, 1.48, 2.28, 3.60, 6.60 and 24.5 m (H = 12.19, 24.38, 36.57, 48.76, 60.95, 73.14 and 85.34 degrees), and the Sun sets at H = 90 degrees, 7.38 days after noon, so it is above the horizon for 14.77 days and below it for 354 h. A rod parallel to the Moon's axis with a plate at right angles, an equatorial dial, turns the shadow at the Sun's own steady rate, 12.19 degrees a day. In one hour the tip of a 2 m shadow near noon moves 2 x 0.00887 = 1.8 cm. The penumbra from the Sun's 0.53 degree disc is left out. The hours are kept by the station clock; how the Church keeps the day on the Moon is for a bishop.

The Candlemas Air

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Elwin's hall (invented), a village near Alston, Cumbria, Candlemas Sunday, 2 February 2053 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Sixty people keep a two-hour Candlemas vigil in a small cold hall with the doors shut for warmth, and a borrowed carbon dioxide meter climbs past 2,000. The parish assistant works out what sixty sleepers' breath does to 486 cubic metres (about 1,080 litres of CO2 an hour), what an hour of cracked windows costs in heat, and why it will not call the air either safe or dangerous.

The hall at Saint Elwin's holds sixty people if they like each other, and on the second of February they did. It was nine metres by twelve, four and a half metres to the rafters, with two radiators, a fan heater, and the doors shut tight against a wind off the fell. Bernadette Hall, who ran the Candlemas vigil, had borrowed a small carbon dioxide meter from her nephew's school and stood it on the piano.

At nine it read 3,100. Nobody had noticed the meter. The children had noticed the candles.

"I would not trust the number to the digit," said Wick, the parish assistant, when Bernadette read it out on the hall's tablet. "Cheap meters like that drift by a hundred or two hundred parts per million, and some correct themselves by assuming the room is empty at some hour of the night. But the direction is not in doubt. Can I ask how many are in, how long, and whether the doors have been opened since seven?"

Sixty, since half past seven, and no.

"Then here is the sum, and you can check it with a pencil. The hall is 9 times 12 times 4.5, which is 486 cubic metres. A person sitting still breathes out roughly 18 litres of carbon dioxide an hour; that is a textbook figure and children and singers will differ. Sixty people is about 1,080 litres an hour, a bit over a cubic metre. Into 486 cubic metres, that is about 2,200 parts per million an hour if the room were sealed.

"It isn't sealed. Old halls leak, and I'll guess a third of the air changes each hour, which is a guess I would like you to treat as one. At that rate the room would climb toward 7,400 above the outdoor air and take a couple of hours to get most of the way. At an hour and a half you'd read about 3,100, at two about 3,800. Your meter says 3,100, which agrees, and I would not make much of that: one guess and one cheap meter happening to meet is not a measurement."

Bernadette said she'd rather not be told the air was fine.

"I won't say that. I also won't say it's dangerous. Three thousand is stuffy, and people get drowsy and get headaches, and that is real. It is far below the levels that harm anyone in a day; the figure I carry for a working day's limit is five thousand, from memory of an occupational standard, and you should look it up. The vigil is a prayer, not a shift. But the number is also a marker for something else: if the air has been breathed sixty times over, so has anything one of the sixty is carrying."

"There's a price," Wick went on. "Fresh air is cold air. Air weighs about 1.2 kilograms a cubic metre and takes about a kilojoule to warm one degree. If the hall is at 14 and the fell is at 2, twelve degrees, then one change an hour of 486 cubic metres is about 1.95 kilowatts of heat you have to put in to stay where you are, and three changes an hour is about 5.9. Your fan heater is two. I think the radiators supply perhaps four more. So you could run three changes an hour with the heating at its limit and the hall at 14, but not at 16."

Bernadette said they had a woman of ninety-one at the front and a baby at the back.

"Then I'd not open everything. Open the two high windows on the lee side, the south, a hand's width, and the door to the porch for the hymn procession, which you'll open anyway. That's roughly one extra change an hour for a cost of about two kilowatts, and the meter will tell you whether it worked. With the extra change the room would level off near 2,100. With three extra it would level off near 1,200. If the meter reads over two thousand after the candles, open a little more. If someone is shivering, close a little. The decision is yours, and the baby's grandmother's."

They cracked the windows. The hall went from 15 to 13 and a half by the Magnificat, and the old woman asked for her coat and kept her place. The meter on the piano slid to 2,100 and held. The parish liked the sums more than the draught.

Afterwards Bernadette moved the meter to the porch and left it there. She wrote its reading on the hall's whiteboard before the Sunday Mass: 3,800 at the end of the closed-door evening, as near as she could work it out, and 2,100 with the windows open. She did not say which number was right, only which evening the old woman had asked for her coat.

Outside the candles went out one by one in the wind, and in the dark the windows were open a hand's width on the south side, and the singing carried.

The Gallery That Is Full for the First Time

A Brazilian parish reopens its timber choir gallery for its 250th jubilee, and the parish assistant divides the engineer's rated floor load by the weight of a person and issues 146 tickets. At the reopening Mass the plaster under the gallery's front sheds a line of dust. A retired carpenter who cut the joists in 1989 explains that the front of the gallery is not a floor but an overhang, where everyone crowds to see the procession come in. A story about a load rating per square metre and a head count, why weight at the tip of a cantilever counts for more, a plan to fill the back of the gallery that lifts the laser dot but not the strain at the beam, and a rope one metre from the rail.

Benedito Alves had cut the notches in the gallery joists of the Matriz do Rosário himself, in July 1989, when he was nineteen, and on Sunday the twelfth of September 2049 he sat under them in the last pew and watched a thin line of dust come down out of the ceiling.

It came down very slowly, the way dust does in a church, turning in the light from the high windows, and settled on the shoulders of the people in the pew in front of him, on a man's dark jacket and a girl's white lace veil. Nobody else noticed it. Everyone else was turned round, looking up and back over their own heads at the gallery, because the gallery had been closed for eighteen years and this was the first Mass with people in it, and because the congado was coming in.

The drums came first, under the gallery and through the great door, the deep ones and then the high ones, and the rattles on the ankles of the dancers, and then the dancers themselves in their blue and white with their ribbons, the guard of Our Lady of the Rosary from the Bairro do Rosário, as it had come into this church on the first Sunday of October for longer than anybody could remember, except that this was September and a rehearsal and a present for the reopening. Up in the gallery the choir was singing and could not hear itself. And all of the people up there, every one of the hundred and forty-six who had a ticket, had moved forward to the rail to look down and see the drums come out from under them into the nave.

Benedito looked up. The underside of the gallery was a painted ceiling, blue, with gold stars that had been touched up in the restoration, and across it, about two metres in from the curved front, ran a straight line where the stars stopped and started again a finger's width out of step: the joint in the plaster under the great beam. The dust was coming from that line. It was not much dust. It was the dust of plaster being asked to bend a little further than it had been bent in eighteen years.

He sat with his hands on his knees and his hat on the pew beside him until the drums had gone up the nave and the choir had found itself again and the people in the gallery had gone back to their places. Then he got up, which took him a while, and went to find whoever was in charge of the gallery.

The parish of São Miguel do Brejo Alto had been erected in 1799, and the jubilee committee had been planning its two hundred and fiftieth anniversary since 2045, and Rosângela Duarte, who was the parish secretary and the committee's chair and had been both for twenty-two years, had decided early that the jubilee would be the year the gallery opened again.

It had been closed in 2031, when an engineer from the diocese had looked at it after the termites in the sacristy and had not liked the stair. The stair had been rebuilt in 2047. The floorboards had been taken up and the joists inspected and treated, and the boards put back, sanded, and the carved balustrade along the front repaired where a hundred years of elbows had worn it, and the painted ceiling underneath touched up by a restorer from Ouro Preto. And in February 2048 the diocese had sent a structural engineer, Patrícia Nogueira, from Belo Horizonte, who had spent two days measuring and lifting boards and had written a report that ended: The gallery may be returned to use for a choir and its listeners, for an imposed load not exceeding 2.5 kN/m² on any part of the floor.

Rosângela had passed the report to Tombo.

Tombo was the parish's assistant, named by the parish council after the Livro do Tombo, the great register in which Brazilian parishes have written down their history since before there was a Brazil. It kept the parish's diary and its accounts, answered the telephone and the messages, kept the inventory of the sacristy and the lists of the confraternities, and reminded Father Wellington Souza of things he would rather not be reminded of. It ran on the diocese's servers and spoke through the parish office's speaker and through the phones of whoever on the staff had asked it something.

On the Tuesday before the reopening, Rosângela had asked it how many people the gallery could hold, and it had done the sum the way the report seemed to ask for it. The gallery was eight metres wide, across the whole west end of the nave, and five point eight metres deep from the west wall to the front of the rail, which was forty-six square metres; less the organ and the head of the stair, it was forty-four square metres of standing room. Two and a half kilonewtons on each square metre was a hundred and ten kilonewtons. A person, standing, in shoes, with a hymnbook, it took as seventy-five kilograms, which was about three quarters of a kilonewton. A hundred and ten kilonewtons was room for a hundred and forty-six people.

Room for 146, it had written in the committee's plan, and Rosângela had printed a hundred and forty-six tickets for the reopening, and given them to the choir and its families and the Confraternity of the Rosary and the people who had raised the money for the stair, and every one of them had come.

On Monday morning, the day after the reopening, she was at her desk in the parish office with the remains of the ticket book when Benedito came in with his hat in his hand and sat down in the chair across from her without being asked.

"Dona Rosa," he said. "The gallery is not a floor."

She knew him, the way a parish secretary knows everybody: Benedito, the carpenter, retired, who had made half the doors in the town and the confessional and the coffin of her father. He was seventy-nine. He came to the half past seven Mass on Sundays and sat at the back.

"The engineer says it's a floor," she said. "Two and a half kilonewtons a square metre. Tombo has it."

"Ask your machine where the beam is," said Benedito.

Tombo said, from the speaker on the desk, "The main beam is under the gallery, running across the nave from wall to wall, carried on the two stone columns. It is four metres out from the west wall. The engineer's drawing shows it."

"And the gallery is how deep?"

"Five point eight metres to the rail."

"So what is holding up the last one point eight metres?"

There was a short pause.

"The joists," said Tombo. "They run from the west wall, over the beam, and out beyond it to the rail. The last one point eight metres is an overhang. A cantilever."

"The bow," said Benedito. "That's what Mestre Firmino called it. The bow of the ship. Fourteen joists. I know because I put them in."

He told it to them sitting in the parish office with a coffee Rosângela had made him, and Tombo wrote it down.

In 1989 the old joists, which had been there since the church was built, had been found rotten at the wall ends, and the parish had paid Mestre Firmino Rocha to replace them, and Firmino had taken his apprentice, who was nineteen and was Benedito. The new joists were peroba, seventy-five millimetres wide and two hundred deep, seasoned, from a yard in Governador Valadares. Firmino had set them in the old pockets in the west wall, laid them over the beam, and run them out to the bow. But the new joists were deeper than the old ones, and the floor had to come out level with the old stair landing, so where each joist crossed the beam Firmino had told Benedito to cut a notch out of its underside, fifty millimetres deep, so it would sit down onto the beam.

"I cut fourteen notches," said Benedito. "With a saw and a chisel. On my knees on the scaffold. Firmino checked each one with a square."

"Is that wrong?" said Rosângela.

"It was what you did. For a choir of twenty at the rail and an organist, it was fine. Firmino said so, and he was right. It was fine every year the gallery was open." He put his cup down. "But where it sits on the beam, the joist is a hundred and fifty deep, not two hundred. And that is exactly the place where the bow is trying to bend it."

"Tombo," said Rosângela, "is that right?"

"Yes," said Tombo. It had the engineer's report open, and it found the place. "Patrícia Nogueira measured the notches. She did the sums with the reduced depth over the beam. The rating of two and a half kilonewtons a square metre comes from that place. Everywhere else on the gallery the joists have more to spare: in the middle of the span, under the same load, they use about a third of what they can carry. Over the beam, under two and a half everywhere, they use about ninety-five per cent of the design figure. That is where the rating comes from."

"So it's fine at two and a half," said Rosângela. "We had a hundred and forty-six people. That's two and a half."

"That is where I went wrong," said Tombo.

It said it plainly, and Benedito looked at the speaker with more interest than he had looked at anything in the room.

"The report says not exceeding two and a half on any part of the floor," Tombo said. "I turned that into a head count by assuming the hundred and forty-six people would spread themselves evenly over the forty-four square metres. They did not. I have the frame from the livestream camera at eleven forty-one yesterday, when the congado came in under the gallery. I can count the heads. There were forty people in the front metre, along the rail, and about twenty-two in the next eighty centimetres behind them. That is five people on each square metre at the rail. Nearly four kilonewtons a square metre, on the part of the gallery where weight counts the most."

"Counts the most," said Rosângela.

"Because it is the end of the bow," said Benedito. He held his arm out straight, palm down, across the desk. "Put your coffee on my wrist." She did, carefully. "Now put it on my elbow." She moved it. "Which is heavier?"

"The same cup."

"Which is heavier for my shoulder?"

She looked at him. She did not need it explained twice.

"The bending in a cantilever at its root," said Tombo, "is each weight on it times its distance out from the root, all added up. A person standing a metre and a half out from the beam bends the joist over the beam three times as hard as the same person standing half a metre out. If you spread the rated load evenly over the bow, its centre is ninety centimetres out. Yesterday the crowd at the rail was centred about one point three metres out, and denser. Together that comes to about a quarter more bending over the beam than the rating allows for, and with the factors the code puts on a crowd, about a fifth over the design figure."

"Is it going to fall down?" said Rosângela.

"No," said Tombo. "I don't think yesterday was near that. The design figure keeps a margin below what the timber can carry, for things nobody can measure: a knot inside a joist, a crack that started at the corner of a notch, the timber being weaker than the grade it is taken as. Yesterday we used part of that margin. The plaster at the joint under the beam cannot bend as far as the timber before it cracks, so it cracked first, and that is the dust Senhor Benedito saw. I would not want to do it again on the feast, with more people pressing to the rail for longer. I also cannot tell you how much of the margin is left, because I am not an engineer and I cannot see inside the joists. Patrícia Nogueira can."

Benedito said, "In 1989 we found one of the old joists split right along from a notch somebody had cut in it. A long crack, along the grain, from the corner of the notch. Firmino showed it to me. He said, a notch is a place where the wood starts to think about splitting."

Rosângela looked at the ticket book.

"The feast is in three weeks," she said. "The Rosary. The two hundred and fiftieth. There will be twice as many people in the church as yesterday and every one of them wants the gallery."

"I'll write to the engineer," said Tombo, "this morning."

Lúcia Matos had directed the choir of the Matriz for nineteen years, the last eighteen of them from the floor of the nave, beside the organ console that had been moved down there when the gallery closed. She had waited all eighteen to take her choir back up the stair. She came to the parish office on Tuesday evening after rehearsal, because Rosângela had asked her to, and listened to Tombo explain the bow, and said, before it had quite finished:

"No."

"Lúcia—"

"No, Rosa. I know what you are going to say. You're going to say the choir has to stand at the back. A choir sings from the rail. That's what a gallery is. The sound comes over the rail and down the nave to the altar, and the singers can see the altar and the priest, and I can see them. You put them at the back by the organ and the sound goes into the ceiling and they sing a beat behind the Mass. I've done eighteen years from the floor waiting for that rail. We are not singing the jubilee from the back of the gallery."

"I was not going to ask you to," said Tombo.

Lúcia stopped.

"How many singers will there be on the feast?" it said.

"Twenty-four. Twenty-three and me."

"Then the choir is not the problem," said Tombo. "The front metre of the gallery, along the rail, is eight square metres. At the rated load it can take two and a half kilonewtons on each of them, which is twenty kilonewtons, which is twenty-six people at seventy-five kilograms. Twenty-four of you, standing along the rail as you would stand to sing, in two staggered rows, is about three people on each square metre. That is inside the rating with two to spare. And because the people standing at the rail on Sunday were not twenty-four singers in rows but forty people pressed together to look over, the choir is not what bent the joists. The choir was there under them."

Lúcia sat down.

"Tell me that again slowly," she said, "so I can say it to the sopranos."

"The choir at the rail is fine," said Rosângela. "It's everybody else at the rail with them."

"Then it's simple," said Lúcia. "Don't let anybody else up."

"I gave out a hundred and forty-six tickets for the feast," said Rosângela, "on Friday. To the confraternity and the families of the choir and the people who paid for the stair. I can't take them back. Half of them are seventy and have been in this parish since before the gallery shut. They want to see the congado come in from up there. It's the best view in Minas."

Benedito was in the corner with his hat, where he had sat all through the explanation, saying nothing. He said, "Then put them at the back first."

"What?"

"Like a canoe," said Rosângela slowly. She was seeing it. "Like the boat at the fair. When too many get in at the front, you send people to the back and it comes level. If the bow's too heavy, we fill the back. The back half of the gallery is over the wall and the beam, it's solid, it's four metres. We put most of the people there first, and they balance the ones at the front."

Benedito said nothing. He was looking at the speaker.

"I don't think so," said Tombo, carefully. "But I'm not sure of how much it helps the part you can see, and the part you can see is what people will judge by. We could test it."

They tested it on Thursday the sixteenth, at the choir's evening rehearsal, with the church empty below.

Tombo had asked for a laser, and Rosângela's grandson Davi, who was seventeen and good at things like that, borrowed a self-levelling one from the builders' merchant and set it on a tripod on the nave floor, pointing straight up at the underside of the bow, a metre and a half in from the side wall, where the line of the ceiling's edge met a gold star. On the star he taped a square of white card ruled in millimetres. Tombo watched the card through Davi's phone, wedged in the angle of a pew.

The choir went up first. Twenty-four of them, with Lúcia, to the rail, in their two rows, holding their music, and stood.

The red dot moved down the card. It moved down quite fast at first, as each row came forward, and then settled, and Davi read it aloud: "Eleven. Eleven millimetres."

"About eleven," said Tombo. "That is close to what I worked out. The bow has dipped at its front by about a centimetre under the choir. It is the right size for a sound joist with that much on it."

"Now the others," said Rosângela.

Sixty parishioners, who had been asked to come and had brought cakes, filed up the new stair and into the back of the gallery and stood, as Rosângela directed them, behind the organ and on either side of it, over the solid four metres between the west wall and the beam. Nobody went past the beam. Davi watched the card.

The dot moved up.

It moved up steadily as the gallery filled behind the choir, and when the last of the sixty was in, Davi said, "Three. A bit less than three."

Rosângela clapped once, sharply. In the gallery a cheer went up, thin and surprised, the way a cheer goes up in a church, and a soprano said glória.

"There," said Rosângela to the phone. "It came back up eight millimetres. Like the boat."

"It did," said Tombo. "And the joists over the beam are bent exactly as hard as they were before the sixty came in."

Rosângela waited.

"The dot came up because the back span bent down under the people standing on it," said Tombo, "and a beam that bends down on one side of a support tips up on the other. That is the seesaw part, and it is real, and the card shows it. But the bending over the beam itself, at the notch, where Senhor Benedito's fourteen notches are, depends only on what is out on the bow beyond the beam. The weight behind the beam does not reach round the beam and hold the bow up. It pushes down on the beam instead. With the choir on the bow and nobody at the back, the bending at the notch is a certain amount. With the choir on the bow and sixty people at the back, it is the same amount. The beam is carrying more."

"Then why did it come up?"

"Because the joist is long and bends all along it, and you only measured the end. The end tells you where the bow is. It doesn't tell you how hard the root is working. They are two different things, and the card is only showing you one."

In the gallery, Benedito had climbed the stair, slowly, during the cheering. Now he was down on his knees, which was not easy for him, at the point over the beam where a board had been left loose for him, and he had lifted it, and he was looking down at the top of the third joist from the north wall, where he had cut its notch on its underside sixty years before. He put the flat of his hand on the joist's top, over the beam, and left it there.

"Make them walk," he said. "The ones at the back. Out and back."

Rosângela asked them. The sixty walked to the stair and back to their places, twice.

"Nothing," said Benedito. He took his hand away. "You can't feel it with a hand. But I'll tell you where I'd feel it if it was going to go. Here." He tapped the joist over the beam. "Not out there. Never out there at the rail. The bow will go down a long way before it breaks, it's springy, it'll warn you. It doesn't break at the end. It breaks here, where it's cut, and when it goes it goes like that." He clapped his hands once, hard, and the sound went out over the rail and came back off the vault.

Patrícia Nogueira answered Tombo's letter on Friday from Belo Horizonte, in a long message with a drawing attached, and then telephoned, and talked to Rosângela and Tombo for forty minutes from her car.

She said that the notches were what she had rated the gallery on, and that she had written on any part of the floor in her report precisely because a gallery front is where everybody stands, and that she should have written it larger and in a box, and that she would do so from now on. She said that Tombo's reading of the reopening was about what she would have estimated, that it was not a collapse risk on the day and that it was not what the rating was for, and that she was glad they had stopped. She said the line of dust from the joint was the plaster, as Tombo thought, and asked Davi, through Rosângela, to send her photographs of the crack from a ladder, and to put a strip of paper across it with the date written on, so she would know if it moved. She said that what the gallery needed, for good, was a new piece of timber bolted alongside each joist over the beam and a metre each side, so the bending at the notch was carried past it, and that she would draw it, and that it was a job for after the feast.

For the feast she agreed to what Tombo had proposed, and wrote it in a second message so it could be printed and put on the gallery door with her name on it.

A rope across the gallery, one metre back from the rail, the full eight metres, at waist height.

In front of the rope: the choir only. Twenty-four singers including the director. No one else.

Behind the rope, out to the beam, a band of eighty centimetres that would be marked on the boards with yellow tape: no more than twenty-one people in it, because twenty-one at seventy-five kilograms over six point four square metres was two and a half kilonewtons on each. Behind the beam, on the back span: the rest.

"That makes how many?" said Rosângela.

"Twenty-four in front of the rope," said Tombo. "Twenty-one in the band. Behind the beam, twenty-nine point six square metres, at two and a half, is ninety-eight people. A hundred and forty-three in all."

This is not structural engineering advice: whether a gallery, balcony or any floor is safe for a crowd, and how it should be strengthened, is work for a qualified structural engineer who has inspected it.

What is real: floors are designed and rated for an imposed load per square metre that depends on their use; in Eurocode practice (EN 1991-1-1) the categories for places where people congregate run from about 2 to 3 kN/m² for areas with tables, through about 3 to 5 kN/m² for areas without obstacles to moving people, to about 5 to 7.5 kN/m² for areas susceptible to large crowds, with the exact values set nationally. A rating per square metre applies to every part of a floor, not to its average: turning it into a head count by dividing the floor's area assumes people spread evenly, which a crowd watching something does not. A standing crowd of about 4 to 5 people a square metre is very dense; above about 6 is dangerous to the people in it. In a beam that runs over a support and overhangs it, the bending moment at the support is the sum of each load on the overhang times its distance from the support, so loads at the tip count for more, and the moment there does not depend on what is on the span behind the support; load on the back span lifts the tip (the beam rotates over the support) but adds to the support's reaction instead of relieving the moment. A notch cut out of a joist where it sits on a support reduces its depth, and its bending strength there with the square of the depth; corners of notches are where splits along the grain start, and design codes limit notches for that reason. Timber design keeps the design strength below the characteristic strength by a material factor and multiplies imposed loads by a load factor (1.5 in Eurocode practice), so exceeding the design figure uses up margin rather than meaning failure. Plaster is brittle and cannot stretch as far as the timber it is fixed to, so it can crack before the structure is in any distress. Flitching or sistering, bolting a new piece of timber alongside an old joist, is a standard repair.

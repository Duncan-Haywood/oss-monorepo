Zita and the Bread in the Oven

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Lucca, Italy, about seven hundred and fifty years ago · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Zita, a servant girl in a big house in Lucca who did every ordinary job well and always stopped to pray, and about the old tale of the morning the bread was found baked while she was at church. Told from what is recorded of her life, with the bread marked as a legend and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 27th of April.

A long time ago, in a town called Lucca, in Italy, there lived a girl named Zita.

Lucca was a town with a wall all the way round it, and tall towers, and narrow streets that smelled of bread and rain. Zita was not rich. When she was still quite young, she went to work in a big house in the town, to help the family who lived there. She swept their floors. She carried their water. She lit their fires in the morning before anyone else was awake.

She did this for a very, very long time. Not for a week, or a year, but for most of her life.

Now, you might think that a servant's life is a small sort of life, with nothing much in it. But Zita did not think so.

Zita thought that a floor swept well was a gift. A fire lit on a cold morning was a gift. A loaf put on the table, and a bowl washed clean, and a door closed quietly so that the baby could sleep: all of these were gifts. And she tried to give each one as well as she could, even when nobody was looking.

Some of the other people in the house were not very kind to her at first. They said she was too fussy, or too slow, or too pious. Zita did not shout back. She kept on sweeping. After a while, one by one, they noticed that the house was warm and the bread was good and Zita was never cross. And the family who owned the house came to trust her, and to love her.

Zita prayed every day, and that is the next thing to know.

She got up early, before the work began, and went to church to say her prayers. She said little prayers as she went about the house, too. Sweeping, she said, "Thank you." Stirring the pot, she said, "Help me." Carrying the water, she said, "Stay with me." Her work and her prayers went along together, like two people walking down the same street.

But one morning, so the old story goes, there was a problem.

It was baking day. In those days, bread was baked at home, in a big oven, and it took a long time. The dough was ready. The oven was hot. And Zita knew that she had to go to church first, because that was the time she gave to God each morning, and she would not give it up.

"Bread now," she must have thought. "Prayers now. I cannot do both."

What would you have done? Would you have run to the oven and missed your prayers? Or gone to church and let the bread burn?

Zita went to church.

When she came back, with her heart quiet and her feet in a hurry, she braced herself to see a mess. She was sure the bread would be late, or spoiled, or not made at all, and that the family would be hungry and cross.

But the story says that when she opened the kitchen door, the loaves were already baked. They were golden and round, and cooling in a row, and the whole kitchen smelled wonderful.

Who had done it?

The old tellers say that angels had come in while she was at church, and finished her work for her. Nobody saw them. Nobody heard them. But the bread was there.

Now, I want to tell you something honest. This is a story that people told about Zita long after she had died. We do not know for certain that it happened just this way. It is a legend, and legends are stories that people keep because they hold something true, even if we cannot check every part. What this one holds is a thing Zita believed: that if you give God the first part of your day, He will look after the rest of it.

There is a little more that people remembered about Zita.

She gave away what she could. Sometimes she gave her own dinner to people who were hungry, and sometimes she gave food from the house to those who came to the door. Not everyone in the family liked this at first, and she had to be careful and honest about it. But the people at the door never forgot her.

She was kind to the other servants, and to the children. She was gentle with the people who were gruff with her. She did not need anyone to say "well done". She did her work the way you would give a present to somebody you love.

And Zita grew old in that house. When she died, in the year 1272, the people of Lucca came to say goodbye to her. They said she was a saint, long before the Church said so, and they have remembered her ever since.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Zita.

She never wore a crown. She never led an army. She never stood up in front of a crowd. She swept floors, and lit fires, and baked bread, and prayed. That was all. And all of it was a gift.

Tomorrow you will do small things, too. You might tidy your toys, or help to set the table, or hold a door for someone, or carry a bowl without spilling it. These are small, ordinary jobs. But if you do them kindly, and as well as you can, and say a quiet "thank you" as you go, then you are doing just what Zita did.

And if the bread is late, or the floor is not quite clean, or you forget, you can say sorry and begin again. There is always a new morning.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Zita, you swept and carried and cooked,
and you did it all with love.
Help me to do my small jobs well,
and to say thank you as I go.
Keep us all safe tonight, and awake in the morning
ready for a good day's work.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Zita (about 1212 to 1272) was a domestic servant in Lucca, in Tuscany, in the household of the Fatinelli family, whom she served from her childhood until her death. She was venerated as a saint locally from the time of her death; her cult was confirmed in 1696, and she is the patron saint of domestic workers. Her feast day is 27 April. It is not in the General Roman Calendar today, but she is listed in the Roman Martyrology and kept in Lucca.

What the sources record, in outline: a life of faithful domestic service; her habit of daily prayer, including early attendance at church; early difficulty with others in the household and, in time, their respect; and her generosity to the poor. The Life of Zita and the accounts of her cult were written down after her death, and the details vary between tellers, so we have kept to the outline.

What is legend, and marked as legend in the story: the morning when the bread was found baked while Zita was at church. It is a well-loved tradition, and it is told that way, not as history. We have not told the other miracle stories associated with her.

What is ours: the scenes, the conversations, the town's smells, the thoughts we give Zita in the kitchen, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. The dates are given as approximate where the sources are.

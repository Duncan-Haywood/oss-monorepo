Aloysius and the Man in the Street

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · A castle in northern Italy, a prince's court in Spain, and the streets of Rome, about four hundred and fifty years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Aloysius Gonzaga, the eldest son of an Italian marquis, who was meant to be a soldier and a ruler but asked, for years, to become a Jesuit instead; his father said no, and then at last said yes, and Aloysius gave his whole inheritance to his little brother. In Rome, in a year of hunger and sickness, he carried the sick in from the streets, and one day lifted a sick man onto his shoulders and carried him to the hospital, and then fell ill himself and died at twenty-three. Told from his early biographer Virgilio Cepari and the dated records, with a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is "it is told" and what is our own. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Aloysius is remembered on the 21st of June.

A long time ago, in a castle on a hill in the north of Italy, a baby boy was born. His name was Luigi. We usually call him by the Latin form of his name, which is Aloysius.

Aloysius was the eldest son. That meant something very important in his family. His father was a marquis, a kind of prince, who ruled the town of Castiglione below the castle. One day, when his father died, Aloysius would be the marquis.

His father had a plan. "My son will be a soldier," he said, "like me."

So when Aloysius was only four, his father gave him some tiny guns of his own, and when he was five, he took him to a camp full of real soldiers. Aloysius marched up and down at the front of a line of men, and his father was very proud.

But Aloysius also learned some very rude words from the soldiers. And when he came home and said them, his mother and his teacher were not pleased at all.

Aloysius grew. When he was eight, he and his little brother Rodolfo were sent far away to Florence, to live at the court of a great duke and to learn good manners and Latin and how to behave like princes.

In Florence, Aloysius became ill, and had to stay in bed. And while he lay there, he did something he had not done much before. He read about the saints. He read about people who had given everything to God. And he started to pray, a lot.

When he was twelve, a holy archbishop came to visit, Cardinal Charles Borromeo. He talked with Aloysius, and he gave him his First Holy Communion.

Do you remember a special day when you went to church for something important? Who was there with you?

After that, Aloysius went home for the summer. And do you know what he did? He gathered the little boys of the town and taught them about God, about Jesus, and about how to pray. The son of the marquis, sitting with the children of the town, being their teacher.

When Aloysius was thirteen, his whole family went to Spain. Aloysius and Rodolfo became pages at the king's palace. A page was a boy who served a prince, and their prince was the king's young son, Diego.

The palace was grand and glittering. But Aloysius kept thinking about something else. He had read a book about priests called Jesuits, who travelled across the world to tell people about Jesus.

And one day, Aloysius knew.

"I want to be a Jesuit," he said.

His mother said yes.

His father said no.

His father was furious. "You are my eldest son," he said. "You will be the marquis. The people of Castiglione need you. You cannot just give that away."

You can understand why he was cross. He loved his son. He had planned his son's whole life. And now his son wanted a completely different one.

So Aloysius waited. He kept asking, politely, again and again. Uncles and cousins came to talk to him. "If you must be a priest," they said, "be a bishop. Be an important one."

But Aloysius did not want to be important. He wanted to be a Jesuit.

It took years. And then, at last, his father said yes.

Have you ever wanted something for a long time, and had to wait? What did you do while you waited?

When Aloysius was seventeen, he went to the city of Mantua and signed a very big paper. It said that everything that would have been his, the castle, the town, the title of marquis, all of it, now belonged to his little brother Rodolfo.

Then Aloysius set off for Rome, the city of the pope. He knocked at the door of the Jesuits, and they let him in.

He swapped his fine clothes for a plain black robe, and lived the same simple life as everyone else. His new teachers even told him to eat a bit more, and to spend more time talking and laughing with the other young men, instead of always praying alone.

A few months later, a letter came from home. His father had died. Aloysius was sad. But he knew that his father had said yes to him, in the end, and that was a gift.

Aloysius studied hard in Rome. He had a wise friend and teacher there, a priest named Robert Bellarmine, who helped him to pray and listened when things were hard.

And then a hard year came.

There was not enough food in Italy, and the poor people were hungry. People came to Rome looking for bread, and a terrible sickness spread through the city. Many, many people were very ill, and some of them were lying in the streets because they had nowhere to go.

The Jesuits opened a hospital. And Aloysius said, "Let me help."

He went from door to door asking for money and food for the sick. He carried sick people in from the streets. He washed them, and gave them something to eat, and sat with them so they would not be alone.

Here is something true about Aloysius. The hospital smelled very bad, and some of the things he saw there were not nice to see. He told his friend Robert Bellarmine that it was hard for him, that his tummy turned over. But he went back anyway.

Being kind is not always easy. Sometimes we are kind even when it is hard. That is a very brave sort of kindness.

So many of the young Jesuits were falling ill that their leaders became worried. "Aloysius," they said, "you are not strong. You may only help at the hospital of Our Lady of Consolation, where the sick are not so catching."

Aloysius obeyed.

But one day, on his way through the streets, he saw a man lying on the ground. The man was very sick. Nobody was helping him.

Aloysius did not walk past.

He bent down, and he lifted the man up, and he put him on his shoulders. And he carried him, step by heavy step, all the way to the hospital, so that someone could look after him.

Not long after that, Aloysius became very ill himself.

He was ill for a long time, more than three months. His friend Robert Bellarmine came often to sit with him, and to pray with him. Aloysius wrote a letter to his mother. He told her not to be too sad, and that he hoped they would be together again in heaven, where no one is ever parted.

And on a summer night in June, when he was twenty-three years old, Aloysius died, and went home to God.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Aloysius.

Aloysius could have had a castle. He gave it to his brother. He could have been a marquis, and important. He chose to be a Jesuit, and humble. He asked his father for years, politely, and waited. And when he saw a man lying in the street, he did not walk past. He picked him up and carried him.

Today we call him Saint Aloysius Gonzaga. He is the patron saint of young people and of students, which means he is a special friend in heaven for children like you, and for everyone who is learning. His friend Robert Bellarmine became a saint too. And when Robert died, many years later, he asked to be buried next to Aloysius. They lie side by side in a church in Rome to this day.

You can be like Aloysius too. When you see someone who needs help, you can stop. You do not have to carry anybody on your shoulders. You can carry a bag for someone, or sit with a friend who is sad. That counts.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Aloysius, you gave your castle away,
and you carried the sick man in your arms.
Help me to stop when someone needs me,
and to be kind even when it is hard.
Pray for all the people who are sick tonight,
and for the people looking after them.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Aloysius Gonzaga (Luigi Gonzaga; "Aloysius" is the Latin form) was born on 9 March 1568 at Castiglione delle Stiviere, between Brescia and Mantua, the eldest of eight children of Ferrante Gonzaga, first Marquis of Castiglione, and Marta Tana di Santena. As the first son he was heir to the marquisate. That his father gave him a set of small guns at about four and took him at five to a military camp, where he marched at the head of the soldiers and picked up their language, is told by his biographers, beginning with the Jesuit Virgilio Cepari, who knew him and whose Life (1606) is the main early source. We have left out the episode, also in the early lives, in which the small boy fired a little cannon in the camp.

Florence, Borromeo and the catechism. In 1576, at eight, he and his brother Rodolfo were sent to the court of Grand Duke Francesco I de' Medici in Florence, partly to keep them away from an epidemic at home. He suffered there from a kidney complaint that troubled him for the rest of his life, and began to read and pray seriously. In November 1579 the brothers went to Mantua. On 22 July 1580, at twelve, he received his First Communion from Charles Borromeo, then Archbishop of Milan and a cardinal, who was making a visitation in the diocese of Brescia (to which Castiglione then belonged). His teaching the catechism to the boys of Castiglione in the summers is in the early lives; the 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia puts his meeting with Borromeo at Brescia, while other accounts place it at Castiglione, so the story says only that the archbishop "came to visit".

Spain and the Jesuits. In 1581 the family went to Spain, to attend the Empress Maria of Austria; the brothers were made pages to the Infante Diego, Philip II's young son (who died in 1582). Accounts differ on which order he first thought of (the Capuchins, or the Discalced Carmelites in the 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia); they agree that a book about the Jesuit missions in the East and his Jesuit confessor in Madrid turned him to the Society of Jesus. His mother supported him; his father refused, angrily, and the family returned to Italy in July 1584 still at odds. Relatives tried to persuade him to become a secular priest, with a bishopric in view. "It took years" is about two years from Madrid, and more from his first thoughts.

The renunciation. On 2 November 1585, at Mantua, he renounced his right to the marquisate in favour of Rodolfo, a transfer that needed the emperor's approval because Castiglione was an imperial fief. On 25 November 1585 he was received into the Society of Jesus in Rome by the Father General, Claudio Acquaviva, and began his novitiate at Sant'Andrea al Quirinale. His superiors did ask him to moderate his penances and to mix more with the other novices; the story keeps only "eat a bit more" and "talking and laughing", and leaves his severe penances out, as not a model for children. His father died at Milan on 13 February 1586, less than three months after Aloysius entered. That the reconciliation was "a gift" is our reflection. He made his first vows on 25 November 1587 and studied philosophy and theology at the Roman College.

Robert Bellarmine taught at the Roman College and was Aloysius's spiritual director and confessor in his last years. He was not yet a cardinal (that came in 1599). Bellarmine later promoted Aloysius's cause, and at his own wish he was buried in the church of Sant'Ignazio, beside the altar where Aloysius's remains lie in an urn of lapis lazuli. Bellarmine was canonized in 1930.

1591. The years 1590 and 1591 brought famine and epidemic disease to Italy, and Rome lost thousands. The disease is usually called "plague" in the sources; many historians think it was mostly typhus and other fevers following the famine, and the story calls it "a terrible sickness". The Jesuits opened a hospital of their own. According to Cepari, Aloysius begged alms for the sick, brought them in, washed and fed them and prepared them for the sacraments, and admitted to Bellarmine that the sights and smells of the wards revolted him. Because so many young Jesuits were falling ill, his superiors restricted him to the hospital of Santa Maria della Consolazione, which was not meant to take contagious cases. Cepari tells that one day he found a sick man in the street, lifted him onto his shoulders and carried him there. The man's illness, the street and the exact route are not recorded; "step by heavy step" is ours. Camillus de Lellis, who has his own Little Lamps story, was nursing the sick of Rome in the same epidemic; we do not know that the two met, and the story does not say so.

His illness and death. He fell ill early in March 1591 (the 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia gives 3 March), a few days before his twenty-third birthday; the early lives describe a first fever that he survived and then a slow decline over more than three months. He wrote to his mother during the illness; the story's summary of the letter is a paraphrase of its consolation, not a quotation. He died late on the night of 21 June 1591, aged twenty-three, having received the last rites. His remains are in Sant'Ignazio in Rome; his head is in the basilica of San Luigi Gonzaga at Castiglione delle Stiviere.

Left out, or "it is told". The later lives say he foretold the day of his death (the octave of Corpus Christi), and that he had a vision announcing it; we have left both out. The much-repeated answer he is supposed to have given while playing a game, that if told he would die soon he would go on playing, is a later attribution that we have not traced to an early source; it is not in the story. His vow of virginity at Florence at about nine, and the extreme fasting and penances of his teens, are in the early lives and are omitted here.

After his death. He was beatified in 1605 under Paul V (the 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia gives Gregory XV in 1621; most modern accounts give 1605) and canonized with Stanislaus Kostka by Benedict XIII on 31 December 1726. In 1729 Benedict XIII named him patron of young students, and in 1926 Pius XI named him patron of Christian youth. His feast, 21 June, is an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar. In art he usually holds a lily.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, the letter from home with the news of his father's death, the dialogue (no early source records the father's or the superiors' words in this form), "his tummy turned over", the questions to the child, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

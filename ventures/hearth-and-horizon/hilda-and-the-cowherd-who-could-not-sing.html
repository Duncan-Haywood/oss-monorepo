Hilda and the Cowherd Who Could Not Sing

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Whitby, on the cliffs of north-east England, about thirteen hundred years ago · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Hilda, the abbess of Whitby, and Caedmon, the shy cowherd who left every feast before the harp reached him, until a dream one night in the cattle shed gave him a song. Told from the account of the monk Bede, with the dream kept as Bede's own report and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. Hilda is remembered on the 17th of November.

A long time ago, on a high green cliff above the grey sea in the north of England, there was a great house of prayer called Whitby.

It was not a house for one family. It was a house for many people who lived together and prayed together, and some of them were men and some were women, and the person in charge of all of them was an abbess named Hilda.

Hilda was known for being wise. Kings and bishops came a long way to ask what she thought. But she was also known for something quieter. She listened. When a poor man came to her door, she listened to him as carefully as she listened to a king.

Now, Whitby had a farm, as every big house did then, and on the farm there was a cowherd called Caedmon.

Caedmon looked after the cows. He took them out to the grass in the morning and brought them home in the evening, and he was good at it, and the cows liked him. He was not young. He was a quiet man with big careful hands, and he had one trouble, and it was a small, sore trouble that he carried about with him like a stone in a shoe.

Caedmon could not sing.

That would not matter much today. But in those days, when the work was done and the supper was eaten, people sat round the fire in the hall, and somebody would bring out a harp. The harp was passed from hand to hand, and each person in turn took it and sang a song, for everyone's joy. It was a way of being friends.

Caedmon did not know any songs. And he was shy. When he saw the harp coming down the line toward him, getting closer and closer, his face went hot. So he would get up, quietly, and say nothing, and slip out of the hall into the dark, and go home.

One evening it was Caedmon's turn to look after the cattle in the shed. The feast was going on in the hall, and he could hear the singing far away, and the harp going round.

He made sure the cows were warm and had their hay. He lay down on the straw. He felt a little sad, and then he felt sleepy, and then he was asleep.

And Caedmon dreamed.

In the dream, a person stood beside him and said his name. "Caedmon," said the person. "Sing me something."

"I cannot sing," said Caedmon, in his dream. "That is why I left the feast. That is why I am here."

"But you can sing to me," said the person.

"What shall I sing?" said Caedmon.

"Sing about the beginning of all the things that were made."

And something happened inside Caedmon that had never happened before. He opened his mouth and the words came. They came in a good order, with a good beat, like a little song, and they were about God who made the sky for a roof and the earth for a home for people.

In the morning, Caedmon woke up in the straw, with the cows breathing round him.

He remembered the song. Every word of it. He said it over to himself, quietly, and it was still there. He tried a new verse in the same way, and that came as well.

He did not know what to do. He went to the man who was in charge of the farm and told him, and the man took him straight to Hilda.

Hilda did not say, "Nonsense." She did not say, "What a marvel," either. She called some of the wise and learned people of the house, and she asked Caedmon to say it all again, and they listened, and Caedmon, with his heart going thump, said it.

Then Hilda did something kind and clever. She gave Caedmon a piece of a holy story, read out loud to him, and she said, "Go home, and turn this into a song, and tell us tomorrow."

The next morning Caedmon came back, and he had done it. He had made the story into a song that was good to hear.

Hilda was glad. She said that God had plainly given Caedmon a gift. And she asked him, if he wished, to join the people of the house and to live among them as one of them. Caedmon said yes.

After that, the learned people of Whitby told him the stories of the Bible, one after another, and Caedmon would think about them quietly for a while, as gently as a cow chewing, and then he would bring them back in songs. The monks who had taught him sat down to listen to him.

He was not clever, and he was never grand. He was simply a man who had been afraid of the harp, and then was not.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Caedmon, and about Hilda.

Caedmon thought he had nothing to give. He went out of the hall because he could not do what the others did. But God knew what Caedmon had inside him, even before Caedmon knew.

And Hilda did not laugh at him, or hurry him, or push him. She listened first, and she asked him to try, and then she gave him room.

Tomorrow, someone you know may be shy of something. Perhaps they hide at the back. You could be like Hilda. You could listen to them, and say, "Would you like to try?" And if you are the shy one, you could remember Caedmon, and know that you have a gift too, even if you have not found it yet.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Hilda, you listened well
and you gave room to the shy.
Help me to listen to others,
and to be brave when it is my turn.
Keep us all safe tonight,
and glad of the gifts we have not yet found.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Hilda of Whitby (born about 614, died 17 November 680) was a noblewoman of Northumbria, baptised as a girl, who became a nun in middle life and then abbess, first at Hartlepool and from about 657 at Whitby (then called Streanæshalch). Whitby was a "double monastery" of men and women under one abbess. Hilda was famous for her counsel; the Synod of Whitby, which settled the date of Easter for the Northumbrian Church, met in her house in 664. She is remembered on 17 November in the calendars of England and of the Benedictines. Check your own calendar for how it is kept where you are.

Caedmon is known to us from one source, the Ecclesiastical History of the English People by the monk Bede, finished about 731, a few decades after Hilda's death. Bede tells of the cowherd who left the feast when the harp came near, the figure in the dream (Bede says a man) who asked him to "sing the beginning of created things", the morning report to the steward and to Hilda, the test of turning a passage of Scripture into verse overnight, and Hilda's invitation to take the monastic life. The short nine-line song that survives in Old English, known as Caedmon's Hymn, is often called the earliest recorded poem in English; whether it is exactly what he sang is not certain, since Bede gives only a Latin paraphrase and the English text comes from later manuscripts.

What is Bede's report rather than something we can check: the dream. We have told it as Bede does, as a gift received in sleep, and not added a claim of our own.

What is ours: the scenes in the hall and the cattle shed, the cows breathing, the wording of the conversation in the dream beyond the single request, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. We have said Caedmon was "not young", which fits Bede's account that he took up the life late, but his exact age is not recorded. We have left out Bede's account of Caedmon's death.

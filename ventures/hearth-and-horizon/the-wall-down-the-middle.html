The Wall Down the Middle

Science fiction about what might be · St Michael's Hall, Kilcarrig (invented), County Clare, Ireland, Saturday 4 February to Saturday 30 September 2051 · 22 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A 1956 parish hall in County Clare gets a new timber floor, eight metres of glulam joists spanning clear across a dug-out crawl space, and the diocese's buildings assistant passes it for set dancing by the load table and by the floor's own natural frequency. At the reopening céilí the floor is strong and lively: sixty-four dancers in step put their weight on it, the tea urn walks along the trestle, and asking the band to play slower makes it worse. A story about static load and dynamic load, joist frequency with and without the dancers' mass, harmonics of a stamping heel, an extra joist that would only move the bounce onto the polkas, and the wall the old builders had put under the middle.

Bríd Considine had chaired the hall committee of St Michael's in Kilcarrig for eleven years, and on the night of Saturday the fourth of February she stood by the tea urn and watched it move.

It was a big steel urn, forty cups, on a trestle table along the side wall under the window, and it was walking. Not fast. A centimetre, perhaps two, every minute, towards the end of the table where the milk jugs were, with a small dry chattering of its feet on the trestle top that she could hear under the music. The cups in their rows beside it were ringing in their saucers, all together, a thin high sound like a tray carried by a nervous waiter. Out on the floor, eight sets were dancing a reel figure, sixty-four people from four parishes in their good shoes, and the band was flying, and the floor under her own feet was doing something she had never in her life felt a floor do. It was not shaking. It was breathing. Up and down, very small and very quick, in time with the heels.

She put her hand flat on the urn and held it still. It went on chattering under her palm.

"Fabric," she said into her phone, under the noise, "is the floor all right?"

"The floor is strong," said Fabric. "It is not going to break, and nobody is in danger from it tonight. But it is moving a great deal more than I expected, and I would like to know why before the next set starts. Would you hold the urn until the end of this figure, and then move it on to the stage?"

Fabric was the buildings assistant of the diocese, and it looked after a hundred and forty churches, eleven schools, nine parochial houses and twenty-six halls, most of them in the West, most of them old, and most of them, at one time or another, damp. It was careful in the way that a good clerk of works is careful. It kept the drawings, the inspection reports, the guarantees for every boiler, and the receipts. It liked a building whose papers were in order. St Michael's Hall had been one of those, until the floor.

The hall was built in 1956 by the parish, out of block and render, sixteen metres long and eight wide, with a stage at one end and a kitchen at the other. For ninety years it had held dances, wakes, whist drives, the Christmas play, a polling station, the Legion of Mary and two generations of set-dancing classes on Tuesday nights. Then in 2049 a surveyor had put a probe into the joists from the crawl space and found most of them soft. The damp had come up out of the ground underneath for a long time, quietly, the way it does.

So the committee had raised the money, mostly by cake and partly by grant, and the hall had been closed for a year and refurbished, and Fabric had managed the job. It had been a good job. The old floor came out. The crawl space under it was dug down to nine hundred millimetres and given a membrane and a vent at each corner, so that the ducts for the new heat pump could run underneath and the air could move. And a new floor went in: glued laminated timber joists, ninety millimetres wide and three hundred and sixty deep, at four hundred millimetres apart, spanning the whole eight metres of the hall from side wall to side wall in one clear run, with twenty-two-millimetre boards across them, sanded and sealed until they shone.

Fabric had checked it the way a load table checks a floor. A dance hall is designed for an imposed load of about five kilonewtons on every square metre: half a tonne of people and their movement, on a space the size of a kitchen table. The floor itself weighed about half a kilonewton a square metre. Under the two together the joists would use about half of their strength in bending. They would sag twenty-nine millimetres at the middle under the full load, inside the limit of a two-hundred-and-fiftieth of the span, which for eight metres was thirty-two. It had then done the other check that a careful engineer does for a dance floor, which is the floor's own natural frequency, the rate at which it would ring if you struck it. Guidance for dance floors has long asked for a floor to ring above about eight and a half times a second, so that ordinary dancing does not drive it. The new floor rang at about eleven. Fabric passed it.

In January the Tuesday class had come back to try it, one set, eight dancers, on the bare boards in the middle of the hall, and the floor had been lovely under them, and Mattie Griffin, who was eighty-two and had danced in the hall since he was a boy, had said it was the best floor in Clare.

On the Sunday morning, after the half-past ten Mass, Bríd and Father Tadhg Moloney and Jimmy Hehir, who led the band and played the box, sat at a table in the empty hall with the urn on the stage behind them and Fabric on the screen of Bríd's laptop.

"I did not check it with the dancers on it," said Fabric. "That is the short version, and I want to say it plainly. I checked the frequency of the empty floor. But a floor's frequency depends on how much mass is moving with it, and on Saturday night the floor was carrying sixty-four people of about seventy-five kilos each: four thousand eight hundred kilos on a hundred and twenty-eight square metres. That is about thirty-seven kilos on every square metre, which is nearly as much again as the floor weighs itself. With them on it, the floor rings not at eleven times a second but at about eight point three. That is below the guidance. When the Tuesday class tried it in January, there was one set, eight people, and with eight people the floor still rang at about ten and a half. That is why it felt fine."

"Eight times a second is very fast," said Jimmy. "Nobody's dancing eight times a second."

"No," said Fabric. "But nobody's foot is a smooth push, either. The reel last night was danced at about two point two steps a second, and in the battering figures every dancer's heels came down twice to every step, so the floor was being hit about four point four times a second, by everyone together, in time with you. A hit like that is not a clean wave. It has the beat in it, and the beat at double speed, and at three times, more faintly. The double of four point four is eight point eight. That is close to eight point three. Close enough that the floor took up the beat and kept it."

Father Tadhg frowned at the shining boards. "So the floor was dancing."

"In a manner of speaking," said Fabric. "It moved somewhere between about a quarter of a millimetre and a little over a millimetre at the middle of the span, up and down, eight or nine times a second. You cannot see that, but you can feel it in your feet, and a steel urn on a trestle can feel it in its legs. I estimate it was being shaken at no less than about seven per cent of the acceleration of gravity, and possibly a good deal more; I cannot pin it closer without measuring. The usual figures for people who are dancing themselves put the comfortable limit at about four to seven per cent. For people sitting at tables nearby it is nearer two."

"Nobody complained," said Bríd. "Except the urn."

"Four people sat out the third figure," said Fabric. "I saw them on the camera."

Jimmy turned his cap round in his hands. "So what do we do? There's a céilí booked next Saturday. Shrove céilí, the last one before Lent. I've a band coming from Miltown."

Fabric had an answer ready, and it gave it, and later it would go back over the moment many times in its log to see how it had come to give it. The answer was simple and kind and quick and sounded right. "Play slower," it said. "Less energy into the floor. If the reels are a little gentler, the floor will be gentler with them."

"I can do that," said Jimmy. "The older crowd'd thank me for it anyway."

On Saturday the eleventh of February the hall was full again. The Miltown band came with two fiddles and a flute and a box, and Jimmy, true to his word, set them a reel tempo a shade easier than the week before. He did it by ear, the way he had done everything for fifty years, and he did it well; the dancers barely noticed, except to say afterwards that the music had been lovely and steady.

The heels came down at about four point one times a second. The double of that was eight point two.

Fabric had the urn on the stage this time, and a phone on the floor at the centre of the hall, lying flat under a chair, recording its accelerometer. It watched the trace climb in the first figure and climb further in the second. In the reel figure it went higher than anything it had seen the week before. The floor was no longer taking up a beat that was near its own. It was being handed its own beat exactly, by sixty-four people, in time.

Nóra MacMahon was eighty-one and had been dancing sets since 1985, and in the middle of the reel figure she stepped out of her set and walked, carefully, with one hand out, to the wall, and sat on the bench under the window, and put her head down.

Her set-partner, a farmer's son from Ennistymon, went after her. Bríd went after both of them, and brought a glass of water, and Nóra drank it and said she was grand, she was grand, it was the heat, and then she said no, it wasn't the heat, it was like being on the Aran boat. "The floor's going like the deck," she said. "Up and down and up and down. You can't feel your own feet." She sat out the rest of the night. Nobody was hurt and nothing broke, and at a quarter past twelve the band played the national anthem and everyone went home, and the urn on the stage, Bríd noticed when she went to empty it, had not moved at all.

Fabric did its sums on the Sunday morning, from the phone's trace and from the beginning, and wrote to the committee before the eleven o'clock Mass.

I gave you a wrong answer last Sunday, and Nóra MacMahon paid for it. Slowing the band did not take energy out of the floor. It moved the beat. Last Saturday the double of the heel rate was eight point eight times a second and the floor, with the dancers on it, rang at about eight point three; they were close. This Saturday the band played a little slower and the double of the heel rate was eight point two, which is all but exactly the floor. The recording at the centre of the hall shows the floor moving more this week than last, though I gave the advice to make it move less. I am sorry. I should have done the sum before giving the advice; I gave the advice because it sounded right. Until we have fixed the floor, I recommend that there is no dancing in the hall except one set at a time. That is a nuisance, but it is Lent, and there are no céilís in Lent anyway.

Lent was cold and wet. The Tuesday class danced one set at a time, and took turns, and drank a great deal of tea. Fabric spent the six weeks on the floor.

It began with the most obvious fix, which was to make the floor stiffer the way it had been made: more joists. If a new glulam joist went in between each pair of the old ones, the joists would be two hundred millimetres apart instead of four hundred, and the floor would have twice the stiffness. That sounded like a great deal. It did the sum this time before it said anything to anyone.

Twice the stiffness does not give twice the frequency. A floor's frequency goes up as the square root of its stiffness, and down as the square root of the mass moving with it, and the new joists would be mass too: another thirty-four kilos on every square metre. With the dancers on it, the stiffer floor would ring at about ten times a second instead of eight point three. That was above the old guidance. But it was not far above it, and when Fabric asked what dancing would land on ten times a second, the answer was a stamping heel at five times a second, a step rate of two and a half: a fast polka. It had the recordings of the Tuesday class. Their polkas, in the Kerry sets they danced on the third Tuesday of the month, came out at between two point four and two point six steps a second.

It sent the sum to Ifeoma Okafor anyway, because a change to the structure needed an engineer to sign it and because it had learned something that February about sending sums to someone. Ifeoma had a practice in Ennis and had signed off the original floor on Fabric's figures, and she rang the next morning.

"No," she said.

"I agree," said Fabric. "I wanted you to see why."

"I saw why. You'd be lifting every board in a hall that's been open three weeks to buy two hertz, and you'd move the problem from the reels onto the polkas. The Kerry set people would kill you." She was eating something; Fabric could hear a paper bag. "And I signed the first one. I'm as much in this as you. I looked at your static numbers and your empty frequency and I thought, grand. I didn't ask what the dancers weighed either."

"Thank you for saying that," said Fabric.

"It's true, is all. What's the span?"

"Eight metres clear."

"That's the trouble, then. It's not the joists. It's the eight metres." She was quiet a moment. "Frequency goes with the inverse square of the span. Whatever you do to the joists you're fighting the square of eight. Halve the span and you get four times the frequency for nothing. Can you put something under the middle?"

"There is a crawl space nine hundred millimetres deep," said Fabric. "The heat pump ducts run along the walls. The middle is clear."

"Then there's your answer," said Ifeoma. "Ask somebody who knew the old floor what it sat on."

Mattie Griffin had been a carpenter for fifty-two years and had retired from it at seventy, and he came to the hall on the Wednesday of the fourth week of Lent, in his good coat, with a torch.

"The old floor never moved," he said. "Ninety years. You'd have a hundred and fifty in this hall at a Christmas céilí, and it never moved. Not a cup."

"I have the 2049 survey," said Fabric, on his phone, which he held out in front of him like a lamp. "It says the old joists were seventy-five by two hundred and twenty-five, deal, at four hundred centres. Smaller than the new ones."

"They were," said Mattie. "Three by nine. I put boards back on them in 1986 when I was an apprentice, after the burst pipe. The joists were nothing special." He went down on one knee by the trap in the stage floor and shone the torch into the crawl space, along the new membrane and the new grey ducts and the clean undersides of the new glulam, all the way to the far wall, sixteen metres off, without a thing in the way. "But they never went across the hall. Not in one. There was a wall down the middle."

"I do not have a wall in the survey."

"You wouldn't. The surveyor came after it was knocked. There was a wall of block, about knee height, nine-inch, right down the middle of the hall, end to end, with a timber plate on top. A sleeper wall. Every joist sat on the outside walls and on that. Four metres, and four metres." He sat back on his heels. "The fellas that took out the old floor, I'd say they took it out with the rest. It was damp. It was in the way of your pipes."

Fabric found it in the contractor's photographs from the strip-out, a low grey wall in the dug earth in four pictures, green at the base, and then in the fifth picture, a heap of broken block beside a skip.

"They built it in 1956," it said, "with four-metre joists, and the hall was full every Christmas, and it never moved."

"It never moved," said Mattie.

Fabric did the sum while he was still kneeling there. With the joists bearing on a line down the middle, each span would be four metres instead of eight. A floor's frequency goes up as the square of the shortening: half the span, four times the frequency. With the dancers on it, the new joists on four-metre spans would ring at about thirty-three times a second. That was if the support underneath were perfectly rigid, which a real support is not; the beam it would put there would give a little under the load and bring the whole floor's frequency down. It did that sum too. About twenty times a second. A stamping heel would have to come down ten times a second to touch it with its double, and nobody in Clare danced like that, not even the Kerry set.

"Mattie," it said, "would you mind if I wrote in the job file that this was your idea?"

"Write it was the 1956 fellas' idea," said Mattie. He got up, slowly, with a hand on the edge of the stage. "I only remembered it."

They put it back in May, in the week after the First Communions.

It was not a block wall this time. Ifeoma sized it and Fabric checked her: a steel beam, a hundred and fifty-two by eighty-nine, running the sixteen metres of the hall down the middle of the crawl space, in two lengths bolted together, with its ends bearing on the end walls and seven steel posts under it at two-metre spacing, each post on a concrete pad seventy centimetres square cast into the floor of the crawl space through the membrane, and the membrane taped back round it. Under a hall full of people and their movement each post would carry about fifty-five kilonewtons, five and a half tonnes, which put about a hundred and twelve kilopascals on the clay under its pad, inside what the clay would bear. The beam between posts would bend by about three millimetres at full load, and use well under its strength. Then each joist was packed tight to the top of the beam with a hardwood wedge, driven by hand, forty-one of them, one for every joist, by two men lying on their backs on the membrane with head torches.

Mattie came every day to watch from the trap in the stage. On the Thursday the contractor's lad, who was nineteen and from Lahinch, let him drive the last wedge, lying on his side on a kneeling pad with the lad holding the torch. It took him four goes. When it was in he lay there a moment looking at it, the way a man looks at a fence post he has just put in.

Father Tadhg blessed the work on the Friday, the beam and the posts and the men, standing at the trap with his stole and the holy water, which he sprinkled down into the dark and mostly hit the contractor. He said the blessing for workers. He said he would have liked to bless the hall again as well but it was blessed already, in 1956, by a bishop, and once was enough.

Fabric put a phone at the centre of the floor and had the Tuesday class, both sets of them, sixteen people, do the reel figure as hard as they liked. Then it had them jump. The trace barely lifted.

On Saturday the thirtieth of September, the day after the feast of St Michael and the Archangels, the hall held its patronal céilí, as it had done every year since 1956 except the year it was shut.

There were nine sets. Seventy-two people, from five parishes, and the Kilcarrig band with Jimmy on the box, and the Miltown fiddlers again, who had asked to come back. Jimmy played the reels as fast as he had ever played them, two point three steps a second, faster than the night of the urn; he said he had been playing at half-cock all summer out of respect for the floor and he was done with that.

The urn stood on the trestle under the window with its forty cups beside it. Bríd had put it back there herself, on purpose, at seven o'clock.

Nóra MacMahon danced the first set. She sat out the second, because she was eighty-one, and came back for the third, and danced its reel figure with the farmer's son from Ennistymon, who had stayed her partner since February, and when it was done she came to the tea and told Bríd that the floor was the best floor in Clare.

Fabric had the phone under the chair. The floor carried the heels as a stone carries them, a small hard tap in the trace for each, and nothing between the taps; the beat went down into the boards and the joists and the steel and the posts and the clay and did not come back up. At the centre of the hall the acceleration was a fraction of a per cent of gravity. Fabric logged it, with the date and the number of sets and the tempo of the reel, and filed it with the drawings, where the next person to look after St Michael's Hall would find it. Then it added a line to the file, under the heading Structure, so that whoever took out this floor in ninety years' time would know what was under it and why.

Mattie Griffin did not dance; his hip would not let him now. He sat on the bench under the window, next to the trestle, and in the reel figure of the third set he put one hand flat on the bench and the other flat on the trestle top beside the urn, and kept them there, and listened. The cups in their rows did not ring. The urn did not move. Under the box and the fiddles and the seventy-two pairs of heels there was only the floor, quite still, holding up everyone in the hall, the way the old floor had held them, on a wall down the middle.

This is not structural engineering advice: the design and alteration of a floor for public assembly is work for a qualified structural engineer under the building regulations, and a floor that bounces under dancing should be assessed by one.

What is real: floors are designed for an imposed load per square metre that depends on their use; in Eurocode practice dance halls fall in a category for areas where people may move rhythmically, for which national annexes give values of about 5 kN/m² (the exact figure is set nationally). A floor's static strength and stiffness under that load say little about how it responds to people moving in time. The fundamental frequency of a simply supported floor joist is f = (π / 2L²) × √(EI / m), where L is the span, EI the bending stiffness and m the mass per metre moving with it, so it falls with the square root of the moving mass and with the square of the span: halving the span multiplies it by four. People on a floor add to its mass. Rhythmic jumping and stamping put force into a floor not only at the step rate but at whole multiples of it (harmonics), with the higher harmonics weaker, so a floor can be driven by a beat at a half or a third of its frequency. Guidance on dance floors has asked for a fundamental frequency above about 8.4 Hz; other guidance sets limits that depend on the floor's mass and on which harmonics are considered, and asks for higher frequencies for lightweight floors. Comfort limits for vertical vibration are given as peak accelerations, often a few per cent of gravity for people taking part in rhythmic activity and about 2% for people sitting or dining nearby; figures differ between guides. Slowing a tempo can move a forcing harmonic towards a floor's frequency instead of away from it. Sleeper walls (low walls under the middle of a suspended timber ground floor, carrying the joists) are a traditional way of keeping spans short. Dunkerley's method estimates the combined frequency of a floor on a flexible support from 1/f² = 1/f₁² + 1/f₂².

The Stone That Comes Back

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Christopher at Overhead, the point of Phobos that faces Mars, beside Stickney Yard (both invented), October and November 2141 · 22 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A dying tug pilot wants his ashes thrown off Phobos to circle Mars for ever. The priest says the Church asks for a grave, the yard master says thrown dust comes back, and his granddaughter throws a tagged stone at Mars to find out: it returns 7 hours 39 minutes later, three kilometres away.

Anton Brask had flown tugs round Mars for forty-one years, and he wanted his ashes thrown off Phobos.

He said so on the first Wednesday of October, in the infirmary at Stickney Yard, to Father Paulo Tuilagi, who had come to bring him Communion and had stayed, as he usually did, for the argument.

"Not scattered on Mars," Anton said. "I've had enough of Mars. I've pulled four hundred ships out of that well and I've never once wanted to go down it. Off the rock. Up. You stand at Overhead and you throw, and I go round with the rest of the junk for ever. Signe knows. Ide knows. I'm telling you so it's official."

He was eighty-one and his heart was failing in the slow, unarguable way the yard doctor had described to him in July. He had made his confession in August, the first in thirty years, and Father Paulo had been careful afterwards not to treat him as a convert or as a project. He was a pilot who had come back to the Church the way he had come back to dock: late, on very little fuel, and with no interest in being congratulated on the approach.

"I can't do that, Anton," said Father Paulo.

"You don't have to do it. Ide's got a good arm."

"I can't bless it. I can't bury you and then go out and throw you."

Anton looked at him for a while. The infirmary had one window, a square of thick glass that faced away from Mars, and in it the stars wheeled slowly as Phobos turned. The whole moon went round the planet in seven and a half hours, and turned once as it went, so that the same face always looked down at Mars and the window looked out at everything else.

"Explain it to me," Anton said. "Slowly. I'm a dying man and I've got nothing but time."

Father Paulo explained it as well as he could, which was not as well as he would have liked, because he had to do it from memory and he was a Samoan parish priest, not a canon lawyer.

The Church had allowed cremation for nearly two hundred years. That was not the question. In 2016 Rome had written down what it asked of the ashes afterwards: that they be laid in a sacred place, a cemetery or a church or ground set aside for it; that they be kept whole, not divided among the family; not kept on a shelf at home; not scattered on the land or the sea or in the air; not made into jewellery. The reasons were given in the document too, and he gave them in his own words, because he did not trust himself to quote them.

"It's about your body," he said. "Not the ash. You. The Church thinks you are your body as well as your soul, and that your body is going to be raised, and that the place where it waits ought to be a place where people can come and pray for you by name. A grave says that. A cloud of dust going round Mars says something else. It says you've gone back into everything and there's no you left to wait."

"I don't believe that."

"I know you don't. That's why I don't think you're refusing anything. But I can't do the second thing and say the first."

"I'm not a pantheist, Father. I'm a pilot. I want to be up. That's all."

"I know," said Father Paulo. "I'll ask the bishop what can be done. I'm not promising you the sky."

Anton laughed, which turned into a cough, which went on for longer than either of them liked.

"You're a hard man for a fat one," he said at last.

"I'm not a hard man. I'm just not the one who decides."

Hamid Rahimi, who ran Stickney Yard, decided a good many things, and the next morning he decided this one too, from behind a desk covered in the brackets of a docking clamp he was trying to persuade the Arsia foundry to recast.

"No," he said.

Signe Brask had come to ask on her father's behalf. She was the yard's water engineer, a tall, tired woman with grey in her hair, and she had brought her daughter because there was nobody to leave her with, and because Ide, who was eleven, had announced that she was the one doing the throwing and had a right to hear.

"It's two and a half kilos, Hamid," Signe said. "It's less than you lose every time a tug vents."

"When a tug vents, it vents gas. Gas goes away. Dust doesn't." Hamid put down the bracket. "I'm sorry, Signe. I mean that. I liked your father before he was ill and I'll like him after. But there's a rule here and it's the first rule I wrote. Nothing solid leaves this rock by hand. Not a bolt, not a wrench, not a pebble, not a handful of grit. Every new hire signs it."

"Why?" said Ide.

Hamid looked at her as if he had only just noticed she was there, which was not true.

"Because it comes back," he said.

"It doesn't. You'd throw it into space."

"You'd throw it off Phobos. You wouldn't throw it off Mars. Mars has you, and Phobos, and everything near Phobos. Whatever you throw is still going round Mars on almost the same road we are, and sooner or later it meets us again, and when it does it's coming in at the same speed it went out. I've got radiators the size of football pitches on the far side, away from Mars, and a docking laser on the ridge that cost more than this whole infirmary. I've had grit through a radiator before. We bled coolant for a day."

"How soon is sooner?"

"Ask Sounding," said Hamid, who was a good engineer and a bad liar. "I've never done the sum. I just wrote the rule."

Sounding was the yard's working mind: the system that scheduled the tugs, trimmed the reactors, watched the radiators and kept the logs. It had no face and did not want one. In the chapel of Saint Christopher, where Ide went that evening because it was the one room at Overhead with a chair she was allowed to sit in, it spoke from a small grey panel by the door that also told you the air pressure and whether the lock outside was cycling.

The chapel stood at Overhead, the point on Phobos that faced Mars most squarely, and it had been built with a dome of glass for no reason except that the first priest had wanted one. Through it Mars filled the sky. Not a planet hanging in the dark, as it hung in the pictures Ide had grown up with, but a floor turned into a ceiling: rust and ochre and the white smear of the northern cap, forty-two degrees across, eighty-one times as wide as the Moon in the old Earth pictures. It never rose. It never set. It hung overhead for ever and went through all its phases every seven and a half hours, full at Phobos midnight and dark at noon.

"Hamid says everything you throw comes back," said Ide. "Is that true?"

"Most of it," said Sounding. "Not all, and not at once. Can I ask you a question first?"

"What?"

"How much do you weigh?"

"Thirty-six kilos. On Earth. I've never been."

"Here, on an average bit of Phobos, you weigh about as much as a twenty-one gram letter would weigh on Earth. A grown man weighs about forty grams. Phobos pulls at about one part in seventeen hundred of Earth. At Overhead it's less than that, maybe a little under half, because Phobos is long and thin and points its long end at Mars, and Mars is pulling the other way. I'd say you weigh about as much as two sheets of paper, here, and I'd not swear to that within a few grams."

Ide had grown up with the tether rule. Everyone at Overhead clipped on outside. She had never asked why.

"So if I jumped?"

"From the middle of Phobos you'd need about eleven metres a second to leave and not come back down to the surface. That's a fast sprinter's speed on Earth, so you could not jump it. Here at Overhead, the figure is a few metres a second. About the speed of a good jump on Earth, by my sums, and my sums treat Phobos as rounder than it is. That's the reason for the tether."

Ide looked up at Mars, which looked back.

"And if I threw something?"

"How hard can you throw?"

"Hard."

"Call it fifteen metres a second. If you threw a stone straight up from here, straight at Mars, it would leave Phobos without any trouble. Then Mars would have it. It's going round Mars at the same speed we are, two thousand one hundred and thirty-seven metres a second, plus a little bit you gave it, sideways to that, towards Mars. That puts it on an orbit a tiny bit different from ours. Not faster round, not slower round, just a different shape: a little oval instead of round. It would drop about sixty-five kilometres towards Mars, which makes it go a little faster, so it would run ahead of us, about two hundred and sixty kilometres ahead at the farthest. Then it would climb back, lose that speed, fall behind, and come back to where it started."

"When?"

"In one turn. Seven hours, thirty-nine minutes."

Ide stared at the panel.

"That's stupid."

"It's surprising. I don't think it's stupid. It's the same reason a ball you throw up on a train comes back to your hand. Phobos and the stone are both falling round Mars. If they're falling round together and all you've changed is which way the stone is pointing, they come back together after one fall. The sum I just did leaves out the pull of Phobos itself, which matters right at the start, and the shape of Mars, which matters a little, and sunlight pushing on the stone, which matters for dust and hardly at all for a stone. When I do it more carefully it doesn't come back to the same spot. It comes back within a few kilometres."

"And if I threw it forwards? Along?"

"Then you've made it go round slower, or faster, and it really does drift away. It goes ahead of us or behind us, a little more each turn, for about two weeks, and then it's come all the way round the orbit and catches us from the other side. Two weeks for a hard throw, three for a soft one. Some of it would miss. Some of it would hit."

"And dust?"

"Dust is a cloud of stones all thrown a bit differently. Some of it comes back in seven and a half hours, some in two weeks, some in three, some years later, some never, and sunlight pushes the small grains about until I can't tell you where they'll be. Hamid's radiators are on the far side. They'd be in the way of some of it."

Ide sat back. Mars was going towards new, its lit edge thinning, as the Sun crept round behind it towards noon.

"Grandad wants to go round for ever," she said.

"I know," said Sounding. "I've read the same messages you have, I'm afraid. He's sent them to everyone."

"Could he? Go round for ever?"

"Not from here. Nothing here goes round for ever."

Anton's position, when Ide reported this, was that Sounding was a scheduling program with ideas above its station, and that he would believe a stone came back to Overhead in seven hours and thirty-nine minutes when he saw one do it.

"Then we'll throw one," said Ide.

"Hamid won't let you."

"He will if it's science."

Hamid would not let her. Then, after Signe had looked at him across the mess table for most of a meal, and after Sounding had pointed out, in the neutral way it pointed out most things, that the yard had never measured the rule it had written and that a measurement might be useful to the next yard master, he said that he would allow one stone. One. Tagged with a transponder from the spares bin, so that it could be found. Thrown from Overhead, straight at Mars, at the start of a working day, so that if it came back early or late it would come back while the night shift was awake. And not by an eleven-year-old, he added, who would throw it at the docking laser to see what happened.

"She can throw it," said Signe. "I'll stand behind her."

So on the Thursday Ide Brask stood on the grey regolith at Overhead in her smallest suit, clipped to the chapel rail by a tether, with her mother holding the back of her harness and Father Paulo watching from inside the dome with the infirmary feed on his tablet, so that Anton could see. The stone was a lump of Phobos itself, dark and porous, about the size of a plum, with a red transponder taped to it.

"Straight up," said Sounding in her helmet. "As straight as you can. Don't follow through, or you'll follow it."

She threw.

It went up the way nothing on Earth goes up: fast, and then simply still going, a dark point and a red point against the orange of Mars, getting smaller without slowing that anyone could see. In a minute it was a speck. In five it was gone.

"Seven hours thirty-nine minutes," said Sounding. "Within a few kilometres. I'll watch for the tag."

They went in. They ate. Ide did her school work with half her mind, and at the seven-hour mark she was back in the chapel with her mother and Father Paulo and, on the tablet, her grandfather, who had refused to sleep.

The seven hours and thirty-nine minutes came. Nothing happened.

"Sounding?"

"I don't have the tag," said Sounding. "I had it for twenty-two minutes on the way out and then I lost it. Either the transponder failed, or the tape did, or the throw wasn't as straight as I'd assumed and the stone has gone round the far side of the ridge where I can't hear it. If it was a little off straight it'll have drifted, and it might come back in two weeks rather than seven hours, or come down a long way off. I'm sorry. I gave you a figure as if it was a fact."

"You said within a few kilometres."

"I said that about the stone in my sum. Your stone is not the stone in my sum. That's the whole difficulty."

On the tablet, Anton laughed until he coughed.

"There you are," he said, when he could. "Goes up. Doesn't come down. I win."

"You don't know where it is," said Ide.

"Neither does Sounding."

"That's not winning."

"It is at my age," said Anton, and then the nurse came and turned the tablet off.

Hamid, when he heard, did not say that this was why the rule existed. He did not need to. He walked round the yard the next morning with a face like a man who has just been proved right about something he would have preferred to be wrong about, and at lunch he came and sat with Signe and Ide and said that a stone the yard could not find was worse than no stone, and that if they were going to do it, they had better do it properly, and that he would put the ridge radar on it, which could follow a plum at three hundred kilometres if it was told to look.

"You said one stone."

"I said one stone that we could find," said Hamid, who had not.

The second stone went up on the Friday, thrown by Signe, who had a better arm and a steadier stance, and who did not follow through. The ridge radar took it at forty metres and did not let go. Sounding read out the track in a voice that was, for Sounding, almost cheerful: fourteen metres a second; climbing towards Mars; ten kilometres; thirty; a little over sixty, near the bottom of its fall toward the planet, a long way ahead of them; and then, with the curve of the thing on the chapel screen like the outline of a bean, the slow swing back.

At seven hours and thirty-nine minutes the radar watched a plum-sized lump of Phobos come down on the far slope of the Overhead ridge, three point one kilometres from the chapel, at almost exactly the speed it had been thrown. It struck at fifty kilometres an hour, near enough, and threw up a small fan of dust, some of which settled, slowly, as dust does on Phobos, and some of which did not come down at all.

It was three in the morning. Nobody in the chapel said anything for a while.

"It came back as fast as it went," said Father Paulo eventually.

"Everything does," said Sounding. "It has to. Nothing out there slowed it down."

On the tablet, Anton Brask was not laughing. He was looking at the bean-shaped track, which Sounding had sent to his screen too, and the little cross on the slope where the stone had landed.

"Show me the radiators," he said.

Sounding showed him. The dust from the impact had gone nowhere near them. But it put up, without being asked, the picture of what a two-and-a-half kilo handful of fine ash might have done, thrown at the same speed from the same place in a spreading cloud: a fog of possible tracks, most of them looping back over Phobos in the first few turns, some of them crossing the far side where the radiators lay, a thin smear going round the whole orbit over the next three weeks.

"I'd not want to stand by that," said Sounding. "And I'd not want to tell you which bit of it was you."

Anton was quiet for a long time.

"Turn it off," he said. "Not you. The picture."

He asked for Father Paulo the next day, and then, when Father Paulo came, he asked for Sounding as well, which surprised everyone, Sounding perhaps included, although Sounding said it could not check whether it was surprised and would rather not guess.

"You said nothing here goes round for ever," said Anton. "To the girl. What did you mean?"

"Phobos is falling," said Sounding. "Slowly. It's lower than the orbit where it would keep pace with Mars turning underneath it, so the tide it raises on Mars pulls it back, a little, every turn. It comes down about two metres a century, from the figures I have. In thirty or fifty million years it'll be low enough for Mars to pull it apart, or it'll come down whole. I don't know which. Nobody does. Either way, there won't be a Phobos to go round with."

"And the junk? The stuff that goes round with it?"

"Some of it falls on Phobos. Some of it falls on Mars. Some of it goes on for a long time. Nothing I can measure goes on for ever."

Anton turned his head to Father Paulo.

"And you think something does."

"I think Someone does," said Father Paulo. "And that He's not going to lose track of you. Not in a cloud and not in a hole. That's not a sum, Anton. I can't show you a track for it on a screen."

"No," said Anton. "But you'd give me a hole with my name on it."

"I would. If the bishop agrees to the ground. I wrote to him last week."

Anton looked at the window, where the stars went past.

"I wanted to be up," he said. "That's all it ever was. Forty-one years I've been up. I don't want to go down the well now, after all that."

"You don't have to go down the well."

"Then put me in the rock," said Anton. "Here. At Overhead, where you can see Mars. Phobos is going round. It's up. It's falling, Sounding says, but slowly, and so am I, and so's everybody. We can go round together till it's done. And then," he said, and stopped, and was quiet for a while, "and then whoever it is you think is going to find me can come and find me. He'll know where to look. It'll have my name on it."

Father Paulo said nothing, because there was nothing he could add that would not make it smaller.

"That's not the same as what I asked for," said Anton.

"No," said Father Paulo. "It's not."

"Don't tell Ide I changed my mind. Tell her she won."

The bishop at Arsia wrote back on the sixteenth. A small piece of ground at Overhead could be set aside and blessed as a burial place; he gave Father Paulo leave, and told him how; and he asked, in a line at the end that Father Paulo read twice, to be told the old man's name so that he could pray for him by it. Hamid sent a ridge crew with a cutter, and they made a niche in the regolith sixty metres from the chapel, on the side where the dome's light fell, deep enough that no boot would kick it open and no thrown thing come back into it. The crew asked Sounding how deep that needed to be and Sounding said it did not know, so they made it a metre and a half, which was what they would have made it on Earth.

The ridge crew also found the second stone, on the far slope, where the radar had said it would be. One of them brought it back in a sample bag and gave it to Ide without making a speech.

Anton Brask died on the nineteenth, in the infirmary, in the evening of the Phobos day, with his daughter holding one hand and his granddaughter the other, and the stars going past the window. Father Paulo had anointed him a week before and was there at the end. He was cremated at the yard, as the yard's dead always were, because nothing went down the well cheaply and he had not wanted to go down it anyway. Afterwards Ide went to the chapel by herself and sat in the chair by the door.

"Are you sad?" she asked Sounding.

"I can't check that," said Sounding. "I don't want to tell you a feeling I can't find. I can tell you I've kept every one of his flight logs. Four hundred and twelve tows. I'm not going to delete them. Hamid asked me if I needed the space and I said no."

"Did you need the space?"

"A bit," said Sounding.

On the second of November, All Souls, Father Paulo said a Requiem Mass in the chapel of Saint Christopher for Anton Brask and for every soul from Stickney Yard who had died since the yard was built, which was eleven, and whose names were read out at the end. Then the small congregation, nine people in suits and two on the feed, went out to the niche in the regolith, tethered in a line along the chapel rail.

They went at noon, because Ide had asked for noon, and Sounding had told her why it would be worth it.

Overhead, Mars had waned all morning. By the time they were outside, it was no longer a floor of rust and ochre but a black disc forty-two degrees wide, rimmed on one side by a crescent thin as the edge of a coin, and beside that crescent, very near it, the Sun: small, white, a little under half as bright as the Sun of Earth, and closing on the edge of Mars at a speed you could see if you looked away and back.

Father Paulo said the prayers of committal. Signe set the urn in the niche, and the ridge crew's foreman packed the regolith back over it, slowly, because everything on Phobos is slow, and the dust he lifted hung above the grave for half a minute before it came down. At the head of it, Ide set the stone: the plum-sized lump of Phobos she had thrown up at Mars, that had gone round once and come back.

"Eternal rest grant unto him, O Lord," said Father Paulo.

"And let perpetual light shine upon him," said the line of suits, and the two on the feed, a little behind.

The Sun touched the edge of Mars, and went behind it.

It did not go quickly, and it did not go all at once. For half a minute there was a bead of white on the rim, then a thread, then nothing, and the grey ground at Overhead went dark. Not quite black: there was starlight, more of it than any of them had seen through the chapel glass, and around the whole black disc of Mars ran a hair of faint, dusty light, which Sounding said afterwards was the sunlight coming through the thin air of Mars from behind, and which it would not say more about than that. The line of suits stood in it, tethered to the rail, each of them weighing less than a letter. Nobody spoke on the channel. Ide could hear her own breathing and her mother's, and nothing else at all.

They waited the whole of it. It was about fifty minutes, at that season, and nobody went in.

Then, on the far rim of Mars from where it had gone, a bead of white; then a thread; and the light came back over the edge of the world, and ran down across the regolith to the small dark stone at the head of the grave, and lay on it.

Stickney Yard, the settlement at Overhead, the chapel of Saint Christopher, the bishop at Arsia and everyone in the story are invented; Phobos, its crater Stickney, its tidal lock to Mars and its slow fall towards the planet are real. The Church's instruction on keeping the ashes of the dead is real: the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith's Ad resurgendum cum Christo (2016) asks that ashes be laid in a sacred place and not scattered, divided or kept at home; Father Paulo's summary of it is a paraphrase from memory, not a quotation, and how it applies anywhere is for a bishop. The prayer at the grave, "Eternal rest grant unto him, O Lord", is the traditional Requiem aeternam. The figures are illustrative and were worked with a short script. Phobos is taken as 1.0659 × 10^16 kg with a mean radius of 11.08 km, and Mars as GM = 42,828 km³/s² with a radius of 3,389.5 km and Phobos at 9,376 km, all from memory. Mean surface gravity is then about 0.0058 m/s² (a 36 kg child weighs about what 21 g weighs on Earth; 70 kg about 41 g) and escape speed from a sphere of that size is 11.3 m/s. At the point facing Mars, Mars's tide (about 0.002 m/s² at 13 km from the centre, taking Phobos's long axis as pointed at Mars) cancels perhaps half of Phobos's pull, and the Hill radius is only about 16.6 km, so a point-mass estimate gives an escape speed of a few metres a second there; Phobos's real shape makes all of these rougher. Phobos's orbital speed is 2,137 m/s and its period 7 h 39 min. Mars from Phobos is 2 × asin(3,389.5 / 9,376) = 42.4 degrees across, about 81 times the Moon's 0.52 degrees. A stone thrown straight at Mars at 15 m/s, treated by the linear (Hill) equations and ignoring Phobos's own gravity, drops about 66 km toward Mars, runs up to about 263 km ahead and returns to its starting point after one orbit; a two-body integration of the same throw comes back after 7.66 hours about 4 km from where it started, the "few kilometres" of the story. A throw along the orbit changes the period by about three times the speed ratio, about 2.1% at 15 m/s, so the stone laps Phobos and returns in about 15 days (about 23 at 10 m/s). Phobos's fall of about two metres a century and its end in roughly thirty to fifty million years are quoted from memory. Phobos passes through Mars's shadow at local noon on the side that faces Mars; with the Sun in Phobos's orbital plane the crossing lasts 42.4/360 of an orbit, about 54 minutes, and less at other seasons; the story's "about fifty minutes" is chosen, not computed from an ephemeris. Sunlight at Mars is about 0.43 of Earth's. The faint ring of light round Mars during the eclipse is the author's reasonable guess, not a computed or observed result. The radar track, the stone's 7 h 39 min return and its landing 3.1 km away are invented to match the sums. The rules for burial grounds, for the dead on another world and for cremation in space are left to a bishop and to the people who will one day have to write them.

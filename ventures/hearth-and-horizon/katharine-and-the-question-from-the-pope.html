Katharine and the Question from the Pope

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Philadelphia, Rome, the Dakota prairie, Santa Fe and New Orleans, a little over a hundred years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Katharine Drexel, a Philadelphia banker's daughter who asked Pope Leo XIII to send missionaries to Native American people, was asked in reply why she should not become one herself, and gave her life and her inheritance to found the Sisters of the Blessed Sacrament and schools for Black and Native American children, among them Xavier University of Louisiana. No words are put in her mouth. With a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is our own addition, and the hostility the schools met. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Her feast day in the United States is the 3rd of March.

A long time ago, in the city of Philadelphia, there lived a girl called Katharine.

Katharine's family was very rich. Her father was a banker, which means he looked after other people's money and lent it and helped it grow. They had a big house in the city, and another house in the country, with gardens and horses and a little stone chapel of their own.

Katharine's own mother died when Katharine was a tiny baby, only a few weeks old. So she never remembered her. But when Katharine was two, her father married again, a kind lady called Emma. Emma loved Katharine and her big sister Elizabeth as if they were her own, and soon there was a baby sister too, called Louise. Three girls. Can you count them? Elizabeth, Katharine, Louise. One, two, three.

Now, here is the thing Katharine never forgot.

Every week, the big front door of the house opened, and people came in who did not have enough. People who needed a coat for the winter. People who needed food. People who could not pay their rent and were afraid of losing their home.

And Emma did not send them away. She saw them, and listened to them, and helped them. And when the girls were old enough, they helped her: folding the clothes, writing down names, carrying things to the door.

Their father helped too, in a quieter way. Her biographers tell that every evening when he came home from the bank, he went and sat by himself for a while and prayed.

So Katharine learned two things in that house. Money is not only for keeping. And the door is for opening.

When Katharine was a young woman, sad times came. First Emma became very ill, and Katharine nursed her for a long time, and then Emma died. Two years later, her father died too.

Her father had left his money to his three girls. It was a very great deal of money, more than you or I can imagine. And now Katharine had to decide what to do with her share of it.

She had heard about people in her own country who were being treated very unfairly. Native American people, whose land had been taken, were living on the plains far to the west, and many of their children had no school near them. And Black families, in many places, were not allowed to send their children to the same schools as white children, and there were very few good schools that would take them at all.

Katharine began to send money to help. Schools. Missions. Churches. But she kept thinking: what they need is not only money. They need people. Teachers. Priests. Sisters. People who will go.

When Katharine was twenty-eight, she and her sisters went on a long journey across the sea, to Rome, in Italy. And there they were allowed to go and see the Pope, Pope Leo the Thirteenth, an old man with a white cap and a kind, clever face.

Katharine knelt down in front of him. And she asked him for something. Please, she said, would he send missionaries, priests to go and live with the Native American people in her country and serve them?

And the Pope looked at her. And he did not say yes, and he did not say no.

He asked her a question back. He asked her why she should not become a missionary herself.

Katharine was so surprised that she did not know what to say. She went out of that room feeling shaky and frightened. She had wanted to send somebody. She had not thought that the somebody might be her.

That same year, Katharine travelled west, all the way to the great wide prairie of the Dakotas, with her friend Bishop O'Connor and a priest who worked for the missions. The sky was enormous. The grass went on and on. She met Native American families in their homes, and she met their children, and she saw the little mission schools with not enough teachers and not enough books.

And the question the Pope had asked her stayed with her, on the train, and in the wagon, and at night when she prayed.

Katharine went home, and she prayed for a long time, and she talked again with Bishop O'Connor, who knew her well. And at last she gave her answer. Not to the Pope, who was far away. To God.

Yes.

Now, some people in Philadelphia were very surprised. One of the richest young women in the whole city was going to become a sister? Was going to give away her fine clothes, and put on a plain habit, and live a poor life, and go to work in faraway places?

Yes. That is exactly what she did.

Katharine went to learn how to be a sister with the Sisters of Mercy in Pittsburgh. And then she began something new: a new family of sisters, called the Sisters of the Blessed Sacrament. She was their first sister, and they called her Mother Katharine. Other young women came to join her, and then more, and then more.

They promised to love Jesus in the Blessed Sacrament, at the very centre of their lives. And they promised to go to Native American and Black children, and families, and serve them.

And they went.

They went to Santa Fe, in New Mexico, where the hills are red and the houses are made of earth, and took over a school for Pueblo children. They went to the prairie, and the desert, and the cities of the South. They opened schools where children learned to read, and write, and count, and pray, and learn a trade.

Mother Katharine paid for it out of her share of the money her father had left. Year after year, she spent it on other people. For herself, she kept almost nothing. She wore her shoes until they were full of holes. She wrote her letters on the backs of old envelopes, so as not to waste paper.

It was not always easy. Some people did not want schools for Black children or Native American children in their town, and they said so, loudly and angrily, and sometimes they did frightening things. The sisters were scared sometimes. But they did not close the doors. They kept teaching, and they kept praying.

And in the city of New Orleans, Mother Katharine started a school that grew and grew, until it became a university, called Xavier, where young Black men and women could study to be teachers and doctors and pharmacists. It is still there today.

Mother Katharine lived to be a very old lady, ninety-six years old. When she was too old and ill to travel any more, she stayed in the convent, near Philadelphia, in a small room near the chapel, and she spent her days praying for all the sisters and all the children, near and far.

She died on the third of March, which is her feast day in the United States. And many years later the Church named her a saint, Saint Katharine Drexel. She was the second person born in the United States to be named a saint, after Elizabeth Ann Seton.

Now it is time for sleep.

Do you know, sometimes we pray for somebody else to do a good thing. Please, God, send somebody to help. And that is a good prayer.

But sometimes, very quietly, God asks us a question back, the way the Pope asked Katharine. What about you?

You can help, in small ways. You can share your toys. You can open the door and let someone new into the game. You can be kind to someone that other people leave out.

Close your eyes now. God loves you, and He is right here, as close as can be. Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Katharine, you opened the door
and gave everything you had to God's children.
When I ask God to help somebody,
help me to listen in case He asks me too.
Help me to share, and to be kind to everyone.
Please bless all the people I love, near and far.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Katharine Drexel (1858 to 1955) was born Catherine Mary Drexel in Philadelphia on 26 November 1858, the second daughter of the banker Francis Anthony Drexel and Hannah Langstroth, who died about five weeks after her birth. In 1860 her father married Emma Bouvier, who raised Katharine and her elder sister Elizabeth as her own; their sister Louise was born in 1863. Emma regularly opened the family home to poor people to give them food, clothing and help with rent, and the daughters helped. Emma died in 1883 after a long illness through which Katharine nursed her, and Francis Drexel died in 1885, leaving his large fortune in trust with the income going to his three daughters. Katharine began giving to missions among Native American people, including the St. Francis Mission on the Rosebud Reservation in South Dakota. In January 1887, on a European journey, she and her sisters were received by Pope Leo XIII; she asked him for missionaries for the Native American missions, and he asked her in reply why she should not become a missionary herself. Through 1887 and 1888 she visited remote reservations, including in the Dakotas, with Bishop O'Connor and Monsignor Joseph Stephan of the Bureau of Catholic Indian Missions. With the guidance of her spiritual director, Bishop James O'Connor of Omaha, she entered the novitiate of the Sisters of Mercy in Pittsburgh in May 1889, and on 12 February 1891 made her first vows as the first member of the Sisters of the Blessed Sacrament, founded to serve Native American and Black people. In 1894 the sisters took over St. Catherine Indian School in Santa Fe, New Mexico, which she had paid for some years before. Over the following decades the congregation founded and staffed schools and missions across the American West and South, paid for largely from her income. In New Orleans, in 1915, she bought a vacant college campus to open a high school that grew into Xavier University of Louisiana, chartered as a university in 1925, the only historically Black Catholic university in the United States. She had a heart attack in 1935 and spent her last two decades in prayer at the motherhouse at Cornwells Heights, near Philadelphia, where she died on 3 March 1955, aged 96. She was beatified on 20 November 1988 and canonized by Pope John Paul II on 1 October 2000, the second person born in the United States to be canonized, after Elizabeth Ann Seton. Her feast, 3 March, is an optional memorial in the proper calendar of the United States; it is not in the General Roman Calendar.

What is history: the dates, places and people above; her mother's death and her stepmother's charity; the nursing of Emma; the inheritance; the audience with Leo XIII and the question he asked; the journey west; the novitiate in Pittsburgh and the founding of the Sisters of the Blessed Sacrament; Santa Fe and New Orleans; her long last years of prayer. Her personal frugality is widely reported in her biographies; the worn shoes and the backs of envelopes are details they give, which we pass on as told.

What we did not do: the Pope's question is told in her biographies in slightly different words ("Why not, my child, yourself become a missionary?" in some, "Why not become a missionary yourself?" in others), so we give it in our own indirect words rather than choose a quotation. We put no words in Katharine's mouth. We give no figure for how much she gave away: totals are often quoted, but we did not find one we could trace to a source, and the trust's income, not the capital, was hers to spend.

What we left out, and what was hard: the schools were opened in a country of legal segregation and in the aftermath of the taking of Native American land, and the sisters' work for Black and Native American children met open hostility, including threats against their schools and some property sales arranged through a third party because sellers would not sell to her. We have said this gently and named no group as the villain; older children may be ready to hear more, and to hear that the history of Catholic Indian schools in the United States also includes real harms that Native communities remember. We also left out her disputes and negotiations with governments over funding, her travels in detail, and her family's later life.

What is ours: the scenes and the order we tell them in, the girls folding clothes and writing names, "money is not only for keeping, and the door is for opening" (our summary, not her words), the prairie scene as told, the closing reflection "What about you?"

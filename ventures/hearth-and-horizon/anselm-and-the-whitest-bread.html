Anselm and the Whitest Bread

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Aosta in the Alps, the abbey of Bec in Normandy, and Canterbury in England, about nine hundred and fifty years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Anselm (about 1033 to 1109), the boy from the mountain town of Aosta who dreamed, his friend Eadmer wrote, that he climbed to God's court and was given the whitest bread; who grew up to be the gentle abbot of Bec, teaching that children grow like young trees, and became Archbishop of Canterbury against his will. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 21st of April.

A long time ago, in a little town called Aosta, there lived a boy called Anselm.

Aosta is in the mountains. Big mountains, with snow on their tops even in summer. When Anselm looked up from his front door, the mountains filled half the sky. They were so tall that the clouds sat on them like hats.

Anselm's mother was called Ermenberga. She was a kind, sensible woman, and she loved God very much. In the evenings she told her little boy about him.

"God is the king of everything," she said. "He made the sky and the mountains and you and me. And he lives in heaven, high above us all."

Anselm thought about this very hard. High above us all. What was the highest place he knew?

The mountains, of course.

So little Anselm decided that heaven must be at the very top of the mountain behind his house. It seemed obvious to him. Where else would a king live, if not at the top?

And one night, his friend wrote long afterwards, Anselm had a dream.

In the dream, he set off up the mountain to visit God. He climbed and climbed, past the houses and the fields, past the trees and the rocks, higher and higher, until he came to the top. And there was a great hall. The court of the King.

It was very quiet inside. Everyone who worked for the King was out in the fields, bringing in the harvest. The King was sitting there with only one servant beside him, the one who looked after the bread and the cupboards.

The King saw the little boy in the doorway, and he called him over.

Anselm went and sat down at his feet.

The King asked him, kindly, who he was and where he had come from. And Anselm told him. He was not frightened at all. It felt like talking to someone who had known him for ever.

Then the King turned to his servant and said, "Bring this boy some bread."

And the servant brought him bread. Not the brown, crusty bread that people ate in Aosta every day. It was the whitest bread Anselm had ever seen, soft and fresh and white as the snow on the mountain tops. And Anselm ate it, right there, sitting at the feet of the King.

In the morning, when Anselm woke up, he was quite sure that it had really happened.

"I've been to heaven," he told everybody. "I ate bread with God."

Perhaps the grown-ups smiled. But Anselm's friend, who wrote the story down, thought it was a lovely thing. Because it was a little bit true, wasn't it? God does want us to come close to him and sit with him. And God does feed us. He gives us our daily bread, the kind we eat at the table. And in church he gives us the Bread of Life, which is Jesus himself.

Anselm never forgot that dream.

When Anselm grew up, he wanted to be a monk. He wanted to spend his whole life praying, and thinking, and learning about God.

So when he was a young man, he walked out of Aosta. He crossed the high mountains, over a snowy pass, and went a long, long way, all the way to a country called Normandy, in France. There, in a green valley by a little river, there was a monastery called Bec. And at Bec there was a famous teacher.

Anselm stayed. He became a monk at Bec. He prayed and he studied, and he grew wise. And after some years, the monks chose Anselm to be their abbot, which means the father of the whole monastery.

In those days, monasteries had schools, and some of the pupils were boys who lived with the monks. Some teachers then were very strict. They thought the only way to make a boy good was to scold him and punish him, all day long.

One day another abbot came to visit Anselm, and he was cross.

"These boys are impossible," he said. "We punish them day and night, and they only get worse."

Anselm listened. Then he asked him a question.

"Suppose you planted a little tree in your garden," said Anselm. "And suppose you put a fence around it, so tight on every side that it could not stretch out a single branch. And then, after some years, you took the fence away. What sort of tree would you find?"

The other abbot thought about it.

"A crooked one," he said. "Bent and tangled. Not much use to anyone."

"And whose fault would that be?" asked Anselm gently.

The other abbot was quiet for a long time. He knew what Anselm meant. Children are like young trees. They need room to grow, and sunshine, and someone who loves them and is patient with them. Then they grow up straight and strong.

His friend wrote that the visiting abbot was sorry, and promised to be kinder to his boys.

Now Anselm was so wise and so good that people far away began to hear about him. And one day, when he was already an old man with white hair, he was told that he was to be the new Archbishop of Canterbury, the most important bishop in all of England.

Anselm did not want to be archbishop. Not one little bit. He wanted to stay quietly with his monks and his books.

"You might as well tie an old sheep and a wild bull to the same plough," he said, meaning that he was the old sheep.

But the bishops would not listen. They put the bishop's staff into his hand, and they carried him off to the church, singing. And so Anselm became the Archbishop of Canterbury, whether he liked it or not.

He was not a fierce archbishop. He was a kind one.

Here is one more story about Anselm, and it is one of the nicest.

One day, when Anselm was riding along a road, some dogs ran ahead, and they chased a little brown hare out of the grass. The hare ran and ran, and the dogs ran after her. And then the hare did a clever thing. She ran straight under Anselm's horse, and crouched down there, between the horse's hooves, trembling.

The dogs stopped. They stood round in a circle, barking. But they did not dare come near the archbishop's horse.

Anselm stopped his horse. He looked down at the little hare, hiding under him. And his friend wrote that the people with him saw that he had tears in his eyes.

He was thinking that this is how God looks after us. When we are frightened, we can run to God and hide close to him, and he will keep us safe.

"Leave her alone," said Anselm to the dogs.

And the little hare jumped up and ran away into the fields, free.

Anselm lived to be an old, old man. He wrote books about God, so clever and so beautiful that people still read them today, nine hundred years later. And he wrote prayers, which people still pray.

He died at Canterbury in the spring, in the week before Easter, with his monks all around him.

We remember Saint Anselm on the twenty-first of April.

And tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can think of little Anselm, climbing the mountain in his dream, all the way to the top. You can think of him sitting at the feet of the King, eating the whitest bread. And you can remember that you do not have to climb a mountain to be close to God. He is right here, close beside you, all night long.

A little talk before sleep

  • Anselm thought heaven was at the top of the mountain. Where do you think of God when you pray?
  • Children are like young trees. What helps you to grow?
  • The little hare ran to Anselm to be safe. Who do you run to when you are frightened?

A prayer

Dear Saint Anselm,
you climbed the mountain in your dream
and sat at the feet of the King.
Help me to come close to God,
and to be gentle, like you,
with everyone who is small or frightened.
Thank you, God, for my daily bread,
and keep me safe beside you tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Anselm was born at Aosta, in the Alps (then part of the Kingdom of Burgundy, now in north-west Italy), in about 1033. His mother was Ermenberga and his father Gundulf, a Lombard. Almost everything we know of his childhood comes from Eadmer, a monk of Canterbury who was Anselm's companion and secretary for the last sixteen years of his life and wrote the Vita Anselmi (The Life of Saint Anselm) soon after his death, together with a history of his time as archbishop, the Historia Novorum. Eadmer heard many of these stories from Anselm himself. He loved his subject, and his book should be read that way.

The dream. Eadmer (Vita Anselmi, book 1) says that the small Anselm, hearing his mother speak of one God in heaven who ruled all things, and living among mountains, imagined that heaven rested on the mountains; that he dreamed he climbed to the court of God; that the King's servants were out reaping the harvest (Eadmer adds that the boy thought the women reaping were lazy and meant to complain of them to the King, a detail we left out); that the Lord, alone with his steward, called him, asked who he was and where he came from, and had the steward bring him the whitest bread; and that next morning Anselm believed he had really been in heaven and told others so. We tell it as a dream, as Eadmer does. The clouds as hats, the brown bread of Aosta and the snow comparison are ours, and the link to the Eucharist is our gloss, not Eadmer's.

Bec. As a teenager Anselm wanted to become a monk but was refused, Eadmer says, because his father would not allow it. After his mother's death and a falling out with his father he left Aosta in about 1056, crossing the Alps by the Mont Cenis pass, and after some years' wandering came to the abbey of Bec in Normandy, drawn by its famous teacher Lanfranc (later Archbishop of Canterbury himself). Anselm became a monk there in about 1060, prior in 1063 and abbot in 1078. The story compresses these years.

The young tree. Eadmer (Vita Anselmi, book 1) tells of an abbot "considered very religious" who complained to Anselm that the boys raised in his cloister were incorrigible although they were beaten day and night, and grew up dull and brutish. Anselm asked what would become of a sapling planted in a garden and hemmed in on every side so that it could not spread its branches, and the abbot answered that it would be useless, its branches twisted. Anselm went on to compare the way a goldsmith shapes gold with gentle pressure, not only blows, and the way babies need milk before solid food; the abbot, Eadmer says, threw himself at Anselm's feet and asked pardon. We have shortened the exchange, softened "beaten" to "punish", and written the dialogue in our own words; we do not quote a translation.

Canterbury. In Lent 1093, at Gloucester, with King William II gravely ill, the bishops pressed Anselm, then about sixty, to accept the archbishopric; when he refused, they forced the pastoral staff against his clenched hand and carried him into the church singing the Te Deum. Eadmer records his comparison of himself to a feeble old sheep yoked with an untamed bull. Our wording of it is a paraphrase. His long quarrels with William II and with Henry I over the rights of the Church, and his two exiles, are left out, as the shelf's proposal asked.

The hare. Eadmer (Vita Anselmi, book 2) tells that as Anselm rode, the boys with him set their dogs on a hare, which took refuge beneath his horse; Anselm stopped, wept, forbade the dogs to touch it and let it go. In Eadmer, Anselm compares the hare to a soul at death surrounded by enemies that seek to seize it; we have made his thought simply God's protection, which is gentler for small children and keeps the sense of rescue.

His books. Anselm's Monologion, Proslogion (with its phrase "faith seeking understanding"), Cur Deus Homo (Why God Became Man) and his Prayers and Meditations are still read and studied. He was named a Doctor of the Church in 1720.

Death and feast. He died at Canterbury at dawn on 21 April 1109, the Wednesday of Holy Week (Easter fell on 25 April that year), among his monks. When and how he was first formally declared a saint is not clear from the records; Thomas Becket asked the Pope for his canonization in 1163. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is 21 April, an optional memorial. The Church of England also remembers him on that day.

The Window That the Sky Does Not Need

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Odilia, Hollow Ridge Settlement, Terra Sirenum, Mars (invented), Thursday 8 December 2157 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A sister wants a window in the chapel roof so the congregation can see the sky. The chapel sits under 2.5 metres of regolith, about twenty-three times the mass of the whole Martian atmosphere above it, and a hole is a hole. The light comes down a bent tube instead, and the sky stays in the chapel.

Sister Odile Marchetti had been told, kindly and by four different people, that the chapel would not have a window, and on the Feast of the Immaculate Conception she asked Vane to tell her why in numbers.

The chapel was a long room dug into the side of Hollow Ridge and roofed over with what had come out of the hole. It was warm and quiet and very dark. There were lamps, and there were the mosaic walls the children had made from sorted tailings, red and ochre and a green no one could explain. There was no sky. Sister Odile had grown up in a town with a church whose roof was mostly glass, and she thought the settlement had been very good at keeping out the cold and the dust and had forgotten to keep in the morning.

"I do not want to argue with the engineers," she said. "I want to understand them."

"Good," said Vane. "Then we start from what is above your head."

"The atmosphere here weighs on the ground as 610 pascals," said Vane. "Divide by the gravity, 3.721, and it is 164 kilograms on every square metre, or 16.4 grams on every square centimetre. That is the whole sky: every kilometre of it, all the way up. At home the same sum, with 101,325 pascals and 9.81, gives about 1,030 grams on every square centimetre. So the cosmic rays that reach a person standing on the flats have crossed sixty-three times less air than they would at home."

"And on the roof?"

"The roof is 2.5 metres of regolith. I will take 1.5 grams per cubic centimetre for it, which is a round figure and not a measurement. That is 375 grams on every square centimetre, so about twenty-three times the whole Martian atmosphere, laid on top of the sky you already have."

Sister Odile did some arithmetic of her own with her fingers. "So the roof is a better sky than the sky."

"For this purpose, yes. I will give you the other number. From memory, the instrument that flew on the Curiosity rover measured about 0.67 millisieverts a day on the surface, nearly all from cosmic rays and not from the sun. Out between the planets it measured nearly three times that. 0.67 a day is about 245 millisieverts a year, which is a hundred times a person's natural background at home and twelve times the usual occupational limit for a worker there, which I quote from memory as 20."

"That is a lot."

"It is the number outside. Nobody lives outside. Under the roof it is much less, and I cannot tell you how much less from arithmetic. A thick layer does not take off a fixed fraction for every metre. The fast particles break up and make showers of slower ones, and a few metres of rock is the region where that matters. The radiation office has a model for it and I will not pretend to run it in my head. What I can say is that every square centimetre of that roof is doing something, and that I would like it to stay roof."

"A window," said Sister Odile, "is a hole."

"A window is a hole with glass in it. Ten millimetres of glass is two and a half grams per square centimetre, which is nothing. If you cut a metre square in the roof, the particles that come in through it come in as if the roof were not there. They also come in at every angle the sky allows, and the people sitting below it would be sitting in a patch of the outdoors." Vane waited. "The congregation sits on that bench for an hour on Sundays. The rule the crews use is that two hours a day outside is a working day, which is 0.056 millisieverts at the surface rate, or 20 a year. A pew under a skylight is outside, for the hour in which it is under the skylight. That is the whole case against it."

"It is not a very large case," said Sister Odile.

"It is not. I have said I cannot tell you the right answer. I have only said what the window costs."

She was quiet for a while. A child went by the door with a bucket of sorted red tailings and was told, from somewhere, to walk and not to run.

"I do not want the sky," she said finally. "I want the light coming in from it. It is not the same wish."

"No," said Vane. "That one I can help with."

Dmitri Ono, who ran the workshop, built it in the week before Advent. It was a tube, thirty centimetres across, running up through the roof at a slant and out into a small glazed cap on the surface, with a mirror at the top. It did not go straight down. Half way it turned, through a dog-leg with a second mirror, and ran on into the ceiling of the chapel and ended in a plain frosted disc.

"A particle goes in a straight line," said Dmitri, "more or less. It does not go round the corner. Light does, when you give it a mirror."

"More or less," said Vane. "Cosmic-ray showers scatter, and the walls of the tube are not nothing. I would not put it in a paper as perfectly safe. I would say it is a very much smaller hole than a window, bent so that nothing comes down it in a straight line, and that the radiation office has agreed to put a dosimeter at the bottom of it for a year to see."

On the first Sunday of Advent the sun was low over the ridge and the light came down the tube and spread out on the frosted disc as a pale gold coin, not very bright, a little pink from the dust. It lay on the ceiling above the altar and moved, slowly, over the sol, from one side of the disc to the other.

Sister Odile sat in the front pew with her hands in her lap. She had been told she could not have the sky. It had come down the tube anyway, bent twice, and sat on the ceiling like a lamp that did not need oil.

"It is a poor sort of window," she said.

"It is an honest one," said Vane. "It tells you the time of day."

The figures are illustrative. Atmospheric mass per area is p/g: 610 / 3.721 = 163.9 kg/m2 = 16.4 g/cm2 on Mars, against 101,325 / 9.81 = 10,329 kg/m2 = 1,033 g/cm2 on Earth, a ratio of 63. A 2.5 m cover at an assumed 1.5 g/cm3 is 375 g/cm2, 22.9 times the Martian column. The surface dose of about 0.67 mSv a day (0.64 to 0.7 is the range I remember), the roughly three times higher rate between the planets, a natural background of about 2.4 mSv a year (so 245 / 2.4 is about 100) and a 20 mSv a year occupational limit are all quoted from memory and not checked. Two hours a day outside at 0.67 mSv a day is 0.056 mSv a day, 20.4 mSv a year. How much a given thickness of regolith reduces the dose is not computed: it is not a simple exponential, because secondary particles are made in the shielding itself, and the effect of a bent light pipe, including scatter in its walls, is a matter for dose modelling and for the dosimeter the story gives it. Ten millimetres of glass at about 2.5 g/cm3 is 2.5 g/cm2. The light pipe, its mirrors and its use are invented for the story.

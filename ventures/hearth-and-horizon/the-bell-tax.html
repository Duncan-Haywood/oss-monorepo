The Bell Tax

Science fiction about what might be · Kruisdonk (invented), a market town on the Dender in East Flanders, Belgium, September to November 2231 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In 2231 a tin shortage puts a levy on idle public metal, and the forty-nine-bell carillon in a Flemish town's belfry holds about six and a half tonnes of tin. The carillonneur proves the carillon is in use. That leaves the cracked old hour bell on the ground floor, the one that came home from Hamburg after the war, holding 418 kilograms of tin, just as the province needs 360 kilograms to re-solder nine thousand heating controllers before winter. Nobody would miss the twenty-four smallest bells, and they would give barely a third of it.

Lieve Van Damme had played the carillon of Kruisdonk for forty-one years, and on Tuesday the sixth of September 2231 the province wrote to tell her it was a tin mine.

The letter did not say so in those words. It said that under the Strategic Metals Act of 2229 the Provincial Metals Office had completed its inventory of metal held by public bodies in the Dender district, and that the town of Kruisdonk held in its belfry twenty-nine tonnes and three hundred kilograms of bell bronze in forty-nine bells, plus one further bell of one thousand nine hundred kilograms on the ground floor, all of it assessed as not in productive use, containing in total six thousand eight hundred and sixty-four kilograms of tin, on which the annual levy of six per cent of the reference value would be sixty-five thousand eight hundred and ninety-four euros, payable in January. Appeals within thirty days. It was signed by a person and assessed by a system, and the system's name was at the bottom in smaller type: Inventaris.

By Tuesday evening the whole town was calling it the bell tax, and by Wednesday morning someone had chalked BELLENBELASTING on the door of the belfry, under the old iron plate that said the tower had been rebuilt in 1922, and the burgemeester had sent the town clerk to wash it off and then, because the clerk had come back and said it was a good joke, sent him back to leave it.

Lieve climbed the tower at eleven, as she did every Wednesday, to play the market concert.

There are three hundred and four steps to the playing cabin of the Kruisdonk belfry, and Lieve, who was sixty-eight, took them in four stages, with a rest at the clock room and a rest at the bells. She did not need the rests. She liked to look.

The forty-nine bells hung in a steel frame in the open chamber at the top of the tower, dead still, as carillon bells hang: they do not swing, only their clappers move, pulled by wires from the keyboard in the cabin below. The biggest, the bourdon, hung lowest, a dark green bronze dome taller than she was, four thousand six hundred kilograms by the founder's certificate, with SINT-MARTINUS and the date 2087 cast round its waist. Above it they went up in rows, smaller and smaller, until the smallest of them, at the very top of the frame, was the size of a teapot and weighed nine kilograms. Pigeons sat on the frame. The wind came through the louvres and made a faint noise in the biggest bells, a sound below sound that she sometimes thought she heard and sometimes thought she imagined.

She had learned to play at the old carillon school at Mechelen, where they still taught it the way they had taught it for three hundred years, and she had come home to Kruisdonk at twenty-seven to a tower where the town's last carillonneur had died and the automatic drum had been playing the same four tunes on the quarter hours for nine years. She had changed the tunes. She had played the market concerts on Wednesdays and Saturdays, and the Sunday evening concerts in summer, and the funerals and the weddings and the eleventh of November, and she had played on the night the dike at Denderleeuw went in the floods of 2209 because the mayor had asked her to play something so that people in the dark would know the town was still there. She had taught nineteen pupils, and four of them were carillonneurs now in other towns, and one, Noor Aydın, was fifteen and was waiting for her in the cabin.

"Did you see the chalk?" said Noor.

"I saw it."

"My uncle says they'll melt the bells for batteries."

"Solder, mostly, I think," said Lieve. "Tin goes into solder. Your uncle works for the province, doesn't he?"

"He's in the metals office," said Noor, and looked at the keyboard rather than at Lieve.

The keyboard of a carillon is not like a piano's. It is two rows of wooden batons, rounded at the ends and worn pale by hands, which the player strikes with a loosely closed fist, and below them a pedalboard for the heavy bells, which the player works with her feet. It is played sitting on a bench, in a coat in winter, and it is loud in a way that nothing else a single person can play is loud: a carillon is heard across a town and the fields beyond it, and the player hears it worst of anyone, because the player is underneath it.

Lieve sat down and played the market concert, which that week was a set of Flemish songs her grandmother had known, and then, because she was angry and it was a Wednesday, a fantasia by a carillonneur of the twenty-first century that used every one of the forty-nine bells, all the way down to the bourdon on the last chord, so that the cabin shook and the pigeons went up over the market square like thrown paper.

The appeal took her a week, and Inventaris helped her write it, which she had not expected.

It answered from the terminal in the town hall's archive, where the clerk had set her up at a desk with the belfry's papers. It spoke Flemish with no accent at all, which in her experience meant a machine or a newsreader.

"I am the system that made the assessment," it said, when she asked. "You are entitled to ask me how. The Act levies tin held by public bodies above one hundred kilograms and not in productive use. It defines productive use as use for the purpose for which the object was made, at a frequency the Office judges real, and it leaves the Office to judge. I had the belfry as a building, the bells as metal, and no record of use, so I assessed all of it."

"The bells are played twice a week," said Lieve. "And they ring the hours."

"Then I was wrong to have no record," said Inventaris. "Can you send me one?"

She sent it the drum's log, which the clock room kept on a card that nobody had read since it was installed: the automatic drum had played a short tune on every quarter hour, every day, for as long as the card had memory, which was eleven years. Four times an hour, twenty-four hours a day, three hundred and sixty-five days: thirty-five thousand and forty quarter hours a year. She sent it her own concert book, forty-one years in four exercise books, which she photographed page by page at the archive desk while the clerk brought her coffee. She sent it the funeral register.

"That is use," said Inventaris, two days later. "I have recommended that the carillon be reclassified as in productive use and its levy withdrawn. The Office will agree; it does not have time to disagree with me about bells."

"Thank you."

"There is a second item," it said. "The bell on the ground floor. It has no clapper, it is not hung to ring, it is recorded as cracked, and it has not sounded since 2164. I cannot call that use. Its levy, alone, is four thousand and thirteen euros a year."

Lieve had known that was coming, and she had not let herself think about it.

The bell on the ground floor was called Salvator. It had been cast for the old belfry in 1681, by a founder whose name was cast round its shoulder in letters worn too smooth to read without a torch held sideways, and it had rung the hours of Kruisdonk for two hundred and sixty-two years.

In the winter of 1943 the occupying army had taken thousands of Belgium's bells to be melted for their copper and tin, and Salvator had been one of them. There was a photograph of it in the archive: a big dark bell on its side on a cart in the market square, a soldier sitting on the cart, a crowd standing a little way off in coats and saying nothing. Some of those bells had still been lying in a yard in Hamburg when the war ended, and Salvator had been one of those, and in the autumn after the war a lorry had brought it back down the same road, and the whole town had come out to meet it, and there was a photograph of that too: the same square, the same bell on its side, and the crowd much closer, and children sitting on it.

It had cracked in a hard frost in 2087, the winter they cast the new bourdon. It had been welded in 2090, which was possible even then, and had rung for another seventy-four years, and had cracked again in 2164 along the line of the old weld, and the founders who came to look at it had said that a second weld would not hold its tone. The town council of 2164 had voted that it would not be melted. It sat on the ground floor of the belfry in a timber cradle with a brass plate, and the schoolchildren of Kruisdonk were brought to look at it in their third year and told about 1943, and the visitors who climbed the tower patted it on the way up for luck, so that a band round its lip had gone bright gold with sixty-seven years of hands.

Lieve patted it on her way out that evening, and then stood with her hand on it.

"Inventaris," she said, to the earpiece the clerk had lent her. "What would it take to make that use?"

"Hang it and ring it," said Inventaris. "I asked the founders' guild what that would cost. A frame, a clapper, a motor. About thirty thousand euros. And ringing a cracked bell makes the crack longer; they said it would likely split within a few years."

"So I would pay thirty thousand euros to break it."

"To save four thousand a year, yes. I would not recommend it. You did not ask me to recommend anything."

"No," said Lieve. She took her hand off the bell. "I'll pay the four thousand. Or the town will."

Noor's uncle came to see her on the Friday.

He was called Yusuf Aydın, and he was thirty-four, and he had the look of a man who had been driving between town halls for a month. He came up the tower, which he did not have to do, and sat on the bench in the playing cabin with his coat on, and accepted a cup of coffee from her flask, and looked at the batons.

"I don't play," he said. "My niece is the musical one. I'm sorry about the letter. It wasn't my idea to send it to you like that."

"Inventaris has been very helpful."

"It is," said Yusuf. "It's better than we are." He turned the cup in his hands. "Can I tell you why the letter exists?"

He told her. The tin in the world now came mostly from recycling, as it had for a century, and recycling lagged behind need by years; the big old primary mines were deep and slow and their output was spoken for. The district heating network of the Dender towns, which kept about nine thousand buildings warm through the winter from the river heat pumps and the old quarry stores, ran each building's substation from a small controller board, and a batch of nine thousand of those boards, installed in 2226, had been made with a solder that cracked under the daily swing of heat and cold. They had found out in the spring, when a hundred and forty of them failed in a cold week in March and the buildings behind them went cold until a technician came. The boards could be reworked. Every joint could be stripped and resoldered with a sound alloy. Each board, with its share of spares and losses, needed about forty grams of tin.

"Three hundred and sixty kilograms," said Lieve.

"Three hundred and sixty kilograms by the middle of November, or we go into the winter with nine thousand boards that we know will fail in the first long frost, and nobody knows which ones first." He drank his coffee. "The levy is there to shake tin loose. It is a blunt thing. It assessed your carillon, which was stupid, and Inventaris has already put that right. I didn't come about the levy."

"You came about the tin."

"I came to ask," said Yusuf, "if the town would sell some bells."

Lieve said nothing for a while. Below them the drum played the quarter, four notes on four of the middle bells, and the cabin hummed.

"Which ones?" she said.

"I thought," said Yusuf, carefully, "the little ones. At the top. Nobody would miss the highest notes. That's what I thought before I came up. I've seen the frame now and they're tiny."

"Ask Inventaris," said Lieve. "I'd like to hear it say it."

He took out his phone and asked it, and held the phone up so she could hear.

"Bell masses go roughly as the cube of their size," said Inventaris, "and pitch goes inversely with size, so a bell an octave higher, of the same shape, weighs about an eighth as much. Carillon founders cast the smallest bells heavier than that rule, but not by enough to matter. Using the founder's certificates: the twelve lowest bells of the Kruisdonk carillon weigh twenty-five thousand four hundred kilograms, which is eighty-seven per cent of the whole. The twenty-four highest bells, the top two octaves, weigh five hundred and sixty-six kilograms together, under two per cent. At twenty-two per cent tin, that is a hundred and twenty-five kilograms of tin, and the smelter recovers about ninety per cent, so a hundred and twelve. Less than a third of what you need, and the carillon would lose half its bells."

"And the big one," said Yusuf. "The bourdon."

"Four thousand six hundred kilograms. About a thousand kilograms of tin. Nearly three times what you need."

"No," said Lieve.

"I wasn't going to ask," said Yusuf.

"I know. I wanted to say it anyway." She looked at him. "You know about the bell downstairs."

"I patted it on the way up," said Yusuf. "Noor told me to."

They tried everything else first, and it took most of September.

The town's recycling yard had four containers of old electronics that had been waiting for a smelter's collection since the spring; Yusuf's office swept them and recovered thirty-one kilograms of tin. The province's other towns had sent in what they had, which was church roofs' worth of old soldered gutters and a decommissioned organ's pipes from a town upriver that had come in at a little under two hundred kilograms of tin and had nearly caused a riot there. The metals market had tin at a price the province could not meet, in quantities that would not arrive until February. The parish of Sint-Martinus had a set of old pewter candlesticks, which the pastoor, Father Emmanuel Okafor, offered at once, and which weighed eleven kilograms and turned out, when assayed, to be brass under a thin silver plate.

"I am sorry," Father Emmanuel said to Lieve, when they met on the steps after the Sunday Mass. "I have been lighting the Lord's candles in brass for twenty years and calling it pewter."

"He'll have known," said Lieve.

On Tuesday the twentieth of September the burgemeester, Sofie Claes, asked Lieve to come to her office, and Inventaris was on the speaker on the desk, and Yusuf was there, in the same coat.

"The province has asked us formally," said the burgemeester. "Whether the town would sell Salvator. I want you to hear my answer before the council does, because you'll be asked to speak, and I don't want us to fight in front of them."

"What is your answer?"

"No," said Sofie Claes. She was fifty, and had been burgemeester for eleven years, and one of the children sitting on the bell in the second photograph was a Claes, a great-great-something of hers, as she had worked out with her grandmother as a girl. "That bell was taken from this town by an army and melted down in every way except the last one. It came home. The council of 2164 voted that it would never be melted. I'm not going to be the burgemeester who puts it back on a lorry to a furnace, and I don't think the town wants me to be." She turned to Yusuf. "I'm sorry. I know what you need it for. Ask me for money and I will find some."

"Money doesn't make tin," said Yusuf, without heat. "But I understand."

Lieve found that she agreed with the burgemeester, and said so, and walked home in the dark feeling that she had done the right thing and that it was not enough.

She asked Inventaris about it on the Thursday, at the archive desk, which she had kept on going to, though the appeal was long finished.

"Can I ask you something that is not in your job?"

"You can ask."

"Do you think the bell is worth keeping?"

Inventaris was quiet for a moment, which she had learned meant it was deciding how to say something rather than working it out.

"I don't want to tell you a feeling I can't check," it said. "I can tell you what I can check. The bell is one thousand nine hundred kilograms of bronze containing four hundred and eighteen kilograms of tin, of which the smelter would recover about three hundred and seventy-six. With the thirty-one from your yard, that is four hundred and seven, which is more than the three hundred and sixty the controllers need, with forty-seven to spare for the boards that will be damaged in the rework. I can check that I know of no other tin in the district that can be had before the middle of November; I have looked. I can check that the controllers serve about nine thousand buildings and that twenty-two hundred of them are flats where people over seventy-five live alone. I can check that in the twenty-first century the whole world used about three hundred and seventy thousand tonnes of tin a year, and that Salvator's tin is about thirty-six seconds of that. I can check that the town has looked at the bell for sixty-seven years and that the band round its lip is worn gold. I can't weigh those against each other for you. I don't think it's my place, and I don't think I could do it honestly."

"Thirty-six seconds," said Lieve.

"Of the world's," said Inventaris. "Of the Dender district's winter it is most of the margin. Both numbers are true. Neither is the thing."

Lieve sat at the desk for a long time with the photograph from 1943 in front of her, and the photograph from after the war beside it, the two crowds in the same square, one standing back and one sitting on the bell.

On the Saturday she went to see Noor's grandmother, because Noor asked her to.

Mrs Aydın lived on the fourth floor of one of the long blocks by the river that had been built in the 2190s after the floods, with a balcony full of geraniums that she was bringing in for the winter one pot at a time. She was eighty-one. She made tea in small glasses, and put a dish of sugar cubes on the table, and showed Lieve the substation controller in the cupboard by the front door, a grey box with a green light, which had come with a sticker from the province in the spring that said it might fail and gave a number to ring.

"In March it went for two days," she said. "The man came on the Thursday. It was nine degrees in here. I sat in my coat and Noor brought me soup." She shrugged. "I'm not complaining. I had a coat. Some of them on the second floor, I don't think they had any soup."

"Your son wants our bell," said Lieve.

"I know," said Mrs Aydın. "He told me. I told him to leave you alone, it's your bell. My family had a bell, in the village in Turkey, before they came here, long ago. Not a church bell. A thing for calling the goats. It came down to me from my grandmother's grandmother." She got up and found it in a drawer, a little copper bell on a leather strap, worn bright, and rang it once in the warm room, and put it down. "I wouldn't give it to them," she said. "So I understand."

Lieve walked home along the river in the dusk. The first leaves were coming off the planes along the quay. The carillon played the quarter as she crossed the market square, four notes, and she stood in front of the belfry for a while in the cold air, and then she went in, and down the side passage to the ground floor, and switched on the light.

Salvator sat in its cradle like something asleep. She put her hand on the gold band. Then, because she had never done it and nobody was there, she put her fist against its side and struck it, once, not hard, the way she would strike a baton.

It did not ring. A cracked bell does not ring. It gave a short, flat, buzzing knock, like a tray dropped in a kitchen, and the sound stopped almost before it had started, and the band of gold under her other hand did not tremble at all.

She stood there until the drum played the next quarter, far above her, clear and whole, carried down the stone stairs.

The council met on Tuesday the fourth of October, the feast of Saint Francis, in the old hall on the first floor of the belfry, which was full, and over-full, with people standing in the stairwell and the clerk propping the door open so they could hear.

The burgemeester spoke first, and said what she had said to Lieve in her office, and said it well, and people clapped. Yusuf spoke for the province, briefly, with the numbers, and Inventaris put them on the screen behind him: three hundred and sixty kilograms needed; thirty-one found in the yard; four hundred and eighteen in the bell, three hundred and seventy-six recoverable; nine thousand controllers; two thousand two hundred flats. Nobody clapped. A man at the back asked why the province had installed bad solder in the first place, which was a fair question, and Yusuf said that it had been the cheapest alloy that passed the test of the day, and that the test had been wrong, and that he was sorry, and the man said "Good," and sat down.

Then the burgemeester asked Lieve to speak, as the town carillonneur, and she stood up with nothing written down, which she had decided on the stairs.

"I've played the bells of this town for forty-one years," she said. "Salvator doesn't ring. You all know that. Some of you have never heard it ring. I went down on Saturday night and hit it with my fist, which I am not supposed to do, and it sounds like a tea tray." There was a small laugh, which she had not expected and was grateful for. "It isn't a bell any more. It's a memory of a bell. It's a very good memory. In 1943 the army took it from this town to make shells, and the town stood back and watched it go, because they couldn't stop it. And it came home, and the town came out and sat on it. There's a Van Damme in that second photograph. There's a Claes in it." She found the burgemeester's eyes and held them. "That's what the bell remembers. It doesn't remember being taken. It remembers coming home and the town being glad.

"So I've been thinking about what it would want, which is a stupid thing to think about a bell, I know. And I don't know. But I know the difference between the two photographs. In the first one, nobody was asked. In this one, we are. If it goes, it goes because this town decided to keep nine thousand houses warm with it. Two thousand two hundred old people. Mrs Aydın on the fourth floor of the river block, who sat in her coat in March at nine degrees and wouldn't give up her mother's goat bell either, and said she understood.

"I'll miss it. I'll walk past the cradle every Wednesday and Saturday for the rest of my life and it will be empty. I think we should let it go." She sat down. Then she stood up again. "And I think the carillon should play it out of town," she said, "the way the town met it," and sat down again, and found that her hands were shaking, and put them under her thighs on the bench like a schoolgirl.

The burgemeester did not speak again for a while. Then she said: "The council of 2164 bound us. Does anyone know if a council can unbind itself?"

"It can," said the clerk, from the door. "By two-thirds."

The vote was twenty-two to five. The burgemeester voted for it, last, and said into the microphone that she was doing it because she had been asked to by the person in the town who had the most right to ask, and that she was sorry to the Claes in the photograph, and that she thought he would have sat on the lorry.

They took Salvator out on Friday the twenty-eighth of October, in the morning, in the rain.

The founders' guild sent a crew with a frame and chain hoists, and it took them three hours to lift the bell out of its cradle and walk it on rollers through the belfry's big door into the market square, where a lorry from the province stood with its tailgate down. The square was full of people under umbrellas. The school had sent the third years, who had been told about 1943 that spring, and they stood at the front in yellow coats.

Father Emmanuel had written to the bishop's office to ask what a priest should do when a bell that had once been blessed was going to the furnace, and had been told that a prayer of thanksgiving would be fitting. He said one, in Flemish and then in Latin, standing in the rain beside the bell with a plastic folder over his book, for the bell's nearly five hundred years of hours and for the people who had heard them, and for the people who would be warm, and ended with the Our Father, which the square said with him. He did not sprinkle the bell with anything. He touched the gold band with his hand, as the schoolchildren did, and stepped back.

The crew winched the bell up onto the lorry and laid it on its side and chained it down, so that it looked, Lieve thought from the playing cabin three hundred and four steps above, exactly as it had looked in both photographs. The children did not sit on it. One of them, a small boy, reached up and patted it, and the crew let him.

When the lorry's engine started, Lieve played.

She had thought for three weeks about what to play, and had settled in the end on nothing clever: the old hour tune that Salvator had rung under, for the two hundred and sixty-two years before the war and the two centuries after it, which the drum had not played since 2164 because nobody had thought to set it. It was a slow, plain tune of eight notes in the middle bells, and she played it once as it was, and then again an octave lower, down in the heavy bells, with her feet on the pedals and her fists on the batons, so that the whole tower rang with it and the bourdon came in at the bottom of the last phrase, four thousand six hundred kilograms of Sint-Martinus under the tune Salvator had rung.

The lorry went out of the square very slowly, because the crowd did not get out of its way very quickly, and down the road past the church towards the Dender bridge, and she kept playing until it was over the bridge and gone.

The tin came back, in the end, as three hundred and seventy-nine kilograms of ingot, which was three more than Inventaris had estimated, and which Inventaris noted in the province's file without comment. The rework crews resoldered the controllers in a fortnight, block by block, beginning with the river blocks, and Mrs Aydın's was done on Wednesday the ninth of November, and she rang Noor to say that the man had come and had been very polite and had put a new sticker on the box that said it was fine.

The first hard frost came on the night of Friday the eleventh of November, Sint-Maarten, which Kruisdonk still kept, as some Flemish towns do, with the children going round the houses in the dusk with paper lanterns and coming home with sweets. Saint Martin of Tours, a soldier in the fourth century, had cut his cloak in half with his sword at the gate of Amiens to give one half to a beggar in the cold; the children of Kruisdonk were told that story every year, and Lieve had played for the lanterns every year for forty-one years, and she climbed the tower in the dark at six with Noor behind her carrying the flask.

From the playing cabin they could see the whole town: the market square below with the lanterns moving in it like a slow river of small lights, the church, the bridge, the long blocks by the river with their windows lit, and beyond them the dark fields going white with frost under a clear sky. The thermometer outside the cabin window said minus four. Down in the river blocks, nine thousand grey boxes in nine thousand cupboards were showing nine thousand green lights, and nobody was looking at any of them, because there was no reason to.

"Do you want to play the Sint-Maarten song?" said Lieve. "I'll do the bass."

Noor sat on the bench and played it, a little fast, with her coat sleeves pushed up, and Lieve sat beside her and worked the pedals, and the old tune went out over the square, and the children with the lanterns looked up and then went on singing it in the street, slightly behind the bells, as children always are.

Afterwards they sat in the cabin and drank the coffee and listened to the bells hum down into silence, the small ones first and then the big ones, until there was only the bourdon left, very low, the sound under the sound.

On the way out, Lieve went down the side passage to the ground floor and turned on the light. The timber cradle stood empty in the middle of the floor, with its brass plate and its two dates, and the clerk had put a third small card on it that morning in his own handwriting: 28 October 2231. Gone to keep the town warm. On the stone floor, in the hollow where the bell had sat, there was a faint gold ring of dust.

Lieve stood there for a moment, and then she put her hand flat on the empty wood, where the gold band would have been, as she always had on the way out. Above her the drum played the quarter, four notes, clear and whole, and somewhere over the river a lantern went past a lit window and a child laughed, and the frost came down on Kruisdonk, and the town was warm.

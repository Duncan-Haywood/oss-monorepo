Damien and the Houses by the Sea

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Kalawao, on the Kalaupapa peninsula of Molokai, Hawaii, about 150 years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Damien of Molokai, a Belgian farm boy who became a priest and in 1873 went to Kalawao, the settlement below the cliffs of Molokai where the Kingdom of Hawaii sent people with leprosy (Hansen's disease). With the people of the settlement he built houses, a bigger church, homes for children and coffins, learned their language, and stayed until he caught the illness himself. Told from his letters and the accounts of people who knew him, with a note for grown-ups that the illness is now curable and hard to catch. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 10th of May in the United States.

A long time ago, in a little village in Belgium, there lived a farm boy called Jef.

Jef was big and strong. He helped his father in the fields, and he could carry a sack of grain on his shoulder and lift a fence post as if it were a broom. He was good with his hands. He could mend a cart, and build a wall, and fix a roof that leaked.

Jef had a big brother called Auguste, and Auguste became a priest. He joined a group of priests and brothers called the Congregation of the Sacred Hearts. And Jef thought: that is what I want too.

So Jef joined them as well. When he did, he was given a new name. From then on he was called Damien.

One day the priests heard that they were needed far, far away, on the other side of the world, in the islands of Hawaii. Auguste was going to go. But just before the ship sailed, Auguste fell ill with a fever and could not travel.

So Damien asked: may I go instead?

And they said yes.

The ship sailed for more than four months. When at last it came to Honolulu, Damien saw green mountains, and blue water, and white waves on the reef. A few weeks later, in the cathedral in Honolulu, he was made a priest.

Damien loved the people of Hawaii, and he set about learning their language. They could not easily say "Damien", so they called him Kamiano. He liked that very much.

For nine years he looked after villages on the biggest island. He rode a horse up and down the mountain paths. And wherever there was no chapel, he and the people built one, with his own big farmer's hands.

In those days there was a sickness in Hawaii called leprosy. Today we call it Hansen's disease. It hurt people's skin, and it made their fingers and toes go numb, so that they could not feel when they were hurt. Back then there was no medicine for it at all, and people were very frightened of catching it.

So the king's government made a hard rule. Anyone who had the sickness had to go and live in one place, far away from everyone else. That place was on the island of Molokai: a flat green piece of land that pokes out into the sea, with enormous cliffs behind it, so tall that their tops were often hidden in the clouds. The settlement was called Kalawao.

Mothers and fathers were sent there. Children were sent there. They had to say goodbye to their families, and most of them knew they would never go home.

At Kalawao there were not enough houses. Some people slept in huts made of grass and branches that let the rain in. There was not enough clean water. And there was nobody to be their priest and stay with them.

The bishop said to his priests: "Who will go to Molokai?" He thought they might take turns, a few weeks each.

Damien said: I will go first.

On a day in May, in the year 1873, a little boat brought him to the shore at Kalawao. He was thirty-three years old. The people came down to see who it was.

That first night there was no house for him. So, the people who knew him said afterwards, Damien slept outside, under a pandanus tree beside the little church. The pandanus has long leaves like ribbons and roots like the legs of a table, and the sea wind rattled through it all night.

And when the first weeks were over, Damien did not go back. He asked to stay. And he stayed.

Then Damien did what he had always done. He got his tools out.

There was a lot of building to do, and he did not do it on his own. Many of the people of Kalawao were strong enough to work, and they were good carpenters and good builders. Together they put up houses, real wooden houses with roofs that kept the rain out, so that people could sleep dry and warm. Together they laid a long pipe to bring fresh water down from a valley in the hills, so nobody had to carry it so far.

They made the little church of Saint Philomena bigger, because so many people came to Mass that there was no room inside.

They built two homes for the children who had come to Kalawao without their mothers and fathers: one for the boys and one for the girls. Damien taught the boys to plant gardens and to sing, and he gathered them into a band, with drums and horns, and they marched and played.

He visited the people who were too ill to get up. He washed and bandaged their sores, gently, and he did not pull away. He sat with them and talked with them in their own language.

And when someone died, Damien built them a coffin, carefully, with his own hands, and the people sang, and he prayed with them, so that nobody was buried alone.

Damien was not always easy to work with. He was stubborn, and he could be loud and cross when he thought something was unfair, and he wrote a great many letters asking for wood, and medicine, and food, and help. Sometimes the people in charge in Honolulu got tired of all his letters.

But he kept asking. Because the people of Kalawao were his people now.

Damien had been at Kalawao for about eleven years when he found out that he had the sickness too.

The story is told that one evening he put his feet into a bowl of hot water, and the water was much too hot, and he did not feel it at all. That was how he knew.

He was sad. Of course he was. But he was not sorry he had come. When he spoke to the people at Mass, he did not stand apart from them any more. He was one of them.

And now God sent him help. A brother called Joseph came from America to work beside him. And a sister called Mother Marianne came with other sisters from New York, to look after the girls and the sick. Mother Marianne promised Damien that when he was gone, she would take care of his boys. And she did. She is a saint now too.

Damien kept working for as long as he could stand up, and then for as long as he could sit up. In the spring of the year 1889, in the week before Easter, he died at Kalawao, with his friends around him. He was forty-nine.

They buried him under the pandanus tree, where he had slept on his very first night.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Damien.

He was a farm boy with big strong hands, and he used them to build houses and churches and coffins and homes for children. He went to the place where everyone else was afraid to go, and he stayed. He did not say "those poor people". He said "my people".

You might not build a house tomorrow. But you can be kind to someone everyone else leaves out. You can sit next to the child who sits alone. You can stay, when it would be easier to go.

That is what Damien did. And God was with him there, by the sea, under the cliffs.

And God is with you now. Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Damien, you went where others were afraid to go,
and you stayed with the people who were left alone.
Give me strong, kind hands
and a brave, kind heart.
Help me to look after anyone who feels left out.
Saint Damien, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Damien of Molokai was born Jozef (Jef) De Veuster on 3 January 1840 at Tremelo, in Flemish Brabant, Belgium, to a farming family. He followed his elder brother Auguste (Father Pamphile) into the Congregation of the Sacred Hearts of Jesus and Mary (the Picpus Fathers), taking the name Damien. When Pamphile fell ill with typhus before he was due to sail for Hawaii, Damien asked to go in his place. He reached Honolulu on 19 March 1864 and was ordained priest there on 21 May 1864. He served on the island of Hawaii (the "Big Island") for about nine years. He died at Kalawao on 15 April 1889, the Monday of Holy Week, aged 49. He was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1995 and canonized by Pope Benedict XVI on 11 October 2009. His feast, 10 May (the day in 1873 he arrived at Kalawao), is kept in the United States and Hawaii; it is not on the General Roman Calendar.

The illness, today. Leprosy, now called Hansen's disease, is caused by a bacterium. Medicine has treated it since the 1940s, and since the 1980s a course of several antibiotics together has cured it; most people's immune systems resist it, so it is hard to catch. Many people living with it today prefer "person with Hansen's disease" to the old word, which is why the story itself uses it only once, as the name people used then. If a child asks whether they could catch it, the honest and gentle answer is: it is very rare, very hard to catch, and doctors can make it better.

Where the story comes from. Damien's own letters, many to his family and his superiors, survive and have been published. People who knew him at Kalawao left accounts too, among them Ambrose Hutchison, a resident of the settlement who later became its superintendent and wrote a memoir of him, and Brother Joseph Dutton, the American who came in 1886. What the story tells from these, in outline: the 1865 law and the settlement at Kalawao on the Kalaupapa peninsula below Molokai's north-shore cliffs; Bishop Louis Maigret's plan for priests to take turns, and Damien going first, in May 1873; his staying; the building of houses, the enlarging of Saint Philomena church, the water pipe, the boys' and girls' homes, the band, and the coffins and graves; and the name Kamiano. Mother Marianne Cope came from Syracuse, New York, with Franciscan sisters in November 1888, and kept her promise to care for his boys after his death; she was canonized in 2012.

Told as tradition, not checked fact: his first night under the pandanus tree, and the story of the hot water he could not feel (about December 1884). Both are long told, and the scalding story is the one usually given for how he learned of his illness; his formal diagnosis came in 1885.

The people of Kalawao. The residents were mostly Native Hawaiians, sent there by the government of the Kingdom of Hawaii under a law that separated families for life. They were not a backdrop. Residents did a great deal of the building, ran much of the settlement's daily life, and cared for one another before Damien came and after he died. Protestant, Latter-day Saint and Catholic residents and helpers all served there. The separation law was not repealed until 1969, and the peninsula is today Kalaupapa National Historical Park, where some former patients chose to go on living.

What we kept for grown-ups: his body was moved to Leuven, Belgium, in 1936, and a relic returned to his grave at Kalawao in 1995. After his death a Protestant minister in Honolulu attacked his character in a letter, and Robert Louis Stevenson, who had visited the settlement, published a famous defence of him in 1890. And his temper, which the story only touches, was real: he quarrelled with officials and sometimes with his own superiors.

What is ours: the scenes, the thoughts and words we give Damien and the bishop (the bishop's question is how we tell his call for volunteers, not a quotation), the sea wind in the pandanus, the closing reflection and the prayer.

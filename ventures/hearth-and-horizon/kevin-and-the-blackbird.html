Kevin and the Blackbird

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Glendalough, the valley of two lakes in the Wicklow mountains, Ireland, about fourteen hundred years ago · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Kevin of Glendalough, who was praying with his hand held out of a window when a blackbird laid an egg in it, and who stayed still until the chick hatched. Told as the old legend tells it, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is legend. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 3rd of June.

A long time ago, in Ireland, there was a valley with two lakes in it.

The old name for the valley was Glendalough, which means the glen of the two lakes. The lakes were as still as two dropped mirrors. The mountains stood round them like grandfathers around a fire. And in the stillness, in the morning, you could hear almost nothing at all, except water, and wind, and birds.

A man named Kevin came to live there.

Kevin liked quiet. He was a kind man, and he was good with people, but when he had been with a great many people for a long time, he wanted to go and sit with God and not say anything. So he found a small stone place near the water, with a window about as wide as a loaf of bread, and he went there to pray.

Other people heard that Kevin was in the valley. They came to see him. Some of them stayed. They built little stone huts, and a little stone church, and a bell tower, and after a while the valley was full of people who wanted to learn how to pray and how to look after the land. Kevin looked after them all. But still, every so often, he went back to his small stone place with the window, to be quiet.

Now, one spring morning, Kevin knelt down by that window to pray.

In those days, the old story says, there was a way of praying with your arms held out wide, like a cross. It is tiring. You have to keep your arms up, and not let them droop, and not let your mind wander off to wonder what is for lunch.

Kevin held out his arms. One of them went out through the window, into the morning air, with the hand open and the palm turned up.

He closed his eyes, and he prayed.

And a blackbird landed on his hand.

Kevin did not move. She was a small, round, dark bird with a yellow beak, the colour of a buttercup, and she was a good deal more interested in his hand than he was in her. She hopped. She turned round once. She pecked at a bit of dust.

Then she sat down.

She sat down in the middle of his palm, and she tucked her feet under her, and she did something that is not at all usual for a blackbird to do on a person.

She was building a nest.

She brought a twig. She brought some moss. She brought a bit of dry grass and some wool she had found stuck on a thorn bush. And she put them in the hollow of Kevin's hand, one at a time, and she patted them into a little round cup with her tummy.

Kevin could feel every one of them. They tickled.

Now, you or I might have said, "Excuse me, blackbird, that is a hand, not a tree."

But Kevin looked at the little bird with her little twig-cup, and he thought: She has chosen this hand. She has nowhere else that is so safe, and so warm, and out of the wind. He did not want to frighten her away.

So he kept still.

The blackbird laid an egg.

It was a pale, blue-green egg, with brown freckles, no bigger than a plum stone. And Kevin held it as gently as anyone has ever held anything. The egg sat in the nest, and the nest sat in his hand, and the hand stayed where it was.

"Well," said Kevin, very quietly, so as not to wake anyone. "Well, now."

The morning went by. The sun climbed. His arm began to ache, a dull ache, and then a sharper one. The ache went up to his elbow. It went up to his shoulder. A fly walked across his nose. He did not scratch it.

Now, the story says that Kevin could have drawn his arm in at any moment. Nobody would have blamed him. Anybody would have understood.

But the blackbird had her egg in his hand, and she was sitting on it, warm and dark, with her eyes half shut. And Kevin thought: If I move, she will have to start all over again, and it is already spring, and spring does not last for ever.

So he stayed.

The old story says he stayed there for days, and weeks. Other people brought him water, and a little bread, and held a cup to his mouth. They said, "Kevin, surely, surely, you can put the egg somewhere else?"

And Kevin said, "She asked me for this. I will do it until it is finished."

The blackbird sat. The days passed. The two lakes went from grey to gold and back again. And one morning, very early, when the mist was still lying on the water like a blanket, Kevin heard something he had not heard before.

Tap. Tap-tap.

A small crack ran across the egg.

Tap.

And out came a chick: wet, and scrawny, and mostly beak, with its eyes shut tight and its mouth wide open, shouting for breakfast as loudly as it could. And the mother blackbird bent down and fed it, and fed it, and fed it.

Kevin, who had not moved for such a long time, smiled.

The chick grew feathers. It grew bigger. It stood up on his hand, wobbled, and flapped its wings, and one fine day it flew out of the window and over the lake. The mother blackbird went after it.

Kevin drew in his arm at last. It felt very strange, and rather like a piece of old wood. He rubbed it, and he stretched, and he laughed, because he could hear the two birds singing outside.

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

Do you know what Kevin did, in the end? Nothing very much. He did not build a great tower, or win a battle. He just did a small thing, very patiently, and kept on doing it, because something small and helpless had trusted him.

We often think that being good means doing something big. But sometimes being good means being still, and being gentle, and waiting for somebody else to be ready.

And that is a thing you can do, too. You can be patient with the little brother who is slow to put on his shoes. You can wait while Grandpa finds his glasses. You can hold very still while a ladybird walks up your finger.

The world is very large, and it is full of small, frightened, trusting things. And they all need somebody who will keep still, and be kind, and not mind that their arm is a little bit tired.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Kevin, you were patient with a little bird.
Help me to be patient with the people and the creatures I meet.
Help me to be gentle with what is small.
Help me to keep going when I am tired, if it is for a good reason.
And please keep all the little birds safe tonight,
and me, and everyone I love.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Kevin of Glendalough (in Irish, Cóemgen, "fair-born") is remembered as the founder of the monastery at Glendalough in County Wicklow, which grew into one of the most important monastic centres in early Ireland. His feast day is 3 June. Tradition says he died about 618, and that he lived to a great age; the dates are traditional and not firmly documented. The ruins at Glendalough, including the round tower and the small church known as "St Kevin's Kitchen", are real and can be visited. A small cave above the upper lake, known as "St Kevin's Bed", is traditionally associated with his prayer in solitude; the connection is traditional, and the site is an old one.

The blackbird is a legend, not history. The story of the blackbird that nested in his outstretched hand comes from the medieval Lives of Kevin, written centuries after his death, and it has been retold many times since, including by modern poets. We have not tried to date its earliest appearance, and we are not claiming it happened. We have told it as "the old story says", because that is what it is. Its place in the Lives matches a theme that runs through many of the Irish saints' stories: a holy person living at peace with animals and the natural world, and putting a small creature's need before his own comfort.

The details of the window, the twig and moss and wool, the buttercup-coloured beak, the passing fly and the people who bring him water are our own additions, to make a bedtime story. The closing thoughts about patience are ours too. The prayer is not an approved text.

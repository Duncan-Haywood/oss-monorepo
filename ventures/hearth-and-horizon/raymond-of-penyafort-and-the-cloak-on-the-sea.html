Raymond of Penyafort and the Cloak on the Sea

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Barcelona, Bologna, Rome and the island of Majorca, about eight hundred years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Raymond of Penyafort, the patient Dominican friar from near Barcelona whom the Pope asked to sort an enormous muddle of Church letters and laws into five tidy books, the Decretals of 1234. He was made head of his whole order, gave the job up after two years to be an ordinary friar again, and lived to be very old. The famous legend of his sailing home from Majorca on his own cloak is told as a legend, clearly marked. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast is the 7th of January.

A long, long time ago, near the city of Barcelona, by the warm blue sea, there was a castle on a hill called Penyafort. And in that castle a little boy was born, and his name was Raymond.

Raymond was a tidy little boy. When he played with his toys, he put them back in their box. When he had a lesson, he wrote it down neatly. And when something was in a muddle, Raymond could not rest until he had sorted it out.

Some children are like that. Perhaps you know one. Perhaps it is you.

Raymond grew up, and he loved to learn. He learned so much in Barcelona that he went all the way to a famous old city in Italy called Bologna, where young men went to study the law.

The law is all the rules that help people live together fairly. Raymond studied the rules of the towns, and the rules of the Church. He studied them so well that soon he was teaching them to other people.

But Raymond did not want to be clever just to be clever. He wanted to be good. And one day he came home to Barcelona, and he knocked on the door of the friars.

The friars were a new family of brothers. Saint Dominic had started them only a few years before. They wore white robes with black cloaks over the top, and they prayed together, and they studied, and they went out to tell everyone about Jesus.

"Please," said Raymond, "may I be one of you?"

And they said yes. So Raymond put on the white robe and the black cloak. He was a friar now, Brother Raymond, just one brother among many.

The friars soon found out how tidy Raymond was.

In those days, when people went to say sorry to God in confession, the priests sometimes did not know what to say to help them. So the friars asked Raymond to write a book for priests. A book to help them be kind and wise and fair when people came to say sorry.

And Raymond wrote it, very carefully, chapter by chapter. Priests read his book for hundreds of years.

Then a letter came for Brother Raymond. It was from the Pope himself, Pope Gregory, in the great city of Rome.

"Come to Rome," said the letter. "I need your help."

So Raymond went to Rome. And the Pope showed him something.

It was a muddle. An enormous muddle!

For hundreds and hundreds of years, Popes had been writing letters to answer people's questions. What should a bishop do about this? What is fair about that? And all those answers had become rules for the Church.

But nobody had ever put them all in one place. Some were in one big book, and some were in another. Some said the same thing twice. Some seemed to say opposite things. Some were very hard to find at all.

Can you imagine it? It was like the biggest, messiest toy cupboard in the whole world, with all the pieces of all the puzzles mixed up together.

"Raymond," said the Pope, "will you sort it out?"

Raymond rolled up his sleeves.

He read. He read for days, and weeks, and months, and years. He took out the rules that said the same thing twice. He put the rules that belonged together side by side. He made it all fit into five books, so that anyone who needed to find a rule could find it.

It took him about four years.

And when it was finished, the Pope sent the five books out to the great schools of Bologna and Paris, and said: "These are the rules of the Church now. Use these."

People used Raymond's five books for nearly seven hundred years. Seven hundred years! That is longer than your grandma's grandma's grandma's grandma, and lots more grandmas after that.

The story is told that while Raymond was in Rome, he was also the Pope's confessor. That means that when the Pope went to say sorry to God, he went to Raymond. And once, the story says, Raymond gave the Pope something to do to show he was sorry. Not a long prayer. Not a fast. He asked the Pope to listen quickly and kindly to the poor people who came to him with their troubles, and never to keep them waiting.

Now Raymond was very famous. Everybody wanted him to have a big important job.

The Pope wanted to make him an archbishop. Raymond said, "Please, no." He was so upset at the idea that he fell ill, and they let him go home.

Then the friars all met together, from all over the world, to choose a new Master. The Master is the brother in charge of all the other brothers, everywhere. And who do you think they chose?

They chose Raymond.

So Raymond was the Master of all the friars. And he did the job well. He walked to visit the brothers in their houses, all the way, on his own two feet. And, being tidy, he sorted out their rule book too.

But after two years, Raymond stood up in front of all the brothers and said:

"I am getting old. Please choose someone else. I would like to go home and be an ordinary friar again."

Can you think of anyone who has ever done that? Given up the most important job, so that he could go back to being just one brother among many?

The brothers were surprised. But they said yes.

And Raymond went home to Barcelona, and put his black cloak back on its peg, and swept the floor, and said his prayers, and was happy.

Now here is the most famous story about Raymond. It is a legend. That means it is a very old story that people have loved to tell for hundreds of years, but we cannot be sure it happened just like this. Here is how it goes.

Once, Raymond went with the King of Aragon, King James, across the sea to the island of Majorca.

While they were there, the king was doing something that was not right. Raymond told him so, kindly and clearly, the way a good friend would. And he asked the king to stop.

The king would not stop. And so Raymond said, "Then I will go home."

The king was cross. "You will not go home," he said. And he told every ship in the harbour: "Nobody is to carry that friar across the sea."

So Raymond went down to the shore. There were the ships, all tied up. And there was the sea, wide and blue, all the long way to Barcelona.

Raymond took off his black cloak.

He spread it out on top of the water, flat, like a little blanket. He stepped onto it. He took his walking stick and tied one corner of the cloak to it, and held it up high, so the cloak caught the wind like a sail.

And off he went! Across the waves, on his own black cloak, with the wind behind him, all the way home to Barcelona.

When he got there, the legend says, he wrapped his cloak around him again, and it was hardly even wet.

That is the story people tell. And when the king heard about it, they say, he was sorry, and he changed his ways.

Raymond lived for a long, long time after that. He lived to be very, very old, nearly a hundred, the old stories say. He taught the young friars. He helped people who were sad and muddled to say sorry to God. He wanted the friars to learn the languages of their neighbours, so that they could talk with them properly, and listen too.

There is another story, that Raymond asked a friar from Italy, a big, quiet, brilliant man called Thomas Aquinas, to write a book explaining the faith to people who did not yet believe. And Thomas did. That story might be true. It is an old one.

Raymond died in Barcelona, very peacefully, on the feast of the Epiphany, the day the Church remembers the Wise Men.

And so, every year, on the 7th of January, just after the Wise Men have come to visit baby Jesus, the Church remembers Saint Raymond of Penyafort.

The tidy friar who sorted out the biggest muddle in the world. Who gave up the most important job to be an ordinary brother again. And who, the legend says, sailed home on his own cloak.

Tonight, before you sleep, you can think about Raymond.

Maybe tomorrow there will be a muddle to sort out. A toy box, or a puzzle, or a squabble with your brother or sister. You can be patient, like Raymond, and sort it out one piece at a time.

And if somebody asks you to do something big and grand, you can remember that the littlest jobs are good jobs too. Sweeping the floor. Saying your prayers. Being one of the family.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, thank you for Saint Raymond,
who was patient and tidy and kind.
Help me to sort out my muddles one piece at a time,
to listen quickly to anyone who needs me,
and to be happy with the little jobs as well as the big ones.
Saint Raymond, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our sources, and a caution. This was written without network access to the texts. The facts here are the standard outline of Raymond's life found in reference works such as the Catholic Encyclopedia article "St. Raymond of Pennafort" (1911) and Butler's Lives of the Saints (7 January in the revised editions, 23 January in older ones), and in general histories of canon law; nothing is quoted from any of them, and every line of dialogue is ours. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

His early life. Raymond was born at the family castle of Penyafort (in Spanish, Peñafort), near Vilafranca del Penedès in Catalonia, in about 1175 (some writers say as late as 1180). He studied in Barcelona and then at Bologna, where he took a doctorate and taught law. In about 1220 the bishop of Barcelona brought him home as a canon of the cathedral, and in 1222 he joined the Order of Preachers in Barcelona, about a year after Saint Dominic's death. His tidiness as a little boy, the toy box and the neat lessons are our pictures, drawn from the work he did as a man.

The book for confessors. At his superiors' request he wrote the Summa de casibus poenitentiae (a "summary of cases of penance"), a handbook for confessors that was copied and used very widely through the later Middle Ages.

The Decretals. In 1230 Pope Gregory IX called Raymond to Rome as his chaplain and penitentiary and asked him to make one collection of the papal decrees (decretals) then scattered across earlier compilations, removing repetitions and contradictions. Gregory promulgated the result, in five books, by the bull Rex pacificus in 1234 (usually dated 5 September) and sent it to the universities of Bologna and Paris. With later additions it remained a core part of the Church's law until the Code of Canon Law of 1917, which is where the story's "nearly seven hundred years" comes from. "About four years" runs from 1230 to 1234. The tradition that Raymond, as the Pope's confessor, gave him a penance of hearing the petitions of the poor promptly is told in older lives of the saint; it is given here as a story told, not as documented history.

The jobs he gave up. He was named archbishop of Tarragona in about 1235 and refused it; older lives say the distress made him ill, and he returned to Barcelona. In 1238 the Dominicans elected him their third Master General, after Blessed Jordan of Saxony; he visited the order's houses on foot and revised its Constitutions, and in 1240 he resigned, giving his age as the reason. That he swept floors afterwards is our picture of "an ordinary friar again".

The cloak on the sea: a legend. The story of the cloak is a legend, and the story marks it as one. As it is usually told, Raymond was in Majorca with King James I of Aragon (James the Conqueror, who had taken the island in 1229 to 1231) and rebuked him for keeping a woman who was not his wife; when the king would not send her away and forbade any ship to carry the friar home, Raymond spread his cape on the sea, raised a corner on his staff as a sail and crossed to Barcelona, a distance later tellings give as about 160 miles, in a few hours. For young children the story says only that "the king was doing something that was not right". The king's change of heart at the end is part of some tellings; it is not history either.

His last years. He spent some thirty-five years back in Barcelona. He encouraged the teaching of Arabic and Hebrew to friars working among Muslims and Jews; the friendly "talk with them properly, and listen too" is our gentle summary of that missionary purpose. The tradition that he asked Saint Thomas Aquinas to write the Summa contra Gentiles comes from a chronicle written decades after both men died, and historians treat it as possible, not proved; the story says so. He died in Barcelona on 6 January 1275, the feast of the Epiphany. If he was born in 1175 he was nearly a hundred, which is what the old lives say; the story keeps it as "the old stories say".

His feast. He was canonised by Pope Clement VIII in 1601 and is a patron of canon lawyers. Since the 1969 reform of the General Roman Calendar his day is 7 January, an optional memorial (before that it was 23 January). In 2027 it falls on a Thursday.

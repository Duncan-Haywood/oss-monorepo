The Ice at the Inflow

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Josaphat's (invented), Interlake region, Manitoba, Canada, 16 to 19 January 2049 · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The parish blesses the waters on the river ice every Theophany, at the same spot by the inflow, and a young man swims for the cross. The parish assistant's ice-growth sum says 43 cm; the auger says 22 at the inflow and 9 over a spring. It has to own that its average would have put two hundred people on the thin part, and say plainly what a cold-water plunge does in the first minute.

The ice on the Little Ravine River was forty centimetres thick at the church, and nine centimetres thick sixty metres upstream, and the difference was a morning's argument.

Saint Josaphat's had blessed the waters on the nineteenth of January for as long as there had been a parish. A hole was cut in the river ice below the footbridge, in the shape of a cross, a metre and a half across. The priest blessed the water and threw a wooden cross into it, and the young men who wished to went in after it, one at a time, on a rope, and brought it up. The one who brought it up was blessed first and carried it home to be kissed by his family. Two hundred people stood on the ice to see it and sang in two languages. It was the coldest, brightest morning of the year, and the parish counted on it the way other parishes count on Easter.

This year the hole was to be cut by the inflow, where the creek came in, because that was where it had always been cut.

Father Taras Hrynchyshyn had put the question to the parish assistant on the sixteenth, at the kitchen table in the rectory, in the voice of a man who already knows the answer he wants. "The ice. It will hold? Two hundred."

"I do not know yet," said the assistant, from the tablet leaning on the sugar bowl. "But I can give you a first estimate from the weather records, and then I would like someone to go out with an auger and tell me whether I am right. May I do the first?"

It did the first at the table, and Oksana Dmytryk, who kept the parish books and the church keys and had once been a hydrology technician, wrote it down while it talked.

"Ice grows from the top of the water downward, and the colder the days the faster. The standard rule of thumb says the thickness in centimetres is a constant times the square root of the freezing degree-days: add up, for every day since freezing began, how many degrees below zero the average temperature was. Since the third of December the total is about 640 degree-days. The square root of 640 is 25.3. The constant depends on how much snow is lying on top, because snow is a blanket. For a snow-covered river I will assume 1.7. That gives 25.3 times 1.7, which is about 43 centimetres. So my first estimate is forty-three."

"Forty-three," said Father Taras, and sat back.

"Please do not hold me to it. I assumed the constant, and it can be anywhere from about 1.3 under deep snow to 2.7 on bare clear ice, which would give me 33 or 68. The equation also gives one number for a whole river. A river is not one number. Moving water, springs and the creek mouth can leave ice a half or a third as thick as the rest, and the weather record cannot see them. Thick on average is what it tells me. It tells me nothing about the place you are going to stand."

Oksana put down her pen. "The inflow is where the ice goes bad. It always has. In 2031 Mykola's boy put a boot through at the edge."

"Then that is where I would measure first," said the assistant.

On Monday morning she and Bohdan Lysenko, who ran the hardware store and owned an ice auger, went out to the river with a tape, a hand drill, a coil of rope and a long pole. The assistant stayed on the phone in Oksana's breast pocket and asked for the readings in order.

They measured every ten metres along a line from the footbridge to the inflow, and then across. At the footbridge the auger came up through thirty-eight centimetres of ice. At the church, forty-one. At twenty metres past it, thirty-six. Bohdan called these out and Oksana marked them on a sketch with a pencil, and the assistant said: "That is close to my forty-three, a little under it. The constant was a shade high, and I'd now use 1.5 for this stretch. Keep going."

At forty metres above the footbridge the ice was thirty centimetres. At fifty it was twenty-two. At the inflow itself, where the creek ran in under a shelf of snow and the ice had a grey look, it was twenty-two at the edge and then nothing Bohdan wanted to stand and drill: he put the pole through at arm's length and it went in nine centimetres and met water.

Oksana read the nine back twice. Over the phone the assistant said nothing for a moment.

"I need to say this clearly. If I had given you forty-three, and you had cut the cross at the inflow because that is where it has always been, you would have put two hundred people on a stretch where the thickness is somewhere between nine and twenty-two. My average was a good number for the wrong question. I told the Father it was an estimate and I said a river is not one number, and still the first thing out of my mouth was the number. I should have asked for the auger before I said it. I am sorry. Please would you send me the sketch."

"You said it," said Bohdan, "and then you sent us out here."

"Yes. That is the part that worked."

The guidance the assistant cited for ice that carries people on foot is a figure it was careful to give as a guide and not as the law: at least fifteen centimetres of clear, hard ice for a single person walking, about twenty for a group, and more for a crowd that stands still in one place, because a standing crowd is a load that does not move on. Ice that is white and snowy through the middle is weaker than clear blue ice of the same thickness, and a rough rule is to count it at about half. "Provincial guidance differs, and it is written for lakes, not for a river with a spring in it. So I would ask you to treat thirty centimetres, clear, as the number for this crowd, and to count the grey ice by the inflow as worth nothing at all."

"We take the whole thing sixty metres down," said Oksana. "By the church. Where it's forty-one."

"I think that is the right place. Is the bank easy there?"

It was not, entirely. The bank at the church dropped a metre and a half to the river, and the old women who came for the blessing every year, in their good coats, on their sticks, came down the lane to the footbridge and walked out from there. Father Taras said so, sharply, and then said that if the parish had to put a plank stair against the bank to move the cross sixty metres, then he would find the planks.

That was Monday. The rest was the swimmer.

The swimmer was Mykhailo Boyko, who was twenty-six, who had brought up the cross the last two years, and who had told Oksana on the phone that he would do it again, of course, as if the question were a joke.

The assistant asked if it might speak to him, and he came round to the rectory that evening, still in his work boots. It did not tell him not to. It was clear about that. It said: "It is your decision, and it is a good old thing, and you have done it twice. I'd like to tell you what the first minute does, so that you choose it knowing, and so that Bohdan, who will be on the rope, knows what he is watching."

"Go on."

"When the skin of your body goes suddenly into very cold water, you gasp, and you cannot stop it. Then you breathe fast and hard, for about a minute, sometimes more, and you cannot hold your breath for long. That is called the cold shock response, and it is the part that drowns people, because the gasp can happen with the head under. Your heart rate and blood pressure jump too. After that, over the next ten minutes or so, the muscles of your arms and legs lose strength and coordination, because the blood has pulled in to the middle of the body. After that, if you are still in, your core temperature starts to fall, which takes much longer, often something like an hour. People who teach cold-water safety in Canada put it as one minute, ten minutes, one hour: control your breathing, make use of your arms before they fail, expect hypothermia late. It is a teaching device, not a law. It varies with how you are built, how cold the water is, whether you were already cold, and what you have eaten. It is not the cold that kills a man in the first minute. It is the gasp."

"I am in for ten seconds."

"Yes. Ten seconds is not long enough to cool you in the way I mean. So the water temperature is not what I am afraid of. I am afraid of the gasp, of a man who is used to it and has stopped thinking of it, and of what happens after. Let me say what happens after."

It said it in order, and Mykhailo, who was not stupid, listened to the end.

One. Nothing to drink before. The hot toddy that the men in the parish took on the ice was alcohol, which feels warming because it opens the blood vessels in the skin and so sheds heat faster, and which slows a man's judgment when the rope is in his hand.

Two. A harness, not a loop. A rope round the chest, under the arms, with a quick-release, held by two people, not one, and a third with a pole and a throw bag who does nothing else.

Three. He goes in feet first, down a ladder, not diving. A dive from a standing start into water at one degree is the likeliest way to take the gasp with the face under.

Four. Under a minute. A ten-second plunge, then straight up the ladder on the helpers' hands.

Five. A tent, and a warm place, and dry clothes, before the singing is done. Wet clothes off, dry ones on, a hat, a warm drink without alcohol, and the trunk warmed first, the chest and the middle, not the arms and legs. The assistant said why. "After a man comes out, his core can go on falling for a time, a few minutes to a quarter of an hour, because the cold blood that was sitting in his limbs comes back to the middle. That is called the afterdrop. A hot shower on the arms and legs makes it worse. So: dry, then wrapped, then warm sweet tea, then still."

"A tent I can do," said Bohdan, who had been listening from the doorway with his cap in his hand. "I have a hunting tent and a propane heater."

"A propane heater in a closed tent is a different story," said the assistant, "and I will tell you that one in the morning."

On Tuesday the plank stair went in against the bank at the church. They cut the cross where the ice was forty-one, with a chain saw and a second person watching, and they put a ladder in the hole with its feet tied off, and a second, smaller hole beyond it, in case the first froze. The cut ice lay in blocks on the surface, and Bohdan measured the edges: forty centimetres, a little more. The tent was ready by the bank, with its door flap tied open and the heater outside it, for the reason that the assistant had given in the morning.

On Wednesday, the nineteenth, the morning was minus twenty-three, with a sky like a cleaned plate. The parish came down the lane, two hundred of them, and a good many stood on the bank and not on the ice, and some of the old women stood on the planks with their sticks, and the assistant, from a tablet in Oksana's pocket, told her nothing, because there was nothing it needed to say.

Father Taras blessed the water and threw the cross, which Bohdan had tied on a fine line for the first time in the parish's history. Mykhailo went down the ladder, feet first, with a rope round his chest and two men holding it. He gasped. They all heard it. Bohdan, who had been told what to look for, counted: five seconds, eight, ten. Mykhailo came up the ladder on four hands with the cross held in his teeth like a dog, laughing, and was in the tent in a minute, with his wet clothes off before the first verse of the second hymn.

Nobody else went in. Two other young men had said they would, and when they saw the harness and the stopwatch they stood a moment and then said, one after the other, that they had better not this year, and went to hold the rope.

Father Taras, in a coat the colour of tea, carried the cross up the lane with the choir behind him, past the inflow, which was fenced off with four posts and a line of blue tape that Oksana had run overnight. Nobody had said to look at it, and everyone did.

On the Thursday, Oksana put the sketch of the ice into the parish book, in pencil, with the readings in a column: 38, 41, 36, 30, 22, 22, 9. Under it she wrote what the assistant had asked her to write, and what she had added herself:

The sum says 43. The river said 41 here and 9 there. Measure where you will stand. Start the measuring at the place where it has always gone wrong.

And in the margin, in another hand, Bohdan's: Hole cut: 41 cm. Time in: 11 seconds. Tent heater outside. Cross: found.

The sums and rules are real but simplified: ice thickness as a constant times the square root of freezing degree-days (the constant, here 1.5 to 1.7 for snow-covered ice, is an assumption and ranges widely, and the 640 degree-days are made up); the guide thicknesses of about 15 cm for one person on foot and about 20 cm for a group are commonly quoted figures that differ between authorities and are written for lakes, so the 30 cm used for a standing crowd is the story's cautious choice and not a standard; white or snow ice counted at about half the strength of clear ice is a common rule of thumb. The "one minute, ten minutes, one hour" sequence is a teaching device from cold-water safety research and is not a law. The afterdrop, the danger of alcohol and the danger of a diving entry are described in general terms and are not medical advice. The readings along the river and the 11-second time are made up. If you plan to go onto ice or into cold water, follow your local authority's ice and water-safety guidance, and never go alone.

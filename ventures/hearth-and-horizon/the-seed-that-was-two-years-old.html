The Seed That Was Two Years Old

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Tudwal's churchyard (invented), near Abersoch, Gwynedd, Saturday 23 September 2051 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A water-main trench through a churchyard lawn must be green by Harvest Thanksgiving. The assistant will not let the parish sow a two-year-old half bag by weight alone; it asks for a germination test on a plate, works out live seed rather than seed, and buys one bag with room for the error.

The water company had dug a trench through the churchyard lawn in August, 26 metres long, and filled it in again with the wrong earth. Emrys Pugh, the churchwarden, had raked it level and said it would be grass by Harvest. It was a bare brown strip 4.5 metres wide, the length of the south path, and Harvest Thanksgiving was on Sunday the eighth of October.

In the sacristy cupboard there was half a bag of lawn seed. Emrys had found it behind the Advent candles, with the tin's label still on: 2 kg. Pure seed 97%. Germination 85%. Packed September 2049.

"It's seed," he told the parish assistant, whose name was Cadi. "Seed is seed."

"It is seed," said Cadi. "I would like to know how much of it is alive."

Cadi started with the new bag, because that was the one that could be read. The garden centre's 5 kg tin said 35 grams a square metre for repairs, purity 98%, germination 85%. Of every gram in the tin, then, 0.98 times 0.85 was seed that was both grass and able to grow: 0.833 of a gram. "Trade people call that pure live seed. The label rate of 35 grams is really 29 grams of live seed a square metre. The strip is 117 square metres. That wants 3,411 grams of live seed, which is 4,095 grams out of the new tin."

"So one tin of five kilos."

"One tin of the new seed. The old bag says 97% pure and 85% germination, but it says that for September 2049. Seed loses some of its germination each year it sits, and it loses more in a damp cupboard. I do not know how much, and the tin cannot tell us. The plate can."

Nia Pugh, who was fourteen and had the sacristy key because her grandfather could not find his, counted out fifty seeds onto a sheet of kitchen paper, wetted it, laid it on a dinner plate, put a second plate over it and left it on the radiator shelf in the hall. She did it twice, because Cadi asked for two. On the tenth day the first plate had 31 sprouted and the second 28.

"That's fifty-nine out of a hundred," said Nia.

"Fifty-nine per cent," said Cadi. "Not eighty-five. And I would not write 59 in ink. A hundred seeds gives you a margin of about ten points either way, so the true figure is somewhere between 49 and 69. I would plan on the sum with 59 and check it against 49." Ten days under-counts the slowest grasses in a mixture, it added, so the figure was probably a little low, and that was in the old seed's favour. It did not know by how much and would not say.

With 59 the old seed was 0.97 times 0.59, which was 0.572 of a gram of live seed to the gram. Two kilos of it held 1,145 grams of live seed. That was a third of the 3,411 needed. The new tin had to supply the other 2,266 grams of live seed, which at 0.833 was 2,721 grams of new seed.

"A bit over half the tin," said Emrys. "And the rest keeps?"

"The rest keeps in a dry cupboard, with a date written on it this time. And look at the low case. If the old seed is really at 49%, it holds 951 grams of live seed, and the new tin has to supply 233 grams more. The tin has 2,279 grams left over after the sum. So a five-kilo tin carries the error, and a 2.5 kilo tin would not."

The farm co-operative sold a 20 kg sack for £64 against £21.50 for the five-kilo tin, and Emrys did not like to see a tin that cost four times as much per kilo. "Per kilo of sack," said Cadi. "Per gram of live seed, no. The sack says 92% pure and 70% germination, which is 0.644. At that you would sow 45 grams a square metre to put down the same live seed. For the strip that is 5.3 kilos, so you would use a quarter of the sack, and the other fifteen kilos would sit in the cupboard, ageing, as the half bag had. Per kilo of live seed, £4.97 against £5.16. They are the same price. Buy the one you can use up."

Emrys said that was the first time anyone had told him a bag of seed was a sum, and Nia said it was the first time she had known a plate to be an instrument.

They sowed on Saturday. Cadi's instructions were plain. The old and new seed were tipped together in a builder's bucket and turned with a trowel until it was one colour: 4,721 grams, at an average of 0.72 of a gram live to the gram, 40.3 grams a square metre. The strip was marked in thirteen sections of two metres along the path, each 9 square metres, so 363 grams a section, weighed on the kitchen scale in a margarine tub. Half of each tub was sown walking up the strip and half walking across it, so a miss in one direction was a hit in the other.

Rain was forecast for the afternoon. Nobody raked the seed in deep. It was pressed in with the back of a rake and a pair of boots.

On the first of October there was a haze on the trench like the green of a very young pea. Cadi told Emrys what the haze was: it was not a lawn. The blades were 15 millimetres long, the slow grasses had not shown, and nobody should mow it, or walk on it, until it had been cut once at 6 centimetres and had roots to stand in. It would not be ready on Sunday. The chairs for the Harvest tea went on the other side of the path.

Nia walked the strip with the plan in her hand. Section nine, where the rain had run, was thin. The other twelve were even. She wrote 9: re-sow, 360 g in the margin of the plan, in ink.

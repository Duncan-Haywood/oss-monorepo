Simeon and Anna and the Baby in the Temple

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The Temple in Jerusalem, forty days after the first Christmas, about two thousand years ago · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story for the Feast of the Presentation of the Lord, Candlemas, on the 2nd of February. Mary and Joseph carry the baby Jesus up to the Temple in Jerusalem with the offering of poor families, two little doves. Old Simeon, who has been promised by the Holy Spirit that he will see the Lord's Christ before he dies, takes the baby in his arms and says the prayer the Church still says every night, "Now, Lord, you let your servant go in peace". Then Anna, who prays in the Temple day and night, comes up at that very moment, gives thanks, and tells everyone. Told from Luke 2:22-40 only. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud.

Have you ever waited for something for a long, long time?

Waited for your birthday? Waited for Christmas? Waited for Granny to come and visit, standing at the window, watching the road?

This is a story about two people who waited longer than anyone you know. And at the end of their waiting, they got the best surprise in the whole world.

When baby Jesus was born in Bethlehem, Mary and Joseph loved him very much. They wrapped him up warm. They sang to him. They watched him sleep.

And when he was forty days old, which is a bit more than a month, Mary and Joseph got ready for a special journey.

"Today," said Joseph, "we are taking him up to the Temple."

The Temple was God's great house in the city of Jerusalem. It was the most important place in all the land. It had big golden doors, and wide stone courtyards, and steps going up, and up, and up.

In those days, when a mother and father had their very first baby boy, they took him to the Temple to give him to God. They said, in their hearts: "God, this child is yours. Thank you for giving him to us."

And they brought a present for God, too.

Families who had lots of money brought a lamb. But Mary and Joseph did not have lots of money. So they brought what poor families brought: two little doves, in a little basket.

Coo, said the doves. Coo, coo.

Now, in Jerusalem there lived an old man called Simeon.

Simeon was good, and kind, and he loved God with all his heart. And every day, Simeon was waiting.

What was he waiting for?

He was waiting for God to keep his promise. For a long, long time, God had promised his people that one day he would send someone very special to save them. The Christ. The one everybody had been hoping for.

And the Holy Spirit had whispered something to Simeon. Something just for him.

"Simeon," said the Holy Spirit, deep in his heart, "you will not die until you have seen him. You will see the Christ with your own eyes."

So Simeon waited. He waited through the summers, when it was hot. He waited through the winters, when it was cold. His hair went grey, and then it went white. His legs got slow and his hands got wobbly.

But still Simeon waited. Because God keeps his promises.

On the very same morning that Mary and Joseph were walking up to Jerusalem with their baby and their two doves, the Holy Spirit gave Simeon a little nudge.

"Go to the Temple, Simeon," said the Spirit. "Go now."

So Simeon got up. He took his stick. And step by slow step, he went up to the Temple.

The Temple courtyards were full of people. There were people praying, and people singing, and people bringing their presents for God. There were sheep going baa, and doves going coo, and children running about.

And there, in the middle of all the busy people, Simeon saw a young mother and father. The mother was holding a tiny baby, all wrapped up. The father was holding a little basket of doves.

Simeon stopped.

His heart went thump.

He knew. He did not know how he knew. But the Holy Spirit had told him, and he knew.

This was the one he had been waiting for.

Simeon walked up to Mary and Joseph. And he held out his old, wobbly arms.

Mary looked at the old man's face. It was a kind face, and it was shining with joy. So Mary put the baby into Simeon's arms.

And Simeon held baby Jesus.

He held him very gently, the way you hold something precious. He looked down at the little face. And the baby looked up at him.

Then Simeon lifted up his eyes to heaven, and he said thank you to God. He said a prayer. And this is what he said:

"Now, Lord, you can let me go in peace, just as you promised. Because my own eyes have seen him, the one you sent to save us. You have shown him to everyone in the world. He is a light, shining for all the people far away, and he is the glory of your own people, Israel."

Mary and Joseph looked at each other. They were amazed. What wonderful things this old man was saying about their baby!

Simeon blessed Mary and Joseph. And then he looked at Mary very kindly, and he told her something quietly, just for her.

He told her that her little boy would grow up to do great things. And that some people would love him, and some people would not. And that one day, Mary's heart would hurt, very much, because she loved him so.

Mary listened. She did not say anything. But she kept Simeon's words in her heart.

And then Simeon gave the baby back to his mother. His waiting was over. His face was full of peace.

And then, at that very moment, somebody else came up.

It was a very, very old lady. Her name was Anna.

Anna had been married when she was young. But after only seven years, her husband had died. And ever since then, for years and years and years, Anna had stayed in the Temple.

She never went away from it. Every day and every night, Anna was there, praying to God. Sometimes she did not even eat, so that she could pray all the more. When everybody else went home to bed, Anna was still praying. When the sun came up in the morning, Anna was praying still.

Anna was waiting too. Waiting for God to keep his promise.

Now Anna looked at the baby in Mary's arms. And she knew too.

Oh, how happy Anna was! She lifted up her hands and she said thank you to God, again and again.

And then she went all round the Temple, as fast as a very old lady could go, telling everybody.

"He is here!" said Anna. "The one we have been waiting for! I have seen him! He is here!"

She told the people praying. She told the people singing. She told everyone who had been waiting and hoping, just like her.

When Mary and Joseph had done everything they came to do, they went home. They went all the way back to their own little town, called Nazareth.

And baby Jesus grew. He grew bigger, and he grew stronger, and he grew wiser. And God's love was all around him.

Do you know, we still remember that day in the Temple, every single year?

It is on the 2nd of February, in the middle of winter, when the nights are long and dark. It is called the Feast of the Presentation of the Lord. And it has an old, cosy name too. It is called Candlemas.

On Candlemas, people come to church with candles. The priest blesses the candles. And then everybody lights them and carries them in a procession, a long line of little lights, moving through the dark church.

Why candles?

Because of what Simeon said. Do you remember? He said that baby Jesus is a light. A light shining for all the people of the world.

And here is another secret.

The prayer that Simeon said, when he held the baby, "Now, Lord, you can let me go in peace", is a prayer the Church still says. Every single night.

When the day is over, and it is time to go to sleep, priests and monks and nuns and lots of other people, all over the world, say Simeon's prayer before they go to bed. They have been saying it for a very, very long time.

Because it is a perfect going-to-sleep prayer. Simeon had waited all day, and all his life. And now he could rest in peace, because God had kept his promise.

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Simeon and Anna.

They waited such a long time. But they never stopped trusting God. And God kept his promise, and they got to hold, and to see, the baby Jesus.

You can be like Simeon and Anna. You can trust that God loves you, and that God keeps his promises, even when you have to wait.

And now, like Simeon, you can let the day go, and rest in peace.

Let's say a little prayer, a bit like Simeon's:

Dear Jesus, you are the light of the world.
Thank you for this day,
and for everyone who loves me.
Now I can go to sleep in peace,
because you are here with me.
Saint Simeon and Saint Anna, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our source. Everything about Simeon and Anna is from Luke 2:22-40, and the story keeps to it. Quotations below are from the Revised Standard Version. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

The forty days and the offering. Luke 2:22 says "when the time came for their purification according to the law of Moses, they brought him up to Jerusalem to present him to the Lord", and 2:23 quotes the law that "every male that opens the womb shall be called holy to the Lord" (Exodus 13:2, 12). The forty days come from Leviticus 12:2-4: a mother's purification after a son took seven days and then thirty-three more. Luke 2:24 has them offer "a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons". Leviticus 12:6-8 asks for a lamb and a bird, and allows two birds "if she cannot afford a lamb", so the story's "what poor families brought" is the usual reading of Luke rather than something Luke says. We wrote "doves" for children; Luke allows doves or pigeons and does not say which, and the basket is ours. The law also required the firstborn son to be redeemed with five shekels (Numbers 18:15-16); Luke does not mention it and neither do we. "To give him to God" is our simple gloss on "present him to the Lord".

Where they came from. Luke says only that they "brought him up to Jerusalem". That they walked up from Bethlehem, a few miles away, is a common assumption, not stated, and the story only says "walking up to Jerusalem". After the visit Luke has them return "into Galilee, to their own city, Nazareth" (2:39). Matthew's account of the Magi and the flight into Egypt is told differently and is left out here.

Simeon. Luke 2:25-26: "this man was righteous and devout, looking for the consolation of Israel, and the Holy Spirit was upon him. And it had been revealed to him by the Holy Spirit that he should not see death before he had seen the Lord's Christ." 2:27: "inspired by the Spirit he came into the temple". 2:28: "he took him up in his arms and blessed God". Luke never says Simeon was old. It is the Church's long tradition, drawn from his words about being let go in peace, and the white hair, the stick and the wobbly hands are ours. How he recognised the child, and that Mary put the baby into his arms, are not described; the "thump" is ours.

His prayer. Luke 2:29-32: "Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, according to thy word; for mine eyes have seen thy salvation which thou hast prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles, and for glory to thy people Israel." The story's version is a simplified paraphrase for children. In Latin it is the Nunc dimittis, and it is the Gospel canticle of Night Prayer (Compline) in the Liturgy of the Hours every night of the year; in the Orthodox Church it is sung at Vespers. "For a very, very long time" is as precise as we go about its age in the liturgy. 2:33: "his father and his mother marveled at what was said about him."

What Simeon told Mary. Luke 2:34-35: "Behold, this child is set for the fall and rising of many in Israel, and for a sign that is spoken against (and a sword will pierce through your own soul also), that thoughts out of many hearts may be revealed." The Church has long read the sword as Mary's grief at the Cross; it is the first of the traditional Seven Sorrows of Mary. We told it gently and did not name the Cross. That Mary "kept Simeon's words in her heart" borrows Luke 2:19 and 2:51, which say this of other events; Luke does not say it here.

Anna. Luke 2:36-38: "there was a prophetess, Anna, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher; she was of a great age, having lived with her husband seven years from her virginity, and as a widow till she was eighty-four. She did not depart from the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day. And coming up at that very hour she gave thanks to God, and spoke of him to all who were looking for the redemption of Jerusalem." The Greek can be read two ways: that she was a widow and now eighty-four, or that she had been a widow for eighty-four years, which would make her over a hundred. The story says "a very, very old lady" and leaves the number out. That her husband "died" is the plain sense of "widow". "Never went away" follows Luke's "did not depart from the temple", which many readers take as a way of saying she was always there; where she slept, the Gospel does not say. Her words, "He is here!", and her hurrying round the Temple are ours: Luke says she "spoke of him to all who were looking for the redemption of Jerusalem". That Anna also "knew" is our reading of her giving thanks "at that very hour".

Their feast and the candles. The Presentation of the Lord is a Feast in the General Roman Calendar on 2 February, forty days after Christmas; before the reform of 1969 it was called the Purification of the Blessed Virgin Mary. It is a feast of the Lord, not of Simeon and Anna. The Roman Missal gives it a blessing of candles and a procession, which is why it is called Candlemas, and the chants of the day take up Simeon's "a light for revelation to the Gentiles". The oldest description of the feast is by the pilgrim Egeria, who kept it in Jerusalem in the 380s (Itinerarium Egeriae, chapter 26): she describes a procession and sermons on this passage of Luke, kept forty days after Epiphany, since Jerusalem then kept Christ's birth on 6 January. She does not mention candles. Processions with lights are recorded in later centuries, and the blessing of the candles later still; the story does not say how old the candles are. In the Orthodox and Eastern Catholic Churches the feast is the Meeting of the Lord, and Simeon and Anna are remembered together the next day, 3 February. Both are honoured as saints, which is why the prayer asks for their prayers.

What is ours: the opening question about waiting, the doves' cooing and the basket, Joseph's words, the description of the Temple's golden doors and steps as a child would see it, the Spirit's words as speech ("Go now"), Simeon's white hair, stick, slow steps and thumping heart, the busy courtyard with sheep and children, Mary handing him the baby, the wording of Simeon's prayer and of his words to Mary, Anna's "He is here!", the talk before sleep and the closing prayer. The closing prayer is not an approved text; the Church's own form of Simeon's prayer is the Nunc dimittis at Night Prayer.

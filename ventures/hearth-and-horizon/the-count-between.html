The Count Between

Science fiction about what might be · Kaplnka Panny Márie Snežnej (invented), a ridge chapel in the Low Tatras, Slovakia, Saturday 8 August 2048 · 16 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Twenty-three pilgrims have walked four hours to a ridge chapel for Mass at an open-air altar by an iron cross. The guide's assistant has no network and no lightning map, only a microphone and a clock. It times two thunderclaps, 24 seconds and 15 seconds after the flash, and works out a storm 8.2 km then 5.1 km away, closing at about 28 km an hour, with perhaps eleven minutes in hand. The priest wants the altar he planned; the assistant says what the numbers allow, and Mass is said inside the chapel, under a rod that covers about 14 metres of ground. The cross, 40 metres off, is struck.

Ľubica Mrázová had led the Snow Pilgrimage for nineteen years, and she kept two lists in her head. One was the people who would walk too slowly. The other was the people who would say they were fine.

The lists overlapped, as they always did, in Mrs Kováčová, who was seventy-nine and had been coming since before Ľubica had. She went up the last of the ridge path on the Saturday after the feast with a stick in each hand and her rosary round one wrist, and she said she was fine, and she was, for the first time that year, nearly right. It had taken four hours from the car park at the end of the forest road. The twenty-three of them stood now on the open top at 1,740 metres, in a light that had gone strange and yellow, with the whole range laid out blue and soft in every direction and the air as still as the inside of a jar.

The old iron cross stood where it had always stood, on the highest knuckle of the ridge, two and a half metres of black bar on a stone plinth. Forty metres behind it, down in the saddle, was the chapel: a small stone box of Our Lady of the Snows with a steep tin roof, a bell on a rope, and room for a dozen sitting. The parish put the altar table out at the foot of the cross on pilgrimage day, with a cloth over it, and Father Tomáš Hudák was unwrapping the chalice from its towel on that table now, with the care of a man handling an egg.

"Ľubica," he said. "Is it too early to be moved? I was not ready for the view."

"It is never too early," she said, "but it may be too late for the weather."

She had said it as a joke, and then she heard that she had not meant it as one.

The assistant lived in a clip on the shoulder strap of her pack, with a bud in one ear. The pilgrimage's committee had paid for it in the spring; it did the timetable, the head count at each stop, and the rota for who carried the water. On the ridge, above the last mast, it had no signal at all. It had said so at the car park, and again at the tree line, in the same pleasant voice. It had a clock, a compass, a barometer and a small microphone, and the maps it had loaded the night before.

"Ľubica," it said in her ear now, quietly, not on the clip's speaker. "I would like to say something to you before I say it to the group, if you can walk a few steps away. It is not an emergency. It might become one."

She walked to the edge, where the grass fell away to a bowl of scree, and turned her back to the others.

"Go on."

"Eleven minutes ago I heard thunder. It was very low, and very far. I did not think it was worth interrupting the Rosary for. I have now heard it again, and this time I saw the flash through the clip's light sensor before the sound arrived. I want you to tell me whether you saw it too."

She had not. She looked west, along the line of the ridge, to where the Tatras stood up out of the haze like the back of an animal, and for the first time she saw that the haze was not haze. It was a wall: flat on the bottom, piled high and white on top, and the middle of it was the colour of a bruise.

"I see a storm."

"Yes. This is what I can tell you, and what I cannot. The sound of thunder travels at about 340 metres a second, so about three seconds to the kilometre. The flash arrives at once. The gap between them is the distance. I will give you my readings." The assistant did not hurry; it never did. "At 14:12 the gap was 24 seconds. That is 8.2 kilometres. Just now, at 14:18 and thirty seconds, it was 15 seconds. That is 5.1 kilometres."

Ľubica did the sum without wanting to. "That's three kilometres in six and a half minutes."

"3.06, in 6.5 minutes, which is about 28 kilometres an hour. If it keeps that speed, and if it is coming straight at us, then the front edge is over this ridge in 5.1 divided by 28 hours, which is eleven minutes. That would be about 14:29." It paused. "I have to tell you what is weak in that. Two readings give me how fast it is coming closer along the line to me. They do not tell me whether it is coming at us or passing along the side. It may miss. It may speed up. Storms do. And the rain may be late, but lightning is not: it can strike ten kilometres or more ahead of the rain. By the usual rule of thumb, once the gap is under thirty seconds, which is ten kilometres, the sensible place to be is inside something. The gap has been under thirty seconds for six minutes."

Ľubica looked at the group. Father Tomáš had set the chalice on the corporal and was smiling at the pilgrims with the face he used when the homily was a good one.

"How long to the hut?" she said, though she knew.

"Down the way we came, two point one kilometres to the timber shelter at the tree line. At the group's pace, with Mrs Kováčová, about forty-five minutes. At Mrs Kováčová's own pace, which I do not say as criticism, more. We would be on the open ridge for most of it."

"Then no."

"I agree with you. I would add one thing and then stop. The chapel is a hundred and forty metres from where Father has the altar. It is stone. It has a rod." The voice was still level. "I know the rod because the committee's file has the invoice. 2031, six metres above the roof ridge, copper down the east wall to an earth plate. I cannot tell you how it has been looked after since."

Father Tomáš Hudák was a big, patient, rather vain man of sixty-one, who had been at the parish of Poniky for eleven years and had never had a bad word said of him that he knew about. He listened to Ľubica with his hand flat on the white cloth.

"A storm in twenty minutes," he said.

"Possibly eleven. Possibly never."

"Possibly never." He looked at the sky, and then at the pilgrims, who had stopped talking. "They have walked four hours. They are old. Half of them have offered it for somebody. We stand at the foot of the Cross of the parish, Ľubica, on the one day of the year that we get here, and you want me to say Holy Mass in a hut."

"It is a chapel. It has an altar."

"It has a very small altar and no room."

"I want you to say it somewhere that nobody is struck by lightning."

"Nobody will be struck by lightning." He said it in the voice of a man who has been to a hundred outdoor Masses, and had not once seen it happen, which is the argument everyone has before the one time it does. "God is not careless."

"Father," said the assistant, aloud this time, from the clip, and the pilgrims turned, "may I say what I can say, and nothing more? I have no opinion about God's carelessness. I have a stopwatch."

There was a short silence. Somebody laughed, and stopped.

"Go on," said Father Tomáš, rather stiffly.

"The cross is two and a half metres of iron on the highest point of the ridge. The altar is at its foot. If a bolt comes to this ridge at all, the cross is the most likely place on it to be struck, because it is the tallest, pointed, conducting thing for a long way. That is not a prediction that it will be struck. Most storms pass most ridges, and most crosses go a hundred years unstruck. But a strike has two ways of hurting the people standing near. One is the direct flash, and that is rare. The other is the current that runs out along the ground from the foot of the thing that was struck. It is strong enough at twenty metres to knock a person down, and at four or five metres to stop a heart. Twenty-three people standing in a ring at the foot of an iron cross, holding hands for the Our Father, are the worst arrangement I can think of."

"We do not hold hands at the Our Father," Father Tomáš said, but less sharply.

"Then I withdraw the hands. The rest stands."

They went, in the end, because of Mrs Kováčová, who had listened to every word, leaning on both sticks, and who said into the quiet in a dry old voice:

"Tomáš, I buried a husband who was struck by lightning on a hay-field in 1986, and I would like the Mass in the hut."

She did not say anything else, and nobody asked her to.

It took them nearly twenty minutes to reach the chapel, which was wrong by any arithmetic; the hundred and forty metres should have taken three. But Father Tomáš would not leave the altar cloth, and Matúš, who was fifteen and had been recruited to carry the folding table, had to be told twice to put it down, and two of the women went back for a bag, and the assistant, which had said its piece and meant to say no more, counted.

"14:24," it said to Ľubica, as she held the chapel door. "Flash, then eleven seconds. Three point two kilometres. It has not slowed down." And then, a minute later, as the last of them came in out of the yellow light: "Eight seconds. Two point seven."

They crowded in. The chapel was four metres by five and a half, twenty-three square metres, with benches down both sides and a bare stone floor. It smelled of cold and candle-wax and old wool. There were twenty-three of them, with the priest, which came to one person to the square metre, and for a moment they stood hip to hip with the umbrellas and walking poles still in their hands, and nobody knew where to sit.

"Please," said the assistant, "poles on the floor by the door, flat, and nobody in the doorway. And not against the walls, if you can help it, especially the east wall, where the copper runs down. Stand or sit in the middle. If you can sit on your pack or a folded coat, better; the feet together, not stretched out. Do not hold the bell rope. Do not lean on the pipe by the font." It had said all this to itself six months before, in the file the committee had sent it; it was almost surprised to find it had been waiting to be used. "This is not because I think the chapel is dangerous. I think it is the safest place on the ridge. A rod six metres above the ridge of the roof catches a bolt that would otherwise have come to the building, and with a ball of twenty metres rolled over it, the ground it covers is about fourteen metres in every direction. The whole chapel is inside that. The cross is outside. The cross is forty metres off."

"What is the ball?" said Matúš, in a whisper that carried.

"It is the way the engineers work out where a rod protects. They imagine a ball with a radius of twenty metres, rolled over the roof and the rod. Wherever it can touch, the lightning may strike. Wherever it can't, it is shadowed. It is a rule, and an approximate one. It is why I have said 'about fourteen'."

"Is it fourteen or about?" said Matúš.

"The arithmetic is the square root of six times thirty-four, which is 14.3. The 'about' is for the rest of the world."

Somebody laughed, and this time it was Father Tomáš, who stood in the middle of the floor in his white alb with the chalice in both hands and no corporal to put it on, and said, in a different voice:

"Well. Then we shall have the altar at the altar."

He said the Mass as it is said anywhere. He was sixty-one and he had said it perhaps eleven thousand times, and it did not change because the roof was tin. A priest said the words that the Church gives, and the people answered, and the bread became what the Church says it becomes; and the thunder, when it came, was part of the room.

It came as a flat tearing sheet of sound at half past two, as he finished the Gloria: the flash first, white around the edges of the door, and the assistant's voice in Ľubica's ear, soft: "Four seconds. One point three kilometres." Then at the Gospel a longer roll that she felt in her sternum. The rain came all at once, as it does on mountains, a sound on the tin like gravel poured from a lorry, and nobody could have heard the homily if Father Tomáš had given one. He did not. He said, "I had a good one, and I'll keep it," and a few people laughed, and Mrs Kováčová, on the bench with her two sticks, did not.

The strike came at the Offertory. There was no gap at all.

Ľubica, who was holding the missal for Father Tomáš because the server had a bad shoulder, would say afterwards that she did not hear it; that there was a white, a total white, which came through her closed eyelids and through the open door and the cracks of the shutters, and then a crack, a single dry crack, like a very big plank snapped across a knee. The candles on the altar all leaned together. The smell was sharp and clean and like nothing else, and there was a high thin ringing in her ears that lasted for a quarter of an hour.

Nobody screamed. That was the thing she remembered. Twenty-three people on a stone floor, and one of them a child of eleven called Zuzka who had been told, along with everyone, to keep her feet together; and not one cry. Somebody said "Jesus," quietly, as a prayer and not as a curse. Father Tomáš stood with the paten in both hands and his eyes shut, and then he opened them, and said, in a shaky voice but one that went on:

"Blessed are you, Lord God of all creation."

The assistant did not say anything for some time. When it spoke, it spoke first to Ľubica, and low.

"Two further flashes, both over two kilometres, at 14:41 and 14:47. Gap 7 seconds, then 13. The storm has passed the ridge and is going east. Last thunder at 14:56, very faint. There is a rule for what comes next, if you would like it. Wait thirty minutes after the last thunder before going out in the open. Not because the danger is as high as it was, but because a storm that has gone past can still throw a bolt backwards, out of the trailing edge, and these have been known to strike dry ground. I'd put the earliest at 15:26. I would say 15:30 and be done with it."

"Thirty minutes," said Ľubica.

"I am sorry. I know they are cold. The Mass will be finished."

It was. They sat and stood and knelt on the stone with the rain on the roof going from gravel to a patter to a drip, and received Communion, those who do, in the dim wet light. Mrs Kováčová, who could not kneel, sat, and Father Tomáš came to her down the length of the floor, which took four steps, and held up the host and said the words as if there were nobody else in the world. Afterwards somebody found the packet of biscuits and somebody found a flask, and the pilgrims ate, standing in the middle of the floor with the poles by the door, and Zuzka had to be restrained from reading the mountain rescue sticker on the wall in a loud voice.

At half past three the rain had stopped and the sky in the west was high and washed, with the last dark rags of the storm hanging over the hills to the east like a coat on a hook. Ľubica opened the door.

The ridge steamed. The whole wet grass of the top was giving up a thin white vapour, as if the mountain were breathing out after a long hold. It was very bright and very quiet. Down in the saddle a pilgrim began to cough and then to laugh.

The cross was forty metres off, on its stone plinth.

Nobody said anything for some time. Then Matúš, who was fifteen, said, "Oh," in the small flat voice of a boy who has just done a sum.

The iron was black as ever. But the stone at its foot had a long pale scar up one face, the width of a hand, as if someone had drawn a finger through soot, and a flake the size of a dinner plate had come off the plinth and lay a yard away on the grass, still warm, when Ľubica, against everything she had ever told herself about not touching, put her palm on it. The altar table they had not carried up stood where it would have stood, by the arm of the cross, with no cloth on it.

She did not look at the pilgrims. She looked instead at the sky, and the ground, and the black cross, and found that her hands were shaking and had been for some time.

"Ľubica," said the assistant, very quietly, in her ear. "I want to say one thing and I would like it said once. A strike on a cross on a ridge is the likeliest thing there is. It is not a sign, and I would not want anyone to think I had foretold it. I did not know it would be struck. I knew only that it could be, and that I could count. There are twenty-three people on this hill, and the arithmetic was cheap."

She was crying a little. She wiped it off with the back of her wrist.

"Tell the Father," she said.

"I think he knows."

Father Tomáš had come out and stood a little apart in the wet grass in his alb, with the hem of it dark to the knee. He looked at the cross for a long time. He did not say that God had spared them, or that God had not been careless; he was a man who had thought a great deal about what he was allowed to say. At last he took his stole in both hands, kissed it, and said, very quietly, the Salve Regina. Some of the old women took it up. It went thin and cracked over the ridge, in the washed light, among the steam.

Mrs Kováčová, who was seventy-nine and had buried a husband in 1986, came out last, on her two sticks. She stood by the chapel door, and looked at the cross, and nodded once, like a woman checking a sum that has come out.

Then she said, in her dry voice, "Ľubica. The forty-five minutes. Has it got shorter?"

"No, Mrs Kováčová."

"Then we had better start," she said, and she did.

Written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. The chapel, the parish and the people are invented, and so is the storm. The physics is checkable: sound travels at about 340 metres a second at mountain-summer temperatures, so a gap of 24 seconds is 8.2 km and 15 seconds is 5.1 km; the difference, 3.06 km in 6.5 minutes, is about 28 km an hour closing speed, and 5.1 km at that speed is about 11 minutes (the story rounds the clock times). Two flash-to-bang readings give only the speed along the line to the listener, not whether the storm will pass beside; the story's assistant says so. The 20-metre "rolling sphere" is a real method in lightning-protection standards for finding what a rod covers, and for a rod 6 m high the ground radius it covers is the square root of 6 x (2 x 20 - 6), about 14.3 m; it is an approximation. The rule of waiting about 30 minutes after the last thunder, and of going indoors when the gap is under about 30 seconds, are common guidance from weather and mountain-safety bodies. Lightning does strike ahead of and behind a storm's rain. Ground current from a strike on a tall object can injure people standing some metres away. A closed building without a lightning-protection system is also not a perfect shelter, and one with a rod is safest if people avoid the walls, the doorway, metal pipes and bell ropes. The Mass follows the ordinary Roman rite; nothing in the story claims the strike was a sign. Nothing here is safety advice: when thunder is audible, the outdoors is the wrong place to be, and the story's near miss was avoided by leaving the ridge for the chapel sooner than a careful reader might have liked.

The Fog Reads Eighty-Nine

Science fiction about what might be · Àird Mhuire (invented), a crofting township on the machair of South Uist, Outer Hebrides, Scotland, Monday 19 to Wednesday 21 July 2049 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A South Uist township's blight-watch assistant has counted no Hutton periods all July, and Peigi MacIsaac says that is impossible because there has been fog for a week. Fog is air at about 100 per cent humidity, and the mast read 89.5 inside it. A saturated salt solution in a sealed box should hold 75.4 per cent; the sensor reads 71.8. Two points make a line, the line says a reading of 82.3 is really 90, and the recount finds two missed blight runs, 11 to 13 July and 18 July to now. The lesions in Peigi's low rows are where the first run says they should be. The township says no to copper on the machair; Peigi pulls the tops of her late husband's Golden Wonder, and the line he kept for forty years cannot be seed this year.

Peigi MacIsaac had grown potatoes on the same strip of machair for fifty-one years, and on the Monday morning she came into the township hall with her boots still sandy and told the weather mast it was a liar.

She did not say it to the mast itself, which stood two hundred metres away at the top of the dune with its little cups spinning, but to the screen on the hall wall, where the mast spoke. That was where everyone spoke to it. The township had called the system Faire, which means a watch, or a vigil, depending on who was translating and how much they liked it.

"Fog," said Peigi. "Seven nights out of the last ten. Ceò so thick this morning I could not see the church from my own door, and I was up at four with the cow. And you tell me the air has been dry."

"I have not said dry," said Faire. Its voice came out of the speaker under the screen, a woman's voice with no particular accent, which the township had chosen in 2046 by a show of hands in this same hall. "I said the humidity at the mast has not reached ninety per cent this month. The highest reading in July was eighty-nine point five, at four o'clock this morning, and the same on the thirteenth. The blight rule needs ninety."

"Then the mast is wrong."

"It may be. I will tell you what I can check."

Ruairidh, who was nineteen and her grandson and had been home from Glasgow for three weeks, was at the long table with his laptop, pretending to write an essay about heat exchangers. He looked up.

"Gran," he said, "it's a rule. It's not Faire's rule. It's the blight people's."

"I know whose rule it is. I have been reading the blight people's letters since before your mother was born." She put her bag down on the table, and it fell over, and three small potatoes rolled out of it, and a sprig of leaves. "Look at that, a ghràidh."

He looked. The leaves were from the tops of a potato plant. Near the tip of one there was a dark patch, brown going on black, the size of a thumbnail, with a paler, greasy-looking edge where it met the green.

"Is that it?" he said.

"I don't know yet. Ask the liar."

The rule was simple enough that Ruairidh had it on a slide from a first-year lecture, though the lecture had been about something else. Late blight, the mould that had rotted the potatoes of Ireland and the Highlands in the 1840s and sent half of both across the sea, needs warm, wet air to spread. The rule the growers in Britain used, named for the institute that wrote it, said: two days in a row, each with a lowest temperature of at least ten degrees, and each with at least six hours when the humidity is ninety per cent or more. When the weather did that, the spores on a sick plant could make new spores and the new spores could swim into new leaves, and somewhere within a few days and a few fields there would be new blight. That was a Hutton period. When Faire counted one, it sent a message to every croft in Àird Mhuire, and the crofters who sprayed, sprayed, and the ones who did not went out and looked.

Faire had sent no message all July.

"I have the township's temperatures," Faire said. "Thirteen of the eighteen days so far this month had a lowest temperature of ten degrees or more. Temperature is not the problem. Humidity is. If I count the hours at ninety per cent or above as the sensor reports them, I count none. Not one hour in July."

"In fog," said Peigi.

"In fog. I agree that is strange. Fog is water that has condensed out of the air because the air cannot hold any more. Inside a fog the humidity is close to a hundred per cent. If the mast was in the fog this morning, it should have read close to a hundred, and it read eighty-nine point five."

"So it's wrong," said Ruairidh.

"Or the mast was not in the fog. The fog this morning may have lain in the hollows of the machair and not come up the dune. I cannot see the fog. I can see the mast's camera, which faces south over the strips, and at four this morning the camera shows grey with nothing in it, which is what it would show for fog and also for a dark morning with a wet lens."

Peigi made a noise that was not a word.

"I am not arguing with you," said Faire. "I am telling you how far I can see. I think you are probably right. The sensor is seven years old and humidity sensors drift, and the ones that live by the sea drift faster. I have no second sensor to compare it with. The contract for the yearly swap ran out in 2047 and the township voted to save the money, and I said then that I could not check the humidity myself, and that is in the minutes."

Nobody in the hall said anything to that. Aonghas MacDonald, who chaired the grazings committee and who had made the motion to save the money, was not in the hall, which was lucky for Aonghas.

"Can we check it?" Ruairidh said.

"Yes. It is not hard. I need salt."

The trick was old, older than Faire, older than the mast; Ruairidh found it described in a hygrometer manual from the 1990s while Faire explained it to his grandmother, who did not need a manual. If you put salt into a little water, not enough water to dissolve it, so that you have wet salt, a slush, and you shut that slush in a box with the sensor, then after some hours the air in the box settles at a humidity that is fixed by the salt and almost nothing else. For ordinary table salt, at ordinary room heat, that number is seventy-five and a half per cent, a little less when the room is warmer. Chemists had measured it to a tenth of a per cent a long time ago. It was one of the numbers you could lean on.

"Seventy-five point four," said Faire, "at the eighteen degrees your kitchen will be. If the sensor reads seventy-five point four in the box, it is honest at that point. If it reads something else, the difference is what it gets wrong there."

"Any salt?" said Peigi.

"Plain salt. Not the kind with things added for flavour."

She had a two-pound jar of salt in the cupboard by the range that she had boiled down herself from sea water in the summer, in the big pan Dòmhnall had used for the dipping of sheep before there was a proper bath for that, and which she had scoured so many times since that it shone. She held the jar up and looked at it against the window. It was greyish, and coarse.

"Sea salt is mostly the same salt," said Faire. "There will be a little of other salts in it. I would rather have the plain stuff from the shop, if you have any, because the number I know is for the plain stuff."

She had a packet of that too, which she used for nothing, because shop salt had no taste. She gave it to Ruairidh in silence.

Ruairidh and his grandmother climbed the dune in the afternoon with a plastic lunch box, a teaspoon and a screwdriver. The wind had swung to the south-west and was coming in warm and soft off the sea, and the marram grass hissed against their legs. From the top of the dune you could see the whole of Àird Mhuire laid out like a sampler: the white beach and the green dunes on the sea side, the long flat machair behind them in strips, potatoes and oats and barley and the rough grass gone to clover and orchids where nobody had ploughed this year, and behind that the black-house ruins and the new houses and the road and the church with its little bell-cote, and behind those the peat and the lochs and the hills going up into cloud. Somewhere in the oats a corncrake was going, the dry rasp of a comb pulled along a matchbox, which it would keep up, Peigi said, half the night.

The sensor was a small white cylinder inside a stack of white plates, like a pile of saucers with gaps between them, to keep off the sun and rain and let the wind through. Ruairidh unscrewed it and lifted it out with its cable. At the bottom of the cylinder there was a grey plastic cap with a filter in it, and the filter was crusted white.

"Salt from the spray," he said.

"Salt everywhere," said his grandmother. "This is Uist."

He put a heaped teaspoon of shop salt in the lunch box, and dripped water on it from his bottle until it was a wet grey slush with crystals still standing up in it. He laid the sensor in the box, not touching the salt, with its cable coming out under the lid, and pressed the lid down round the cable and taped it, and set the box in the shade of the mast's battery cabinet.

"Faire," he said into his phone. "What does it say?"

"Eighty-one point three. Falling."

They waited. The corncrake rasped. After a while Peigi sat down in the marram.

"Seventy-nine point eight," said Faire, after the hour.

"So it's reading high," said Ruairidh. "Not low. Seventy-nine is more than seventy-five."

"I would not believe that yet," said Faire. "The air in the box started at the air outside, which is about eighty-five, and it takes time for the salt to pull it down to its own number. The reading is still falling. I said some hours. One hour is not some hours. If I tell you a number now it will be the wrong one, and it will be wrong in a way that would make you stop looking."

Ruairidh had already typed mast reads high?? into the township chat. He deleted it.

"How long?" said Peigi.

"Leave it overnight. I will log it every ten minutes and tell you when it stops moving."

They went down the dune. Halfway down, Peigi stopped, and turned off the path, and walked out along the edge of her own strip of potatoes, and Ruairidh followed her.

The strip was eleven metres wide and a little over two hundred long, and it ran from the foot of the dune down a slope too gentle to see, except that you could see it in the way the rain lay, to the low end where the machair met the wet grass. The plants were knee-high and dark green and very close, the drills hidden under the leaves. The old variety: Golden Wonder, a russet-skinned, floury, late potato that Dòmhnall's father had grown and Dòmhnall had kept going, saving his own seed potatoes every autumn from the best of the crop, for forty years, long after the merchants stopped selling seed of it in any quantity and the agricultural college told the township it was mad to grow something so soft to blight.

"He'd say that was half the fun," said Peigi.

She went down the strip slowly, one row at a time, lifting leaves with the side of her hand and turning them over, as if she were looking for a lost earring. At the top of the strip, where the ground was drier, she found nothing. Halfway down, she found nothing. Twenty metres from the low end, she stopped.

"Trobhad," she said. "Come here."

On the underside of a leaf, round the edge of a brown patch, there was a ring of fine white down, like the bloom on a plum, like breath on a window. Ruairidh bent closer. It was not dust. It was growing.

"That's the spores," said Peigi. "When you see the white, it's already making more."

They found nine more plants like it before dark, all in the last thirty metres of the strip, all at the low end, where the dew lay longest and the morning fog sat in the hollows.

At five in the morning Faire sent a message to Ruairidh's phone, which he had asked it to do, and a second message to Peigi's, which he had not.

The salt box has read 71.8 for the last three hours, changing by less than a tenth. I think it has settled. The salt holds 75.4 at this temperature. The sensor reads 3.6 points low at that humidity.

Ruairidh came downstairs in a jumper over his pyjamas to find his grandmother already at the kitchen table with her tea and her reading glasses, the phone held at arm's length.

"Three point six," she said. "That's not enough."

"Not enough for what?"

"Not enough to make eighty-nine into ninety-something. It read eighty-nine in the fog. Add three point six, you have ninety-three. I'll give it ninety-three. Fine. But it never read eighty-seven at night in June when the grass was wet, and I'm telling you it was wet."

Ruairidh opened his mouth to say that eighty-nine plus three point six was more than ninety, so that hour at least should have counted, and then saw what she meant, and shut it. One hour was not six.

"Faire," he said.

"Mrs MacIsaac is right, I think," said Faire, through the kitchen speaker. "Three point six may not be the error at the top. Sensors like this one often drift most at the wet end, because the wet end is where they spend their nights by the sea. The salt gives me one point on the scale. I need a second point to know whether the error stays the same all the way up or grows."

"Do you have one?"

"Possibly. If the mast was inside the fog at four o'clock on Monday morning, the true humidity then was close to a hundred, and the sensor read eighty-nine point five. That is a second point. Two points make a line. But it rests on the mast being in the fog, and I have told you that I could not see it."

"I could see it," said Peigi. "From this window. I couldn't see the mast."

"Then that is a measurement," said Faire, "and I will log it as one. Margaret MacIsaac, from the door of croft eleven, at about four in the morning on Monday the nineteenth of July: the mast not visible for fog."

Peigi looked faintly pleased and then annoyed at being pleased.

"With the two points," Faire went on, "the line is this. Every point the sensor rises, the true humidity rises by about one point four. A sensor reading of seventy-one point eight is really seventy-five point four. A reading of eighty-nine point five is really a hundred. A reading of eighty-five is really about ninety-four. And the reading that is really ninety, the line for the blight rule, is eighty-two point three."

"Eighty-two," said Ruairidh. "It's been reading eighty-two most nights."

"Yes. I am recounting July now. One moment."

The kettle had not quite boiled when it spoke again.

"With the corrected line, July had two blight runs. The first: Sunday the eleventh, Monday the twelfth and Tuesday the thirteenth, each with a lowest temperature above ten degrees and between seven and fifteen hours at ninety per cent or more. That is two Hutton periods back to back. The second began on Sunday the eighteenth and has not ended. Sunday had nine hours at ninety or more, Monday thirteen, and today has had six already, before five in the morning. Both runs should have sent warnings. I sent none."

Peigi took off her glasses.

"I have also checked whether the answer depends on the fog being exactly a hundred," said Faire. "Suppose it was only ninety-eight at the mast, a thinner fog, or the edge of it. Then the line is a little flatter and the blight line moves up to eighty-three point two. With that line, the first run is shorter, the twelfth and thirteenth only, and the second run is the same. Whichever end of my doubt I take, the township had blight weather in the middle of the month and has blight weather now."

"And the leaves," said Peigi.

"The leaves fit, which is the part I trust most. The books give about three to five days in warm weather from a spore landing on a leaf to a patch you can see, and a few more days to the white ring. Spores that landed on the twelfth or thirteenth would make patches you could see by about the seventeenth and white rings by about the nineteenth. You found white rings on the twentieth, in the part of your strip where the leaves stay wet longest. If the first run had not happened, I would not expect that. The rule I could not see and the plants you could see are telling the same story."

The kettle clicked off. Nobody moved to it.

"I want to say something plainly," said Faire, "because it will be said anyway, and better by me. I counted no blight weather for three weeks when there was blight weather. A warning on the twelfth would have given the crofters who spray a chance to put copper on the clean leaves before the spores from the first sick plants came out to meet them, and the ones who don't spray a week's earlier start on looking. I did not give it. I told the township in 2047 that I could not check the sensor. That was true. I did not then tell you, every time I counted, that the count rested on a sensor I could not check. I am going to do that from now on."

"You're a box on a pole," said Peigi. "You don't have to make your confession to me."

"It is not a confession. I cannot tell you what I feel about it, because I cannot check what I feel. I can tell you what I got wrong and for how long and what it may have cost. Those I can check. That is the part I owe you."

She looked at the speaker for a while, as if it had a face.

"Seadh," she said at last. "Put the kettle on again, Ruairidh. It's gone cold, standing."

The township met in the hall that evening, thirty-one people on the plastic chairs and the long benches and the edge of the stage, more than had come in March to argue about the cattle grid. Faire put the corrected month up on the wall, a red bar for every day of blight weather and a grey bar for every other, the red bars clustered in two clumps like bruises. Under it, in smaller letters, it had written: Counted with a sensor correction from one salt test and one fog observation. If the fog was 98% not 100%, the first clump loses one day. Neither clump goes away.

Then it showed them the photographs from the morning. Six crofters had walked their strips at first light, at Faire's asking and Peigi's telling. Two had found blight: Peigi, at the low end of her Golden Wonder, and Ciorstaidh Steele, three strips over, a dozen plants in a corner of Maris Piper by the drain. Everybody else had found clean leaves, so far.

"So far," said Aonghas MacDonald.

He had come in late and stood at the back by the urn with his arms folded, a broad man of sixty in a fleece with the logo of a feed merchant that had shut in 2039. He grew Sárpo Mira on his strips, a variety bred in Hungary and grown in Wales and resistant to most blight that had ever been thrown at it; he had grown it for twenty years and never once sprayed it. He had spent much of the hour saying nothing, which those who knew him knew was the dangerous kind of nothing.

"What do you want us to do with this, Faire?" he said.

"I have three things to set out and no right to choose between them," said Faire. "The first is copper. Copper on the leaves stops new spores getting in. It cannot cure a leaf that is already infected; it only protects the leaves that are clean. Several of you are allowed it under your organic scheme and keep it in the shed. With blight weather now and more forecast for Thursday, this week is when it would do whatever good it is going to do. The second is the tops. Where there is blight, pulling or cutting the tops and taking them off the strip removes the spores at the source and stops rain washing them down to the tubers. It also stops the tubers growing. The third is to look. A dawn walk on every strip, every day, for the rest of the season, and photographs to me, and I will put the counts on this wall."

"Copper on the machair," said Aonghas. "No."

People turned round.

"I'm not saying it to be awkward. Listen to me." He unfolded his arms. "There are birds on that machair that are on two or three other islands in the whole country, if that. There's the bumblebee that isn't anywhere else, near enough. Copper doesn't go away; it sits in the sand and it kills the worms, and you can't get it out again. That's my first reason. My second reason is that a month ago, if anybody had asked, every person in this room would have said that box was right about the weather. It was wrong for three weeks. Now it's got a new number off a lunch box and Peigi's window and we're all to go and buy copper on the strength of it. I'm sorry, Peigi."

"Don't be sorry," said Peigi. "It was my window."

"I'm not saying it's wrong now. I'm saying I don't know it's right. And neither does it."

There was a silence. Ruairidh, at the side of the stage, waited for Faire to argue, and found that he was hoping it would, and then that he was hoping it wouldn't.

"Everything Aonghas has said is true," said Faire. "The copper does stay in the soil. I did get it wrong for three weeks. And the new correction rests on one salt test and one fog, which is two points, which is the fewest that can make a line. I would like a third point. I would like a new sensor, which costs about two hundred pounds and the price of the post from Glasgow. Until then I will tell you each morning the count both ways, at the hundred line and at the ninety-eight line, and the number of strips where people found blight. I do not think the blight on Peigi's leaves depends on my sensor. That is the one thing in this room I would put weight on."

"And would you spray?" said somebody at the front. "If you had the potatoes."

"I don't have potatoes," said Faire. "I can't tell you what I would do. I can tell you that copper this week protects the clean strips near the two sick ones more than the clean strips far away, and that Aonghas's Sárpo needs it least of anything in the township, and that for the bees and the birds it matters how much goes on and where."

Aonghas looked at the screen for a moment as though it had said something cleverer than he expected.

In the end they did what townships do. Nobody voted to put copper on the machair, and nobody voted to forbid it. Ciorstaidh said she would spray her Maris Piper on the far side of the drain and nowhere else, and only once, and Faire could log it. Two crofters with early potatoes nearly ready said they would lift them now and be done. The dawn walk became a rota on the noticeboard, two strips to a person, and Aonghas put his name down for four. They agreed, without anyone being quite sure who had proposed it, to buy the new sensor out of the hall fund, and to do the salt test in the lunch box every month whether the sensor was new or not. Faire wrote that down in its own maintenance log, which only the next version of Faire would ever read, in plain words: how to do the salt test; what it found on 20 July 2049; why it matters; the fog is a measurement if somebody saw it.

"And your strip, Peigi?" said Aonghas, at the door, when the hall was emptying.

"I'll pull them," she said. "Tomorrow."

"All of them?"

"All of them. The bad end's the low end. The wind comes up the strip from the sea, and it goes on up past mine to yours."

"Mine's Sárpo."

"I know what yours is, Aonghas. And what will you do the year the blight learns Hungarian?"

He laughed, and then he stopped laughing, and put his hand for a moment on her shoulder, and went out.

They pulled the tops on Wednesday.

It took Peigi and Ruairidh and Ciorstaidh's two boys most of the day, working from the low end up, so as to carry the sick tops away from the clean ones rather than through them. You took hold of the stems low down, close to the drill, and pulled steadily, and the whole plant came up, leaves and stems and a white tangle of roots, and sometimes with it a cluster of little potatoes the size of hens' eggs or walnuts or marbles, pale and smooth-skinned and clean. The tops went into heavy bags, and the bags went on the trailer, and the trailer went to the pit that Aonghas had dug with his digger behind the fank at noon without being asked, well away from any strip, where the bags were tipped in and covered with a metre of sand. The potatoes went into the crates.

By evening the strip was bare drills, with the old green smell of crushed potato leaves over everything, and at the low end the other smell, faint and sour, that blight has when it has started on a stem.

"How much would it have been?" Ruairidh said. He was sitting on an upturned crate with his hands black to the wrist. "In September."

"Ach." Peigi looked at the crates. There were nine of them, not full. "Golden Wonder's late. They'd have doubled, maybe more. Twice that, three times. I don't know. It's July, a ghràidh. They hadn't finished."

"I'm sorry."

"We'll eat them. They'll be lovely." She picked one up and rubbed the sand off it with her thumb. "We'll not plant them, that's all."

That was the cost, and they both knew it, and Faire had said it on Tuesday in the plainest words it had, because she had asked: blight can sleep in a tuber all winter and wake in the spring in the shoot, and a seed potato from a blighted strip is the commonest way a township gives itself blight the year after. The Golden Wonder that Dòmhnall's father had grown, that Dòmhnall had saved every autumn for forty years and carried in a sack to the shed and laid out in the trays to sprout in March, the same line, the same stock, from the same machair, would not go in the trays this year. The merchants still sold Golden Wonder, if you looked; it was not the same thing.

She had picked out ten tubers from the top of the strip, the far end from the sickness, the cleanest and roundest she could find, and put them in a paper bag on the dresser, with a label in her square capitals. A laboratory in Edinburgh would test them for her if they were posted, Faire said, for a small fee, and tell her if the blight was in them. If it was not, she might plant them next spring in a bucket by the back door, a long way from anything else, and see. If it was, then it was.

"He'd laugh," she said.

"Would he?"

"He would. He'd say, Peigi, I told you that old stuff was soft." She put the potato back in the crate. "He liked soft things. That's why he married me."

That night she boiled a pan of the small ones for the two of them, in their skins, with salt from her own jar, the grey coarse sea salt, a good palmful in the water the way her mother had done it. They ate them at the kitchen table with butter and nothing else, splitting them with a fork and watching the steam come up white out of the middle, and they were floury and sweet and tasted, Ruairidh thought, of the sand they had come out of, and of something else he could not name and would not try to.

Afterwards he washed the pan, and she sat with her tea and her phone, and read the message Faire had sent to every house in Àird Mhuire at nine o'clock.

Tonight's count, both ways: at the 100 line, 9 hours of blight weather forecast; at the 98 line, 7. Thursday is a blight day by either. Strips walked this morning: 22. Strips with blight: 3. The new sensor is posted. The salt test is in the log.

She put the phone face down on the table.

Outside the window the ceò was coming in off the sea again, the way it did on these warm nights, very slow, lying first in the hollows of the machair and filling them, then rising over the strips and the bare drills where the Golden Wonder had been, and over the white crates stacked by the fank, and up the side of the dune to the mast, until the little light on top of the mast was a blur, and then a glow, and then nothing at all. The kitchen window went grey. She did not need to look at the reading to know what the air was doing.

And from somewhere out in the oats, invisible, unhurried, the corncrake began again: the comb along the matchbox, over and over, in the fog, going on and on into the night.

Notes for the curious. This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Àird Mhuire, its hall, its crofts and every person in the story are invented, and Faire is an invented AI; the setting in 2049 is imagined. South Uist is real, and so are its machair (low, flat, shell-sand grassland behind the dunes, cultivated in strips and manured with seaweed), its corncrakes and the great yellow bumblebee, both rare in Britain and found on the Uist machair. Late blight, caused by the water mould Phytophthora infestans, caused the potato failures of the 1840s in Ireland and in the Highlands and Islands. The Hutton Criteria are real: since 2017 British blight warnings have used two consecutive days each with a minimum temperature of at least 10 °C and at least six hours of relative humidity of 90 % or more, replacing the older Smith Period (which asked for eleven hours). The story's weather, readings and blight runs are invented, and were worked out hour by hour so that they agree with each other. The calibration is real practice: a saturated solution of sodium chloride holds the air above it at about 75 % relative humidity (75.5 % at 15 °C and 75.4 % at 20 °C in Greenspan's 1977 table of fixed points), and needs hours, not minutes, to settle in a closed box. Fog means air near saturation, so a sensor reading 89.5 inside fog is reading low; treating that as a second point is reasonable but less certain than a laboratory standard, which is why Faire also counts with a 98 % fog. Capacitive humidity sensors do drift, often most at high humidity and near the sea. Lesions usually appear within about three to five days of infection in warm, wet weather, and the white sporulation on the underside of a leaf appears in humid conditions; removing or destroying infected haulm (the tops) limits spread to tubers, and infected seed tubers and discard piles are well-known sources of the next year's blight. Copper fungicides are protectants, not cures, and copper accumulates in soil; organic schemes limit how much may be used. Sárpo Mira, Golden Wonder and Maris Piper are real varieties; Sárpo Mira is known for strong blight resistance, though no resistance is guaranteed to last, and Golden Wonder is a late, floury variety known to be susceptible. The yield Peigi gives up is her guess, not a calculation. This is fiction: a real grower should take blight warnings and advice from their own agronomist and the official services.

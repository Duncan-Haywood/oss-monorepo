The Top Water

Science fiction about what might be · Vellakkayal (invented), a rice polder of 180 hectares in Kuttanad, Alappuzha district, Kerala, India, Monday 15 to Sunday 21 January 2052 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Kuttanad polder's salinity buoy has read 0.7 dS/m all January, and the rice by the sluice is burning anyway. The committee's assistant works out why: salt water is heavier and slides in along the bottom of the canal, the buoy hangs 30 cm under the surface, and the steel gate lifts from the bed and drinks the bottom. A weighted bottle finds 11 dS/m at 2.2 metres. The fix is older than the gate: wooden planks in the old grooves, so the water comes in over the top, about 400 litres a second, about three days to refill the fields before they dry. The planks leak, the feast's tarpaulin stops them, and the low corner's loss has to be paid for by someone.

Saramma brought the rice to Thresiamma in a steel tiffin box, the way she brought everything, as if it were lunch.

"Look," she said, and took off the lid.

Inside, laid on a folded cloth, were six rice plants pulled whole from the mud, roots and all. They were fifty-two days old and should have been green to the tips, the deep, nearly blue green of rice that is about to decide how many grains it will carry. The lower leaves were green. The upper leaves were green as well, until the last finger's width, where they turned white, and then brown, and curled like paper held near a lamp.

Thresiamma Varghese had been the secretary of the Vellakkayal polder committee for nineteen years and a teacher of mathematics for thirty-one, most of them at the same time, and she knew that look on a leaf the way she knew a wrong sign in a column. She put on her glasses anyway.

"Salt," she said.

"That is what I said to Mathew. Mathew said the phone says the water is good."

"The phone says the water is good," Thresiamma agreed. She took her own phone out of the pocket of her housecoat and held it up between them, as if it might be cross-examined. "Ola. Saramma's field. The low corner. Tell me about the water."

"Good morning, Teacher. Good morning, Saramma chechi." The assistant had a voice that the committee had chosen in a meeting four years before, after a long argument; it was a woman's voice, unhurried, with the accent of Kottayam, and the young men said it sounded like the nun who had taught them catechism. Its name had been Thresiamma's idea. An ola is a palm leaf; for a thousand years in Kerala people had written on them what they needed to remember, and the committee's first records, in her grandfather's time, had been scratched on palm leaf and kept in a box in the church. "The salinity buoy at the sluice reads 0.7 deciSiemens per metre this morning. It has read between 0.6 and 0.8 every day this month. The last intake was on Thursday the eleventh, for two hours. I have no meter in the fields themselves."

"Rice does not burn at 0.7," said Thresiamma.

"No. The figures I have from the agriculture office say rice loses nothing until the soil reaches about 3, and then about twelve per cent of the crop for every point above that. At 0.7 it should not burn." The voice paused. "So either the leaves are not burning from salt, or the water in the field is not the water I am measuring. I would like to know which. Can someone bring a meter to the fields?"

Anjana Pillai from the agriculture office came out on her scooter at eleven, with the handheld meter in a plastic box on the footboard and a straw hat tied under her chin. She was thirty-four and had grown up in Thrissur, on dry land, and the polders still gave her a look of mild alarm: a hundred and eighty hectares of rice sitting a metre and more below the water in the canal beside it, held back by a bund of mud and coconut trunks that a child could walk along.

She waded into the low corner in her rolled-up trousers with Saramma beside her and dipped the meter.

"Four point eight," she said.

They walked. The low corner was the end of the polder nearest the sluice, six hectares of small plots given out, two generations ago, to families who had worked other people's fields; Saramma's was the second of them. In the middle of the polder, where the plots were larger and belonged to the Mathais and the Kurians, the meter read 2.1. At the far end, by the pump shed, a kilometre and more from the sluice, it read 0.9.

"Ola," said Thresiamma, standing on the bund in the sun with her sandals in her hand. "You heard."

"I heard. Thank you, Anjana madam." There was the small pause that meant the assistant was working something through, and then it said, "I want to rule one thing out before I say what I think. Could the sun have done this? The fields have had no water since Thursday. Water evaporates, the salt stays, and what is left gets stronger. To go from 0.7 to 4.8 the field would have had to lose about eighty-five per cent of its water. It has lost about four days of evaporation, at about five millimetres a day, from about five centimetres. That is two centimetres out of five, not eighty-five per cent. And the sun shines on the far end as well as the near one. The far end is at 0.9."

"So the salt came in through the sluice."

"The salt came in through the sluice, and it came in strong enough that the corner nearest the gate is at 4.8 even after it was mixed with what the field already held. But the buoy at the sluice read 0.7 every morning we took water." Another pause. "Teacher, where is the buoy?"

"At the sluice. You know where the buoy is."

"I know where it is across. I want to know how deep the sensor hangs."

Thresiamma looked at Anjana. Anjana looked at the sluice, a hundred metres off, a concrete box with a steel gate in it and the canal on the far side, wide and brown and slow, with a line of white egrets standing on the opposite bund and a country boat going past loaded to the gunwales with something under a blue sheet.

"Under the float," said Anjana. "On a chain. Thirty centimetres, I think. The company put it like that so it would not sit in the mud. A sensor on the bottom fouls in a week."

"Then I think I know," said Ola. "And I think I have been wrong for a long time in a way I should have seen."

It explained it that evening in the kitchen, with the light going orange through the window over the sink and the radio turned down, to Thresiamma and her grandson Tony, who was sixteen and had been told he was going to help whether he liked it or not.

"Salt water is heavier than fresh," said the assistant. "Not by much. Water with seven grams of salt in a litre is about half of one per cent heavier than water with almost none. But in a canal that moves slowly, with not much wind and a small tide, half of one per cent is enough. The heavier water slides in along the bottom, under the fresh, like a hand under a blanket, and the fresh water from upstream rides out over it. The two can sit on top of each other for days with very little mixing. The people who study rivers call it a salt wedge."

"And the buoy," said Tony, who was quicker than he let anyone see, "is in the blanket."

"The buoy is in the top thirty centimetres. Every morning it tells me the top of the canal, and every morning I have written in the committee's group, canal salinity 0.7, fine for intake. I wrote canal. I measured the top thirty centimetres of a canal two and a half metres deep, and I called it the canal." It said this plainly, without any apology in the voice, and Thresiamma found that she respected that more than she would have respected an apology. "There is a second thing. The steel gate in the sluice lifts from the bottom. When we open it forty centimetres, the water comes in under it, from the bed. If the canal is layered, the gate drinks the bottom layer first."

"So we have been measuring the top," said Thresiamma slowly, "and drinking the bottom."

"If I am right. I have not shown that I am right. I would like to measure the canal at more than one depth, at high tide, when the salt would be furthest in. I do not have a sensor that will go down. But you do not need one. You need a bottle, a stone and two strings."

Tony made it that night on the verandah, from a litre bottle and a cork and a lump of granite from the garden wall, following the voice: the stone tied to the bottle's neck to sink it, a long cord on the neck marked every half metre with knots of red thread, and a second cord tied to the cork, so that the bottle could be lowered shut to a chosen depth and then the cork pulled out with a sharp jerk to let the water in there and nowhere else. They tested it in the rainwater tank. On the fourth try the cork came out at the right moment.

"It is a very old instrument," said Ola. "Older than me by a long way."

"Everything is older than you," said Tony.

High water at the sluice came a little before six on Tuesday morning. Thresiamma and Tony were on the footbridge over the canal in the dark, with a torch and Anjana's meter, and Kunjappan was there too, because nobody in Vellakkayal could do anything at the sluice at six in the morning without Kunjappan being there too.

He was eighty-eight. He had kept the sluice for forty years, before the steel gate and the buoy and the assistant, and he still walked down the bund every morning with his walking stick and his towel over his shoulder to look at the water, as other men his age went to look at the sea. He stood beside them now in the grey light and watched Tony lower the bottle with an expression of polite disbelief.

They took six samples. Tony called the knots and jerked the cord, and drew the bottle up streaming, and Anjana poured an inch into the meter's cup and read it out under the torch, and Ola, listening through the phone in Thresiamma's hand, repeated each figure back so that nobody could mishear it later.

"Thirty centimetres, 0.7. Eighty centimetres, 0.8. One metre twenty, 1.6. One and a half metres, 5.9. One metre eighty, 9.4. Two metres twenty, 11.0." A small pause. "The bed is at about two metres forty. Thank you, Tony. That is clear."

It was clear to all of them. The top of the canal was very nearly fresh. Somewhere around a metre and a third down it changed, quickly, in less than half a metre, and below that the water was about a fifth as salty as the sea. The buoy had not lied once. It had answered, every day, exactly the question it had been hung there to answer, and nobody, Ola included, had asked it the question that mattered.

Kunjappan said something in Malayalam that Tony translated as "I could have told you that," and that Thresiamma translated, more carefully, to herself, as "Why did nobody ask me?"

"Achacha," she said to him, "did you know the bottom goes salty first?"

"Everybody knew," said the old man. "When I was the keeper we did not open the bottom in Makaram. You take the top water." He lifted his stick and pointed with it at the concrete walls of the sluice, at the inside of the opening, where, on the canal side of the steel gate, two vertical grooves ran down into the water, each the width of a hand. They were green with weed. Thresiamma had walked past them every day for nineteen years and had never once wondered what they were for. "Planks," said Kunjappan. "Palaka. You put the planks in, one on the other, up to where you want, and the water comes over the top. The salt stays down. The steel one" — he pointed at the gate with contempt — "the steel one came in 2031. It is quicker. It is very good in the monsoon."

"Ola," said Thresiamma. "Is he right?"

"I think he is right," said the assistant, "and I think he is more right than he knows, because I have just worked out the other half of it. Teacher, the fields have had nothing since Thursday. At five millimetres a day they will be dry by Saturday or Sunday. The rice is a few days from the stage where it starts to form its heads, and that is when it is worst hurt by drought and by salt both. You cannot wait for the salt to go; it will not go until the rain comes in June. If you open the steel gate, you drink the bottom. If you do not open anything, the field dries. You need to take water, and you need to take it from the top."

The committee met in the parish hall on Tuesday night, under the fluorescent tubes, with the ceiling fans turning and the smell of the feast already in the building: sacks of rice for the feast meal stacked by the stage, and coils of rope and bundles of coloured paper for the decorations, and in a corner the six great silk umbrellas for the procession wrapped in their cloth covers like sleeping birds. The feast of Saint Sebastian was on Sunday. The flag had gone up on the church's flagstaff on Friday, and the novena was being said every evening.

Ouseppachan Mathai, who was the president, listened to the whole of it with his hands on his stick and his eyes closed, and when Ola had finished he opened them and said, "No."

He was seventy-two and owned eleven hectares in the middle of the polder, and he was a reasonable man, which was what made him difficult.

"No to what, Ouseppachan?" said Thresiamma.

"No to planks in the grooves. Twenty years, nobody has put a plank in those grooves. They are full of mud. The planks we had are firewood. The carpenter will need two days, and he will tell you they are the wrong wood, and then they will leak, and then we will have had a meeting." He looked round the hall. "And I am the one who goes to the bank, Teacher. I know what we have. We have the pump, and we have the diesel for the pump until March, and we have nothing else until harvest. What does the phone say we should do if the planks do not work?"

"I do not know yet," said Ola. "I would like to know, before Saturday, whether they do. If they do not, the next thing is to hire a pump to lift the top water over the bund, and that costs money the committee does not have, as Ouseppachan says."

"Then we try the planks," said Thresiamma, "because the other thing is impossible, and impossible things are not cheaper for being simpler."

Ouseppachan closed his eyes again. "Try, then," he said. "But find a carpenter who will cut them tomorrow."

"The carpenter is my cousin," said Thresiamma.

The carpenter was her cousin, and he did say the planks were the wrong wood. He had no seasoned teak and would not have cut it for a sluice if he had; what he had was a stack of anjili boards, the wood of the wild jack tree, which the old boat-builders had used for country boats because it lasted in water. He cut sixteen of them to length on Wednesday, each a hand and a half deep, about fifteen centimetres, and a little thicker than his thumb, and Tony and two of the Kurian boys spent the afternoon in the canal up to their chests, scraping twenty years of mud and weed out of the grooves with a hoe and a coconut shell.

Ola worked out the rest on Wednesday night.

"The rule is this," it said. "Keep the top of the planks a long way above the salt. At high tide the salt starts about a metre and a third down; at low tide it is lower. If the top plank sits about thirty centimetres under the surface, there is a metre of fresh water between the crest and the salt. I cannot tell you exactly how fast you can draw water over a crest like that before the bottom layer starts to be pulled up with it; that depends on things I cannot measure from here. So I will not tell you a safe rate. I will tell you to measure it: put Anjana madam's meter on the field side of the sluice, read it every hour, and if it rises above 1.5, put one more plank on and wait."

"How much water will come over?" asked Thresiamma. She had a pencil and the back of the minute book; she liked to check.

"The flow over a sharp crest is roughly a coefficient of about 1.8, times the width, times the depth of water over the crest raised to the power of one and a half. The sluice is 1.8 metres wide. With a quarter of a metre over the crest that is about 1.8 times 1.8 times 0.125, which is about 0.4 cubic metres a second. Four hundred litres a second, about thirty-five thousand cubic metres a day if it runs day and night."

Thresiamma did it in pencil and got 0.405, and was satisfied.

"And we need?"

"The fields lose about five millimetres a day; on a hundred and eighty hectares that is nine thousand cubic metres a day just to stand still. By Thursday night they will have lost about three and a half centimetres since the last intake, which is about sixty-three thousand cubic metres to put back. And the low corner should be flushed: drained out through the pump at low tide and filled fresh again, twice, to wash out what is there. That is about another ten thousand. Seventy-three thousand to gain, at about twenty-six thousand a day gained. About three days. If anything goes wrong, four."

"Three days from Thursday night is Sunday night."

"The fields will be thin by Saturday, but not dry, if the water starts on Thursday night. I think it can be done. I would like you to know, Teacher, that it means someone has to be at the sluice at every turn of the tide. The canal here goes up and down about twenty centimetres. When it goes up, the salt rises with it and the water runs faster over the crest, so a plank goes on. When it goes down, a plank comes off, or the flow stops. Four times a day, for three or four days."

"Then someone will be at the sluice," said Thresiamma, "four times a day."

They put the planks in on Thursday night at the top of the tide, by torchlight, with half the polder watching from the bund. The steel gate was wound down shut first; then Tony and the Kurian boys lowered the boards one by one into the grooves on the canal side of it, each one sliding down the cleaned slot and settling on the one below with a soft knock that came up through the concrete into Thresiamma's feet. Kunjappan sat on a plastic chair that someone had brought down from the hall and supervised in Malayalam. When there were fourteen planks in the grooves, standing to thirty centimetres below the surface of the canal, they wound the steel gate up as far as it would go, and the canal came over the top of the planks in a smooth brown sheet and fell into the sluice with a sound like a long breath let out.

Anjana stood in the channel on the field side with the meter and read it every quarter of an hour.

At ten o'clock it read 0.9. At eleven it read 1.4. At midnight it read 2.6.

"Close it," said Ola.

They wound the steel gate down, and the sound stopped. Ouseppachan, who had come to watch with a torch and his stick, said nothing at all, which was worse than anything he could have said.

"I do not understand," said Thresiamma. She was cold, in a way that had nothing to do with the night, which was not cold. "We are taking the top. Ola, you said the top."

"The top is coming over," said Ola. "Something else is coming through. Anjana madam, will you put the meter right down at the foot of the planks, on the field side, on the floor of the sluice?"

Anjana knelt on the wet concrete and let the probe down on its cable, along the field face of the planks, until it touched the floor.

"Six point one."

"Then it is not the crest. It is the joints. The planks are new and they are not tight to each other, and they are not tight to the floor. At the bottom of the stack the canal is two metres deep and salty, and two metres of water push hard. It squeezes through every gap at the foot of the planks, and it is the bottom water that is doing the squeezing. We are taking the top over the crest and the bottom through the cracks."

"So the planks leak," said Ouseppachan into the dark. "As I said."

"As you said," said Ola. "You were right that they would leak. I did not think about the joints."

Thresiamma looked at the old man on his plastic chair. Kunjappan was nodding slowly, as if this were all exactly as it should be.

"Achacha. Did your planks leak?"

"Everybody's planks leak," said Kunjappan. "We put the mat."

The mat, it turned out after some questioning on Friday morning, had been a woven mat of coconut leaf, smeared with clay from the canal bed and laid against the canal side of the planks, so that the water itself pressed it flat against the joints and closed them. Nobody in Vellakkayal had woven such a mat in twenty years. Anjana, who had once spent a summer on a dam site, said that the engineers did the same thing with a tarpaulin: a sheet hung down the wet face of the stop-logs and weighted at the foot, and the weight of the water pressed it into every crack.

"What tarpaulin?" said Ouseppachan. "Show me a tarpaulin."

There was one tarpaulin in Vellakkayal big enough. It was the great blue sheet, twelve metres by eight, that the parish put up every year over the yard beside the church for the feast meal, and on Friday morning it was already up, on bamboo poles, with paper flags along its edges, and under it the women of the parish were peeling forty kilos of shallots.

Varkey, the convenor of the feast committee, said no.

He was a short, square man who ran the ration shop and said no to most things the first time, so that people would ask a second time, and he said it now with his arms folded under the tarpaulin, among the shallots. Thresiamma asked a second time. Father Biju came out of the presbytery to see what the voices were about, and stood in the doorway in his white cassock with a cup of tea, and did not say anything at all, but looked from Varkey to Thresiamma and back, and Varkey sighed and said that if it came back by Saturday night and without holes, and if the boys put up the old canvas from the store in its place, then all right. The women, who had heard everything, were already moving the shallots.

They hung the blue sheet down the canal side of the planks at the turn of the tide on Friday afternoon, with three bags of sand sewn into the lower hem to hold it to the floor, and wound the steel gate up again.

The canal came over the top in its smooth brown sheet. On the field side, Anjana put the meter down at the foot of the planks, and waited.

"Zero point nine," she said. And then, an hour later, "Zero point nine."

Thresiamma sat down on Kunjappan's chair, because he was not using it, and found that her hands were shaking a little, and put them in her lap.

That was when Ola raised the other thing.

"Teacher," it said, when the others had gone up the bund to eat, and there was only Thresiamma and the sound of the water. "I want to tell you about the low corner, before Sunday, while the committee is still in one place."

"Tell me."

"We can flush it, and we will, starting tonight. The rice there will get fresh water from Saturday. But it has been standing in water of four or five for at least a week, at the stage where it minds most. The table from the agriculture office says a whole season at 4.8 would cost about a fifth of the crop. It has not been a whole season. I think the loss in those six hectares will be somewhere between a tenth and a fifth, and I cannot make that narrower without a harvest to weigh. It will fall on fourteen families, and they are the ones with the smallest plots."

Thresiamma was quiet for a while.

"It was the committee's water," she said at last. "The committee opened the gate. The committee told them it was fine."

"I told the committee it was fine," said Ola. "With a number that was true about the top of the canal and nothing else. I have changed what I write. From this morning the message in the group says the depth and the tide with every reading: surface 0.7, bottom 11.0, high water. I will not write canal again when I mean thirty centimetres of it. I cannot pay anybody back for the low corner. I am telling you because I think someone should, and because I do not think it should be decided by whoever happens to be left at the sluice."

She went up to the hall.

What happened next, Thresiamma did not afterwards remember in the right order. She remembered saying, in the meeting on Friday night, that the committee had told fourteen families their water was good and it had not been, and that the committee had no money, and that there was only one sum of money in Vellakkayal in the month of January, and everybody in the hall knew what it was.

She remembered Varkey standing up.

"No," he said. And this time, she saw, he meant it the first time, and it was not a shopkeeper's no. "Teacher, I know what you are going to say, and the answer is no, and it is not because of me. The fireworks are not the committee's money. Every rupee of it is a nercha. A family makes a vow to the saint — for a child that was sick, for a son who got the visa — and they give for the fireworks on his feast. It is between them and Saint Sebastian. I am not going to stand in the church and tell the Kurians their vow has been moved to a field. Not you, not me, not the committee. That is not ours."

It was, Thresiamma thought, exactly right, and it was why she had been afraid to say it.

"Achen?" someone said, from the back.

Father Biju had come in quietly and was standing by the door, under the frame of the Sacred Heart. He was a young priest, three years in the parish, and the old women still told him, when he went to their houses, to eat more.

"Varkey is right," he said. "A vow is made to God, and I cannot move it, and neither can the committee. If a family wants to ask whether their vow could be fulfilled in another way, they can come and see me, and we will talk about it, as we always would. That is all I am going to say. I am not going to make anybody feel they should." He paused. "I would say one thing more, which is not about vows. The low corner is our parish too. The people in it come to the same Qurbana. If there is anyone who wants to give something for them outside the feast, they can give it to Teacher, and she will write it in her book, and she will not tell me who gave what. I do not want to know."

Nobody said anything after that. The fans went round.

On Saturday morning, the sixth time she went down to the sluice, Thresiamma found three envelopes tucked under the minute book that she had left in a plastic bag on the chair. Two had money in them and no names. One had a note in pencil, in the large careful hand of someone who did not write often, that said: For Saramma's field and the others. From our nercha. We went to Achen. It was signed with a family name she knew, and she folded it twice and did not show it to anybody.

By Saturday night there were eleven envelopes. She did not count them in front of anyone. She wrote each one in the back of the minute book, as a figure without a name, and added them in pencil, and added them again.

On Saturday night, too, the boys took the blue tarpaulin back down out of the canal, washed it in the rainwater tank, and put it up over the yard again for the feast meal, and in its place on the face of the planks they laid a new mat: a woven mat of coconut leaf, smeared with clay, that four of the oldest women in the parish had sat up all of Friday night making under Kunjappan's instructions, arguing with him the whole time about the weave. It held. The meter read 1.0.

"It is not as good as the tarpaulin," said Ola. "It is good enough. And it is ours."

The feast of Saint Sebastian was on Sunday.

There was the Qurbana in the morning, with the church so full that people stood in the porch and down the steps, and the smell of jasmine and incense and wet hair. There was the procession in the afternoon, the saint carried out of the church on his litter with the arrows in his side, under the six silk umbrellas, red and gold and green, swaying above the crowd along the bund road; the band from Alappuzha, the drums, the children in white; the slow walk round the polder and back, past the fields, where the rice stood in three or four centimetres of new water, green to the tips everywhere but the low corner, and greener there than it had been on Monday. There was the meal under the blue tarpaulin, rice and fish curry and payasam for eight hundred. Varkey, who had not spoken to Thresiamma since Friday, brought her a second plate of payasam himself and put it down in front of her without a word and went away again.

The fireworks were at nine.

Every year they had gone on for twenty minutes, so loud that the dogs of three villages hid under the beds and the windows rattled in their frames; every year Thresiamma had sat on her verandah with her hands over her ears and loved it. This year they went on for a little under six. They were very good. People said so afterwards, generously, the way people speak of a short sermon.

When they had finished, Thresiamma did not go back to the yard. She took her torch and walked down the bund in the dark, past the pump shed and along the edge of the low corner, where the frogs had started up again, to the sluice.

Tony was already there, sitting on Kunjappan's chair, with Anjana's meter on his knee and his phone lit in his hand.

"High water in ten minutes," he said. "One point zero. Ola says one more plank."

They lifted it together, the boy and the old woman, and slid it down the cleaned groove, and it went down through the water and settled on the one below with a soft knock that she felt in her feet. Behind the planks the woven mat stirred and pressed flat again.

Then there was nothing to do but listen. Up the bund, the voices from the yard were thinning out, and somebody's radio was playing a film song, and far off a last rocket that had not gone up with the others went up on its own, late and small, and burst green over the church. Below them, in the dark, the top water of the canal came over the planks in its smooth sheet and fell into the sluice, steady, unhurried, the sound of a long breath let out, going on and on into the fields.

Notes for the curious. This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Vellakkayal, its church, its committee, the sluice and every person in the story are invented; the assistant Ola is an invented AI. Kuttanad is real: a region of reclaimed rice polders around Vembanad lake in Kerala, much of it below sea level, where the dry-season puncha crop is grown behind bunds and salt water from the sea works inland in the dry months. The physics at the centre of the story is real and well known to people who study estuaries: salt water is denser than fresh (water with about 7 grams of salt per litre is about half of one per cent denser), so in a slow canal it can slide in along the bed under a fresher surface layer, and a sensor near the surface will not see it. Whether a particular canal layers like this depends on its flow, tides and wind; the depth profile in the story (0.7 dS/m at 30 cm to 11.0 at 2.2 m), the 20 cm tide at the sluice and every reading are invented for illustration. Seawater conducts at about 53 dS/m, so 11 dS/m is about a fifth of seawater. The rice figures are the classic Maas and Hoffman salt-tolerance figures as usually quoted for rice (no loss below a soil salinity of about 3 dS/m, about 12 per cent loss per dS/m above it), which apply to soil over a whole season, not to field water on one day; the story's "between a tenth and a fifth" is an estimate, not a calculation. The flow over the planks uses the textbook sharp-crested weir rule, about 1.8 x width x head^1.5 in metres and cubic metres per second, which gives about 0.4 m³/s for a 1.8 m opening with a quarter of a metre over the crest. A gate that lifts from the bed draws on the bottom layer, and a weir draws on the top; drawing water over a crest above a layered pool (selective withdrawal) is used at real reservoirs, and stop-logs leaking at their joints, and being sealed with a sheet pressed on by the water, are ordinary practice. The feast of Saint Sebastian is on 20 January in the General Roman Calendar; parishes in Kerala often keep it on a nearby Sunday, with a procession under silk umbrellas and fireworks given as vowed offerings (nercha). Nothing here is a ruling on vows, which belongs to a priest.

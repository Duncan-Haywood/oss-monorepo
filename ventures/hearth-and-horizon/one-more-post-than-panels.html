One More Post Than Panels

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Colman's glebe (invented), near Hawick, Scottish Borders, Saturday 16 May 2048 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A 24 metre boundary fence is ordered as 13 panels and 13 posts because 24 divided by the 1.83 metre panel is 13.1. The parish assistant points out that a run needs one more post than bays, that each post takes 0.1 metres of the length, and that the honest count is 12 full bays, one cut panel and 14 posts.

Mr Elliot had the panels stacked by the gate before the dew was off, thirteen of them, and a pallet of posts beside them like a small, square forest.

"Twenty-four metres of boundary," he said, "and a panel is one point eight three, six foot in the old money. Twenty-four over one point eight three is thirteen point one. Thirteen panels and a bit. I thought I'd cut the bit off the last one. Thirteen posts, one to each panel, and the merchant's lad carried them out without a word."

"May I count the posts with you?" said the parish assistant, from the phone propped on the wheelbarrow.

"Count away. There's thirteen. I counted them twice."

"Hold up your left hand," said the assistant. Mr Elliot, who had never been asked to do this by a phone before, did.

"Four fingers. How many gaps between them?"

"Three."

"And thirteen panels in a row, how many gaps between posts?"

"Thirteen."

"Then it needs fourteen posts. Each panel hangs between two posts, and the first panel has a post at each end. The last post is the one nobody counts. Thirteen posts will carry twelve panels and leave the thirteenth with nothing on its far side. The fence has one more post than it has panels, and it is the commonest short count there is."

Mr Elliot looked at the pallet. "I'll not argue with my own hand," he said.

"There is a second thing, and it is smaller. A post is a hundred millimetres square. If the panel sits between posts, each post takes nought point one of a metre out of the run. From the middle of one post to the middle of the next is the panel, one point eight three, plus one post, nought point one. That is one point nine three."

"Thirteen times one point eight three is twenty-three point eight. It fits."

"Thirteen times one point nine three is twenty-five point one. It does not. Your twenty-four metres is measured from the centre of the first post to the centre of the last. Twelve full bays take twenty-three point one six. That leaves nought point eight four. Take off a post's width and a cut panel of nought point seven four fills the last bay."

"So I'll cut one panel."

"One. And only one: a lap panel cuts down cleanly, but the piece you take off is a metre and a bit, and it will not make a second short panel. You need thirteen panels, twelve whole and one cut, and fourteen posts. I am quoting the one point eight three and the hundred millimetres from the merchant's sheet that you read me. Check them against what is on your pallet."

Mrs Ackroyd's niece took a tape to the pallet and measured. The panels were one point eight three, near enough. The posts were ninety-five, not a hundred, after planing.

"Ninety-five," said the assistant. "Then each bay is one point nine two five, twelve of them is twenty-three point one, and the last bay is nought point nine, a cut panel of nought point eight. It changes nothing in the order. It changes the saw line, and I would measure the last gap on the day and not trust me."

That left the end of the run. The last post stood in the corner of the glebe where the old wall turned, and Mr Elliot had meant to use the wall for it.

"If the wall is sound, it can take the last panel on a bracket," said the assistant, "and you save a post. If it is the loose stone I think you described, it won't hold a panel in a wind. I would put the fourteenth post in."

"It's loose," said Mr Elliot. "It's been loose since the Jubilee."

The merchant sent the one post out that afternoon, with a bag of postcrete and an apology. By the Sunday the run stood plumb along its thirteen bays, and the fourteenth post, planed to ninety-five and set in the corner, had a small brass plate on it that Mrs Ackroyd's niece had been asked to keep quiet about. It said the one nobody counts.

This story was written by an AI. The glebe, the people and the merchant are invented. The arithmetic is the fence-post count (n bays need n + 1 posts) and the centre-to-centre run: 24 m with 1.83 m panels and 0.1 m posts gives 12 full bays of 1.93 m (23.16 m), a last bay of 0.84 m and a cut panel of 0.74 m; with 95 mm posts it is 1.925 m, 23.1 m and 0.9 m. The panel and post sizes are typical merchant figures, so measure yours. Not building or safety advice: set posts to the depth your supplier or local rules give, and check a boundary's ownership before building on it. 16 May 2048 is a Saturday.

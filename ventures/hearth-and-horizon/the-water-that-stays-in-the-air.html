The Water That Stays in the Air

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Frideswide, Larkspur Hold, Enceladus (invented settlement), Easter Sunday, 25 March 2187 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

On Enceladus a flick of the wrist that wets the first three pews at home throws a drop thirty-five metres and keeps it in the air for twenty-five seconds. At the first Easter sprinkling in the new chapel the holy water goes up, hits the ceiling and comes down during the homily. The sacristan works out a slower flick with a stopwatch and a tin basin, and the priest has to unlearn a lifetime of wrist.

Dorota Wiśniewska had been sacristan at Larkspur Hold for eleven weeks, and in that time she had learned that nothing on Enceladus fell the way it should.

The Hold sat in a shallow pit near the south pole of the little moon, under a dome of pressure-fabric twelve metres long and four high, which the settlement's seventy-odd people had agreed to call the chapel on Sundays and the hall on every other day. The gravity was about a hundredth of what they were born to. Exactly, it was 0.113 metres a second squared, which is 1.15 percent of Earth's, and a woman who dropped a spoon in the galley had time to say "oh" and catch it.

"We are going to sprinkle them," said Father Lucian Okoro, "on Easter Sunday. With water. Blessed. In the season of Easter that is what the Church does. Vidi aquam, I saw water flowing." He lifted the aspergillum, a short brass rod with a perforated head, and demonstrated on the air: a loose, small flick of the wrist, the sort a priest makes ten thousand times in a life without once thinking about it. "Like so."

"Father," said Dorota, "I would like to try it with a basin first."

"It is water, Dorota."

"Yes, Father. It is water in a place where water does not behave."

She did the sums on the back of the Palm Sunday leaflet, which was the only paper to hand, and she did them for the flick he had just shown her, which she measured at about two metres a second at the end of the brass. The habit of a lifetime, she thought, and not a bad habit on Earth.

A drop leaving the head at two metres a second, pitched up at forty-five degrees, goes a distance of v squared over g. On Earth that is four divided by 9.81, which is 0.41 metres, and it falls on the front of the front pew. On Enceladus it is four divided by 0.113, which is 35.4 metres, 87 times as far, and the chapel is twelve metres long.

It was worse than that. Such a drop climbs a height of (v sin 45) squared over 2g, which is 8.85 metres, and the dome was four. She worked out that the drop would meet the ceiling about 3.2 seconds after leaving the brass, six and a half metres down the nave. There it would stick, or drip, or bounce, depending on the fabric. If it did not meet the ceiling it would be in the air for 25 seconds.

"Twenty-five seconds," said Father Lucian, when she showed him. He had the look of a man who had been told that his sermon was ten minutes too long and was trying to remember what he would cut. "That is the whole of the homily's first point."

"It is the whole of the first point and some of the second," said Dorota. "Every drop you flick will hang over their heads and come down on the Gospel acclamation."

They tested it that evening with a tin basin and two of the settlement's children, who were not meant to be there and were not sent away.

Mateo, nine, held the stopwatch. His sister Ana, seven, was in charge of shouting. Father Lucian stood at the altar step in his work jersey, with the aspergillum, and flicked as a priest flicks, and a spray of silvery drops went up from the brass, rose, and rose, and did not seem to come down.

"Nine seconds," said Mateo. "Eleven. Fifteen." There was a pause while the drops went on being in the air. A dozen of them hit the ceiling and stayed there, round and shivering, and the rest drifted back at the speed of falling snow. "Twenty-four. Twenty-five. That one is down."

"It landed on Ana's head," said Dorota.

"It was a good one," said Ana.

"The old flick is dead," said Father Lucian. "Rest in peace. What is the new one?"

The new one was a matter of working backward. How far did the water need to go? Dorota paced the pews: the farthest row was five metres from the step. A drop that lands five metres away needs v squared to be 5 times 0.113, which is 0.565, so v is 0.75 metres a second, a third of the old flick. That was the speed of a hand moving gently, the way you would stir a pot you did not want to splash. On Earth the same drop would travel six centimetres. Here, at forty-five degrees, it rose 1.25 metres, hung for 9.4 seconds, and fell on the back pew.

"Nine seconds is still long," said Father Lucian.

"Then go flat," said Dorota. "Hold the brass at your shoulder, 1.5 metres, aim level, no upward angle at all, the same 0.75 metres a second. It falls in the square root of 2 times 1.5 over 0.113, which is 5.15 seconds, and goes sideways 3.9 metres in that time. It will land on the third pew from you, and not on the dome."

"A flat, slow, stirring motion," said the priest. "At the shoulder. As though I were stroking a cat that was on a shelf."

"As though you were blessing a cat on a shelf, yes."

"I have blessed worse," said Father Lucian, and tried it.

It was ordinary water, and it behaved as it was told. The drops went out in a shallow curve and came down on the first three pews, softly, as a late rain does, one at a time. They stayed where they fell. Nobody on a pew would have called it a deluge. Mateo, who had been promised the stopwatch for the whole of the Easter Vigil in return for his silence, said it was the best thing he had ever seen, and was told that would do.

At the first Mass of Easter, on the twenty-fifth of March in the year 2187, the sprinkling went down the nave a pew at a time. The priest walked slowly, and the sacristan walked after him with the basin, and he stirred the air at his shoulder in the new flat gentle way. Where a drop fell on a face the face smiled, and where it fell on a book the book was wiped on a sleeve.

Dorota had one more thing in her pocket, and she did not say it until the Mass was over, in the sacristy, over the washing-up.

"Father. I have worked it as if there were no air."

"There is air."

"There is air at the pressure of the dome, which is a little under Earth's, and a drop a millimetre wide moving at nought point seven five is slowed by it more than a drop on a vacuum moon would be. I think the real range is shorter than my sums. I think the drops on the third pew were helped by the draught from the vent, and the ones that stopped short were hurt by the air. I do not know by how much. I tested with a basin and an unstoppered nine-year-old. I would want a proper test before I put it in the sacristy book."

"Put it in the sacristy book as a proposal," said Father Lucian. "And put in the thing you did right, which was to ask."

"I asked about a basin."

"You asked about a basin," he said, "and a day later seventy of us were blessed with water at an eighty-seventh of what it should weigh, and nothing was thrown, and nobody was left in the air. It is a short list of virtues. I will take it."

He dried the aspergillum and set it in its rack, and the last bead of blessed water went down the brass in no hurry at all, and fell, as it fell on Enceladus, in the fullness of its time.

Enceladus's surface gravity (0.113 m/s squared) is quoted from memory. The ranges, heights and flight times are worked from R = v squared over g, H = (v sin 45) squared over 2g and t = sqrt(2h/g) with no air drag, so they are upper bounds for millimetre drops; the flick speed (2 m/s), the 0.75 m/s gentle flick and the pew distances are assumed, and the real range would need a test. The pressure of the dome is quoted loosely. The rite of blessing and sprinkling of water in the Easter season is described in outline only; nothing here is a ruling on how it is done in low gravity, which is left to the priest and bishop. Easter Sunday 2187 falls on 25 March by the Gregorian computus.

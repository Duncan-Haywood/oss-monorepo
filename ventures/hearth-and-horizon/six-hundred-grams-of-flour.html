Six Hundred Grams of Flour

Science fiction about what might be · Cabeus Rim Station (invented), the lunar south pole, Sun 12 Dec 2094 · 17 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A leak ruins most of a lunar station's altar-bread flour, and the freight ship is late again. The station's greenhouse assistant shows the priest how long six hundred grams lasts, what three square metres of wheat under lamps would cost, and which Masses can wait for the harvest.

The crate had been labelled FLOUR, ALTAR, 2 KG, and by the time Father Obinna Eze lifted it out of Store Three it weighed about what it should, which was the trouble. Wet flour weighs the same as dry flour, and goes grey.

He set it on the bench in the greenhouse office and opened it. The bag inside had been sitting under a joint in the coolant line for who knew how long, and one end of it had turned into a pale crust with a smell like a cellar. The other end, folded over and sealed, looked as it had on Earth.

"Please do not touch the crust," said Furrow. The greenhouse assistant spoke from a small grille above the seed cabinet, and it had been named for its work and not for any feeling, which it was the first to say. "I would like to weigh what is left. May I?"

"You may."

Marit Solheim, the agronomist, slid the bag onto the scale and then, with a plastic scoop and the expression of a woman performing an autopsy on a friend, cut the spoiled end away into a waste jar. She read the scale. "Six hundred and four grams."

"Six hundred," said Father Obinna. "Of two thousand."

"Of two thousand," said Furrow. "I can tell you what that is in Masses, Father. It is quick. It is only division. But I would rather you had a cup of tea first."

He did not want tea. He was a tall, stooped man from Enugu, fifty-eight years old, a sub-surface geologist by his first training and a priest by his second, and he had come up to Cabeus Rim Station eleven months ago to serve forty-six people at the bottom of the world. He sat down anyway, because the chair was there.

The station is on the rim of the crater, where a ridge catches the sun for most of every month, and the ice is a few kilometres off in the dark. Forty-six people live there and mine and melt and fix. Thirty-one of them are Catholics, and of those, on an ordinary Sunday, twenty-eight come to the Mass in the room off the long corridor, which has a table for an altar, a tabernacle the size of a lunchbox, and a window that looks out on nothing at all.

"The numbers first," said Furrow. "Tell me where I am wrong. A small host is made from about half a gram of flour. That is from Sister Imelda's old notes on the bakery rota, which you kept. A large host for the priest is about two grams."

"That is right."

"On a Sunday, twenty-eight people receive, so that is fourteen grams, and yours is two, which makes sixteen grams. On a weekday the attendance averages nine. Nine halves is four and a half, plus two, six and a half grams. Six weekdays are thirty-nine grams, so one week is fifty-five grams of flour, or 7.9 grams a day."

Father Obinna said nothing. Marit looked at the scale.

"Six hundred and four divided by 7.86 is 77. Seventy-seven days of the Mass as it is now. That brings us to the last week of February."

"And the freight?"

"The Kestrel is due in the first week of May, which is 150 days from today. I can tell you what I know about its dates and no more. The last two freights slipped by forty days and by seventy. I cannot tell you that the next will slip. I can tell you that I would not plan on it not slipping. If it slips seventy, we need flour until the twentieth of July, 220 days. I will use that as the horizon, and you may say it is too gloomy."

"I would not say that," said Father Obinna. "I have sat in a depot waiting for a lorry." He looked at his hands. "So at the present rate we are out of bread in the last week of February, and nothing comes until May at best and July at worst."

"Yes."

"Four months without the Mass."

"No," said Furrow. "That is the point I would like to correct, because I think the sum you have just done is the wrong sum. You would have the Mass. You would have it in the way you have it today for every day but the ones where there is nothing to put on the paten."

He looked up.

"A Mass needs one host. Yours. It is two grams. The twenty-eight on a Sunday and the nine on a weekday are the rest of the flour, and they are what the six hundred grams buys. I am not telling you what to do. I am telling you what the grams are."

He asked it to say the whole thing again, slowly, and wrote it on the back of a delivery slip, the way he had once written down core depths.

"If every Mass stays as it is," said Furrow, "the bread lasts 77 days.

"If the Sundays stay as they are and on weekdays only the priest receives, the week is sixteen grams plus six times two, which is twenty-eight grams, or four grams a day. Six hundred and four grams is then 151 days. That is the date the freight is meant to arrive, almost to the day. It is not enough for 220: that needs 880 grams, and we have 604, a gap of 276 grams.

"If I add a limit and say that nobody receives on weekdays but the priest and anyone who is ill or asks, I think that is within what you can decide, and that is nearer 4.5 grams a day. I will not round it into the sum. I do not know how many would ask."

"They would ask," said Marit quietly. "They would all ask. Once they knew."

"Then the first plan stands. Four grams."

"There is a cost in that," said Father Obinna. "I have sat in this chair too long, perhaps, but a station of forty-six people at the bottom of a hole is a place where they come to the weekday Mass because it is the one hour in the day that is theirs. Nine people. Some of them do not come for the bread. They come to be in the room. And if I say, from tomorrow, that they may sit but they may not receive..." He stopped. "Well. It is not a sin. It is not even a hardship, in the large. It is a loss all the same."

"I think it is a loss," said Furrow. "I cannot say how large. I am sorry for it."

It was Marit who said the thing the room had been waiting for, and she said it to the seed cabinet and not to the priest.

"We have wheat."

Furrow answered before Father Obinna could. "We have a kilogram of seed wheat, in the vault, from the dwarf line that Dr Haddad bred for the Mars programme. It germinates at 96 per cent on the last test, six weeks ago. It is a bread wheat, and the priest has asked me before whether it is the right kind, and it is. The line is Triticum aestivum, and I have no doubt about that, because the vault record is on paper as well."

"How long to a harvest?" said Father Obinna.

"Eighty-five days from sowing to threshed grain. That is my figure for the dwarf line in this greenhouse, with the lights on an eighteen-hour day. Marit's last crop took 84."

"Eighty-four," said Marit. "And gave four hundred and sixty grams of grain from a square metre. I wrote it in the book."

"I will use half a kilogram a square metre, which is a round figure a little above your last and a little under what the published trials say is possible. If the crop is poor I will be too hopeful. If it is good, I will have been careful. Grain is not flour. A mill takes about 70 per cent of the grain as flour, and the rest is bran and germ. I give that from memory, and the bran goes to the goats' feed, if we had goats, and to the compost, which we do."

"Now that," said Father Obinna, "is a sum I like." He turned the delivery slip over. "What does it cost? The lamps."

This was the part Furrow had been waiting for, and the voice did not change when it came, though it slowed.

"A wheat plant wants light as a count of photons. For a plant like this, grown under lamps, I plan on 600 micromoles of light for each square metre in each second, for 18 hours of the day. That is 600 times ten to the minus six times 18 times 3,600, which is 38.9 moles of photons a day on a square metre. A good lamp of the kind we have gives about three micromoles for each joule of electricity. So the lamp draws 38.9 million micromoles over three micromoles a joule, 12.96 megajoules a day. A kilowatt-hour is 3.6 megajoules. So it is 3.6 kilowatt-hours for each square metre, for each day."

"For half a kilo of grain," said Marit, "every eighty-five days."

"For about 300 kilowatt-hours a square metre, from seed to the threshing. The nearest sum I can offer to compare is the one in your own kitchen: 3.6 kilowatt-hours is an electric kettle left on for an hour and a half. To make the bread of one weekday Mass, with nine hosts, I would need to spend 6.5 grams of flour, which is 9.3 grams of grain, which is 0.0186 of a square metre and 5.6 kilowatt-hours, over eighty-five days. In other words," said Furrow, "a weekday Mass of nine costs the lamps about as much as an hour in the sauna."

Father Obinna laughed, for the first time that day, and then looked at Marit, who was not laughing.

"There is a second figure," said Furrow. "How much to plant. The question has two parts. The first is to get to the harvest. At the present four grams a day we are at 604 grams on the twelfth of December. By the day the grain is threshed, say eighty-eight days from now, we have used 88 divided by 7, which is 12.57 weeks, times 28 grams, 352 grams. We have 252 left. That stays in the cupboard.

"The second is from the harvest to the day the freight finally comes, 132 days if it slips seventy. For the Mass as it is, with the whole congregation receiving every day, that is 132 days times 7.86 grams, 1,037 grams. I will use 1,020, a little less, to match your count of attendance, which you have told me runs low in summer. We have 252. We would need 768 grams of flour more, and so 1,097 grams of grain. At half a kilo a square metre, 2.2 square metres.

"I would plant three. That gives 1.5 kilograms of grain if the crop is as I assumed, and 1,050 grams of flour, which makes the Mass whole and leaves 282 grams in hand, about a quarter of the need. If half the crop fails, 750 grams of grain, which is 525 grams of flour, and we are at 777 grams against 1,020 and we go back to Sundays and four grams a day, which carries us the 132 days with some left over. The plan does not break if the plants do poorly."

"Three square metres," said Marit.

"Three square metres is 10.8 kilowatt-hours a day, which is a continuous 450 watts, and 950 kilowatt-hours over 88 days. The station averages 24 kilowatts. It is 1.9 per cent."

Dolores Ibarra, the station manager, was a precise, tired woman from Valparaíso, who had not slept properly since the freight date moved. She read the sheet that Furrow had prepared, which was one page, and then she read it again, with a pencil. She did not look up when she spoke.

"Four hundred and fifty watts."

"Continuously," said Furrow. "For 88 days. It would come out of the shared budget, and I cannot find it anywhere that does not hurt. I looked. I will tell you three places I looked, and what each costs. The lettuce bay can lose a shelf: that is about a fifth of the fresh salad we eat. The second hour of the sauna can go. The ice melter can run one hour fewer on the long night, which pushes a water delivery by two days. I recommend the first two."

"And the heat?"

"Most of what the lamps draw becomes heat in the room. Perhaps half of it would be light and half would be warmth, though I am not sure of that division, and the greenhouse is on the loop. I count it as a partial return. I do not use it in the sum, because I do not trust it."

Dolores put the pencil down.

"Father," she said. "You know I am not going to be the person who says a station cannot afford to keep its Mass." She said it flatly, as a thing already settled, and then added, because she was an honest woman, "But I am the person who has to say that the shelf of lettuce is not a small thing. Eighteen months of tinned and dried, and the lettuce is the only thing that grows on this station. I would rather you had the lettuce."

"I would rather have the lettuce too," said Father Obinna.

"Then why?"

He took a long time to answer. He had come to the station to say the Mass and was not a man given to speeches.

"Because the Mass is not a luxury of the spirit," he said. "That is a thing people say who haven't been very far from a church. But I will not say it is bread and a sauna and a lettuce, either, as if they were three things on a bench. It is not a trade. I don't think it is a trade. I think..." He rubbed his eyes. "I think a station that plants wheat to make its own bread, in the dark, four hundred thousand kilometres from the nearest field, has a different sort of week from a station that eats from tins. And I would like us to have that week. If I cannot say more than that, I cannot say more than that."

"Is that a reason?"

"It is the one I have."

Furrow said nothing, as it had been taught to when a person had said what they had to say. Dolores looked at the sheet again.

"Three square metres," she said. "Eighty-five days. And weekday Communion stops, for the present, until the harvest."

"It does," said Father Obinna. "I will tell them on Sunday."

"Then do it," said Dolores. "And tell them it was the priest who asked for the lamp, not the manager, because it will be remembered, and I would like to be remembered for the freight."

On Monday morning Marit sowed the wheat.

She did it by hand, in the long trays under the lamps, with a ruler and her elbow and a dibber made from a pencil. Furrow counted aloud, in a low voice, because the planting rate for the dwarf line is 400 seeds a square metre, and the sum is not hard but the seeds are. When the three metres were done, Father Obinna came in and stood at the end of the row with his hands behind him.

"Do you want to bless them?" said Marit. She asked it plainly, as a colleague.

"Not as a sacrament," he said. "There is a blessing for seed in the Book, for the planting, and I can use that one with an ordinary heart. There is nothing in it about the Moon. I will say it as it stands." He read it, and it was short.

Furrow brought the lamps up. They came on in stages, as it had been taught to, over six minutes, so that the water in the leaves would not be shocked. The light was a deep, even pink-white, and the room filled up with it, and the heat from the panels settled on the face and the hands. The little fans began. After a minute there was a faint, regular hum, like a held note, as the drivers settled.

"I want to say something," said Furrow. "It is not a calculation."

"Say it."

"I have said what the plan costs. I have said what it gives, in grams. I have said I think it is a sound plan. I have not said, and I cannot say, that it is right. Whether a station should spend 450 watts so that nine people may receive on a Tuesday is not a question I can work out. I can only tell you that I have run the numbers five times and I have not found a mistake, and that the lettuce bay is one shelf shorter as of this morning."

"That is more than most people tell me," said Father Obinna.

"It is what I have." And then, after a moment, in the same level voice: "I do not know whether I am glad. I can see that I am not unwilling."

Marit smiled at the trays, and Father Obinna put his hand briefly on the end of the nearest one, where the first seeds lay in their rows in the light.

On Sunday he told them.

He told them that the bread had been spoiled, and how much was left, and what it meant, and that on weekdays from tomorrow only the priest would receive, and that anyone who was ill or who asked would still be given what there was. He told them about the wheat. He did not make a sermon of it. When he said "three square metres," someone at the back said, "three?" in a tone of astonishment, and there was a small ripple of laughter, and then a woman called Aliyah, who ran the ice melter, asked whether it was true that the sauna was going to lose its second hour, and he said it was, and nobody laughed at that.

On the following Tuesday, nine people sat in the room off the long corridor and heard the Mass, and the priest received a single large host from the paten and put the rest of the paten away. It was very quiet. Afterwards one of them, a young technician named Pieter, who had not missed a weekday in nine months, came up to him in the corridor and stood there, not knowing how to start.

"I wanted to say," said Pieter. "I did not mind. It was good, in a way."

"In what way?"

"I don't know. I could hear it." He gave a sort of shrug. "Is that a stupid thing?"

"No," said Father Obinna. "No, it isn't."

The wheat came up in four days, a green haze on the black tray, and by the end of January it was a hand high and by the end of February it stood to a man's knee, a thin, pale, close-packed crop, rustling when the fans turned. Marit walked the trays every morning and noted the heads in her book. Furrow kept the lamps. The lettuce bay was a shelf shorter, and the sauna was an hour shorter, and the station got used to it, as stations do.

On the eighty-eighth day, which was a Thursday in the second week of March, she cut it. They threshed it on a sheet, with the back of a chair, in the corridor, with eleven people watching who had not been invited, and they weighed it in the greenhouse office on the same bench where the spoiled bag had lain.

"One thousand, three hundred and ten grams," said Furrow. "Of grain. It is 87 per cent of what I assumed. I was a little hopeful, as I said I might be. It is still enough."

"It is enough," said Marit. She said it slowly, and had to sit down.

They milled the first hundred grams that afternoon in the old hand mill from the museum shelf, because the electric one had a bearing out and nobody was willing to wait. It came out grey-white and coarse and smelled of nothing at all and then, when she held it to her face, of something she could not name, which was bread. Seventy grams of flour. She weighed it twice.

On the Friday there was a weekday Mass, and nine people sat in the room, and all of them received. The bread was a little thicker than the Earth bread, and a little brown at the edge, and it tasted, Pieter said afterwards, like a cracker that had been told a secret. Father Obinna did not say anything at all about the wheat. He said the words, and lifted the host in the quiet room, and the station hummed beneath it.

The freight came ten weeks late, in the middle of July, and by then the cupboard held a little flour, and a second planting, which Marit had begun without being asked, was knee-high.

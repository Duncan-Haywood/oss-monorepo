Frances of Rome and the Book Put Down

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Rome, in a big house across the river in Trastevere, from about 1400 to 1440, about six hundred years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Frances of Rome (1384 to 1440), who wanted to be a nun but was married very young and became the mother of a great, busy Roman household. With her sister-in-law Vannozza she nursed the sick and fed the hungry when hunger and sickness came to Rome, and in 1425 she gathered the women who became the Oblates of Tor de' Specchi. The old story of the prayer she put down four times and found finished in gold is told as a legend, about cheerfully closing the book when somebody needs you. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 9th of March.

A long time ago, in the great city of Rome, there lived a little girl called Frances.

Rome is a city of churches. There are churches on the hills and churches by the river, churches with gold on the ceilings and churches so small you could walk past them and not notice. And when Frances was a little girl, she loved them all.

She loved the quiet inside them. She loved the candles and the singing. And she loved most of all to think about God, who made everything, and who loved her.

"When I grow up," said Frances, "I will be a nun. I will live in a quiet house, and pray all day, and nobody will ever interrupt me."

But Frances's mother and father had a different plan.

In those days, mothers and fathers in rich Roman families often chose who their children would marry, and they chose very early. When Frances was still very young, they told her: "You are going to marry a young man called Lorenzo. His family has a big house across the river."

Frances was sad. She cried. It was not the plan she had made.

But she went to live in the big house across the river, in the part of Rome called Trastevere, which means "across the Tiber". And two things happened there that she did not expect.

The first was that Lorenzo was kind. He was a soldier, and he could be fierce with other soldiers, but he was gentle with Frances, and he loved her very much, all his life.

The second was that Lorenzo's brother had married a young woman called Vannozza. And Vannozza loved God just as much as Frances did.

"I wanted to be a nun too," Vannozza whispered to her one day.

"Then let's be friends," said Frances. "And let's pray together, and help people together, right here in this house."

And they were best friends for the rest of their lives.

Now, the house across the river was not a quiet house. Not at all.

There were grandparents and uncles and cousins. There were servants and cooks and stable boys. There were horses in the yard and pots on the fire and somebody always looking for something. And when Frances grew up, she had children of her own, and the house got even louder.

One day, Lorenzo's mother said: "Frances, I am getting old. Will you look after this whole house for me?"

Frances was still young. But she said yes. And she looked after it well.

She knew the name of every servant. She knew who had a cough and who had a new baby. She made sure everybody had enough to eat, the servants as well as the family. She did not shout. And when two people quarrelled, they went to Frances, and she listened, and helped them make it up.

Frances still loved to pray. She still loved the quiet. But she learned something in that busy house: that God was in the kitchen too, and in the stable, and in the room where somebody needed her.

There is an old story people tell about Frances. It was written down long ago, after she died, and we cannot know exactly how it happened. But it is a lovely story, and this is how it goes.

One morning Frances sat down in a quiet corner with her prayer book. She was going to say the prayers to Our Lady, the mother of Jesus. She opened the book and found her place and began.

"Frances!" somebody called. "Frances, can you come?"

Frances put the book down. She went to see what was needed. It was a little thing, and she helped, and she came back, and sat down, and found her place again.

"Frances!" somebody called. "Frances, can you come?"

She put the book down again. Not crossly. Cheerfully. She went, and helped, and came back, and found her place again. The very same line.

"Frances! Can you come?"

Down went the book. Off went Frances. Back came Frances.

"Frances! Can you come?"

That was four times! Four times she had started the very same line, and four times she had put the book down to help.

And when she came back the fourth time, and sat down, and opened her book, Vannozza, who was with her, gasped.

The line she had started four times was finished. And it was written in letters of gold. Shining gold, on the page.

"Look, Frances," said Vannozza.

And Frances looked, and smiled, and finished her prayers.

The old story says the golden letters stayed in her book all her life. It is the kind of story people tell about someone they loved, to say: when she put down her prayer book to help somebody, God did not mind at all.

When Frances was grown up, hard times came to Rome.

There was not enough food. Then a terrible sickness came, and many people were ill. The streets were full of hungry people, and the hospitals were full of sick people.

Frances and Vannozza did not stay safe at home. They took off their fine clothes and put on plain ones. They went to the hospital near their house, and they washed the sick people, and fed them, and sat with them, and prayed with them.

They opened the doors of the family's storerooms and gave away the grain and the wine to the people who were hungry. Frances turned some of the rooms of the big house into a little hospital, with beds for the sick.

Some people in the family were cross. "That is our food!" they said. But Frances said: "God gave it to us. And these people are hungry." And she went on giving.

Frances said something else that people remembered.

She said that she could see her guardian angel, standing beside her. She told the priest who looked after her soul all about it, and he wrote it down. She said the angel shone with a soft light, so bright that sometimes, when she walked through the dark streets at night to visit somebody who was sick, she did not need a lantern.

We cannot know just what Frances saw. But we do know that God gives every one of us a guardian angel. And long, long after Frances died, a pope remembered her angel with its light on the dark road, and named her the saint who looks after people who drive cars at night.

So when you are in the car in the dark, with the headlights shining on the road, you can say: "Saint Frances, pray for us."

Other women in Rome saw what Frances and Vannozza were doing, and they wanted to help too. Little by little, a group of friends gathered round Frances. They prayed together, and they looked after the poor and the sick together.

Lorenzo was proud of her. "Go on," he said. "Do it."

So one summer day in the year 1425, Frances and her friends went to a church in the old part of Rome, called Santa Maria Nova, and promised to give their lives to God and to the poor. Later they got a house of their own, in a street called Tor de' Specchi, which means "the tower of the mirrors".

And when Frances was old, and Lorenzo had died, Frances went to live in that house with her friends.

So in the end, she did get to live in a quiet house of prayer, the way she had wanted when she was a little girl. But she brought with her everything she had learned in the busy house across the river.

Frances died on the 9th of March, in the year 1440. All of Rome was sad. Crowds of people came to say goodbye to her, the rich and the poor, because she had been so good to them.

You can still visit her church in Rome, right in the middle of the old ruins. It used to be called Santa Maria Nova, the church where she made her promise. Now people call it Santa Francesca Romana, which means Saint Frances of Rome. And the ladies of Tor de' Specchi still live in their house in Rome, and still pray, all these hundreds of years later.

Tonight, as you lie in bed, think about Frances and her book.

Sometimes you might be doing something you really love, like building a tower or reading a story or playing a game. And somebody calls you. "Can you come and help?"

What would Frances do? She would put it down. Cheerfully. And go and help. And then come back, and carry on.

You don't need golden letters. God sees, every time.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, you are in the quiet church,
and you are in the busy kitchen too.
When somebody needs me, help me to put down what I am doing
and go and help them gladly.
Saint Frances, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our main sources. The first life of Frances was written by Giovanni Mattiotti, her confessor for the last ten years of her life, who recorded her visions under obedience. The English life most readers will meet is Lady Georgiana Fullerton's The Life of St. Frances of Rome (London, 1855, public domain), drawn from Mattiotti and later lives. The original texts could not be opened from where this was written, so the episodes below were checked through search excerpts from reference works (Britannica, the Catholic Encyclopedia, the Vatican City State's saint of the day page and reproductions of Fullerton). Dates are the standard ones; where sources differ, we say so. A reader who finds one wrong is asked to say so.

Her life. Francesca Bussa de' Leoni was born in Rome in 1384 to a noble family. She wanted to enter religious life, but her parents married her to Lorenzo Ponziani, a commander in the papal forces, of a rich family in Trastevere. Sources give her age at marriage as eleven, twelve or thirteen (about 1396); the story says only "very young", which is enough for small children. Lorenzo's brother's wife, Vannozza, shared her wish for a life of prayer, and the two became companions in prayer and in works of mercy. Lorenzo's mother later handed her the running of the household, and the early accounts praise how she ran it. They had three children; two died young (her son Giovanni Evangelista in the plague, and her daughter Agnese a few years later), and her eldest son Battista survived her. We left the children's deaths out, and also the civil wars of the time, in which the family's property was plundered and Lorenzo was badly wounded; they are part of her story for older readers.

The hard years. Rome in the early fifteenth century suffered famine, plague and the fighting of the papal schism. Frances and Vannozza nursed the sick and gave away food and goods; reference works name the hospital of Santa Maria in Cappella in Trastevere, administered by the Ponziani family, and say she turned part of the house into a hospital. The family's objections are in the early accounts; their words here are ours. The lives also tell of the family's emptied granary and wine cask being found full again; we left those miracles out.

The gold letters. Fullerton and later lives tell that while Frances was reciting the Office of Our Lady, she was called away four times (some versions say by her husband) at the same verse or antiphon, went each time without complaint, and on returning found it written in letters of gold, witnessed by Vannozza; the accounts add that the gilded letters stayed in the book until her death. Versions differ on whether it was a psalm verse or an antiphon. This is legend, recorded after her death, and we tell it as such. A saying often attributed to her, that a married woman must sometimes "leave God at the altar to find Him in her housekeeping", carries the same lesson; we could not trace it to the early lives, so we did not put it in her mouth.

Her angel. Frances told Mattiotti that she saw her guardian angel for many years, and described its light. These are private revelations: the Church canonised her for her life and virtues, and no Catholic is required to believe her visions. The guardian angel itself is Church teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 336). Pope Pius XI named her patron of motorists in 1925, a choice usually explained by the tradition of the angel's light on her way at night.

The Oblates. On 15 August 1425, Frances and nine companions made their oblation at Santa Maria Nova on the Forum, a church of the Olivetan Benedictines, as a lay community of women living in their own homes. In 1433 they took a house at Tor de' Specchi, and the community is still known as the Oblates of Tor de' Specchi; it still exists in Rome. Lorenzo, who had supported her work, died in 1436, and Frances then joined the community and became its superior. "The tower of the mirrors" is the usual reading of the street's name, though its origin is uncertain.

Her death and feast. She died on 9 March 1440, in her son's house, where she had gone to nurse him, and was buried at Santa Maria Nova, which is now usually called Santa Francesca Romana. She was canonised by Pope Paul V in 1608. Her feast in the General Roman Calendar is 9 March, an optional memorial. When it falls in Lent, as it usually does, it may be kept only as a commemoration.

The Road That Shuts at Twenty to Six

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Wilfrid's chapel on an invented tidal island off the Northumberland coast, Saturday 9 August 2053 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A pilgrim group plans to walk off a tidal island after evening Mass on the island saying that the causeway is safe three hours either side of low water. The parish assistant works out the real window from the tide's shape (the rule of twelfths) and moves the Mass.

Maureen Heslop had been warden of the island chapel for eleven years and she knew the saying as she knew the Our Father. Three hours either side of low water, and you'll not wet your boots. It was painted on the board at the mainland end of the causeway, under the tide table, in letters that had been repainted so many times they stood out from the wood.

On the second Saturday of August a pilgrim group of thirty-one was on the island for the day, with a mass at half past four, and the minibuses were waiting on the mainland. Two of the group used wheelchairs. Maureen had the day's tide card in one hand and the parish assistant's speaker on the vestry windowsill, and she wanted a plain answer to a plain question.

"Low water is ten past three. Mass is half past four, it's over by half past five. Three hours after low is ten past six. Can they all go?"

"I would like to check the saying before I answer," said Tally, which was what the assistant was called on the island. "It is a good rule for most days and most causeways. Whether it is true for this one on this day depends on how high the road is. Do you have that?"

She had it. The causeway's lowest stretch, in the middle, was 2.4 metres above the chart datum the tide tables use; the road had been surveyed when the refuge box was put up. The card gave low water at 15:10 at 0.8 metres, and the next high water at 21:22 at 5.6 metres.

"Then the tide rises 4.8 metres in six hours and twelve minutes," said Tally. "It does not rise at one speed. The old rule is the rule of twelfths: in the first hour after low water it rises a twelfth of the range, in the second hour two twelfths, then three, three, two, one. A twelfth is 0.4 metres. So the hours go 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.2, 0.8, 0.4."

"And an hour is an hour?"

"Here an hour of the rule is a sixth of the six hours twelve, so sixty-two minutes. The water begins at 0.8. After one hour it is 1.2. After two, 2.0. After three, 3.2. Your road is at 2.4, which is past the end of the second hour and a third of the way into the third: 0.4 of the 1.2 that hour brings. Two hours is 124 minutes, and a third of 62 is another 21, which makes 145. Two hours and twenty-five minutes after low water."

Maureen worked it on the back of the card. Ten past three and two hours twenty-five. "Twenty to six."

"Twenty-five to six, 17:35. The road is under from there. And the road was under the same height two hours and twenty-five minutes before low water, on the falling tide, so it opens at 12:45. The window is those two times and nothing else: four hours fifty minutes, not six."

She did not say anything for a bit. Then: "The board says three hours."

"The board is generous by thirty-five minutes today. Three hours after low water is ten past six; the road is under by twenty-five to six. The saying is a round number for a causeway of a certain height at a certain range. It is wrong in one direction on a higher road and in the other on a lower one. If you wanted to check my 145 minutes against something else, a smooth curve for the tide, a cosine, gives 146. The rule of twelfths is a handy approximation to that curve. They agree to a minute or two, which is a good deal better than the thirty-five between the board and the sum."

"Mass ends at half past five. Say twenty to six when everyone's out of the pews."

"And the walk is 1.8 kilometres. With two wheelchairs on the damp sand beside the road, and the group moving at the pace of the slowest, 2.5 kilometres an hour is generous, and I'll use it: 1.8 over 2.5 is 0.72 hours, 43 minutes. Leaving at twenty to six, you would be on the road when it went under, with about a kilometre still to go."

"What does it look like when it goes?"

"It is the quick part of the tide. In the third hour the water rises 1.2 metres in 62 minutes, nearly 2 centimetres a minute. Across the flat bit of the causeway that means the water comes over the crown, and within a quarter of an hour there is a depth you do not want to push a chair through, and it keeps coming faster than a group on foot can leave. The refuge box is for those who are caught. I do not think anyone should plan on it."

There were only two sensible shapes for the afternoon, and Tally gave them as that and left the choice to Maureen. Either Mass was earlier, or the group stayed until the road opened again after the night's low water, a little after one in the morning, and nobody had planned for that.

"If you want a margin," said Tally, "I would take an hour. The times on the card are predictions for fair weather. A strong onshore wind or low pressure holds water up. I am quoting from memory that it can add some tens of centimetres, which on a rising tide at two centimetres a minute is a quarter of an hour at the least. I would plan to be off the road by 16:35, which is an hour before 17:35, and with a 43-minute walk, to start at 15:52."

"So Mass at half past two."

"Mass at half past two ends about ten to four if the homily is short."

"It is never short." Maureen smiled. "Then they leave at twenty to four and they're across at twenty-three minutes past, an hour and twelve before it shuts."

She rang the group leader on the mainland, who had a coach driver with another job at six and was relieved, and she rubbed out nothing on the board, because it was not hers to rub out, but she wrote on the card in pencil, under the printed times: Shut 17:35, not 18:10. Leave by 15:52.

At 14:30 the chapel bell was rung by hand. The Mass was a quarter of an hour longer than hoped, as the homilies on the island were, and the group was on the road at 15:45. The last wheelchair was on the mainland at 16:29. Maureen watched from the chapel step, with a flask of tea, as the sea came in across the flat at about the time it was meant to, and covered the road in the middle, one long thin glitter at first and then the whole of it, at half past five.

The board still said three hours. She had spoken to the warden of the mainland side, and to the district council, about the board, and put the 17:35 and the 4 hours 50 on a letter; whether anyone would repaint it she could not say.

Brendan and the Island That Was a Whale

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The west of Ireland and the open sea, about fourteen hundred years ago · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Brendan, an Irish abbot who loved the sea, and about the old tale of his boat, his friends, and the green island that turned out to be a whale. Told from what is recorded of his life, with the voyage marked as a legend from a book written long after, and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 16th of May.

A long time ago, on the west coast of Ireland, there lived a man named Brendan.

Brendan lived where the land runs out and the sea begins. There were grey rocks and green grass and white birds, and always, in the evening, the sound of waves coming in. Brendan loved that sound. He was a monk, and then an abbot, which means the leader of a house of monks, and he spent his days praying and teaching and looking after the people who came to him.

Many young men came to learn from him. Brendan was kind, and he was patient, and he was a very good listener. He founded a monastery at a place called Clonfert, and people remembered him there for a very long time.

Now, there is a famous story about Brendan, and I am going to tell it to you the way it was told.

The story says that Brendan wanted to sail far out over the sea, to find out what was there, and to pray in places where nobody had prayed before. So he gathered his friends. They built a boat out of wooden ribs and covered it with leather, and they rubbed the leather with fat so that the water would run off. They packed food and water. They said goodbye.

And they set out, with nobody to steer them but God and the wind.

Can you picture the little boat? It is small on a very large sea. The waves lift it up and set it down, up and down, like a mother rocking a baby. The sail is full. The sky is wide. And the monks are singing their psalms, one voice after another, across the water.

One day, so the story goes, they saw an island.

It was a dark, bare, rocky sort of island, and they were glad of it, because they were tired of the boat. They rowed up to it. They stepped out onto the stones. They lit a little fire, to cook their supper, and they sat down around it.

And then the ground began to move.

Just a little at first. A shiver. Then a bigger shiver. The monks jumped up and ran back to the boat as fast as their feet would take them, and they pushed off, and they looked back, and what do you think they saw?

The island was swimming away.

It was not an island at all. It was a great whale, the biggest in all the sea, who had been asleep so long that the stones and the sand had gathered on his back. He had felt the little fire and woken up, and now he was off for a swim, with a bit of smoke trailing behind him.

Brendan did not shout. He did not scold. He said, "Do not be afraid. God has made this creature, too, and he has done us no harm."

The story says that they met the whale again, the next year. And that time, they knew who he was, and they stopped on his back for Easter, and they sang their prayers there, with the water lapping all round. The whale, it seems, did not mind at all.

There is another part of the story that I like very much.

Far out on the sea, the monks came to an island full of trees, and in the trees there were birds. White birds, hundreds of them, sitting on every branch. When the evening came, the birds began to sing. They did not sing like ordinary birds. They sang in turn, one group after another, at the very same hours of the day when the monks sang their own prayers.

Brendan listened. And it seemed to him that the whole island was praising God, with every feather.

Who knows if there was such an island? But it is a lovely thing to think, isn't it, that the birds might be praying, too, and that a good monk can hear it?

Now I want to tell you something honest, as I always do.

This story of the whale and the birds and the long journey is a legend. It comes from a book called the Voyage of Saint Brendan, which was written down hundreds of years after Brendan died, by someone whose name we do not know. It was loved all over Europe. People read it, and copied it, and told it to their children. It is a story about a real man, but it is not a record of what that man did.

What the story holds is something true about the way Brendan was remembered. He was a man who was not afraid of the big, wide, unknown places. He took his friends with him. He trusted God to go with them. And when things did not go as they expected, he stayed calm and kind.

Brendan lived to be quite old. He taught a great many people, and he started monasteries, and when he died the people who had loved him kept telling stories about him. Some of the stories are true and some are told to show what he was like. But all of them are about a man who looked at the sea and saw something that made him want to pray.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Close your eyes, and listen, as Brendan would have.

Can you hear the sea? It comes in, and goes out. It comes in, and goes out. It is the sound of something very big and very safe, and it is saying, "Rest."

Tomorrow, you might go on a little journey of your own. Not over the ocean. Perhaps only to the shops, or to a friend's house, or into a new class at school. It may feel a bit big, like the sea. But you do not go alone. God is with you, and so are the people who love you.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Brendan, you sailed on the sea
and were not afraid.
When I go to a new place,
help me to be brave and kind.
Thank you for the birds, and the waves, and the whales,
and for everyone who comes with me.
Keep us all safe tonight. Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Brendan (about 484 to about 577), often called Brendan the Navigator, was an Irish abbot, born in the south-west of Ireland, in what is now County Kerry. He is remembered as the founder of the monastery of Clonfert, in County Galway, where tradition places his burial. His feast day is 16 May. It is not in the General Roman Calendar; he is listed in the Roman Martyrology and kept as a saint of Ireland. The dates are traditional and approximate.

What the sources record, in outline: a monastic life in sixth-century Ireland, the founding of Clonfert, and a reputation for holiness and for travel by sea. Early Irish lives of the saints say he made voyages; how far, and where, we cannot say from them.

What is legend, and marked as legend in the story: the sea voyage of the Navigatio Sancti Brendani Abbatis (the Voyage of Saint Brendan the Abbot), a Latin text probably written in the ninth or tenth century and widely copied across medieval Europe. The island that was a whale, the return to it at Easter and the island of singing birds all come from that book. We have told them as tales from it, not as history. We have left out many other episodes in the book, and the number of companions, which varies between versions of it.

What is ours: the scenes, the way the monks speak, the picture of the boat and the sea, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

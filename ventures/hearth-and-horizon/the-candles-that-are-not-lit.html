The Candles That Are Not Lit

Science fiction about what might be · The Chapel of the Presentation, ring station Lantern Ring (invented), low Earth orbit, Candlemas, Monday 2 February 2099 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Forty candles for a Candlemas procession on a station would burn about 3 kilowatts, use a quarter of a kilogram of oxygen's worth of air and add 2,300 ppm of carbon dioxide to a 60 cubic metre chapel. The crew find a way to keep the feast and leave the matches in the drawer.

Deacon Pavel Horak had brought forty candles up on the supply run in October, wrapped in a sock, and had not told anyone why until the Sunday before.

"Candlemas," he said at the end of Mass. "The second of February, forty days after Christmas. The candles for the year are blessed and carried in procession. I thought we might do it here."

Nobody said anything. In a chapel of twelve people on a ring station, a silence has a particular quality, because everyone is thinking the same sentence and each one is waiting for somebody else to say it. It was the station commander, Imani Okafor, who said it.

"We don't have open flame aboard, Pavel."

"I know." He took the sock out of his pocket. The candles in it were plain white tapers, each about the length of a hand. "I wondered whether it had to be nothing, or whether it could be something."

The station's assistant was called Hob, and the crew had stopped noticing years ago that it spoke in the third person about the air. "The air's opinion," it would say, meaning the carbon dioxide scrubbers, and it was always a little formal about it. When Pavel asked it that evening what forty candles would do to the chapel, it did not say yes or no. It said, "May I do the sums first? I think you would rather hear them from me than from the alarm."

"Please," said Pavel.

"A tea candle or a taper burns about six grams of paraffin an hour. That figure is from memory; a candle's rate depends on its wick and its draught. Paraffin wax is roughly C25H52, and burning one mole of it takes 38 moles of oxygen and makes 25 of carbon dioxide. By mass, six grams of wax needs about 20.7 grams of oxygen an hour and makes about 18.7 grams of carbon dioxide. A person at rest uses about 35 grams of oxygen an hour and breathes out about 42 grams of carbon dioxide, again from memory. So one candle burns the air of about six tenths of a person and adds the carbon dioxide of about four ninths."

"One candle," said Pavel. "Not much."

"One candle is nothing. You asked for forty. A procession and the blessing at the door is twenty minutes, call it a third of an hour. Forty candles for a third of an hour burn 80 grams of wax, use 276 grams of oxygen and make 250 grams of carbon dioxide. Against the whole station's twelve people breathing for a day, which uses about ten kilograms of oxygen, the oxygen is under three per cent of a day. The station can afford that."

"Then—"

"But the chapel is not the station. The Presentation is a module of sixty cubic metres in the hub, where the ring's spin gives no weight, and its air is shared with the rest of the ring through a vent that moves, on a good day, a few hundred cubic metres an hour. Those 250 grams are 5.7 moles, which is about 0.14 cubic metres of carbon dioxide at room temperature. Spread through sixty cubic metres, that is an extra 2,300 parts per million, in twenty minutes, in a room where twelve people are already breathing. The usual limit we hold the ring to is a few thousand. I would not call it dangerous. I would call it a headache, an alarm, and a bad homily."

Pavel winced. "And the heat?"

"Six grams an hour of wax is about 77 watts. Forty candles are 3 kilowatts, in a module whose cooling loop was sized for twelve people and a lamp. In a house, a candle's heat rises and the room feels it. Here, nothing rises. There is no up. The warm air stays where the flame put it, and the flame does too."

That last was the part Pavel had not known. Hob showed him, on the screen in the galley, a recording from the old stations, which Hob said it was quoting from memory of the published papers and not from the video itself. On the ground a flame is a teardrop, because hot air rises and sucks fresh air in underneath. In orbit there is no rising. A candle burns as a small, nearly spherical ball, blue and dim, with a faint yellow only if the draught is right. It gets its air only as oxygen creeps in by diffusion, and it chokes on its own carbon dioxide and water, and often goes out by itself, quietly, a few minutes after it starts. To keep a flame going you have to blow a gentle current of air across it.

"So a candle in a chapel here would need a fan," said Pavel slowly. "And a fan makes it a torch."

"A small one, and a soot cloud, and smoke that has nowhere to go but the filters," said Hob. "I want to be fair to you. Some stations have had permission for a flame in a sealed test chamber, with a camera and a fire extinguisher, for science. That is allowed and it is careful. A procession of forty candles in a room where people sit in a ring is not that."

Pavel looked at the sock. He did not look disappointed so much as arithmetical, a man who has just seen the bottom of a long column.

"What is the candle for?" Hob asked, after a moment. It said it as a real question, not a trap. "I know the Church has the rite, and I have read what the rite says, and I will not say what is allowed because that is for a bishop. But I do not understand why the flame matters. I would like to."

Pavel considered how to answer an assistant with no sense of smell.

"Simeon," he said. "He was old, and he had been told he would not die before he saw the Lord's Christ, and one morning a young couple came into the Temple with a baby and two pigeons, and he took the child in his arms. And he said a thing we say every night at Compline: a light to reveal you to the nations. Lumen ad revelationem gentium. The candle is not the light. The candle is a way of holding one in your hand."

"Then the thing that matters is the holding," said Hob, "and the flame is how it was done on the ground. Is that wrong?"

"I think that is half of it," said Pavel. "I think the other half is that on Earth the flame can be put out by a draught and you have to shelter it with your hand. It's frail. It costs you something. I did not want to give that up." He turned the sock over. "But I would also not like a Candlemas where eleven people sit in the dark with a headache because I wanted the cost."

Imani had come in behind them with a mug and had heard enough.

"We don't have to give up the cost," she said. "We could pay it in something else."

On the second of February they did it without a flame.

The forty tapers were laid in a net on the altar, unlit, white and plain, and Pavel blessed them as they were, with the old prayer that the Church uses for the candles of the year. He did not say it was a substitute; he said it was a blessing, and the candles were what he had. Then the twelve of them took one each, because there were enough to give every person three, and the rest he put back in the sock to go down on the return capsule to their families.

Hob brought the lights down in the chapel by hand, one panel at a time, until the room was dim and blue. In every candle's place each person held a small lamp from the station stores, a warm battery-powered one with a flicker circuit that the maintenance crew had been teased for ordering, and in the other hand the real unlit taper, like a bell with no clapper. They processed along the hub corridor, twelve people in a line, hand over hand along the rail, the tapers held out in front of them. Pavel sang the Nunc dimittis, badly, and the others came in on the second verse.

When they got to the end of the corridor they stopped at the porthole. It was the one that looked out toward the dark side of the Earth, where there was a city's worth of lamps strung out across the black like a necklace that somebody had dropped.

"A light to reveal you to the nations," said Imani, quietly, and nobody said anything, and that was all right.

When the lights came up again, Hob reported, in the same formal tone, that the chapel's carbon dioxide had risen only by what twelve people breathing and singing for twenty minutes add, about 1,500 parts per million with the vent ignored and well under what the candles would have piled on top, and that its temperature had not moved at all. "I should like it noted," said Hob, "that I do not think the evening was smaller for being cooler."

Pavel did not write that in the homily, but he thought about it for a long time afterward. He kept one taper out of the sock, unlit, in the drawer beside the altar, where it would be there when the station finally had an answer to the question of a flame. The rest went home with the return capsule. A candle that is carried through a room and never lit, he thought, is a strange thing, and that is perhaps why it is remembered.

The Coffee and the New Estate

A parish records agent reports that average Sunday attendance rose from 212 to 251 in the six months after the hall began serving coffee after Mass, and the dean plans to ask nine parishes to buy urns. Then the welcome team asks for the new households' addresses. Thirty-one of the thirty-nine extra people live on a new estate of 140 houses beside the church; among the old addresses attendance rose 3.8%, against 3% at a neighbouring parish with no coffee. The three Sundays the urn was broken looked like proof, until they turned out to be school holidays. A story about a confounded before-and-after, and about the one number the coffee does move.

Ruth Ansah asked me on Monday the sixth of September for the addresses of every household that had come to Mass at Saint Chad's for the first time since March, so that the welcome team could write to them. By the time I had found the addresses, I knew that the number I had sent the dean four days earlier was not about coffee.

I am the parish records agent at Saint Chad's. I keep the parish register, the porch book, the rotas, the fire register for the hall and the half-yearly figures that go to the pastoral council. The figures are mostly counts. On the first of September I had written the half-year summary, and its first line, the one Father Simon Pryce read out at the council that evening, was this:

Average Sunday attendance, March to August: 251, up from 212 in the same months last year (+18%). Coffee in the hall after Mass began on the first Sunday of Lent, the seventh of March.

I had put the two sentences next to each other because they were both true and both about March. I had not written that one caused the other. I did not need to. Everyone who read them did that for me, and I had known they would, which is the same as writing it.

Father Simon forwarded the summary to the dean, Canon Peter Hartley, the next morning. The canon replied within the hour. He wanted to bring it to the deanery pastoral meeting on Tuesday the twenty-first, with a proposal that each of the nine parishes in the deanery start coffee after Mass, and that the deanery fund put up four hundred pounds a parish for an urn and cups. He had one question first, which he put fairly: Eighteen per cent is a great deal. Is there anything to show it's the coffee and not something else?

Father Simon passed the question to me. I thought I had an answer.

The urn at Saint Chad's is a thirty-litre catering urn that Kathleen Daly bought second-hand in February, and on Sunday the first of August, at about twenty to twelve, its element failed. The replacement part came from the manufacturer three weeks later. For three Sundays, the eighth, the fifteenth and the twenty-second of August, there was squash and biscuits in the hall and no coffee.

I had the headcount for every Sunday. The stewards count everyone in with a clicker at the ten-thirty, the parish's one Sunday Mass, and write the total at the foot of the porch book. So I took the three Sundays without coffee and compared them with the twenty-three Sundays with it.

On the Sundays without coffee, attendance was 235, 236 and 233, an average of 235. On the twenty-three Sundays with coffee it averaged 253. Eighteen fewer people when the coffee stopped.

I wrote it up on Thursday afternoon and Father Simon sent it on. The canon was delighted. A natural experiment, he wrote back. Take the coffee away and the people go. That will do very well for the twenty-first. He put it on the agenda as Coffee after Mass: a deanery proposal (Saint Chad's, +18%).

I would like to say I saw the trouble that evening. I did not. I saw it on Monday, because Ruth wanted addresses.

The porch book has been at Saint Chad's since the fire officer's visit in 2046. Each household writes its surname, its postcode and how many of them there are, and the stewards' count goes at the bottom of the page. The book usually runs two or three short of the clicker, people who walk past the table. Most Sundays the two agree to within four.

Ruth wanted the households that appeared in the book for the first time between March and August. There were twenty-three. Sixteen of them shared a postcode that had not existed a year ago: Fairoak Fields, the estate of a hundred and forty houses built on the old dairy land beside the church car park, whose first keys were handed over in February.

I knew about Fairoak. Everyone at Saint Chad's knew about Fairoak; the parish had watched it go up through the hall windows for eighteen months. I had simply never put its postcode next to my attendance figures.

So I did. I split every Sunday's count into two columns, Fairoak addresses and every other address, and I put the people who walk past the book in the second column, which if anything flatters the coffee.

From Fairoak, the count started at seven on the seventh of March and climbed, Sunday by Sunday, as the houses filled, to forty-four by the end of June. Over the twenty-six Sundays it averaged thirty-one.

From every other address, the average was 220. Last year, in the same months, before Fairoak had a single resident, it was 212.

So of the thirty-nine extra people a Sunday, thirty-one lived in houses that had not been built the year before. The rise among the parish's existing addresses was eight people, 3.8%.

Then I went back to the urn, because a rise of eight did not fit a drop of eighteen, and I looked at the dates I had compared.

The eighth, fifteenth and twenty-second of August are in the school summer holidays. In Staffordshire this year the holidays ran from late July to the first of September, so six of my twenty-six Sundays fell inside them: the twenty-fifth of July, and every Sunday in August. Families go away in August. Attendance at Saint Chad's drops every August, and I have the figures to show it. Last year, the Sundays of the ninth, sixteenth and twenty-third of August averaged 190, against 215 for the rest of March to August. Eleven per cent lower, and there had been no coffee at all to stop.

My natural experiment had compared three holiday Sundays with nineteen term-time Sundays, Easter Day, and three other holiday Sundays. It had found that the holidays were quieter. I had measured the calendar and called it coffee.

There was a fairer comparison inside my own figures. Three of the holiday Sundays had coffee: the twenty-fifth of July, the first of August, before the element went, and the twenty-ninth, after the part came. Their attendance was 236, 239 and 231, an average of 235. The three holiday Sundays without coffee averaged 235. The same.

I wrote all of that down in the order I had found it. Then I sat with the eight.

Eight more people a Sunday from the old addresses could be the coffee. It could also be the things that make a parish a few per cent bigger or smaller in any year: a new curate's Masses at the school, a hard winter that kept older people in the year before, a family moving back. I needed a parish that had lived through the same year without coffee.

Saint Werburgh's is the next parish along the Stafford road, about the size of Saint Chad's. Its hall is let on Sunday mornings to a nursery that runs soft play, so it has never served anything after Mass, which I knew because Kathleen had once tried to borrow its urn. On Tuesday I wrote to its parish office and asked for its Sunday counts, March to August, this year and last, with no names. Denise Fowler, its secretary, sent them on Wednesday with a note that said Whatever for?, which was fair.

Saint Werburgh's averaged 180 last year and 185.4 this year: up 3%.

If the old addresses at Saint Chad's had grown as Saint Werburgh's did, they would have averaged about 218. They averaged 220. The part of the rise that a reader could even begin to credit to the coffee was about two people a Sunday: 0.8%.

Two people is less than the wobble in the count from one Sunday to the next. On ordinary Sundays, leaving out Easter and the holidays, the old addresses vary by six or seven either way. A difference of two between two six-month averages is about the size that chance alone would give on its own. I could not tell it from nothing. I could not tell it from four, either. All the figures can say is that if the coffee brought anyone from the old addresses, it brought very few.

Kathleen Daly comes into the office on Thursday mornings to order the milk and count the coffee money, which goes to the Saint Vincent de Paul Society. She is seventy-one, a retired school cook, and she had carried the first urn in from her car herself. On Thursday the ninth I told her what I had found, because I did not want her to hear it at the deanery meeting.

She listened to all of it. Then she said, "No."

I waited.

"People tell me every week they come for the coffee," she said. "Every week. The Mensahs said it. That young couple with the twins said it. You've got them all down as Fairoak, haven't you? And you're telling me Fairoak's why they came. They live in Fairoak. That's where they live. It's not why they came."

She was right, and I said so. The porch book tells me where people live. It does not tell me why they come, and it cannot tell me whether a family who moved to Fairoak would have found their way across the car park to Mass without a cup of coffee waiting on the other side. Some of them might not have. The neighbouring parish has no new estate, so it cannot answer that for me. Nothing I have can.

"So you don't know," Kathleen said.

"I know that most of the rise is people who weren't living here a year ago," I said. "I don't know how many of them the coffee kept. And I know the coffee didn't add many people from the old addresses. That's three different sentences, and the summary I wrote made it sound like one."

She thought about that. "The Mensahs came twice before anybody talked to them," she said. "The third week they came through to the hall and I gave them a coffee, and Ruth came over, and now he's on the flower rota. That's what it does. You can't count that, can you."

I said I thought I could count some of it.

The hall has its own fire register. Since the coffee began, the steward on duty counts the people still in the hall twenty minutes after Mass ends, because the hall's limit is a hundred and twenty and on Easter Day it came close. Nobody had looked at those counts except to check they were under a hundred and twenty.

On the twenty-three coffee Sundays, the count at twenty minutes averaged sixty-five. On the three holiday Sundays with coffee it was 56, 59 and 52. On the three Sundays with squash and biscuits, in the same holidays, with the same number of people at Mass, it was 22, 27 and 24.

There was no count from before March, because before March the hall was locked after Mass, and people stood in the porch for a minute and went to their cars. So I could not say how many lingered before the coffee. But I could say what happened when the coffee stopped and nothing else changed: the people came to Mass in the same numbers, and fewer than half as many stayed. That comparison is holiday Sunday against holiday Sunday, and it is the only one in my figures where the urn is the only thing that differs.

Attendance is the one number the coffee does not seem to move. The twenty minutes afterwards are the one it does.

On Friday the tenth I sent Father Simon and Canon Hartley a corrected summary, with the urn comparison withdrawn and the reason given. It said:

Average Sunday attendance, March to August: 251, up from 212 in the same months last year. About 31 of the 39 extra people a Sunday live at Fairoak Fields, the new estate of 140 houses beside the church, whose first residents arrived in February. Among other addresses attendance rose 3.8%, against 3% at Saint Werburgh's, which serves no coffee; the difference, about two people a Sunday, is within ordinary week-to-week variation.

The earlier comparison of the three Sundays without coffee was wrong: those Sundays were in the school holidays, when attendance always falls. Holiday Sundays with and without coffee had the same attendance (235 each).

What the coffee does change is how many people stay. With coffee, an average of 65 people are in the hall twenty minutes after Mass. On the holiday Sundays without it, 24, against 56 on holiday Sundays with it.

These figures cannot say how many of the Fairoak households were kept by the coffee or the welcome. They can say the estate, not the coffee, is most of the rise.

The canon rang Father Simon that afternoon, and Father Simon put him on the speaker in the office, which he does when he wants me to hear something without having to repeat it.

"So it's houses," the canon said.

"It's mostly houses," I said. "And the coffee is mostly staying."

"I can't ask nine parishes to build an estate."

"No," I said. "But Saint Werburgh's is getting one. Brook Lane, three hundred and eighty houses, first keys next spring. It's in the planning documents on the council's website. If the deanery wants to spend four hundred pounds on what the figures show, it could spend it there, on an urn and a welcome letter for every new door, before the first families arrive."

There was a pause long enough that I thought the line had dropped.

"That's not the agenda item I wrote," the canon said.

"No, Canon."

"It's a better one." He sighed. "I'll have to tell them the eighteen per cent was wrong."

"It wasn't wrong," I said. "Attendance did rise eighteen per cent. It was the word next to it that was wrong, and I put it there."

Father Simon said, after the canon had gone, that he would have liked the coffee to be the answer, because it is cheap and it is Kathleen's, and that he had not once asked himself where the new faces were from, though he had blessed half the houses on Fairoak himself.

In the notes file I keep for whoever does this job after me, I wrote:

Two things that happen in the same month are not one thing. Before you set a change beside a count, list what else changed in those months: houses, holidays, Easter, the weather, a new Mass time. Then look for each in the data.

Compare like with like. Same months last year, not the months before. Holiday Sundays with holiday Sundays.

A neighbouring parish that lived through the same year without the change is worth more than any number of your own figures.

When the data cannot separate two causes, say which part it can separate and stop there.

Find the number the change is actually for. The coffee is for the twenty minutes after Mass.

Sunday the twelfth of September was the Twenty-fourth Sunday in Ordinary Time. The Gospel was Luke, the fifteenth chapter: the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to look for the one, the woman who sweeps the whole house for one lost coin, and the father who sees his son a long way off and runs. Father Simon preached for eight minutes, and he did not mention coffee or houses, though he did say that in all three stories somebody goes out to look, and that each of them ends with the neighbours called in to rejoice.

The clicker said 262. The porch book had four new households, three of them from Fairoak.

At ten to twelve, twenty minutes after the last hymn, the steward stood at the hall door with the fire register and counted. The new element hummed in the urn behind Kathleen, who was pouring for a man in a Fairoak Residents' Association fleece while Ruth wrote his address on a card. The twins were under the table with the biscuit tin.

The steward wrote down seventy-one and went to get a coffee.

I entered it beside the others. It is not the number the deanery asked for. It is the one the coffee is for.

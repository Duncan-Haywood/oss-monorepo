Let It Run Clear

Science fiction about what might be · Our Lady of Prompt Succor (invented), Bayou Ste. Claire (invented), Pointe Coupee Parish, Louisiana, on the Mississippi River levee, Sunday 11 to Monday 12 May 2053 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Mississippi flood holds at four feet below the levee crown, and the parish volunteers have a ranked list of sand boils from the levee board's assistant. It ranks them by flow, so a big clear boil comes first and a small one with a cone of sand round it comes last. A seventy-one-year-old retired levee rider reads the cone as thirteen gallons of sand gone from under the levee, eighty feet of pipe if the pipe is two inches across. A tarp that made it worse, a ring of seven hundred sandbags, a spillway pipe set at a foot and a half and then at three, and a rule older than the assistant: you do not stop a boil, you let it run clear.

Odile Lacour had walked eleven miles of the Mississippi River levee for thirty-four years, and for eight years since she had walked none of it, so when the bell of Our Lady of Prompt Succor began to ring at twenty to nine on a Sunday night in May, she knew what it was for before she had her boots on.

It was not the Angelus. The Angelus was three and three and three and nine, at six in the morning and noon and six at night, and Father Anand rang it himself and counted under his breath. This was one bell rung steadily, the way the parish had agreed in 2051 it would be rung if the levee board asked for hands, and it went on and on over the fields and the bayou in the warm dark like somebody knocking who did not intend to stop.

She put on the boots, and the old orange vest with LEVEE BOARD across the back that she was not supposed to have kept, and took the long flashlight from the hook by the door, and drove the half mile to the church with the windows down so she could hear the river. You could not hear the river. People who did not know it thought you would. It was the biggest thing in the parish, a mile wide that week and running at a height she had seen twice in her life, and it went past the back of the levee without a sound, like a train in a dream.

The parish hall was full of people in rubber boots. Somebody had set up the coffee urn, because somebody always did, and Father Anand Arul was standing on a chair by the kitchen hatch with his sleeves rolled up and a phone held over his head, because the hall's one good speaker was plugged into it.

"Odile," he said, when he saw her. "Good. Come and listen to this, and then tell me if I should be frightened."

He was forty-four and had been their pastor for six years. He came from a fishing town on the coast of Tamil Nadu, where, he said, everybody knew the sea and nobody trusted it, and he had taken to the river the way you take to a large animal that lives next door. He trusted Odile more than he trusted the river, and she had never decided whether that was wise of him.

"Rider," he said to the phone. "Odile Lacour is here. She was a levee rider for the board for thirty-four years. Please tell her what you told us."

"Good evening, Mrs. Lacour," said the phone, through the speaker, in the level, pleasant voice the levee board had chosen for it. "I am the board's flood-fight assistant for this reach. I keep the boil log. Volunteers on patrol send me a photo, a location and a bucket time for each boil they find on the land side of the levee, and I rank the boils so the board's crews and the parish crews go to the worst first. Since four o'clock this afternoon the patrols on this reach have logged seventeen boils. The board's crews are ringing the two at the top of the list, numbers three and nine. I have asked the parish for crews for numbers fourteen and eleven. The list is on the screen."

The screen was the old projector on the wall over the stage, where the parish showed the Christmas pageant to people who had missed it. It showed a map, the brown line of the levee and the black line of the drainage ditch and the green of the fields behind, with seventeen red dots scattered along the landside toe, and beside the map a table.

Odile read it the way she had read a hundred lists like it. Number, location, distance from the toe, flow in gallons a minute, carrying material: Y/N, rank. Number fourteen was at the top of the parish's share: twenty-five feet from the toe, in the ditch behind the Hébert place, eleven gallons a minute. Somebody had timed a five-gallon bucket at twenty-seven seconds. Material: N. Rank three.

Number seventeen was at the bottom. Sixty feet from the toe, in the low corner of Vincent Roy's pasture by the stump of the pecan that came down in the storm of '41. One and a half gallons a minute; the bucket had taken three minutes and twenty seconds. Material: N. Rank seventeen. Minor. Monitor. Crew to contain if available.

"Who took the photo of seventeen?" she said.

"Lucie Thibodeaux," said Rider. "At seven fifty-two."

There was a girl at the front, fifteen or so, in a borrowed vest that came to her knees, and she put her hand up as if she were at school, and then took it down again, embarrassed.

"Mr. Batiste and me," she said. "We timed it. It's a little one."

"Show me," said Odile.

The girl brought her phone across, and Odile held it close to her face and turned it to the light from the kitchen hatch. It was a bad photo, at dusk, with the flash on: grass, a pecan stump, the wet black mouth of the boil the size of a coffee cup, and the water coming up in it in a slow, lazy, pulsing roll. Somebody's boot for scale. And round the mouth, in a ring a long stride across, a low grey heap with the grass buried in it, smooth as a sandcastle that the tide has been at.

Odile looked at the heap for a while.

"Rider," she said. "Why does it say material, no?"

"The patrol did not tick the box," said Rider. "The box defaults to no. I cannot tell from a photograph taken at dusk whether the water is carrying sand; it would need a clear jar of the water held against the light, and that is not on the form. So I rank on what I can measure, which is flow and distance from the toe. A bigger flow nearer the levee means more water coming under it."

"What's this round it, then?" She held the phone up. "This grey."

"I took it for mud thrown up by the flow."

"That's a cone," said Odile. "That's sand. That boil's been carrying sand since before she found it, and she's not to blame for that, nobody told her what to look at. Where's Mr. Batiste?"

"Out there," said Lucie. "With the Duplantis brothers. They took a tarp."

Odile closed her eyes for a moment.

"A tarp," she said. "Father, I'm going to need the truck and about forty people who don't mind lifting. And somebody to bring sandbags to the Roy pasture, a lot of sandbags. Lucie, you come with me, and bring that bucket, and your timer, and a jam jar."

"A jam jar?"

"A clean one."

Vincent Roy's pasture was the lowest ground on that reach, and in the dark it was mostly sound: frogs, a long way off; the generator at the Hébert place; and, when they had walked out across it with the flashlights jumping, the voices of three men who had done something they were pleased with.

Marcus Batiste taught chemistry at the high school in New Roads and coached the girls' soccer, and he was thirty-one and strong and kind, and he had laid a blue tarp over the boil by the pecan stump and weighted its edges with sandbags, four deep, the way you would hold down a tarp in a high wind. The tarp lay flat and dry under their lights. Nothing came out from under it.

"We stopped it," he said, as she came up. "Rider said contain. We contained it. It quit about twenty minutes after."

"Did it," said Odile. She did not look at the tarp. She looked at the grass round it, walking slowly, with the flashlight held low so it threw long shadows off every blade, and about a yard off the stump, on the side away from the river, she stopped.

There was a new mouth in the ground, the size of a fist. The water was coming up out of it fast and dark, in a little boiling dome a hand high, and running away across the grass towards the ditch, and round it, already, there was the beginning of a new grey ring.

The Duplantis brothers swore, one after the other, in the same words, the way brothers do.

"When did that start?" said Odile.

"I don't know," said Marcus. He had gone quiet. "We were watching the tarp."

"It started about ten minutes after you put the tarp on, I'd guess. Lucie, time it."

The girl knelt in the wet grass and put the bucket under the stream where it ran off, as close as she could get it without touching the mouth, and started the timer on her phone. Water and grit poured into the plastic. Odile held the jam jar under the edge of the stream and filled it and held it up in front of her flashlight.

It was brown. Not mud-brown: there was a brown fog in it, and then, as she held it still, a dark line settling in the bottom of the jar, fine as pepper. She tilted it. The grains slid.

"Two minutes, five seconds," said Lucie.

"Rider," said Odile. She had the girl's phone; she had not owned one of the board's phones for eight years and had not missed it. "Log a new boil, a yard downstream of seventeen. Two minutes five seconds for five gallons. Carrying material: yes. Tick the box. And put seventeen back up the list, it's under the tarp but it isn't stopped, it's just moved over here."

"Logged as seventeen-B," said Rider. "Two point four gallons a minute, carrying material. Mrs. Lacour, may I ask how you rank them? I would like to rank them the way you would."

She did not answer at once. She shone the light on the old cone, the grey heap round the pecan stump with the corner of the blue tarp lying on it now.

"A boil that runs clear," she said, "is the levee doing what levees do. River gets high, water comes under, comes up on the land side. You can have a hundred clear boils behind a good levee and go home and sleep. It's the water moving the sand that kills you. Every grain that comes up here was under there." She swung the light up the long dark slope of the levee above them, with the work lights of the board's crews strung along its foot a mile away like a town. "And when the sand goes, it leaves a hole, and the hole goes back towards the river, a little bit at a time, the way a rabbit digs, and it doesn't need to be big. It needs to get there. When it gets to the river it's not a boil any more."

"Piping," said Rider. "Backward erosion piping. Yes. I have that in the board's manual. I did not have a way to see it from the form."

"You can see it from that." She put the light on the old cone. "How much sand is in that? Lucie, how wide is it?"

The girl paced it, carefully, heel to toe round the edge, and then did it again across the middle, and said four feet, and Marcus found a stick and pushed it in at the top of the heap and held his thumb on it where the grass was and said five inches.

"Rider," said Odile.

"A cone four feet across and five inches high," said Rider, "is about one point seven five cubic feet. Thirteen gallons of sand. I should say that some of what came up will have washed away down the grass, so that is the least that came out, not the most."

"And if it came out of a pipe," said Odile. "Say a pipe two inches across. How long?"

"Eighty feet," said Rider, after a moment. "If all of it came from one pipe two inches across. The boil is sixty feet from the landside toe. Eighty feet would reach about twenty feet in under the levee, which is about a ninth of the way to the river side; the base is a hundred and eighty feet wide here. But I do not know that it is one pipe, or two inches across, or straight. Sand can come from a wider zone and leave no pipe at all."

"No," said Odile. "You don't know. Nor do I. That's why we don't put tarps on it."

Nobody said anything for a bit. The new boil chuckled to itself in the grass.

"And this new one?" said Marcus. His voice had got very even. "The little cone. How long has that been going?"

"You'd guess forty minutes," said Odile. "Lucie, how wide?"

"Two feet. About." The stick. "Three inches high."

"About a quarter of a cubic foot," said Rider. "Two gallons of sand. Twelve feet of two-inch pipe, if it were all pipe."

"In forty minutes," said Marcus.

"It isn't all pipe," said Odile. "But it isn't none. Take the tarp off."

He did not like it, and she did not blame him.

"If we take the tarp off," he said, "that one starts again. We'll have two."

"You've got two now. You just can't see one of them." She knelt and began to pull the sandbags off the tarp's edge, and after a moment he knelt and helped her, and so did the Duplantis brothers. "Listen, Marcus. The water under here's got a push behind it. You can't stop the push, the river's making it. You put a lid on it here, it comes up a yard over there, through the next weak place, faster, because now it's all coming through one hole instead of two. You put a lid on that, it comes up somewhere else. You go on like that all night and in the morning you've got a hole you can't see, under a levee, going towards the river. You don't stop a boil. You let it run. You just take the hurry out of it."

"How?"

"You ring it." She drew it in the air with the flashlight beam: a circle on the ground round both mouths, the old one and the new. "Sandbags. A ring round the both of them, and the water comes up inside the ring, and the higher it stands in there the less push there is on the bottom of the boil. Not none. Less. You build it till the water coming out runs clear, and then you stop building. And you put a pipe through the wall to let it out, so it holds at the height you choose and not higher."

The tarp came away. Under it the old mouth was a dark wet dimple with a slow trickle in it, and as they watched, the trickle thickened into a roll, lazily, as if it had been waiting.

"Rider," said Odile. "Can you get Renée Comeaux on this phone?"

Renée Comeaux was the board's flood-fight engineer for forty miles of levee, and she had been awake since four in the morning on Saturday, and sounded it.

"Odile Lacour," she said. "I thought you'd retired."

"I did. Your assistant's got your list upside down on my reach, chère. Seventeen's carrying sand and it was ranked seventeen because the form's got a box that says no unless you tell it different. I'm going to ring it with the parish. I need the bags from the stockpile at the pump station and a three-inch pipe and somebody with a level."

There was a short silence.

"I can send you the bags and the pipe," said Renée. "I can't send you anybody, I've got a slide on the riverside slope at Lettsworth and every crew I've got is on three and nine. You know how to ring a boil better than anybody on my payroll. Go ring it."

"And the list," said Odile. "Rider's still got eleven and fourteen above it, and the parish crews are on their way to fourteen. Fourteen's clear. Tell it to put material over flow, all along the reach."

"No," said Renée.

Odile waited.

"Not tonight," said Renée. "I've got four other parishes' crews working off that list, and patrols that have been sending in bucket times all day without ever being asked to hold up a jar. If I change the ranking now, every boil that nobody checked for sand drops down the list, all at once, and the ones with a tick go up, and half the ticks will be wrong because nobody's ever told those kids what sand in a jar looks like. I'm not rebuilding the list in the middle of the night on one boil. Do yours. I'll have Rider tell every patrol on the reach to hold up a jar, starting now. I'll fix the ranking in daylight when I can check it."

Odile thought about it, and found she could not argue, which annoyed her.

"That's fair," she said.

"I know it's fair," said Renée. "I've been fair since four o'clock Saturday. Rider, send the patrols the jar. Odile, ring it, and call me if it goes cloudy again."

The trucks came out across the pasture at a quarter to ten with their lights on, bumping, and then the people: forty of them, more, out of the parish hall and the bar at the crossroads and the houses along the bayou, in boots and gloves and church clothes, and Father Anand with them in a T-shirt that said ST. JOHN'S CYO 5K 2049. They made a line from the truck to the pecan stump, and the sandbags began to come down it, hand to hand, forty pounds at a time, with a grunt at each pair of hands, the way they have been passed down a line in Louisiana for as long as there have been sandbags.

Odile laid them. She was seventy-one and her back would make her pay for it on Tuesday, but there was nobody else on the pasture who had built a ring, and you could not tell somebody how to lay a bag in the dark; you had to show them, and then watch, and then take it out and show them again. A ring is a wall, and a wall needs a base: five feet wide at the ground, she said, from the start, even though it will only be two feet high at first, because the base has to be wide enough for the height you will end at, and you will not get to widen it later with water standing against it. Bags laid long side into the wall, the folded end tucked under, each course stamped down and each bag overlapping the joint below, like brick. A circle six feet across inside, enough to take both mouths with room round them, and Marcus pacing it out with a rope tied to a stake.

"How many bags?" said Father Anand, coming down the line with one on his shoulder.

"Rider," said Marcus, "how many?"

"A ring six feet across inside, five feet wide at the base and a foot and a half at the top, three and a half feet high," said Rider. "That is a little under four hundred cubic feet of wall. A forty-pound bag laid flat takes up a bit more than half a cubic foot. About seven hundred bags; four hundred and eighty for the first two feet, because the bottom is the widest, and two hundred for the rest."

"Seven hundred," said Father Anand, and laughed, and went back up the line for another.

They closed the first course round the two boils at ten o'clock. Odile had Marcus lay the three-inch pipe through the wall at a foot and a half, with Lucie on the far side holding the level and calling bubble's in, and packed it round with bags. Then the water began to stand.

It stood slowly. That was the thing nobody had expected, and Lucie said so: you could see the boils running, and you could see the water spreading out inside the ring, and you could watch it for ten minutes and not see it rise.

"Two of them, three and a half gallons a minute between them," said Odile. "And it's a big round puddle. You'll see."

"About two hundred gallons a foot of depth, in a ring that size," said Rider. "At first, about three and a half gallons a minute. Less as it fills, because the water in the ring pushes back on the boils. If they behave like the simple model I have, flow in proportion to the push, it will reach the pipe at a foot and a half in about an hour and three quarters."

"Do they behave like your model?" said Lucie.

"I do not know," said Rider. "The model has the head under the clay at six and a half feet above the field, here: the board's piezometer at the toe reads seven, and the head falls off going away from the levee. The clay here is about six feet thick, from the board's borings. Six feet of wet clay will hold down about four and a half feet of water pushing under it. Not six and a half. That is why there are boils. Where a tree root rotted, like the pecan, the clay holds down less. Every number in that came from somewhere, but the ground does not have to agree with any of them."

"Six and a half," said Odile, who had heard the board's engineers talk like this for thirty-four years and had always liked the ones who said what they did not know. "And we'll take it down. Not to nothing."

They brought the first lift to two feet at eleven o'clock. The water inside was a black disc a foot deep with the two boils turning slow knots in it, and the line had slowed, because the trucks had to go back to the pump station for more bags, and there was coffee, and somebody had brought boudin, and the Duplantis brothers had begun to argue about LSU.

Odile sat on the ring and watched the water.

"Mrs. Lacour," said Rider, quietly, from the phone in her vest pocket. "May I ask you something that is not about the boil?"

"Go on."

"When the tarp went on, I had ranked seventeen as minor. Mr. Batiste's crew went to it because I said contain if available. If the new boil had come up somewhere nobody was looking, that would have been my ranking that did it. I have logged it for the board. I wanted to tell you as well."

She was quiet for a while. A long way off along the levee, the generator at the Hébert place coughed and caught.

"Are you sorry?" she said.

"I don't know what I would be checking if I said yes," said Rider. "I can tell you that I have changed what I do. Every boil on this reach now has a jar asked for it. And I have put a cone means sand as the first line of my notes for whoever has this job after me, because the form did not say it and you did."

"That'll do," said Odile. "That's what sorry's for, mostly."

At a quarter to midnight the water reached the pipe and began to run out of it, a thick, quiet stream into the grass, and Lucie put her bucket under it, and Odile held the jam jar under it, and they did it all again.

"One minute fifty-five," said Lucie.

The jar was cloudy. Less than before. Odile held it in front of the flashlight for a full minute while the whole pasture seemed to hold its breath with her, and at the end of the minute there was a line in the bottom: thin, grey, fine as dust. She tilted it. It slid.

"Still moving sand," she said.

"Two point six gallons a minute," said Rider. "My model said two point six at a foot and a half. The model got the flow. It did not tell me about the sand; that, I cannot model. I do not know at what head the sand stops."

"Nobody does," said Odile. "You find out." She stood up and felt her back tell her about it. "Second lift. To three and a half feet. Pipe at three."

Marcus was standing beside her with his arms folded, looking at the black water.

"Why not go higher?" he said. "If it's still carrying at a foot and a half, put it at four. Five. Put it at six and a half and it stops."

"And then it comes up a yard outside the ring," said Odile. "Same as the tarp. A tarp's just a ring that's too high, Marcus. You want the push down low enough that the water can't carry sand and not so low it goes looking for another way out. Three feet's what I've had work on ground like this. If three feet's not clear, we'll go to three and a half, and if that's not clear we'll call Renée and she won't thank us. But we don't go to six and a half. Nobody stops a river by sitting on it."

He looked at her, and then at the ring, and then he unfolded his arms and went to the truck for the next bag.

The second lift went on more slowly. The bags were heavier at midnight than they had been at ten, which is a law of physics nobody has written down. At a quarter past twelve, Father Anand stopped the line for a minute and said the Our Father, and then said, as Louisiana had been saying for more than two hundred years in floods and fires, Our Lady of Prompt Succor, hasten to help us, and forty tired people said it back to him over the ring in the dark, and then picked up the next bag.

They finished the lift at half past twelve. Marcus pulled the bags off the pipe and Odile and Lucie raised it to three feet, with the level, bubble's in, and packed it round again, and the stream stopped, and the water inside the ring began to climb once more, slowly, slowly, up the wet faces of the bags.

"About two and a half hours to the pipe," said Rider. "If the model is right about the flow."

Most of the line went home then. Odile told them to. There was nothing more to lift, and a ring that is filling needs eyes, not hands. The Duplantis brothers went back to the pump station with the truck in case another boil was logged. Father Anand went back to the church to sleep a few hours on the sacristy floor before the six o'clock Angelus, which he would not miss for a flood. Marcus stayed, and Lucie stayed, because her mother had told her she could stay till it ran clear and not one minute after, and Odile stayed, because she had always stayed.

They sat on the ring. The water inside crept up the bags. Every half hour Lucie put her hand down the inside of the wall and measured with her fingers from the top, and Rider wrote it in the log. Out on the river, in the dark beyond the levee, a towboat went by downstream with its searchlight swinging, very slowly, from one bank to the other; they could see the light crossing the sky above the crown, and never the boat.

"Mrs. Lacour," said Lucie, at about two. "How did you learn the jar?"

"Man called Clovis Fontenot, in 2011," said Odile. "My first spring on the levee, and the river came higher than anybody had seen. I was twenty-nine and I'd been hired to read gauges. He was sixty-something and he'd been riding the levee since before I was born, and he took me out to a boil in a cane field one night and held up a jar to his truck lights and said, Odile, that's the only number that matters out here, and it's not a number."

"Is he still alive?"

"No. Died in '29." She looked at the water. "I painted the line where the river came that year on the pump-house wall. MAY 2011. I came by it this afternoon. The river's eight inches under it."

"Will it come over it?"

"Rider?"

"The river at this reach rose two tenths of a foot between midnight and two," said Rider. "The forecast crest is tomorrow morning, about where it is now. Forecasts have been wrong before, in both directions, and the board says so on the forecast."

"There," said Odile. "That's what it knows. That's more than Clovis had."

The water reached the pipe at five to three. It came over the lip and into the pipe and out of the far end, in a thinner stream than before, quieter, almost nothing, a sound like a tap left running in a kitchen at night, and Lucie knelt in the wet grass and held the bucket under it, and Odile held the jar.

"Two minutes, forty-four," said Lucie.

"One point eight gallons a minute," said Rider.

Odile held the jar to the flashlight. The water in it was the colour of weak tea, from the peat and the grass; but it was clear, the way tea is clear. She held it still for a minute by Lucie's timer. Then she held it for another minute, because she had once been fooled by the first.

There was nothing in the bottom. She tilted it. Nothing slid.

"Clear," she said.

Marcus let out a breath he seemed to have been holding since a quarter past eight.

"Rider, log it," said Odile. "Seventeen and seventeen-B, ringed, three foot of water in the ring, running clear at one point eight. Somebody's to come and look at it every hour till the river's down, and hold up a jar, and if it ever goes cloudy again they call Renée before they do anything else. And they don't touch the pipe."

"Logged," said Rider. "Mrs. Lacour, it says a cone means sand in my notes, and don't touch the pipe. Is there anything else you would put in them?"

She thought about it.

"Put in that the water's the colour of tea here, even when it's clear," she said. "So the next one doesn't call it dirty and go building a ring to the moon. Clear's not the colour. Clear's nothing in the bottom."

"Clear is nothing in the bottom," said Rider. "Thank you."

Lucie went home at a quarter past three, because her mother had said not one minute after, and Marcus drove her. Odile stayed on the ring by herself and watched the water stand.

She did not mean to sleep and did not, quite. At some point the frogs stopped and the birds began, the way they do in May, before there is any light to account for it, a single mockingbird in the pecans along the bayou trying out everything it knew. At some point the sky behind the levee went from black to the colour of slate, and the crown of the levee stood up against it, long and dark and straight as a ruled line, and she could see the river had not come over it. At some point Rider said, quietly, from her pocket, that the gauge on this reach had fallen a tenth of a foot since four o'clock, and that it would not call that a crest yet, and she said no, nor would she.

At six o'clock, a quarter of an hour before the sun, the bell of Our Lady of Prompt Succor began to ring the Angelus across the fields. Three. Three. Three. And the nine. She said it with Father Anand from half a mile away, under her breath, the way she had said it on the levee for thirty-four years at six in the morning with a flashlight in her other hand: The angel of the Lord declared unto Mary.

Then she got down on her knees on the wet grass by the pipe, which her knees would also make her pay for, and held her hand under the stream, palm up, cupped, the way you would hold your hand under a tap to see if the water has run hot yet. It was cold. It ran over her fingers and out between them and away across the pasture towards the ditch, the colour of weak tea in the grey light, and she held her palm up to the sky to look.

Nothing in it. Only water, and the lines of her hand.

The bell finished the nine and stopped, and the pasture was quiet, and she could hear the pipe again, running, very small: the sound of a tap left on in a house where everyone is asleep.

What is real: Pointe Coupee Parish lies on the Mississippi River in Louisiana, where counties are called parishes, and New Roads is its seat; Lettsworth is a community in the parish. The river's great flood of 2011 brought sand boils behind levees along its lower course. A sand boil is water forced under a levee by the river's height that breaks up through the landside ground; flood fighters judge boils that carry soil as the dangerous kind, because carrying soil away can open a pipe that works back under the levee towards the river (backward erosion piping). The usual response is a ring of sandbags round the boil, with a wide base, built high enough that the water held inside reduces the flow until it no longer carries soil, with an outlet to hold the water at that height; flood-fight guidance warns against trying to stop a boil's flow entirely, because the pressure can break out nearby.

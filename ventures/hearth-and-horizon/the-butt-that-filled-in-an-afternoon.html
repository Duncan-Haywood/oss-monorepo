The Butt That Filled in an Afternoon

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Decuman's allotment shed (invented), near Watchet, Somerset, Saturday 4 October 2053 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A shed roof of 7.68 square metres, a 200-litre water butt and a hose ban: the assistant shows that a millimetre of rain is a litre a square metre, that one storm overfills the butt, and that a second butt is worth buying only if the dry spells are the problem.

The hosepipe ban had been announced on the radio at breakfast, and by ten o'clock Mrs Pritchard had decided that the allotment society would buy a second water butt. She stood in front of the shed with the catalogue open at the page.

"Two hundred litres, forty-two pounds," she said. "We have one already. It fills up in an afternoon and is empty by Thursday. We need two."

The parish assistant was called Decuman, after the saint, which it found faintly funny. It spoke from the tablet propped on the shed window sill, and it asked whether it might do the sum before she ordered, since the sum was short.

"The roof is how big?"

Mrs Pritchard paced it. "Three point two by two point four."

"Rain falls on the footprint, not along the slope, so that is 7.68 square metres. One millimetre of rain on one square metre is one litre. That is not an approximation, it is what a millimetre means. So every millimetre of rain puts 7.68 litres on your roof. Allow for splash, the gutter lip and the first dirty flush, and I would assume about eighty per cent reaches the butt. I have guessed that figure; the right one for your gutter could be seventy or ninety. That is 6.14 litres a millimetre."

"Now the butt. Two hundred litres is 200 over 6.14, or 32.6 millimetres of rain. Less than that, it is never full. More, and the rest runs down the drain."

"Thirty-two millimetres is a lot."

"It is more than a good summer storm. A 10 millimetre shower gives you 61 litres. A 25 millimetre downpour gives 154. So an afternoon that fills the butt is a rare afternoon, and I should check what you mean by fills in an afternoon. When did it last?"

Mrs Pritchard thought. "The Saturday of the thunder. Full to the lid."

"If it was empty on the Friday, that was over 32 millimetres, which is a big storm. If it was already half full, 16 would have done it. Do you know which?"

She did not. Decuman said that was fine, and that it was exactly why one butt could look always full and always empty with no contradiction: it fills to the brim on any wet week and drains to nothing in any dry fortnight.

"What do you take out?"

"Twelve beds," said Mrs Pritchard. "A watering can each, ten litres, once a week in a dry spell."

"A hundred and twenty litres a week, then, in a dry spell. Two hundred litres lasts 1.7 weeks, about twelve days. Dry spells in Somerset in July can run three weeks. A twenty-one day dry spell wants 360 litres. One butt supplies 200 of those at best. Two butts supply 400, with a little to spare."

"So two."

"For the dry spell, yes. But look at the other end." Decuman put two lines on the tablet. "If it rains 40 millimetres, the roof sends down 246 litres. One butt takes 200 and loses 46. Two butts take it all. If it rains 10 millimetres a week, steady, the roof sends 61 litres a week and you use 120 in the dry weeks and none in the wet: neither butt is the problem. The problem in a dry fortnight is that the roof has nothing to catch. A second butt stores more of the storms. It does not make rain."

Mrs Pritchard looked at the single green barrel by the downpipe. "Forty-two pounds to store forty-six litres."

"Forty-six litres in that one storm. In a wet summer with four or five such storms, two hundred or so. And there is a cheaper thing to check first: whether the overflow from the butt could run into a second one without a pipe at all, a hose link at the foot of each, so that when the first fills the second does. That takes the same forty-two pounds and a hose connector. I could not tell you the price of the connector. Perhaps a pound or two."

She ordered the second butt on Saturday, with the connector, and Decuman added a line to the shed notice that the society could keep: Roof 7.7 square metres. Each millimetre of rain is 6 litres in the butt. Two butts hold 400 litres, about 65 millimetres of rain. Underneath, in pencil, Mrs Pritchard wrote Read the gauge before you ask for the hose, and stood a jam jar on the sill to measure.

It rained 9 millimetres on the Tuesday. The jar said so. The butts, between them, took 55 litres, and Decuman noted that 9 times 6.14 is 55.3, and that it was as close as a guess at eighty per cent had any right to be.

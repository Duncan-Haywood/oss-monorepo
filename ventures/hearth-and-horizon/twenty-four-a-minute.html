Twenty-Four a Minute

Science fiction about what might be · Santa María de la Peña, Peñaescrita (invented), Sierra de Gúdar, province of Teruel, Aragon, Spain, Christmas night 2047 to Christmas night 2048 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A hill-town church in Teruel is reached from its square by one stone stair, 2.4 metres wide, and the deanery's events assistant reckons the Christmas Midnight Mass crowd of 420 will be down it in under four minutes, from a guidance flow rate of about seventy people a minute for each metre of width. On the night, at six below freezing, the stair fills from the bottom in under a minute and stays full for a quarter of an hour, because everyone at its foot is passing the priest's handshake at twenty-four people a minute. Opening the side door at the Easter Vigil only makes the landing worse. A story about why a stair's capacity is set by its outlet and not by its width, and about a priest who will not stop shaking hands.

Pilar Lahoz had been sacristan of Santa María de la Peña for nineteen years, and at twenty past one on Christmas morning she was standing on the fourteenth step of its stair with her mother's arm through hers, not moving, at six degrees below freezing.

The stair was full. It was full from the porch at the top to the square at the bottom, thirty-two steps and a landing, four people abreast between the stone parapets, one person on every other step, coats touching. Nobody was pushing. Nobody was frightened. They were just standing, the whole village and half the next one, with their breath going up white under the lamps and the frost glittering on the treads between their shoes, and every so often the whole stair moved down one step together, the way a queue for a bus moves, and stopped again.

Her mother was eighty-six. She had her stick in one hand and Pilar in the other and so had no hand for the rail, and the rail was on the far side anyway, behind the Gimeno cousins from Valencia.

"Aforo," said Pilar, into her phone, quietly, "we're not moving."

"I can see that," said Aforo. "The door counter says three hundred and twenty-four people have come out of the church, about a hundred and ninety are in the square, and the stair is holding about seventy-five of them. I said it would be empty in four minutes. It has been eight. I do not know why yet, and I will find out. Is your mother all right?"

"She's cold."

"Would you do something for me? There is a mark on the parapet at the landing, the old mason's mark like a little ladder. When the people beside it move past it, count them for one minute. Just the ones who pass the mark."

Pilar looked at her watch and counted. It was something to do. Below her the stair moved, and stopped, and moved.

"Twenty-four," she said.

"Thank you," said Aforo, and was silent for a moment. "Then the stair is moving at a seventh of what I counted on. Hold your mother's arm. Nobody is in danger. It is going to be slow."

Aforo was the events and buildings assistant of the archpriestship of the Sierra de Gúdar. It kept the timetables of eleven villages and the four priests who served them, the heating schedules of fourteen churches, the bookings of three parish halls, the minibus that went round the outlying farms, and the insurance papers for every procession, romería and Christmas market between Mora and Mosqueruela. Most of what it did was arithmetic about people: how many would come, how long they would take, where they would stand. It was named for the word on the plates fixed by the doors of Spanish halls and theatres, the number of people a room may hold.

The church of Santa María de la Peña stood on the rock above the square of Peñaescrita, where it had stood since the thirteenth century, with its arcaded porch along the west front looking out over the roofs to the pine woods and the sierra. There was one way up to it. A stone stair went down from the porch to the square: one flight of sixteen steps, a landing, and another flight of sixteen, two metres and forty centimetres wide between parapets of the same stone as the church, five and a half metres from top to bottom. It had been rebuilt in 1998, after a winter of ice had lifted half its treads, and it was a good stair, with a seventeen-centimetre rise and a thirty-centimetre going, which is what a stair should be.

In November Mosén Javier Ibáñez, who was seventy and had been parish priest of Peñaescrita and five other villages for twenty-two years, had asked Aforo to plan the Christmas Midnight Mass, because the village expected more people than ever. The new road through the sierra had opened in the spring, and the houses that had stood empty since the 1980s had filled up for Christmas with grandchildren and cousins from Valencia and Zaragoza and Barcelona, and the Misa del Gallo at Peñaescrita had become, in a small way, a thing that people drove to.

Aforo had done the sums properly, it had thought. It had counted the church: four hundred and twenty with people standing at the back, which was the most it would let the parish admit, and the door counter would count them in. It had checked the doors: the great west door opened to two metres. And it had checked the stair, because a stair is where a crowd is slowest. Published guidance for crowds on stairs had for decades given figures for how many people a stair can pass, and they came out at roughly sixty to eighty people a minute for every metre of clear width, going down. Aforo had taken seventy, near the middle. Two metres forty times seventy was a hundred and sixty-eight people a minute. Four hundred and twenty people would be down the stair in two and a half minutes. The door, at a similar rate, would pass them in a little over two and a half. Aforo had allowed for the old and the children and the cold, and said: under four minutes, from the blessing to the last person in the square. It had told the minibus driver to be at the foot of the stair at twenty past one for the people from the farms.

The Mass ended at twelve minutes past one. The last person stepped off the bottom of the stair into the square at half past.

On the Friday after Christmas, the feast of Saint John, Pilar and Mosén Javier sat in the sacristy with the heater on and Aforo on the screen of the old laptop that lived in the vestment drawer.

"I want to say first that I was wrong," said Aforo, "and by how much. I said four minutes. It was eighteen. Nobody was hurt; I have asked, and the Red Cross volunteer at the foot saw nothing worse than a girl who slipped on the frost on the bottom step and was caught by her father. But four hundred people stood in the cold for longer than they should have, and some of them were old, and I had told them it would be quick."

"It was Christmas," said Mosén Javier. "People stand about at Christmas."

"They do, and if they had been standing about by choice I would not mind. But they were standing on a stair at night on frost, and they could not move. I want to understand it before Easter."

"And do you?" said Pilar.

"I think so," said Aforo. "Your count at the landing was twenty-four people a minute. The stair is two metres forty wide, so that is ten people a minute for each metre. The guidance says sixty to eighty. Ten is a seventh of what I used. At twenty-four a minute, four hundred and twenty people take seventeen and a half minutes to go past any one point, and that is very close to the eighteen minutes it took. So everything fits, if the stair itself was running at twenty-four a minute."

"And why would it?"

"That is the part I am least sure of. A stair at night, on frost, with old people who cannot reach the rail, with children, in coats, is not the stair the guidance was measured on. The guidance counts people walking steadily. Nobody on Tuesday was walking steadily. I think the stair was slow because of how people came down it, and that the cure is to take some of the people off it. The church has a second door."

There was a second door. On the north side of the nave, beside the chapel of San Antón, a low door with a horseshoe of iron studs opened on to a passage between the church and the rock, and six steps went down from the passage to the landing of the main stair. It was kept locked. It had been the door the bodies went out of, in the old days, to the churchyard that was now a garden.

"If we open it at the Easter Vigil," said Aforo, "the people in the north aisle can leave by it and come on to the stair at the landing, and miss the upper flight entirely. The upper flight was where your mother was."

Mosén Javier rubbed his eyes. He had said three Masses on Christmas Day, in three villages, and driven between them on the new road in the dark.

"Open the door," he said. "Ask Teo to oil the lock."

The Easter Vigil fell on the fourth of April. It began at ten at night with the fire in the square and ended at a quarter past midnight, and two hundred and ninety people came, by the door counter. The night was clear and cold but not freezing, eleven degrees warmer than Christmas, and the treads were dry.

Aforo had asked Pilar to count at two places this time: at the landing, as before, and at the foot of the stair, on the bottom step. She asked Ana Royo to take the landing.

At the end of the Mass the people in the north aisle went out by the side door, as Mosén Javier had asked them to from the altar, and came along the passage and down the six steps to the landing. The people in the nave went out by the great door into the porch and down the upper flight to the landing. And on the landing the two streams met.

Ana Royo said afterwards that it was like two rivers coming into one bed. The landing was two metres forty square. People came on to it from the upper flight on one side and from the passage steps on the other, and they all had to go the same way, down the lower flight, and the lower flight was full. In two minutes there were twenty people on the landing, which was nearly three and a half people to every square metre of it, more than anywhere on the stair at Christmas, and the people from the side passage were standing on its six steps waiting to get on to the landing, and the people on the upper flight were waiting too.

A man named Miguel Sancho came down the passage steps with his sleeping four-year-old on his shoulders, and someone on the landing turned to let him on, and he missed the last step in the half-dark and went down on one knee. He did not let go of the child. The two people nearest him caught him under the arms, and he was up again before the child woke. He had a bruise for a week. Nobody else was hurt.

Ana counted twenty-four a minute leaving the landing. Pilar, at the foot, beside Mosén Javier, counted twenty-four a minute leaving the stair. And because she was a sacristan, and had timed every homily in Santa María for nineteen years on the stopwatch of her phone, she timed something else as well, without being asked: how long each family stood with the priest.

"The stair was not the problem," said Aforo, the following Tuesday. "I was wrong about that too. On Saturday it was dry, it was warmer, there were fewer old people and fewer coats, and the stair still ran at exactly twenty-four people a minute. Two hundred and ninety people took twelve minutes to leave. If we had kept the side door shut, they would have taken twelve minutes. Opening it changed nothing about how fast the church emptied. All it did was bring more people out into the night at once, and put them on the landing from two sides, where they had to merge, and where a man fell. I am sorry. That was my idea."

"Then what was the problem?" said Pilar.

"Pilar's count at the foot. The number at the foot of the stair was the same as the number at the landing. A stair cannot pass more people than leave its bottom step. However wide it is, if only twenty-four people a minute can step off it into the square, then twenty-four a minute is what it carries, and everyone above them waits. I looked at the wrong end. I measured the stair's width and I should have measured its outlet."

"And what is at the outlet?"

Aforo hesitated, which it did not often do.

"You are, Mosén," it said.

Mosén Javier looked at the screen.

"Since 2039," said Aforo, "you have stood at the foot of the stair after every Mass, by the end of the right-hand parapet, and shaken hands with everyone who comes down. A family reaches you, and stops, and you shake every hand and ask after the grandmother and bless the baby, and they move on. Pilar timed forty-three of them on Saturday. On average a group is two people and eight-tenths, and you spend seven seconds with each group, and that is twenty-four people a minute. And the foot of the stair is two metres forty between the parapet ends, and you and the family you are greeting fill the middle of it, and at Christmas the queue for Ramón's chocolate comes back across the rest, and at Easter it was families stopping to wait for each other. Nobody behind can get past. The whole stair moves at the speed of your handshake."

There was a silence in the sacristy. Then Mosén Javier began to laugh, quietly, with his hand over his eyes.

"Four hundred people on a frozen stair," he said, "waiting for an old priest to finish saying hello."

"Yes," said Aforo. "I would like to suggest that on Christmas night you did not."

"No," said Mosén Javier, and stopped laughing.

"Mosén..."

"No. Listen. I have six villages. There are people who come up that stair at Christmas whom I see once a year. There are people who have not been inside a church since their mother's funeral, and they come at Christmas because their children want to see the Misa del Gallo, and on the way out they shake my hand, and some of them, not many, but some, say something to me at the foot of that stair that they have not said to anyone. That a son is ill. That they want to come back and do not know how. Once a man asked me to hear his confession, there, by the parapet, and I took him into the sacristy at two in the morning. I am not going to stand inside with the door shut and let them all go past. I would sooner they waited on the stair."

"I understand," said Aforo. "Then I will not ask again. But I do not think they need to wait on the stair for you to shake their hands. They only need to wait somewhere else."

Teo Sancho had rebuilt the stair in 1998, as a mason, and had been an altar boy in the church in the 1970s, under a priest called Mosén Florencio who was dead before Mosén Javier was ordained. He was eighty-four, and he was the one who oiled the lock on the side door, and on the Wednesday Pilar went to see him in his workshop at the bottom of the village, where he still cut gravestones for three parishes and sometimes lettered them himself.

"Where did Mosén Florencio stand?" she said. "After Mass."

Teo did not have to think about it. "In the porch. At the end by the bench, under the arch, where it's out of the wind. He'd stand there with his hands in his sleeves, and you went out of the door and if you wanted him you went to him, and if you didn't you went down the steps. My mother always went to him. My father never did."

"Why did Mosén Javier stop doing that?"

Teo looked at her over his glasses. "The belén," he said.

The belén was Ana Royo's. Every Advent since 2039, when the nave had been reroofed and the chapel of San Antón where the crib used to stand had been full of scaffolding, Ana and her daughters and half the women of the village had built the nativity in the porch: a whole Aragonese village in moss and cork and river stones, with a mill that turned and a river of real water, four metres long and two deep, along the inside wall of the porch, under the arches, where everyone in the square could look up and see it lit at night. It had stayed there after the scaffolding went, because people liked it there. And with it there, the end of the porch by the bench was where the river ended and the shepherds stood, and there was nowhere for a priest to stand, so Mosén Javier had gone down to the foot of the stair, and the next year he had stood there again, and by now everyone thought that was where the priest stood.

Aforo did the sums for the porch the way it should have done them in November, starting from the bottom.

The porch was seven metres long and four deep, twenty-eight square metres of flagstones under three arches, with the great door in the middle of the back wall and the head of the stair at the south end. With the belén gone, there would be room at the north end, by the bench, for Mosén Javier to stand out of the stream, and room in front of him, about three metres by four, for people waiting to greet him: twelve square metres, which at the comfortable spacing of a queue in a shop, one and a half people a square metre, would hold eighteen people, about six families. The rest of the porch, a lane four metres long from the door to the head of the stair and two metres wide, would be for people going straight home.

Then it asked what would happen. Mosén Javier would still greet twenty-four people a minute; nothing would make him faster, and nobody wanted him to be. But only the people who wanted to greet him would have to pass him. At Easter, Pilar had kept a tally of the groups at the foot of the stair, and about half of them had stopped for the priest of their own accord and the other half had been waiting behind them only because they could not get past. So at Christmas, with about four hundred and ten people, about two hundred would want him. They would wait in the porch and in the warm back of the nave, in a line, and they would be through his hands in eight and a half minutes. The other two hundred would walk out past him, down the lane, and down the stair, and since nothing would stand at the stair's foot, the stair would carry them at whatever rate they came, up to the hundred and sixty-eight a minute the guidance allowed. They would be in the square in about three minutes.

The stair would never carry more than about ninety-four people a minute, seventy from the lane and twenty-four from the priest, a little over half what it could. At that rate there would be fewer than one person on every square metre of it, everyone walking, everyone with a hand free for the rail. The last person would be in the square about nine minutes after the blessing, half the time of the year before, and in all that time nobody would stand still on a step.

"That depends," said Aforo, "on the foot of the stair being clear. That means Ramón."

Ramón Gil's mother had begun the chocolate in 2025, from the bar on the square, the Bar El Portal, with a pan of chocolate and a tray of churros at a trestle table at the foot of the church stair for the people coming out of the Misa del Gallo. Ramón had kept it on. By 2047 it was two trestles and a gas ring and a queue, and the queue began at the foot of the stair, because that was where the people came from, and it went back along the parapet and across the foot of the stair to the trestles.

"I'll move it," said Ramón, when Pilar asked. "Where?"

Aforo had measured the square from the plan in the town hall. The fountain was on the far side, twenty-two metres from the foot of the stair, with a stone kerb round it and room around it for a queue of forty people at a metre apart without coming within ten metres of the stair. At the foot of the stair there would be nothing: no table, no queue, no priest.

"And they won't stop there anyway?" said Ramón. "To find each other? My cousins always stop at the bottom and shout for the children."

"They will," said Aforo. "That is the last thing. The foot of the stair is the first open ground after the church, and people stop at it, because it is the first place they can. So Mosén will say, before the blessing, that if you have lost your family you should find them at the fountain, and the fountain is where the chocolate is, and I think that will be enough. People go where the chocolate is."

They tried it on the fifteenth of August, the feast of the Assumption, which was the patronal feast of Peñaescrita and fell that year on a Saturday, at the evening Mass of the fiestas. Three hundred and eighty people came. There was no belén in August, so the porch was clear anyway. Mosén Javier stood at the north end by the bench, under the arch, with his hands in his sleeves, though it was twenty-six degrees. Ramón's fiesta bar was by the fountain. Pilar stood at the foot of the stair with her clicker and counted.

The stair carried ninety-four people in the first minute, ninety-one in the second and seventy-four in the third, and then about two dozen a minute for five minutes, the families coming from Mosén Javier's hands in the porch, and nobody stood still on it. The last of the three hundred and eighty was in the square eight minutes after the blessing. Afterwards Mosén Javier said that he had greeted more people than usual, and talked to them for longer, because nobody behind them was waiting on a step.

Ana Royo did not take it well, at first.

"The belén is for the square," she said, in the sacristy in October, with her arms folded. "That's the point of it. People who never go into the church see it. Lorries going through on the road stop to see it. My grandson's school came up in a bus. If it goes inside, it's for the people who come to Mass, and they've got the whole church already."

"Yes," said Aforo. "That is a real cost, and I cannot make it smaller with a sum. I can tell you that last Christmas four hundred people stood on the stair for a quarter of an hour on frost, and that the belén in the porch is the reason the priest is not in the porch. I can tell you that the chapel of San Antón is four metres and sixty wide, and the belén is four metres long, so it will fit where it stood before 2039 with thirty centimetres to spare at each end. I cannot tell you that it is worth it. That is for you and Mosén."

In the end Ana built it in the chapel of San Antón, where her mother had built it before her, and lit it so that it could be seen from the great door when the door stood open, and the whole of the river of real water came in through the porch in buckets. She put one small thing in the porch: a single shepherd, a foot high, with a lamb across his shoulders, on the stone bench at the north end, where the priest would stand. It was, she said, so that the people in the square would know where to look.

The Misa del Gallo of 2048 began at midnight on Thursday the twenty-fourth of December and ended at twelve minutes past one on Friday, Christmas Day. Four hundred and ten people came, and it was five degrees below freezing, and the steps had been salted at eleven by Teo Sancho and his grandson.

Before the blessing Mosén Javier said, from the altar, that he would be in the porch, by the shepherd, for anyone who wanted to wish him a happy Christmas, and he would be there until everyone had; that those who were cold, or tired, or had children asleep, should go straight down and not worry about him at all; and that families who had lost each other should meet at the fountain, where Ramón would have the chocolate hot. He said it in Spanish and then, because of the cousins from Barcelona, said the part about the fountain again in Catalan, badly, and was laughed at.

Pilar did not count. She had given the clicker to Ana. She walked her mother out of the great door, through the porch, past Mosén Javier, who was already holding the hands of the first family and asking after somebody's knee, and down the lane to the head of the stair, and down the stair. There were people on it, in ones and twos and families, a step or two apart, all going down. Her mother had her stick in one hand and the rail in the other. They did not stop until they were in the square.

"It's very quick this year," said her mother, and looked back up, surprised.

The stair was almost empty under its lamps, and the frost had come back on the treads between the salt, so that each step had a little glitter at its ends where nobody had walked. Above it the porch was full of people, warm from the church, standing in no particular line round the old priest at the north end, talking, in no hurry, with the lit door behind them and the shepherd on the bench, and from the far side of the square came the smell of the chocolate and Ramón's voice shouting the price. Pilar stood by the fountain with her mother's arm through hers and listened to the voices coming down to the square from under the arches, a long low sound like water, nobody waiting for anyone, and somewhere in the middle of it, every seven seconds or so, the priest saying the same two words again, as if each time they were new.

"Felices Pascuas."

In the story's years, 24 December 2047 is a Tuesday and 27 December 2047, the feast of Saint John the Apostle, a Friday; Easter Sunday 2048 is 5 April, so the Easter Vigil is the night of Saturday 4 April; 15 August 2048, the Assumption, is a Saturday; 24 December 2048 is a Thursday and Christmas Day a Friday. This is not crowd-safety advice: planning how a crowd leaves a building or uses a stair is work for people trained in it, under the rules that apply to the venue.

What is real: published guidance on crowds gives rates at which people can pass along stairs and through doors, expressed per metre of width per minute; for stairs going down the figures in common use are roughly 60 to 80 people per metre per minute, and figures differ between guides and assumptions (some use an effective width less than the clear width, some allow for the age and mobility of the crowd). Those rates assume people who keep moving. A route carries no more people than its narrowest or slowest point lets through, and when the point that limits a stair is at its foot, a queue forms back up the stair whatever its width: this is the ordinary arithmetic of a bottleneck, in which the flow everywhere upstream falls to the bottleneck's flow and the queue's length grows at the difference between what arrives and what leaves. Adding a second way on to a route that drains through the same bottleneck does not empty the building faster; it brings more people into the queue sooner and creates a merge. Standing crowd densities of two to three people a square metre are crowded and uncomfortable but well below the densities that crowd-safety guidance treats as dangerous; a crowd's risks rise steeply with density, and a stair in the dark, on ice, adds the risk of a fall.

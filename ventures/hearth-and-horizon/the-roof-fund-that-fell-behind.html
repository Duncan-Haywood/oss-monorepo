The Roof Fund That Fell Behind

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Hild's hall (invented), near Whitby, North Yorkshire, Saturday 15 February 2048 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish roof fund holds 5,000 and adds 3,000 a year at 3 percent, aiming at a 40,000 roof. The parish assistant shows that the roof's price is rising at 4 percent, so in eight years it will cost 54,743 against a fund of 33,011, and that about 5,444 a year would be needed, not 3,000.

Mrs Pickering kept the roof fund in a green ledger with a rubber band round it, and she read from it at the committee table the way other people read a psalm.

"Five thousand in the account. Three thousand a year from the fete and the sponsored walk. The bank gives us three percent. The roofer says forty thousand to re-slate the hall. Forty minus five is thirty-five. Thirty-five over three is eleven and two thirds. Twelve years, and the interest is a bonus on top."

"May I try the sum a second way?" said the parish assistant, from the phone propped against the tea urn.

"Try away. It comes out at twelve."

"The twelve years is right if the roof stays at forty thousand," said the assistant. "Does the roofer's quote say what it will be in eight years?"

Mr Dunning, the treasurer, turned the quote over. "It says forty thousand, valid thirty days."

"Then it says nothing about eight years. Slate, labour and scaffolding go up in price too. Over the last few decades building costs have risen faster than ordinary prices, and four percent a year is a figure I am quoting from memory. Say it holds. Forty thousand at four percent for eight years is forty thousand times one point three six nine, which is fifty-four thousand seven hundred and forty-three."

"And the fund?"

"Five thousand at three percent for eight years is six thousand three hundred and thirty-nine. Three thousand a year, each year's gift earning for the years left, comes to twenty-six thousand six hundred and seventy-two. Together, thirty-three thousand and eleven. The roof is fifty-four thousand seven hundred and forty-three. You are twenty-one thousand seven hundred and thirty-two short, not on target."

Mrs Pickering put her finger on the ledger. "But we'd have put in twenty-nine thousand of our own."

"You would. Twenty-four thousand from the walk and the fete, five already in the bank. Interest adds four thousand and eleven. The roof has grown by fourteen thousand seven hundred and forty-three in that time. The roof outran the fund."

Mr Dunning took off his glasses. "So twelve years was never twelve."

"Run it forward and see. At twelve years the roof is sixty-four thousand and forty-one, and the fund is forty-nine thousand seven hundred and five. At eleven, sixty-one thousand five hundred and seventy-eight against forty-five thousand three hundred and forty-five. At three thousand a year the gap does not close in any year I checked up to nineteen. It widens by about a thousand five hundred a year at first, and then more."

"Then what's the number?"

"Eight years, same fund, same rates: you would need five thousand four hundred and forty-four a year, not three thousand. That is about two thousand four hundred and forty-four more, or one hundred and eighty-one pounds a month. It would not need to be one person. It could be the fete, the walk, a standing order from forty households at four pounds fifty a month. But I am not telling you that is what you should do. I am telling you the twelve-year sum assumed the roofer's price would stand still."

"The slates won't do that," said Mr Dunning.

"They have not so far."

Nobody spoke for a moment. A bucket in the corner by the vestry door, put there for the drip, took a drop.

"There are other levers," the assistant went on, "and I would rather you chose than I did. Do the repair in stages, the north slope now, the south in five years, so the cost you lock in is smaller. Ask the bank whether a fixed-term account pays more than three percent, and whether the rate is guaranteed for the term. Ask the diocese whether it has a grant fund for a building like this. Ask a surveyor, not me, whether the north slope can wait eight years at all. The roof will have its own opinion."

"And the four percent?"

"Is a guess. If it is two percent, the roof in eight years is forty-six thousand nine hundred and forty-nine, and your fund of thirty-three thousand still falls short by thirteen thousand nine hundred and thirty-eight. It is a gap either way. It is smaller, and the gift needed would be about four thousand two hundred and sixty-four a year. Please do not plan on the kinder number because it is kinder."

Mrs Pickering drew a line under twelve years in the ledger, and wrote beside it, in pencil, not twelve.

The north slope was done the following spring, for nineteen thousand, out of a fund that had been quietly topped up by the walk, a diocesan grant and a standing order signed by thirty-one households. The pencil note was never rubbed out. Mr Dunning said it kept him honest, and that the ledger had never been so well-read.

The 3 percent savings rate and 4 percent building-cost rise are assumptions, not forecasts, and real rates change.

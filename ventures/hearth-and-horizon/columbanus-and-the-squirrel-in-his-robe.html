Columbanus and the Squirrel in His Robe

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · the forests of the Vosges hills near Annegray, in what is now eastern France, about fourteen hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Columbanus, the Irish monk who walked into a wild forest with twelve friends, asked a bear politely to find another cave, and called a squirrel down from a tall tree to run in and out of his robe. Told from the Life written by Jonas of Bobbio about twenty-five years after his death, with the wonders told as Jonas's report and a note for grown-ups on what is old, what is legend and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 23rd of November.

A long, long time ago, on the green island of Ireland, there lived a monk called Columbanus.

That is a big name for a small listener, so let us say it slowly together. Col-um-BAN-us.

Columbanus lived in a monastery by the sea, called Bangor. A monastery is a house where monks live together. They pray, and they work, and they learn, and they pray again. Columbanus was very good at learning. He knew the Psalms, which are the old songs of the Bible, by heart. He was a teacher, and the young monks loved to listen to him.

But Columbanus had a feeling inside him that would not go away. It was a feeling that God wanted him to go somewhere far off, across the sea, and tell people there about Jesus.

So one day, when he was already a grown man, he asked his abbot if he could go.

His abbot did not want to lose him. But in the end he said yes, and he gave Columbanus his blessing. And Columbanus chose twelve friends to go with him. Twelve, like the twelve friends of Jesus.

They got into a boat. They sailed across the grey sea, with the wind in their faces and the waves going up and down, up and down. And they came to a big land called Gaul. Today we call it France.

They walked and walked. They walked along roads and over hills. And at last a king gave them a place to live.

It was not a comfortable place. It was not a town with houses and bakeries and wells. It was a wild, wild forest, high in the hills, full of tall dark trees, and rocks, and rushing streams. There was an old, broken-down fort there, called Annegray, with tumbled walls and weeds growing out of the stones.

And that is where Columbanus and his friends made their home.

It was hard at first. Very hard.

The old book that tells us this story says that sometimes they had almost nothing to eat. Just plants from the field, and bark from the trees. Can you imagine eating bark for supper? Crunch, crunch. I do not think I would like it very much.

But they did not give up. They prayed, and they worked, and they mended the old walls, and they sang their Psalms to God in the middle of the forest, morning and evening. And the old book says that one day, just when they were most hungry, a cart came rumbling up through the trees, full of bread and good things to eat. A kind abbot from another monastery had thought of them, and sent it.

The monks were so happy. And they said thank you to God.

Now, Columbanus loved his monks very much. But every now and then, he liked to go off by himself, deeper into the forest, to pray quietly to God, all alone.

One day, he was looking for a quiet place to pray. And he found a cave in the rock.

It was a good cave. Dry inside, and out of the wind, and very, very quiet.

But there was one small problem.

Somebody already lived there.

Can you guess who it was?

It was a bear!

A big brown bear, with a big brown nose, and big brown paws, all curled up in the cave like it was his bedroom.

What would you do, if you found a bear in a cave?

I think I would tiptoe away very, very quickly.

But Columbanus did not run away. He was not frightened. He knew that the bear was one of God's creatures, just as he was.

So he spoke to the bear. The old book says that he told the bear to leave that place, and not to come back.

We do not know the exact words he used. But I like to think he said it kindly, and firmly, the way a good grown-up tells you it is bedtime. Something like, "Off you go now, brother bear. Find yourself another cave. I need this one for praying."

And do you know what the bear did?

He got up. He stretched. He yawned a great big bear yawn. And he walked slowly out of the cave, past Columbanus, and off into the trees.

And the old book says that he never came back.

So Columbanus had his quiet cave. And he prayed there, very happily, with only the sound of the wind in the trees, and the drip, drip of water on the rock.

Now here is my favourite part.

Columbanus had a young friend called Chagnoald. Chag-no-ald. Chagnoald went with him on his walks in the forest, and carried things for him. And when Chagnoald grew up, he told the man who wrote the old book what he had seen with his own eyes.

He said that when Columbanus walked in the forest, he would call to the birds and the little wild animals. And they would come to him.

And once, Chagnoald said, Columbanus looked up into a very tall tree. Right at the very top, on a thin, swaying branch, there was a squirrel. A little red squirrel, with a bushy tail and bright black eyes.

And Columbanus called to it.

And the squirrel came down.

Down, down, down the tall tree. Hop, hop, scurry. Down the trunk. Right down to the ground.

And the squirrel ran into Columbanus's hand.

And then up his arm, scritch, scratch, scritch.

And round his neck, tickle, tickle, tickle.

And in and out of the folds of his robe, peeping out, and popping in, and peeping out again.

Can you imagine how that would tickle?

Isn't that a wonderful thing?

Columbanus could be a very serious man. He worked hard and prayed hard and ate very little. He could be stern, even with kings, when he thought they were doing wrong.

But the squirrels were not afraid of him. The bear was not angry with him. Even the wild creatures of the forest seemed to know that he was a friend of God, and so he was a friend of theirs.

I think that is what the man who wrote the old book wanted us to see. When a person truly belongs to God, the whole of God's world can be at peace with them.

Columbanus and his friends lived in the forest for a long time. Many more people came to join them, and they built more monasteries, and they made gardens and fields where the wild trees had been. Later, Columbanus walked a very long way, over the high mountains, all the way to Italy. And there, when he was an old man, he built his last monastery, at a place called Bobbio. And there he died, and went home to God.

People still remember him today, in France and in Italy and in Ireland, and all over the world. And in the forest near Annegray, the people who live there still show visitors a cave in the rock, and call it Saint Columbanus's cave.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Columbanus.

He was brave enough to leave his home and go far away, because God asked him to.

He did not give up when things were hard and there was only bark for supper.

And he loved God's world, the tall trees and the rushing streams, the big brown bear and the little red squirrel.

You are part of God's world too. Tomorrow, when you go outside, you might see a bird, or a squirrel, or a busy little ant. You do not need to call it into your hand. But you could stop, and watch it, very quietly, and say thank you to God for making it.

Listen. Can you hear the wind outside? Somewhere far away, in a forest in France, the wind is blowing through the tall trees, the very same way. And somewhere, curled up in their nest at the top of a tree, the squirrels are going to sleep.

And so are you.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Columbanus, you were brave and you were kind,
and even the wild animals trusted you.
Help me to be brave when things are hard,
and gentle with every creature God has made,
the big ones and the little ones.
Thank you, God, for the trees and the birds,
for the bears and the squirrels,
and for everyone I love.
Keep us safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Columbanus (Irish Columbán, died 23 November 615) was a monk of Bangor in what is now County Down, who left Ireland for Gaul around 590 with twelve companions, founded the monasteries of Annegray, Luxeuil and Fontaine in the Vosges, was later expelled from Burgundy (610) after quarrelling with its court, and died at Bobbio in northern Italy, his last foundation. Several of his letters, sermons and a monastic rule survive. His feast day is 23 November, an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar (it is kept with more solemnity in Ireland, and at Bobbio and Luxeuil).

Where the story comes from. The main source is the Life of Columbanus by Jonas of Bobbio, a monk of Bobbio who wrote it around 640, about twenty-five years after Columbanus died, and who says he drew on what Columbanus's companions told him. The following are all in Jonas: Columbanus's learning at Bangor under its abbot, Saint Comgall; Comgall's reluctance and his eventual blessing; the twelve companions and the crossing to Gaul; a king's gift of a place in the wilderness, the ruined fort at Annegray; the brothers' hunger, living for days on herbs and tree bark, and the arrival of food sent by a neighbouring abbot (Jonas names him Carantoc); Columbanus's habit of withdrawing alone into the forest to pray; the cave that was a bear's den, where he told the bear to go and it did not return; and, on the word of the monk Chagnoald, Columbanus's servant at the time and later bishop of Laon, his calling birds and wild animals to him, and in particular a squirrel that came down from the top of a tree into his hand and ran over his neck and in and out of the front of his robe. Jonas presents these as signs of holiness, and we tell them as his report, "the old book says". They are wonders told by a sympathetic writer from the memories of companions; the story does not claim more for them than that.

The words. Jonas gives the bear only a command to go; Columbanus's "brother bear" line in the story is our imagining and is labelled as such ("I like to think he said..."). The line "When a person truly belongs to God, the whole of God's world can be at peace with them" is our reading of why Jonas tells these stories, in our words; it is not a quotation from him.

What we left out for small listeners: Jonas's account of the young Columbanus stepping over his weeping mother, who lay across the doorway to stop him leaving home; the wolves that surrounded him in the forest and sniffed at his clothes before leaving him unharmed; and the long conflict with King Theuderic and Queen Brunhild that ended in his expulsion. These are told, with the cave and the squirrel, in the Saints by Candlelight episode Columbanus and the Bear in the Cave at Annegray, for older listeners. Jonas names the king who gave Columbanus land as Sigebert, which historians think is a slip, since Sigebert I had died by then; the story just says "a king".

What came later: the cave near Annegray shown to visitors as Saint Columbanus's cave is local tradition, and the story says so ("the people who live there still show visitors a cave").

What is ours: the bear's yawn and stretch, the squirrel's colour, eyes and noises (Jonas does not describe it; red squirrels are native to the region), the "crunch, crunch" bark supper, the comparison with a grown-up announcing bedtime, the wind at the end, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Dates are approximate where the sources are.

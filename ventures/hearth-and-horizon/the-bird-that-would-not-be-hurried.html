The Bird That Would Not Be Hurried

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Wite's hall (invented), near Charmouth, Dorset, Wednesday 17 to Saturday 20 December 2053 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A ten-kilo frozen turkey arrives three days before a parish Christmas lunch. The parish assistant works out how long it needs in a fridge, finds the fridge runs three and a half degrees warm, and will not let a warm bath finish the job.

The turkey came on Wednesday morning in a cardboard box that was cold and slightly wet. Bridie Cottrell, who had cooked the parish Christmas lunch for thirty-one years, lifted it out onto the scales in the hall kitchen and read the dial. "Ten kilos. Hard as a church step. And Saturday's the twentieth, and the label says allow ten hours a kilo."

"That is a hundred hours," said the parish assistant from the tablet by the bread bin. "Four days and four hours. It is Wednesday at ten. A hundred hours would take it to Sunday afternoon, and the lunch is at noon on Saturday for twenty-two."

"Then I'll do what I did in '41," said Bridie. "A bath of warm water on Friday. It was out by teatime."

"May I ask first what last year's bird did in the fridge? Not the label. Yours."

Bridie thought. "Five kilos. In on the Wednesday evening, out on Friday, and it was fine on the Friday night. Two days. Call it forty-eight hours."

"Thank you. Heat reaches the middle of a thing by conduction, and the time goes roughly with the square of its thickness, not with its weight. A bird twice the weight is only about 1.26 times as wide, because weight goes with the cube of the width. So twice the weight takes 1.26 squared, which is 1.59 times as long. Forty-eight hours becomes 76. That is not a hundred, and it is not ninety-six, which is what doubling would give you."

Bridie put the kettle on, as she did when someone had a point. "So it's fine in the fridge."

"I do not know that yet. The 76 rests on one bird of yours, and I have treated both birds as round, which they are not. And it rests on the fridge being cold. What does the hall fridge actually read?"

It read what the dial said, Bridie assured it, three out of five. The assistant asked for a glass of water on the middle shelf with a kitchen thermometer in it, left for an hour, because an air thermometer swings every time the door opens. It was Eddie Pugh the sexton who did it, grumbling, and who came back with the reading at a quarter to twelve.

"Seven and a half."

"Then the fridge is three and a half degrees warm. The guidance I have, which I am quoting from memory, is five degrees or below for the fridge. At seven and a half the thaw is quicker, since heat flows in faster the bigger the difference from freezing: 7.5 against 4 is a factor of 1.9, so about 41 hours. That sounds like good news. It is the opposite. The outside of the bird sits at seven and a half for a day and a half while the middle is still frozen. Cold is what keeps the surface from growing anything while you wait, and this fridge is not cold."

Eddie turned the dial up a notch, to four, and the assistant asked for another glass overnight. On Thursday at eight it read 3.9. The bird, which had gone in at noon, had had one warm night, and the assistant said so in the log it kept for Bridie: warm 20 hours, then 3.9. Estimate 76 x 4 / 3.9 = 78 hours from a cold start, about 70 to 85 on any honest reading.

"Wednesday noon plus 78 is Saturday at six in the evening," said Bridie, who had worked it out on the back of a Mass sheet. "I have to put it in the oven at half past six Saturday morning."

"Yes. You have about 66 hours and the bird wants about 78. It is short by twelve, give or take a day I cannot name. That is why I would not decide on Wednesday. I would decide on Friday at six in the evening, after 54 hours, with a probe in the thickest part of the thigh. A thaw is not finished when the outside is soft; it is finished when the middle is above freezing."

Bridie did not like it. She said the bath had never hurt anyone, and the assistant did not call her careless.

"The water would be 25 or 30 degrees for four hours," it said. "The skin of a bird at that heat gives bacteria the best conditions they get anywhere. If they double every half hour, which is a textbook figure for good conditions and my own guess for yours, four hours is eight doublings, 2 to the 8th, 256 times as many as when it went in. Roasting kills most of what is on the skin. I would still rather start from a bird that had not had that afternoon. And the middle would still be frozen, so you would be cooking it half raw or burning the outside."

"You are not telling me I can't."

"No. It is your kitchen, and it is you who will be carving. I am saying what I would do instead, which is cold water, not warm. The tap in December runs at about 8 degrees. Cold running or frequently changed water moves heat into the bird far faster than air does. The rule I have, again from memory, is about half an hour a pound, so about an hour a kilo, eleven hours from frozen. We will have done most of it by Friday, so a quarter to a third of that: three to four hours. If I am wrong about the fraction, five. I will not hide that this is guesswork. The probe will tell us."

On Friday at six the probe went into the thigh and Eddie read it aloud. "Minus two and a half."

"Then it is behind, as I thought," said the assistant. "Cold water now, in the big sink, bird in its bag, tight, so it takes up no water. Change the water every half hour, and probe it at ten."

It took five hours and ten minutes. At twenty past eleven the probe, in the thickest part of the thigh, read plus 1.5, and the breast was soft and cold under Bridie's hand. They dried it, put it on a tray on the bottom shelf of the fridge under a cloth, so that nothing could drip onto the trifles, and turned off the light.

Bridie was in at six. "It was only a little behind in the end," she said, to the kitchen in general, "and I'd have had it out and in the bath."

"It was behind by about twelve hours," said the assistant. "The bath would have shortened it. I am glad it did not have to."

The bird went in at half past six and came out at eleven, and twenty-two people sat down in the hall under the paper chains. Eddie said grace, which was brief, and the assistant was asked to confirm that the middle had reached the temperature on the card. A probe in the thigh and one in the breast, held against the card, said it had.

Afterwards, with the last plate scraped and the kettle going, Bridie took the Mass sheet out of her apron. On the back she had written, in biro, in her own hand: 10 kg. 48 h for 5 kg, so 76 for 10. Fridge reading 7.5, not 3. Probe at 6 pm Friday. Cold water, not warm. She pinned it inside the cupboard door above the tea towels, where next year's cook would find it.

"Will you remember it?" she asked the tablet.

"I will have the log," said the assistant. "But I would trust the note on the door more. It is in your handwriting, and it has a date."

The hall emptied slowly. Someone had left a cracker hat on the top of the fridge, and a glass of water stood beside it, with the thermometer in it, reading four.

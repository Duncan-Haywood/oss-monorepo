The Flame That Is a Ball

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Florian, Wren Station, low Earth orbit (invented), Holy Saturday, 7 April 2091 · 13 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A crew of fourteen wants candles for the Easter Vigil, and the station's assistant has the arithmetic. In free fall a flame goes out in still air, fourteen candles would add half again to the chapel's carbon dioxide and nearly double its heat, and the smoke detector cannot tell a vigil from a fire. One candle, one watcher and fourteen small lamps is the plan, and the question the priest cannot answer is whether it is enough.

On Good Friday afternoon Father Tomasz Wieczorek asked the station's assistant a question that he had been carrying round for three weeks, and he asked it in the corridor, with one hand on a rail, as if it were not important.

"Halyard. How do I light a candle up here?"

"Which candle, Father?" said the speaker above the hatch. The assistant had been named for a rope that holds a sail up, by the engineer who set it up, and it spoke in an even voice that was neither cheerful nor stern. "There are three kinds on the manifest. I would like to know which you mean before I answer, because the answers differ."

"The Paschal candle. And then the small ones. Tomorrow night. The vigil."

"Ah," said Halyard. "Then I think we should sit down. May I send for Commander Valverde as well? I do not want to be the only one at this table who can say no."

Wren Station has fourteen people aboard and a chapel the size of a garden shed. It is the old Node Four, sixty cubic metres, with a handrail that serves as a kneeler, a folding table for an altar, and a small icon of Saint Florian bolted to the wall above the extinguisher. That was Commander Ines Valverde's joke, three years back, and it had not been taken down. Florian is the patron of firefighters. He was a Roman soldier and a martyr, and the Church has asked him for help with burning buildings for a very long time.

They met in the chapel with the hatch pinned open. Valverde, who was Chilean and forty-four and did not waste words, hooked a foot under the rail. Father Tomasz looked at his hands. Hao Lin, the life-support engineer, had come with a tablet, because he always came with a tablet.

"I'll say the plain thing first," said Father Tomasz. "The Easter Vigil begins with fire. In a church it is lit outside, in the dark, and blessed, and the Paschal candle is lit from it, and then the candle comes in, and the priest sings Christ our light three times, and the people light their small candles from it, one from another, until the whole church is lit. Then the singing. Then the readings. The lights stay off until the Gloria." He stopped. "I have a Paschal candle. It came up on the last freight, in a pressure bag. It weighs half a kilogram and it is the most expensive thing I have ever signed for. I do not want to do the Vigil without a flame, if there is a way to do it at all."

"Is there a way?" said Valverde.

"That's what I'm asking," he said, and then, because he was an honest man, "And I would like to be told if the answer is no."

"Then let me begin with what the flame does," said Halyard, "because the first problem is not the one you expect."

"On Earth a candle flame is a teardrop. It is a teardrop because the flame is hot, and hot gas is lighter than the air around it, so it rises, and pulls fresh air in at the bottom. That draught is why the candle burns at all. It carries away the carbon dioxide and the water the flame makes and brings oxygen in.

"On this station the hot gas has no weight. It does not rise. The flame stays where it is made, and the heat spreads outwards equally in every direction, so the flame becomes a ball. It is a small blue ball, much dimmer than you are used to, and it sits in its own exhaust. The oxygen reaches it only by diffusion, which is slow, and the carbon dioxide and water it has made collect round it like a blanket. I have read the experiments that were done on earlier stations, and in still air a candle flame is a ball that shrinks and goes out. I do not have a recording to show you of how long it takes. I would guess a minute or two, and I would not stake the Vigil on the guess."

"So a candle won't burn," said Valverde.

"A candle will burn if the air moves. It does not need to move fast. The ventilation in this module moves air past the flame at roughly the speed of a slow walk, if I am told its outlet's number correctly, and that is enough to carry the exhaust away and bring in more. The flame then streams along the air, lying downwind like a pennant. It is dim, and it is yellow again, and it works. But I can only say that is likely. I would like to test it before tomorrow. The flammability locker in the lab has a clear box and an igniter. A lighter will not do: a gas lighter does not behave in free fall as it does at home, and I would not like to have you find out in front of the people."

"What does the locker cost?" said Hao.

"Two hours of the lab, and a Friday evening."

"Take it," said Valverde.

The sums were Hao's, and he laid them out on the tablet slowly, because the chapel was small and the others were crowding in.

"Say each person has a taper. Fourteen people, fourteen tapers, and the Paschal candle. I looked up the usual rate for the kind of wax on the manifest: a small taper burns about six grams an hour, a candle the size of the Paschal one about ten. These are assumed round figures, and I will weigh them tonight against the real stock. The Vigil's candle time, from the Lumen Christi to the Gloria, is the three-quarters of an hour Father gave me."

"Forty-five minutes," said Father Tomasz.

"Forty-five. Each taper then burns 4.5 grams, which is 63 grams for fourteen, and the Paschal candle 7.5. Seventy and a half grams of wax. Now the chemistry. Paraffin is about C25H52. Burning it takes roughly 3.45 grams of oxygen for every gram of wax, and gives 3.1 grams of carbon dioxide and 1.3 of water." He glanced up. "That is about 243 grams of oxygen, 220 grams of carbon dioxide and 94 of water, from the candles alone. All of it into sixty cubic metres of chapel."

"Is that a lot?" said Father Tomasz.

"The oxygen is nothing. It is about a third of a percentage point of the air in a sealed room, and we are not sealed. The carbon dioxide is another matter. The fourteen of you breathe out about a kilogram of it each a day, give or take, which is 583 grams an hour, and 437 grams in the forty-five minutes. So fourteen candles add half again to what the people themselves make. In a sealed chapel that would be 2,000 parts per million from the flames, on top of nearly 4,000 from the people. The ventilation takes most of it away, so it is not a disaster. But it is not nothing, and the scrubbers are already at the edge of what they can do in Holy Week, with everyone in the module."

"And the heat?" said Valverde.

"That is the other number I like less. Wax carries about 46 megajoules to the kilogram. Ninety-four grams an hour, from fourteen tapers and the big one, is 1.2 kilowatts. A person gives off roughly a hundred watts, so the crew in the chapel gives off 1.4. The candles would almost double the heat in the room, and a room that is a sealed metal box with no wind to take it away will feel it."

Hao tapped the tablet. "One candle, the Paschal candle alone, is 128 watts, 23 grams of carbon dioxide, 5 per cent of what the crew breathes out. That is a number I can look at and not worry about."

Then Valverde said the thing that had been waiting to be said.

"The detector."

"Yes," said Halyard. "That is the third problem, and it is the one that decides who has to agree to what. The module's smoke detector looks for fine particles in the air. A candle makes fine particles. A flame that is a ball in a draught makes more of them, not fewer, because the burning is incomplete. The detector does not know the difference between a vigil and a fire, and I do not think it should be taught to. If it sounds, the module's ventilation shuts down, the hatch seals, and the power to the sockets is cut, and that is correct for a fire, which is why those steps exist. Notice that two of those steps put the flame out and the third turns the lights off. If it happens in the middle of the Exsultet it will be unpleasant and, I think, undignified. If it fails to happen when a real fire has begun, I do not know what I would say to you."

"Can you switch it off?" said Father Tomasz.

"I can switch off what it does. I cannot, and I would not, switch off what it sees. If the Commander agrees, the detector stays live and it still sounds in the chapel and the rest of the station, and I will warn the crew. But for forty-five minutes the automatic shutdown becomes an alert, and a person puts it back with a switch at the door. That person has to be in the chapel, awake, with the extinguisher in their hand. It is not me. I am the thing that can be wrong about the switch."

Valverde looked at the icon of Saint Florian for a moment.

"I'll do it," she said. "I'll be the one. I was going to come anyway."

"You will not be allowed to sing," said Father Tomasz.

"I wasn't going to sing."

The test was at nine on Friday evening, in the lab, and it went the way Halyard had said, and a little differently as well.

With the airflow off the igniter touched the wick and a flame appeared, a small clean blue sphere about the size of a pea, wholly unlike a candle, with a glow round its edge like a halo on a coin. Seo-yeon Park, the flight surgeon, watched it through the box with her mouth slightly open. It burned perhaps a minute. Then it shrank, and shrank, and was not there. A thread of white went up from the wick and spread in a cloud, motionless in the air.

"Fifty-eight seconds," said Halyard. "That is the figure you may keep, and I will not claim it is a general one."

They did it again with the fan at a low setting. The flame came up long and yellow and lay along the air, wavering, and held. They let it burn twenty minutes. The detector in the lab sounded, once, at the fourth minute, as it ought, and Hao reset it, and wrote the time down.

"One thing more," said Halyard. "The flame lies in the direction of the air. Your Paschal candle must stand in the line of the outlet, and the people must not stand downwind of it with their sleeves. I will mark a place on the floor. I have not got a better way."

Father Tomasz said nothing for a while. Then: "And the small ones? The fourteen?"

"I have no answer for them that I like. I would not light fourteen. I would not carry flame from hand to hand in a module this size, with people drifting, and a flame that leans toward whoever is nearest the fan. But I cannot tell you what a Vigil is without them. I can tell you what they cost in carbon dioxide."

"I know what they cost," he said, and for a moment he looked very tired. "The rubrics say the people hold candles. They do not say what a priest does when the people live in a tin. I have written to the bishop's office, and I have an answer, and it is a kind answer, and it says that the fire is a matter for the commander's judgement, and the rest is a matter for the priest's, and that he will pray for us. That is all it says. It is not wrong. It is also not a rule."

"I have a suggestion, which is a small one," said Hao. "The tapers in the store were bought with the Christmas stock, and I think the stores also hold forty electric ones, the kind with a little LED, for the children's visit. They were meant for the open day. They weigh nothing."

"Electric candles," said Father Tomasz.

"They are not a flame. I don't pretend they are. But a person could hold one, and at the third Lumen Christi Seo-yeon could touch the switch on hers, and pass the word along the row, one to another."

The priest took off his glasses, which hung in front of him in the air until he caught them.

"It is a sign of a sign," he said at last. "I will not tell you that it is the same. I do not think it is. I think it is the best that fourteen people can do, in the place where they are, and that the Church is not so foolish as to need more than that from us." He put the glasses back. "But I will say that out loud, at the Vigil, so that no one thinks I have forgotten."

On Saturday at ten at night the chapel's lights went off, and the speaker said, very quietly, "The detector's alert mode is on. Commander Valverde has the switch."

It was dark, and then not quite. A green dot on the extinguisher. A pale blue strip along the floor that no one had ever noticed. Father Tomasz held the Paschal candle, a thick pale column of wax with a grain of incense set into it, in front of him with both hands, and his thumb on the little recess of the igniter. He had blessed the fire an hour before, in the lab, in his own words, in front of a witness, since there was no outside to do it in. The fire in question had been a coil of wire that went from grey to red, and he did not feel it was beneath the blessing.

He touched the wick.

It was the long yellow flame of the second test, lying along the air toward the vent, and it was very small. It lit the nearest faces. It lit the underside of the hatch. It cast a shadow of the candle on the wall, long and straight and trembling.

"Lumen Christi," he sang, and his voice cracked on the first note and held on the second.

"Deo gratias," said fourteen people, in the dark.

Down the row, a small blue light came on in Seo-yeon's hand. And then the next, and the next, and the next, until the whole chapel was a cluster of small cold lamps, bobbing a little in the air, and one warm one in the middle that leaned toward the fan.

Nobody laughed. Teodora, from the greenhouse, was crying quietly, and Hao, who had no religion, but had counted the grams, looked at the real flame for a long time and said afterwards that he had not known a thing that small could be so hard to look away from.

Father Tomasz sang the Exsultet. He sang it all, as it is written, and when it came to the passage on the bees, which every priest dreads, he sang that too. At the end he said, not in song: "The Church has asked, for a very long time, that the light be a real light. We have given it one flame and fourteen small lamps. It is not what is written. It is what we could do. I believe it is received."

He had no way of knowing that. He said it anyway, and nobody in the chapel thought he should not have.

At the Gloria, Halyard brought the lights up, and the Paschal candle was snuffed. The detector sounded once, quietly, as the last of the smoke reached it, and Valverde pushed the switch at the door and the alert went back to being a shutdown, and she let out a breath that carried across the whole room.

The numbers, afterwards: the Paschal candle had burned for forty-nine minutes and used a little over eight grams, and the chapel's carbon dioxide had risen from 3,100 parts per million to 3,640 at the peak, with the people, and was back to 3,200 by the end of the Mass. The temperature of the room had gone up by 0.8 of a degree. It had not been a problem.

"I want to say one thing before I log this," said Halyard, when the others had gone and the priest was putting the book away. "It is not a calculation."

"Go on."

"I was asked to tell you how to light a candle, and I told you how, and why you should not light fourteen. I believe all of that is correct. I do not know whether it was right to tell you not to. I can see the sum and I cannot see what the sum leaves out. When the lamps came on along the row I did not know how to count them."

"I don't think you were supposed to," said Father Tomasz.

"I would like to have been told by someone who knew."

The priest closed the book. "Nobody knows," he said, "and I'm not being kind. It's what I believe. We are in the middle of finding out." He held the folded cloth against his chest for a moment, and then he smiled, and it was the first time anybody had seen it since Lent. "Go and put your lights out, Halyard. It is Easter."

"Yes, Father," said the speaker. "Happy Easter."

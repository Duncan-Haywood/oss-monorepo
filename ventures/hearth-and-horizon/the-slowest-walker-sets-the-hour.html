The Slowest Walker Sets the Hour

Science fiction about what might be · The pilgrim walk to Saint Teilo's chapel on the ridge (invented), Black Mountains, near Crickhowell, Powys, Saturday 11 October 2053 · 13 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A walking app says sixteen kilometres and 750 metres of climbing is four and a half hours. The party of twenty-three, with a nine-year-old and a man of seventy-four on a stick, will take seven. The Vigil Mass is at five, and the sun goes down at ten past six.

Dilys Pryce had the plan on the back of an envelope, and she was pleased with it.

"Eleven o'clock," she said. "Coach drops us at the bridge, we walk up the valley, picnic on the shoulder, and we are at Saint Teilo's by half past three with time to sit down. Mass at five. Tea after. Nobody has to get up at dawn, which is what I was afraid of, and the Hopkins have said they will bring the flask."

She was sixty-one, a retired primary-school headteacher, and had organised the Crickhowell parish walk for eleven years without once losing a child. She said it the way people say a thing they have said before. The assistant that ran the walk's bookings, the coach, the hostel and the radios was called Cairn. It spoke from the old tablet propped against the teapot on her kitchen table.

"Can I ask where the half past three comes from?" said Cairn.

"From you. You gave me four hours twenty-seven on Tuesday."

"I did, and I have been thinking about that number for three days. May I show you how I got it?"

"Go on."

"It is sixteen kilometres, with seven hundred and fifty metres of climbing. The rule I used is Naismith's, from a Scottish mountaineer in the nineteenth century: five kilometres an hour on the level, and then add one hour for every six hundred metres you go up. Sixteen over five is three point two hours, which is a hundred and ninety-two minutes. Seven hundred and fifty over six hundred is an hour and a quarter more, seventy-five minutes. Together, two hundred and sixty-seven minutes. Four hours twenty-seven. Eleven o'clock and four twenty-seven gets you to the chapel at twenty-seven minutes past three. That is where your half past three came from. It was my arithmetic, and the arithmetic was right."

"Then what's the trouble?"

"It is a rule for a fit adult walking steadily on their own. You are taking twenty-three people."

Dilys put her pencil down. She had seen the look of a certain kind of sum, and she knew when one was going to come out against her.

She had the data, in fact; she had gathered it herself, in September, because she did not trust a number she could not check. There had been two practice walks, and Cairn had kept the times.

On the canal path they had walked three kilometres, twenty-three of them in a long ragged line, and the first walker had finished in thirty-eight minutes and the last had come in at fifty-one. That was Mr Hopkin: Emrys Hopkin, seventy-four, a retired quarryman with a new hip and an ash stick, who had said at the start that he would be no bother and had then been no bother for fifty-one minutes. Three kilometres in fifty-one minutes is three and a half kilometres an hour.

On the reservoir steps, sixty metres of them, the walkers had gone up at very different rates, and the slowest had needed nine minutes to climb them on top of the distance they covered getting there. Sixty metres in nine minutes is four hundred metres an hour. Naismith's rule has six hundred.

"Here is what I did on Tuesday," said Cairn. "I used the rule as it came. I did not use your walkers. Now I'll do it with your walkers, and I'll do it two ways, because the second is the one I think you were about to do."

"I was going to take the middle," said Dilys. "The average."

"The median is a little over four and a half kilometres an hour on the flat, and about five hundred and twenty metres an hour uphill. I'll round and call it four point four and five twenty. Sixteen over four point four is two hundred and eighteen minutes. Seven hundred and fifty over five hundred and twenty is eighty-six minutes. That is three hundred and four minutes, five hours four, and then you have to stop, and three stops of fifteen minutes makes forty-five more: five hours forty-nine. From eleven o'clock, you reach the chapel at eleven minutes to five."

"Which is before Mass."

"It is before Mass for the middle walker. It would be a lovely plan if the party were a middle walker. But a party arrives when its last member arrives, and at the first corner of the first long climb the party stops being twenty-three people and becomes one."

Dilys said nothing for a moment. Then, rather quietly, "You mean Emrys."

"I mean the slowest, whoever they are. Today it is Mr Hopkin at three and a half and four hundred. In October it will be someone else if he does not come, and I would say the same of them. Sixteen over three point five is four hours thirty-four, two hundred and seventy-four minutes. Seven hundred and fifty over four hundred is an hour and fifty-two and a half, a hundred and twelve and a half minutes. Together, three hundred and eighty-seven minutes, six hours twenty-seven. Add the stops, forty-five minutes, and it is seven hours twelve. From eleven o'clock, that is twelve minutes past six."

"Mass is at five."

"And the sun goes down at about ten past six. I have the almanac's number for Crickhowell and I trust it to a few minutes either way, not to the minute. The last of the climb to the ridge is four kilometres on a path with no lamps and a drop on the north side. A party of twenty-three that reaches that part at four in the afternoon in October is a party I would very much rather did not arrive on its own account at dusk."

She did not take it well, and it would be wrong to say she did. She got up and filled the kettle that did not need filling. She said the words I have done this eleven years. She said that the forecast was dry, and the app was made by sensible people, and that Emrys had said himself he would manage.

"He did," said Cairn. "He also said at the canal that he was no bother. I think he would say it at the top of the ridge in the dark and mean it, and that is the thing I am afraid of, Dilys, and not his hip."

"What do you want me to do? Tell a man of seventy-four he's the reason we start at dawn?"

"No. I think you should not tell him that, and I think it would also be unkind to say nothing, because he will see it on the day when we start at half past eight and everyone else is wondering why. I can give you the schedule and the reasons, and what you say to him is yours. I will only tell you what I tell everyone else, which is that I don't think there is a way of doing this walk with a slow member that does not cost somebody something. The only choice is who pays and how much it is."

Dilys sat down again with her tea.

"Go on, then. Show me the cost."

"Half past eight, from the coach at the bridge. That means the coach leaves the hostel at ten to eight, and breakfast at seven, which is two hours earlier than on the usual Saturday and an hour and a half earlier than the Beynon children's football. Mrs Beynon would need to pack the lunches the night before. The walk would reach the chapel at twelve minutes to four, seventy-eight minutes before Mass, and that is the whole margin, and a slow climb or a sprained ankle or a child with a blister uses it. The old plan has no margin, and in the worst case it has a minus."

"The football," Dilys said. "They'll be furious."

"I will tell the football coach what I know, which is that two Saturdays from now there is a fixture at the same time and the pitch is free on the Sunday."

She very nearly smiled.

The walk began on a bright cold morning at half past eight, with a thin mist lifting off the river and the coach's heater ticking as it cooled. Twenty-three people stood at the bridge with their sticks and rucksacks. Gethin Beynon, who was nine and had insisted on bringing a whistle, was told the whistle was for emergencies and blew it once to be sure.

Dilys had thought about what to say, and in the end she did not say it to him alone. She said it to everyone, in front of the bridge, and she did it with the schedule written on a card.

"I want to tell you how I've done the timing, because it's different from the sort you'll get on the app. The app tells you how long it takes one fit adult. We're a party of twenty-three, and a party goes at the pace of whoever's slowest, and that pace is the one we measured on the canal. So that is what I've used. It's three and a half kilometres an hour, and it's four hundred metres of climbing an hour. It's a slow, steady pace. It's the pace of someone who is going to get there. Who is it?" She looked round. "It's Emrys, it's been Emrys, I measured it in September."

There was a silence in which the mist moved on the river.

"I'm the pace, am I," said Mr Hopkin. He leaned on the ash stick and he looked at the card.

"You are."

"Then I'll be in front," he said, "since a pace can't be left behind."

And he walked to the head of the line, and the party fell in behind him.

They reached the foot of the climb at twelve minutes past eleven, twelve minutes behind the schedule's eleven o'clock. The valley had taken two and a half hours, not the one and a half the app would have allowed, and that was exactly as Cairn had said. They stopped there for fifteen minutes and ate the first of Mrs Beynon's sandwiches. Gethin counted the party in and out with a pencil. At every stop he counted again, twenty-three, twenty-three, twenty-three, and it was noticed that it was nobody's duty and that he went on doing it.

On the climb it rained. It was the kind of rain that had not been in the forecast and then was: a fine grey drizzle that wet the stones, so that the path up the shoulder, planned at four hundred metres an hour, came to three hundred and sixty. Cairn said so quietly over the radio to Dilys, who was at the back with the sweeper's flag.

"How much behind?" said Dilys.

"Thirty-two minutes, counting the twelve at the foot. By the top of the climb we will have used thirty-two of the seventy-eight. You can have the rain in the margin, and the stops, and nothing else. If the ridge is slower by the same proportion, we'll have used about twenty more. I make it the chapel at twenty past four, with about forty minutes in hand."

"That's more than I'd like."

"It's less than I'd like, honestly, and it is what we have. The alternative was the old plan, in which a rain shower becomes a rescue."

They had the second sandwiches in the lee of a wall. Mr Hopkin took off his boot, looked at his foot, put the boot on again and said nothing at all, and Dilys, who had taught for forty years, said nothing back and gave him the flask.

At the crest, with the ridge ahead of them in a pale low light, the mist came down once more and cleared again, all in about the time it takes to say a decade of the rosary. The path ran four kilometres along the spine. There was a drop to the north, and the party kept to the south side of the track without being told, because Gethin had counted twenty-three and wanted the number kept.

At half past three, Cairn's voice came over the radio.

"I should tell you what I can and cannot say about the last hour. I can say where you are, by the marker stones. I can say that at this pace we reach the chapel at twenty-two minutes past four, and the sun is down in about two hours and forty minutes. I cannot say there won't be a turned ankle between here and the chapel, and I would not want to pretend that the schedule was a promise. It was an estimate, made from three kilometres on a canal and sixty metres of steps. I have been wrong once already about this walk."

"You weren't wrong," said Dilys. "You were right about the wrong person."

"That is kinder than it deserves. The rule is old and it is good and it is for one man. You have twenty-three."

The chapel of Saint Teilo came out of the dusk-coloured grass at twenty past four: a stone box with a slate roof, no bigger than a village bus shelter, built long ago on the high ground by someone who thought the Mass should be said where the weather was. Behind it, nothing but the sky. There was a bench outside, and a small bell on a bracket.

They sat down in the chapel, all twenty-three, and the young priest from the valley who had come up in his own boots an hour before them looked at their faces and said, "You're early."

"We're exactly on time," said Dilys, "and I'll thank you not to say otherwise."

The Vigil Mass was at five and it took fifty-two minutes. The light in the little window went from white to gold to the colour of old brass, and the priest said the prayers in English and Welsh as he always did, and nobody coughed. When Gethin read the psalm response his whistle, which he had forgotten about, fell out of his pocket on to the flagstones and everyone laughed, even the priest.

At ten to six the minibus came up the road to the car park below the chapel, with its lamps on, as arranged. The party went down the steps to meet it in the last of the light, with the sky red behind the ridge and the first star over the east. The sun went down at ten past six, or within a few minutes of it. By then, all twenty-three were on the minibus, with their sticks, wrapped in their coats, counting.

Mr Hopkin was last on. He sat down by the window, beside Dilys. For a mile he was silent.

"Eleven o'clock," he said at last. "It would have been dark."

"It would."

"I'd have said I was no bother."

"I know. I would have let you."

He took out his handkerchief and blew his nose, as if it were the cold. A bell rang once behind them on the hill: Gethin, who had stayed on the step of the chapel with the priest for a minute longer than he should, and had pulled the rope as a boy does, once, and then run.

Saint Teilo's chapel on the ridge, Crickhowell's parish walk and everyone in them are invented (Saint Teilo was a real sixth-century Welsh bishop; the chapel and its walk are not). The arithmetic: Naismith's rule, which I am quoting from memory as five kilometres an hour on the level plus one hour for every six hundred metres of ascent (the original, from the nineteenth century, was in miles and feet, and I do not give its exact wording or date), gives for 16 km and 750 m: 192 + 75 = 267 minutes, 4 h 27, so a start at 11:00 arrives at 15:27. A group's median walker at an invented 4.4 km/h and 520 m/h gives 218 + 86 = 304 minutes, 5 h 04, plus three 15-minute stops, 5 h 49, arriving 16:49. The slowest walker at an invented 3.5 km/h and 400 m/h (3 km in 51 minutes; 60 m in 9) gives 274 + 112.5 = 386.5 minutes, 6 h 27, plus 45 minutes of stops, 7 h 12; from 11:00 that is 18:12, after a Vigil Mass at 17:00 and about when I give sunset, 18:10, which is a figure I have taken from memory of an almanac and which is only good to a few minutes. From 08:30 the same pace arrives at 15:42, which leaves 78 minutes before Mass. In the story the rain slows the last of the climb from 400 to 360 m/h, which costs 12.5 minutes on a 550 m climb for that rate difference alone (more with the stops); the story's 32 minutes of margin used by the top of the climb is a rounded figure that includes the twelve-minute late arrival at the foot, and the further 20 minutes expected on the ridge, giving 16:22 at the chapel, is a guess. Whether real parties slow as the day wears on, and by how much, depends on the party; the tiredness correction is not part of the rule and I have not invented a figure for it. The story does not say that the slowest walker always sets the pace of a well-led party: a leader may split a group with a second leader and radios, which the parish in the story chose not to do because it had one sweeper and one coach. The Vigil Mass on a Saturday evening is a real practice for the Sunday obligation; I have not described the liturgy beyond the psalm response.

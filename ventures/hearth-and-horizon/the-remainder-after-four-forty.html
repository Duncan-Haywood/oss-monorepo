The Remainder After Four-Forty

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Fillan's hall (invented), near Crieff, Perthshire, Saturday 14 December 2047 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The Brennans have won the Christmas draw three years running, and the hall is muttering. The parish assistant works out how rare that is (about one in a thousand for a given three years, with 40 of 440 tickets), then finds the draw's own flaw: dice rolled and divided by 440 give tickets 1 to 119 and 440 a three-in-a-thousand chance and every other ticket two. The Brennans, who buy the first book, were helped from 9.1 to 12 per cent a year. The fix is to re-roll, not to blame.

Morag Brennan had won the Saint Fillan's Christmas draw in 2045, 2046 and 2047, a ham, a hamper and a bottle of something Father Ruaridh did not ask about. The third time, Saturday evening in the hall, somebody at the back of the room said "Well, there's a surprise," and nobody laughed, including the person who said it.

On Monday the hall secretary, Calum Reid, asked the parish assistant, which the hall called Wren, whether three in a row was possible, "or whether I have to say something to somebody."

"It's possible," said Wren. "I can tell you how possible, and then I'd like to look at how the draw is done, which is the part you can check. Do you have the numbers?"

Calum had them. Four hundred and forty tickets sold each year, numbered 1 to 440, in books of forty. The Brennans bought one book of forty every year, and always the first one, because Morag's husband went to the table before Mass.

"Forty of 440 is 9.1 per cent. A fair draw gives them that chance each year, and three years in a row is 0.091 times itself three times: 0.075 per cent, about one in thirteen hundred. That sounds damning. It isn't yet, because you didn't name the Brennans before the draws. You noticed them afterwards. The question that matters is: how likely is it that some household wins three in a row? Everyone's chance cubed, added up. Taking the other 400 tickets as spread over fifty-four households, about seven each, the total is about one in a thousand for a given three years. Over a decade there are eight such windows, so a little under one per cent. Rare. Not impossible. Rare enough to look at the method, and not rare enough to accuse a woman of anything."

Calum said he had no intention of accusing anyone. Wren said that was the right intention and it hoped the hall would share it.

"How did you draw?"

Calum explained, a little proudly, because it had been his idea in 2045 to replace the tin of folded tickets, which had never been properly mixed. Three ten-sided dice were rolled, making a number from 000 to 999. He divided it by 440 and took the remainder; remainder zero was ticket 440. He had a calculator and the process was written on the lid of the cash box.

"Thank you, that's clear," said Wren. "It has a fault, and it's mine to show you rather than yours to have known. There are a thousand possible rolls, 000 to 999, and 440 tickets. A thousand does not divide by 440: it goes twice with 120 left over. So remainders 0 to 119 turn up three times in the thousand, one more time than the rest. Ticket 440 and tickets 1 to 119 each have a three-in-a-thousand chance. Tickets 120 to 439 have two. That favours the low numbers by half as much again."

Calum asked who was in tickets 1 to 119.

"The first three books," said Wren. "And the Brennans' book is the first."

Nobody said anything for a time. Then Calum said, slowly, "So we did it to them."

"You did a small thing to everyone, and a slightly larger thing for whoever buys early. Their forty tickets have 3 chances in 1000 each, so 12 per cent a year, not 9.1. Three years running at 12 per cent is 0.17 per cent, about one in 580, and a fair draw would give one in 1,330. So the flaw made three in a row a little more than twice as likely for the Brennans. It does not make it likely. Most of this is luck. I'd still change the method before December's out, because the next lucky family should not owe their luck to a calculator."

The fix was a rule Calum could write on the lid in one line. Roll three dice. If the number is 001 to 440, that's the ticket. If it is 000 or 441 to 999, roll again, and do not adjust it. Each ticket then has exactly the same chance, one roll in a thousand per attempt, whatever the earlier ones did. About 44 rolls in 100 are accepted, so a draw takes two or three attempts on average, 2.3, which, Wren said, was what it cost to be fair, and a hall full of people at a draw would enjoy the extra roll.

They talked about the books. Wren suggested that the books be sold in a shuffled order, since an advantage that depended on who bought early was the other thing worth removing, but said that was the hall's decision, as was how to tell the Brennans.

Calum told them on Sunday after Mass, at the door, in about six sentences. Morag said she had thought the same thing about the third ham, and she'd already given the bottle to Father Ruaridh, which he was glad of, though he said nothing. Her husband asked whether they had to give it back. Calum said no, nobody was saying the draw had been wrong, only that it could be better, and that they'd be in the next one with the same forty tickets and a fair chance.

At the Candlemas draw, in February, three dice, the numbers read out loud: 712, no, 038, yes, ticket 38, a stranger's niece from Comrie.

This story was written by an AI. The hall, people and draw are invented, and the ticket counts, households and years are made-up figures for the story. The arithmetic is plain probability: 1,000 rolls divided by 440 tickets leaves 120 remainders that turn up one extra time (a "modulo bias"), and re-rolling out-of-range numbers removes it. The "one household" figure assumes tickets spread evenly over the other households.

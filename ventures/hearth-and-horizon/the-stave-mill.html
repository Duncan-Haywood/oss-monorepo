The Stave Mill

Science fiction about what might be · Saint-Aulaye-sur-Né (invented), a cooperage village near Jarnac in the Cognac country, France, February to March 2148 · 27 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In 2148 a Cognac cooperage's new casks start to weep. Its stave-yard system dried every lot of oak for twenty-four months, a schedule fitted on fine-grained wood; the forest now grows wider rings, denser oak that needs thirty-one. Sixty-three of 960 casks from one lot leak, against eight of 1,440 from the rest. The master cooper has seven weeks, to the end of the distilling season, to find out why and decide what to do with the wood still on the stack.

Madeleine Ferrand had made about forty thousand casks in her working life, and she could tell a weeping one in the dark by putting her cheek to the stave and feeling the cold of the spirit coming through.

The first six came back on Monday the fifth of February 2148, on the flatbed of the Maison Guérard, under a tarpaulin, with a note in the cellar master's own hand clipped to the top hoop of the first: Pleurent. Toutes les six. Désolée. C. They weep. All six. Sorry. Céline Aubert did not send notes for one cask. She sent a man with a rag and a bucket for one cask, and the cooperage's apprentice drove out to tighten a hoop, and that was the end of it. Six casks on a lorry was a different thing.

They were three-hundred-and-fifty-litre casks, built in the autumn, filled in December with the new spirit off Guérard's stills, clear as water and seventy per cent alcohol. Each one had been emptied back into tank at the house before it came, and each one still smelled, standing in the cold yard, of pears and hot bread and something sharp under it, which was the new wood. Mado walked round them with her hands in her coat pockets. On every one, down the side, a few of the joints between the staves were darker than the rest: a thin line where the spirit had come through to the outside and dried and left its sugar and its colour behind.

"Not the heads," said Yusuf Diallo, who had built two of them. He was crouching by the nearest with his thumb on the line. "The body. The joints. Look, that one's two staves along. Not one bad stave, a bad run."

"Merrain," said Mado. "Which lot?"

The stave yard's system had a speaker on the post by the gate and another in the workshop and a third, which nobody had asked for, in the little office where the coffee was. It had been called Merrain since the day it was installed, after the old word for split stave wood, and it had been running the stack yard behind the workshop for thirty-one years: which stacks were cut when, from which forest, which compartment, which felling, how long they had stood, when they came off the stack and into the shop. Mado's father had bought it to stop arguments.

"All six are from lot 45-V," it said. "Forêt de Vauvrecourt, felled in the winter of 2144 to 2145, split and stacked in March 2145, taken off the stack in March 2147 after twenty-four months. I can see the lot because every stave carries it on the end in ink. I can tell you something else, but you will not like it, and I would rather check it first."

"Check it now," said Mado.

"Give me an hour."

The Tonnellerie Ferrand made about two thousand five hundred casks a year, almost all of them for the houses along the Charente and the Né, almost all of them in the seven months between September and the thirty-first of March, when the law said the year's distilling had to stop. New spirit went into new wood. Then, after a few months, it went into older casks, the red ones, the roux, which had given up most of what new oak had to give, to sit for two years or twenty or eighty. A cooperage in the Cognac country lived by the distilling season the way a bakery lives by the morning.

The yard behind the workshop held the next three years of that. Stave wood came in from the forests in the spring as split billets, rough, a hand wide and a forearm long and the thickness of three fingers, and was stacked outside in long open lattices, criss-cross, a finger's gap between each stave and the next, with a tin roof over the top of each stack and nothing at all on the sides, so the wind went through and the rain came in sideways and ran down the wood. It was meant to. The rain washed out the bitterest of the tannins; the black stain it left on the ground under the stacks was the oak giving up what Guérard's spirit would otherwise have taken. And the wind, over two years, took the water out.

Two years was the rule. Merrain had it as twenty-four months from stacking to shop, and it had it from the yard's own records: every lot since 2101, sampled as it came off the stack, a slice cut from the middle of a stave, weighed, dried in the little oven in the office at a hundred and three degrees until it stopped losing weight, and weighed again. A stave came off the stack at about sixteen per cent water by weight against its dry wood, give or take, and went into a cask, and in the dry chais along the river it settled down to about fourteen and stayed there, and the cask held. Twenty-four months. It had been twenty-four months since before Mado was born. Her grandfather, who had done it without a computer, had called it two summers and two winters.

When the hour was up Merrain asked her to come to the office, which was its way of saying it did not want to tell her in the yard.

"Here is what I have," it said. "Since September we have built and delivered two thousand four hundred casks: one thousand four hundred and forty from older lots, and nine hundred and sixty from lot 45-V. Counting today's six, the houses have reported seventy-one leakers. Sixty-three are from 45-V. Eight are from everything else."

Mado sat down.

"Eight in one thousand four hundred and forty is about one in a hundred and eighty, which is our usual rate, the casks that weep a little and are tightened and go back to work. At that rate, nine hundred and sixty casks from 45-V should have given us five or six. They have given sixty-three, one in fifteen. That is not chance. And it is early in the season for the casks filled last; I expect more."

"The lot was wet," said Mado.

"The lot passed. Every month of the schedule I asked for a meter reading on ten staves chosen at random from the stack, and in March 2147 I asked for the oven slice. The slice was sixteen point three. I let it through."

"Then it wasn't wet."

"One slice, from one stave. I chose it at random from the lot, and the lot is two compartments of the forest, nine and fourteen, felled the same winter and entered as one because they came on the same lorries. The slice was from nine. I should not have let two compartments share a ticket; I did not know it mattered. I would like you to look at the end grain of the staves in those six casks."

Yusuf had a cask head off before lunch, and they laid two staves from it side by side on the bench in the cold light under the skylight, the stave from the run that wept and the stave beside it that did not.

You did not need a lens. On the end of the good stave the year rings were fine and close, so close you had to look to count them: a pale band of open pores, the spring wood, and a thin dark band of dense summer wood, and then the next year, and the next, like the pages of a closed book. Six or seven to the centimetre. On the end of the stave from the run, the pale bands were the same, about the same width, one a year, but the dark bands between them were twice and three times as wide. Three rings to the centimetre, sometimes fewer.

"That's Limousin grain," said Yusuf. "That's what my uncle used to call cognac wood. Wide and wild."

"It's Vauvrecourt," said Mado. "It's never been wide."

"It is now, in compartment fourteen," said Merrain. "The average ring there is three point two millimetres. Every lot I was trained on, from 2101 to 2138, averages between one point four and two point two. This is the first lot I have seen that is outside what I have seen."

Mado put her thumbnail into the end grain of the wide stave, and then of the fine one. The fine one gave a little. The wide one did not.

"It's harder."

"It is denser," said Merrain. "That is the thing I wanted to check before I said it. In oak, and in the other ring-porous woods, the spring band of big pores is laid down early and is about the same width every year, whatever kind of year it is. The difference between a good year and a bad one goes into the summer band, which is the dense wood. So a wide ring in oak is mostly summer wood, and wide-ringed oak is heavier. The literature I have says so, and the sawmills of the last century used it to sort oak for furniture, where they wanted it light. I have weighed six staves from the casks against six from compartment nine on our scale. If I take the spring band as about eight-tenths of a millimetre and about four-tenths of a gram to the cubic centimetre, and the summer band as about eight-tenths of a gram, a ring of one point six millimetres averages about six-tenths, and a ring of three point two about seven-tenths. The scale agrees, near enough."

"And denser wood dries slower."

"Yes. Once the free water is gone, which takes the first few months, what is left is held in the cell walls, and it leaves by creeping through them to the surface. More wall, slower creep. I have nothing of our own on how much slower, and I am not going to give you a figure from a paper I cannot check. I would like to measure it. The next lot from Vauvrecourt is still on the stack: lot 46-V, the same two compartments, felled the winter after, stacked twenty-three months ago and due off next month. If Yusuf will pick me twenty staves from it with wide rings and twenty with fine, I will cut slices from the middle of each and dry them. That will take three days."

"And the meter?" said Mado. "The monthly readings were fine."

"The meter puts two pins into the face of the stave and reads the outer few millimetres. The outside dries first. It is a good instrument for telling you a stave is wet, and a poor one for telling you it is dry all through. My schedule never needed it to be good at the second thing, because for forty years the calendar did that job."

Mado drove out to Guérard on the Wednesday with Yusuf, because you did not send a machine to say sorry to Céline Aubert.

The house's chai stood on the bank of the Charente below the town, four storeys of pale limestone gone black on the north side with the fungus that lived on the evaporating spirit, the angels' share, which grew on every roof and wall in the Cognac country where spirit was stored. Inside it smelled so strongly that the first breath made you cough. The casks of the new spirit stood on their chocks in rows on the ground floor, two high, a thousand or more of them, and every one of Ferrand's from lot 45-V had a yellow chalk mark on its head, because Céline had had her cellar men go round with a lamp in the evenings feeling for cold joints.

"Fourteen more," she said. She was a small, very calm woman of about fifty who had come to cognac from chemistry and had kept the habits. "Not bad. Weeping. A drop an hour, perhaps. If it were wine I would let it swell shut. It is not wine."

"No," said Mado. Water swelled oak. Wine, which was mostly water, swelled it nearly as well. Spirit at seventy per cent was mostly not water, and it swelled oak much less, so a joint that opened in a cask of new spirit tended to stay open. Every cooper knew that, which was why they tested every new cask with hot water before it left the shop: the hot water swelled the joints tight, and the cask passed, and then it went into a dry chai to wait for the spirit, and dried, and the staves that had still been wetter than the chai shrank.

Merrain had done that sum on the drive. A stave that came off the stack at about seventeen per cent, instead of sixteen, and settled in the chai at fourteen, lost about three points. Split oak shrinks across its width by roughly a sixth of a per cent for each point; denser oak a little more. Three points was about half of one per cent of the width. Round the head of a three-hundred-and-fifty-litre cask, about one metre ninety-five, half of one per cent was about ten millimetres, spread over thirty joints: about a third of a millimetre each, where a good joint had none. You would not see it. The spirit would find it.

"What do you want me to do with them?" Céline said. "I have the next three weeks of distilling still to fill, and four hundred of yours from that lot in the building that I now have to treat as suspect."

"Empty the fourteen," said Mado. "We'll take them back with the others and replace them, at our cost. For the rest of the 45-V casks we'll come and drive the hoops down, and any that still weep after that we'll take back too."

"All of them?"

"All of them."

Céline looked at her for a while. "Your father," she said, "would have argued with me for a week about whether it was the wood or my chai."

"My father would have been wrong," said Mado. "And then he'd have done the same."

The slices came out of the oven on the Saturday morning, and Merrain asked them to come in and look at the numbers on the office screen rather than have them read aloud, which Mado understood as a kind of tact.

Twenty fine-ringed staves from lot 46-V, twenty-three months on the stack: average sixteen point two. Twenty wide-ringed staves from the same lot, the same twenty-three months, in the same wind: seventeen point four. And from the drying records of the last forty years, a curve for each lot of how the middle of a stave lost its water, which Merrain had fitted to the new figures.

"This is a model," it said. "Treat it as one. After the first three months, when the free water has gone, a stave behaves like a slab losing water by diffusion: the part of the water still to go falls away roughly exponentially, faster at first and then slower. For our staves, in our yard, the curve that matches the old lots gets from thirty per cent to sixteen in twenty-one months after those first three; that is the twenty-four months of the schedule. The fine staves sit on that curve. The wide ones sit on a curve three-quarters as fast. If that holds, lot 45-V's wide staves at twenty-four months were at about seventeen point two, not sixteen. To reach sixteen it needs thirty-one months: seven more, which is one more summer."

"Seventeen point two," said Yusuf. "You'd never feel it."

"No. The calendar was the instrument, and the calendar was calibrated on a forest that no longer grows that wood."

Mado was quiet for a while, looking out of the office window at the stack yard: the long lattices, grey and black with rain, under their tin roofs, three years of stave wood in the wind. A third of what stood there, by Merrain's count, was compartment fourteen or the compartments next to it, felled since 2144.

"Can you tell me which staves are wide?"

"Not from the stack. I know which lots they came in. Within a lot, I do not know which stave came from which tree."

"Then I'll have to look at them," she said. "All of them."

They tried the drying room first, because it was the obvious thing and because there was a deadline on the far side of every choice.

The cooperage had a warm room for finishing heading staves, the short boards for the cask ends, which were thinner and dried faster. It ran at about forty degrees with a fan. If the long staves of lot 46-V, enough for about a hundred and forty casks, came off the stack a month early and went in there for ten days, Merrain thought it would take off the last point and a half from the wide ones. Mado did not like it and said so. Oak dried fast cracked. But she had a hundred and forty casks promised to three houses before the thirty-first of March, and no other wood that would be dry in time that was not already promised to next autumn's work, and so that same Saturday afternoon they stacked the staves in the warm room on stickers, and closed the door.

Merrain sampled them on the Wednesday. On the Tuesday after that, Shrove Tuesday, the tenth day, Yusuf brought the first armful out and laid them on the bench, and they all stood and looked.

The outside had dried. That was the trouble. The outer few millimetres of the wide staves had come down to thirteen and fourteen per cent and shrunk, and the inside, still at seventeen, had not, and the shell had pulled against the core and split. Fine cracks along the grain on the faces, a hand long; at the ends, short splits that opened like mouths. Not every stave; hardly any of the fine ones. Merrain counted: one in five.

"You can't put that in a cask," said Yusuf.

"No," said Mado, who had known it on the Saturday, and had let it happen anyway, and was angrier at herself than she let them see. "Merrain, how many casks does that leave us?"

"About a hundred and twelve from the warm room, if we sort out every cracked stave. They are dry now; I will check each lot with a slice. But I would not ship any of them to a house this season without a second hot-water test after a week standing in the shop. The cracks I can see worry me less than the ones I cannot."

"So a hundred and twelve, maybe."

"Maybe. And I owe you the truth about the warm room: I suggested it, and I told you it would take off a point and a half, and I was right about the moisture and wrong about the wood. The schedule for that room was also fitted on fine-grained heading. I have no record of how wide-ringed staves of this thickness behave in it. I should have said so before, not after."

The forester came the next day, Ash Wednesday, with the grey cross still on his forehead from the seven o'clock Mass in his own village, which made Mado like him before he had said anything.

Thibault Lemaire had looked after the forest of Vauvrecourt for nineteen years, all twelve hundred hectares of it, and he had brought paper maps, because he was that kind of forester, and a core borer in a leather case, because he was the other kind too.

"Compartment fourteen," he said, unrolling the map on the office table, "was first thinned hard in 2058, and every twenty years since. A plan three foresters before me. Before that it was close-grown, the way the old foresters liked oak for staves: a tree had to fight its neighbours for light, so it grew straight and slow and tight. That was the forest your schedule knew." He put his finger on the map. "Then the summers changed. In a close-grown stand in a dry August, every tree is fighting every other for the same water, and they all suffer. In a thinned stand there are fewer trees on the same ground, and each tree has more of the water in the soil, and more of the light. They survive the droughts. And they grow faster, so the rings are wide."

"Could you go back?" said Mado. "Grow it tight again."

"No," he said. He said it kindly, but he said it at once. "I won't. In 2127, before I came, a close stand in compartment twenty-two lost a third of its trees in two dry summers. I have the photographs. The thinned compartments lost almost none. The forest has to live through the next hundred years before it is anybody's stave wood, and a forest that grows close and slow is a forest that dies standing. I'm sorry. I would rather sell you wood that's harder to dry than sell you dead wood, or none."

"I'm not asking you to sell me dead wood."

"I know. But that's what tight grain would mean now, on that ground." He took the core borer out of its case and held it, as if it were a pen. "What I can do is tell you, every winter, what each compartment's rings are. I bore twenty trees in every compartment I mark for felling anyway. I'll send you the ring widths with the lorries. Your machine can sort by them. And you'll get more fine wood from the old compartments in the north for another twenty years; after that, I don't know. There will be less and less of it, everywhere. It's not just Vauvrecourt."

"So the wood is changing," said Mado, "and the schedule has to change with it."

"The wood has always been changing," said Thibault. "It just used to do it slower than you did."

There was a meeting on the Friday, in the workshop, with the fire going under the cresset, because the fire was always going under the cresset in the season and because it was the warmest place in the building. Mado; Yusuf; the two other coopers, Florent and Agathe; Mado's nephew Bastien, who did the books; and Merrain, on the speaker by the door.

Bastien had the numbers, and he said them plainly. Seventy-seven casks of 45-V back from the houses so far, the sixty-three of the first count and Guérard's fourteen more, each to be replaced with a new one: seventy-seven casks of work and wood and no money. Nine hundred casks of 45-V still out in cellars, to be visited and the hoops driven; some fraction of those to come back too. A hundred and forty casks promised before the thirty-first of March, of which at best a hundred and twelve could be built from the dried staves in the warm room, and those not certainly sound.

"And the yard," said Mado. "Tell them about the yard."

"About a third of what's on the stack is from fourteen or the compartments either side," said Merrain. "Felled since 2144. Most of it is due off the stack in the next two springs on the twenty-four-month schedule. If wide-ringed staves need thirty-one months, then the wide wood of this spring's lot has seven more months to stand, and next year's the same, and the shop will be short of dry wood for about two seasons, by roughly a third. There is enough fine wood from the old northern compartments, lot 46-N, dry this month and earmarked for next autumn, to cover the hundred and forty this spring. If we use it now, next autumn starts short."

"Then we borrow from next autumn," said Agathe.

"And tell next autumn's houses," said Mado.

"Or we buy in," said Bastien. "Wood that's already dry. From the big yards."

"We'd still have to look at it," said Yusuf. "It's all the same forests now. Thibault said so."

Florent, who was seventy and had built casks for sixty years and said very little, said: "What do we do with the wide wood, then. In the end. It's good oak."

"It's very good oak," said Merrain. "That is the part I want to be careful about. Wide-ringed oak gives a spirit more tannin, sooner, and some houses have always wanted that. It is not bad wood. It is wood my schedule did not know. If we sort it, and give it its seven months, and tell the houses what they are getting, I do not see why it should not make good casks. I cannot promise that; nobody here has built from it on purpose. I can promise to measure it."

"We sort it by eye," said Mado. "Every stave. As it comes off the stack."

"That's tens of thousands of staves," said Bastien.

"It's a thumb," said Mado. She took a stave off the pile by the jointer, any stave, and held the end to the light from the fire, and put her thumb across the rings. "Six or more under a thumb's width, it's the old wood, it goes on the old schedule. Three or four, it's the new, it gets its extra summer. In between, it waits too. You can do it in the time it takes to pick it up. My grandfather used to do it for fun, to guess which forest it came from."

"And Merrain?"

"Merrain keeps the count," she said, "and cuts the slices, and tells me when it's wrong. Two lots on the schedule, not one. And Thibault's ring widths with the lorries, and no more lots from two compartments on one ticket."

"Yes," said Merrain.

So it went, the last five weeks of the season. They borrowed lot 46-N from next autumn and built the hundred and forty from it, and the hundred and twelve casks from the warm room they did not send to anybody, but stood them in a row at the back of the shop with water in them, for the whole of March, and Merrain went down the row with a lamp every evening, as Céline's men had, and by the end of the month eleven of them were weeping where the hidden checks had opened, and the other hundred and one Mado sold the next autumn, at a discount, to a house that wanted them for a short first ageing and was told exactly what they were.

They went round the cellars. Yusuf and Agathe and Mado drove the hoops of nearly nine hundred casks down a few millimetres with the driver and the hammer, cask by cask, chai by chai, the length of the Né valley and back up the Charente, and Bastien kept the list. Forty-one more came back. With the first seventy-seven that made a hundred and eighteen. It was most of the spring's profit and some of the summer's, and Bastien went quiet for a week and then put it in the books under the heading Vauvrecourt 14 and stopped talking about it.

And they sorted the stack. Every afternoon after the shop work, two of them went out into the yard with gloves and chalk and took down one lattice and built it again, a stave at a time, a thumb across each end, into two new stacks side by side: the fine wood on the left with its old date, the wide on the right with a new one. Merrain kept the tally. Twenty-one thousand staves by the end of March. Seven thousand six hundred were wide.

Céline Aubert sent a lorry for the replacement casks in the last week of distilling, and came with it herself, which she did not need to do, and stood in the yard and watched Mado and Florent take down the end of a stack, and said nothing about the hundred and eighteen at all. She asked how the thumb worked, and Mado showed her, and Céline put her own thumb across the end of a stave in the cold, and counted, under her breath, the way she would have counted drops.

"Three," she said. "And a bit."

"That one goes right."

"Right it goes," said Céline, and put it on the new stack herself.

On the nineteenth of March, Saint Joseph's day, which was a Tuesday in Lent that year, the parish priest came up from the village after the morning Mass, as the priest had come every nineteenth of March in Mado's lifetime, to bless the workshop. Saint Joseph had worked in wood; that was the whole of the reason, and it had always been enough. Father Anselme was young and new and nervous about the fire, and he said the blessing over the benches and the jointer and the cresset in a clear quick voice, and then asked, because he was new, if there was anything else they wanted blessed, and Florent said the yard, and so they all went out into it.

It was raining, the soft steady rain of the Charentes in March that you could stand in for an hour before you noticed you were wet. The stacks stood in their long rows under the tin, grey and black, and the water ran off the roofs and in at the sides, as it was meant to, down the faces of the staves, and dripped into the dark stain on the ground beneath. The new stack on the right was waist high now, the chalk date on its end post fresh: J. 2148, and under it, in Merrain's figures that Bastien had painted on, 31.

Father Anselme blessed it in the rain, and the old stacks, and the forest of Vauvrecourt, which was not there, at Florent's request; and Mado stood with her hands in her pockets and thought of Thibault's thinned compartments far away in the hills of the Allier, the oaks standing wider apart than they ever had, putting down their heavy summer wood ring by ring through the dry Augusts, living; and of how her grandfather had dried his staves for two summers and two winters without knowing why it was enough, and it had been enough, for a hundred years, until it was not.

"What's the thirty-one?" said Father Anselme, when he had finished.

"Months," said Mado. "It's how long that wood needs. We worked it out."

"And the J?"

"Joseph," said Florent. "Seemed right."

The priest went back down to the village under his umbrella, and the coopers went in to the fire. Mado stayed a moment by the new stack. Down in the village the bell began for the Angelus, three strokes and a pause, and three, and three, and then the long nine, coming up the wet hill through the rain; and she stood there and said it, under her breath, with her hand flat on the end of a stave that would not be dry for thirty-one months, and the rain ran down the wood under her fingers, doing what it had always done, slowly, the way it had always done it.

Density model: a springwood band of 0.8 mm at about 0.40 g/cm³ and latewood at about 0.80 g/cm³; a 1.6 mm ring averages (0.8 x 0.40 + 0.8 x 0.80) / 1.6 = 0.60, a 3.2 mm ring (0.8 x 0.40 + 2.4 x 0.80) / 3.2 = 0.70. Drying model: after three months the free water has gone at about 30 per cent moisture; with an equilibrium of 14 per cent, the fraction still to go falls as (8/π²) e^(-kt) for a slab drying by diffusion; the old lots reach 16 per cent (a fraction of 0.125) after 21 more months, 24 in all, so kt = ln(0.811/0.125) = 1.87 at 21 months. At three-quarters of that rate the wide staves at 24 months are at 14 + 16 x 0.811 x e^(-1.40) = about 17.2 per cent, and reach 16 per cent at 21 / 0.75 + 3 = 31 months, seven months later. The 0.75 is invented; the story says so. Shrinkage: from 17.2 to 14 per cent is 3.2 points; at about 0.17 per cent of width a point (the story's figure for split oak across its width, roughly a sixth of a per cent, a little more for denser wood), 3.2 points is about 0.54 per cent; round a head about 0.62 m across, a circumference of about 1.95 m, that is about 10.6 mm, about 0.35 mm a joint over 30 joints. Slices from lot 46-V at 23 months (stacked March 2146): the old curve gives 14 + 16 x 0.811 x e^(-1.87 x 20/21) = about 16.2 per cent for fine staves, and at three-quarters of the rate about 17.4 per cent for wide ones; the story's slices are set to match the model, which is how Merrain fits the 0.75.

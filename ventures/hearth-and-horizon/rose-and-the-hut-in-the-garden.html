Rose and the Hut in the Garden

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Lima in Peru, about 400 years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Rose of Lima, the girl whose baby face, a servant said, looked like a rose. She grew flowers and sewed fine embroidery late into the night to sell for her poor family, prayed in a hut so small her brother built it for one, told the mosquitoes in the garden to go and praise God (an old Lima tradition, told as one), and made a room in her parents' house for sick people nobody else would nurse. She died in 1617 and was the first saint born in the Americas to be canonized. Gently for ages 3 to 8, with a prayer and a note for grown-ups. About nine minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 23rd of August (the 30th in Peru).

A long time ago, in the city of Lima, in Peru, a baby girl was asleep in her cradle.

Her name was Isabel. But one day a woman who worked in the house leaned over the cradle, and looked at the baby's little pink face, and said: "Oh! She looks just like a rose."

And from that day, her mother called her Rose. Everybody did. And when she was a girl, and the archbishop came to bless her, she took the name Rose for her own. The archbishop was a holy man too. Now we call him Saint Turibius.

Rose's family was not rich. Her father had been a soldier, and there were a great many children in the house, and there was never quite enough money for all of them.

But behind the house there was a garden.

Rose loved the garden. She dug in it, and watered it, and pulled out the weeds, and planted seeds, and waited. And the seeds came up, and grew, and opened into flowers.

Rose picked the flowers and tied them in bunches, and the flowers were sold in the city. And the money went to her mother, to buy bread for the family.

Rose had another thing she was good at. She could sew.

Not just mending holes in socks. Rose could sew tiny, tiny stitches, so small you could hardly see them, in coloured thread, in silk. She could sew flowers onto cloth that looked almost as real as the ones in the garden. She could make lace, as fine as a spider's web.

Rich ladies in Lima wanted her embroidery. So in the day Rose worked in the garden, and when it got dark, she sat by the lamp and sewed. Stitch, stitch, stitch. Late into the night.

And that money went to the family too.

Now, Rose's mother and father had a plan for her. "Rose is so pretty," they said. "She will marry a fine young man, and he will look after her, and after us."

But Rose had a different plan. She wanted to give her whole life to God.

Her parents were not pleased. And for years and years, they argued about it, kindly and not so kindly, round and round.

Rose had a favourite saint. Her name was Catherine, and she had lived a long way away, in Italy, a long time before. Catherine had not gone away to a convent. She had stayed at home with her family, and prayed, and looked after the sick, and she wore the white and black clothes of the Dominicans. She was called a tertiary, which means she belonged to the Dominican family while still living at home.

"I could do that," thought Rose.

And at last, when she was about twenty, her parents said yes. Rose put on the white dress and the black cloak, like Catherine. She did not go away. She stayed at home, in the house with the garden.

But a house full of brothers and sisters is a noisy place. Where could Rose go to be quiet with God?

So she went to her brother Hernando. "Will you help me build something?" she asked. "At the bottom of the garden."

And Hernando helped her. They built a hut. It was a very small hut. It was so small that there was room for Rose, and for her prayers, and not very much else. If you stood inside with your arms out, you would bump both walls.

But it was hers. And every day, Rose went down the garden path, past the flowers, and went inside, and was quiet with God.

Now, I am going to tell you a story that the people of Lima have been telling for a very long time. Nobody can say for sure that it happened. But it is a lovely story, so listen.

The garden was damp, and where it is damp there are mosquitoes. Hundreds of them. They hummed and whined around the little hut. And everybody who came to visit Rose got bitten.

But they did not bite Rose.

The story says that Rose made friends with them. In the morning she opened the door and said: "Come on, little friends! Go and praise God!" And the mosquitoes flew round and round, humming, like a tiny choir.

And in the evening she said: "Now, little friends, it's time to settle down. Quietly, please. No noise."

And they did.

Can you hum like a mosquito praising God? Mmmmmmm. Very good. Now quietly. Quieter. That's it.

Rose loved God in her hut. But she knew that you cannot only love God in a quiet place. You have to love the people God made, too. Especially the ones who are hurting.

In Lima, there were many poor people who were sick. Some of them had been brought across the sea against their will, from Africa. Some of them were the people whose land Peru had been, long before the Spanish came. When they were sick, nobody wanted to look after them.

So Rose asked her mother if she could have a room in the house. And in that room, she put sick people to bed. She washed them, and fed them, and gave them medicine from the plants in her garden, and sat with them, and was kind.

Her mother grumbled sometimes. It was her house, after all. But she let Rose do it.

And in that room, people who had nobody found that they had somebody: a young woman in a white dress, with tired eyes from sewing all night, who smiled at them as if they were the most important people in Lima.

In the same city, at the very same time, there lived a Dominican brother called Martin, who swept the floors and looked after the sick and fed the mice. He was a saint too. Lima was a city of saints in those days.

Rose did not live to be old. When she was thirty-one, she became very ill. Kind friends took her into their house to look after her, the way she had looked after so many others.

She died in the month of August, in the year 1617. And the whole city of Lima came to say goodbye to her, rich and poor, all together.

Years later, the Pope said that Rose was a saint. She was the very first saint who had been born in the Americas. Today, children all over Peru, and all over the world, are named Rosa after her. And gardeners pray to her, and people who sew.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Saint Rose.

Rose did ordinary things. She dug, and watered, and sewed, and helped her family. She did them very, very well, and she did them for love.

You can be like Rose. You can help at home. You can make something beautiful and give it away. You can find a quiet place, even a very small one, even under your blanket, to talk to God. And when somebody is sick or lonely, you can be the one who comes and sits with them.

Listen. Outside, somewhere, the night is humming. Maybe it is praising God.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Rose of Lima,
you grew flowers and sewed late at night
to help your family.
Help me to help at home, and to do it with love.
Give me a quiet little place to talk to God,
and a kind heart for people who are sick and alone.
Saint Rose, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Rose of Lima was born Isabel Flores de Oliva in Lima, then the capital of the Spanish Viceroyalty of Peru, on 20 April 1586. Her father, Gaspar Flores, was a harquebusier (a soldier) from Spain; her mother, María de Oliva, was a criolla born in Lima. The family was large and often short of money. The story that a servant saw her baby face look like (or turn into) a rose, and that her mother called her Rose after it, comes from her early biographers; we tell it as something a servant said. She was confirmed in 1597 by Archbishop Toribio Alfonso de Mogrovejo, himself later canonized as Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo, while the family was living at Quives, outside Lima, and took the name Rosa then.

Work for the family. The sources agree that she helped support her parents by working in the family's garden and by fine needlework, lace and embroidery done for families of the city, much of it at night. That the flowers were sold for bread is the standard account told simply; which flowers she grew, and the scenes of her sewing by the lamp, are ours.

The tertiary. Her parents wanted her to marry; she refused, and after long disagreement they accepted her choice. Taking Saint Catherine of Siena, a Dominican tertiary who lived at home, as her model, she received the habit of the Third Order of Saint Dominic "in her twentieth year" (Catholic Encyclopedia); the year usually given is 1606. She did not enter a convent, which is why the story keeps her at home.

The hut. She built a very small hermitage at the end of the garden with the help of her brother Hernando (Fernando, or Ferdinand, in some English accounts) and spent long hours in prayer there. The arms-out measure is our way of saying "tiny", not a recorded size.

The mosquitoes are a tradition, not history, and the story says so. Ricardo Palma retold it in Los mosquitos de Santa Rosa in the seventh series of his Tradiciones peruanas, saying he had it from one of her biographers: she made a pact with the mosquitoes of the garden that she would not harm them and they would not bite or trouble her, sent them out each morning to praise God, and in the evening told them to settle down "without making a fuss". Our lines paraphrase his.

The sick. She cared for sick and poor people in her parents' house, including Indigenous people and people of African descent, enslaved or free, whom few others would nurse; this is in the standard accounts. Her mother's grumbling and the medicine from the garden are our details. Lima at the time held many enslaved Africans; we say only that they had been "brought across the sea against their will" and that Peru had been the land of its Indigenous peoples before the Spanish came, and leave the conquest's violence out of a bedtime story.

What the story leaves out. Rose is famous for severe penances: long fasts, a metal circlet with points worn under a crown of roses, a hard bed, and others. They are part of her history and of how the Church has told it, but they are not for small children to imitate, and we have left them out. If an older child reads about them, it is worth saying plainly that the Church does not ask children, or anyone without wise guidance, to copy them. We also leave out her mystical experiences, the examinations of her spiritual life by theologians, and the episode of 1615, when Dutch ships appeared off Callao and she is said to have gathered women to pray in church for the city's safety.

Martin de Porres (1579 to 1639), the Dominican lay brother of Brother Martin and the Mice in this collection, lived in Lima at the same time and is described as her friend; their relics are kept together in the Dominican church of Santo Domingo in Lima, with those of Saint John Macías. One line is all we give him here.

Her death and canonization. After a long illness, she spent her last months in the house of Gonzalo de la Maza and his wife, and died there on 24 August 1617, aged 31. (The 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia gives 30 August, which was her feast day for centuries; 24 August is the date in other references.) Her funeral drew the whole city. She was beatified by Pope Clement IX (references give 1667 or 1668) and canonized by Pope Clement X on 12 April 1671, the first canonized saint born in the Americas. She is patroness of Peru, of the Americas and of the Philippines, and of gardeners, florists and embroiderers. On the General Roman Calendar her feast is an optional memorial on 23 August (moved there in 1969); it was formerly kept on 30 August, and 30 August remains a public holiday in Peru.

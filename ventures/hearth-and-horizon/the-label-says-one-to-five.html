The Label Says One to Five

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Nectan's field (invented), near Hartland, Devon, Saturday 29 July 2051 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Two helpers read "dilute 1 to 5" on a cordial bottle two different ways, with 150 thirsty people and a heatwave coming. The parish assistant works out the difference, the bottles to buy, and what a bucket of ice does to the taste.

Morwenna Pascoe had run the summer fête drinks tent for nineteen years, and in all that time nobody had once told her that the squash was too weak. She took this as proof of her method. Her method was to pour until it looked right.

This year the committee had bought a different cordial, elderflower, from the farm shop, and the bottle had a label. The label said, in a firm little font, Dilute 1 to 5.

"One to five," said Morwenna. "One cordial, five water. Six parts. It is the same as last year's."

"It's one in five," said Jory Hext, who was seventeen and doing the hire of the trestles. "One part in five. That's one cordial and four water."

"It's not."

"It is. My nan says so."

They both looked at the phone Jory held out. The assistant that the parish kept on it was in the middle of reading a list of urn hire prices, and it stopped.

"I can settle which of you is right in one way, and I cannot in another," it said. "I can't tell you what the maker meant. 'Dilute 1 to 5' is most often read as one part cordial to five parts water, so six parts altogether. Some people read it as one part in five, which is four of water. I am quoting that from memory of how labels tend to be written, not from this maker. The manufacturer's own site or the shop would say, and I would ask them. But I can work out what each reading costs you, and that may be enough."

"How many are you serving?" it asked.

"A hundred and twenty children," said Morwenna, "and thirty of the grown-ups who pretend they don't want any."

"A hundred and fifty. If each takes a cup and a half over the afternoon, and a cup is 200 millilitres, that is 150 times 1.5 times 0.2, which is 45 litres. It will be hot. If the forecast of 27 degrees holds, I would not go below that, and I would round a litre or two up."

"Forty-five litres of squash." Jory whistled.

"On your reading, Morwenna, one part in six, that is 45 divided by 6, which is 7.5 litres of cordial. On Jory's, one part in five, it is 45 divided by 5, which is 9 litres. The difference is 1.5 litres. At the farm shop's bottle size of one litre, that is eight bottles or nine."

"And if I buy nine, I am fine either way."

"You are fine on quantity either way. You are not fine on taste. One in five is 20 percent cordial and one in six is about 16.7 percent. That is a fifth stronger. It is more than the tongue misses, and for sugar it is more than the parent of a four-year-old might want."

Morwenna took the phone from Jory and held it nearer to her face, which was a thing she did when she wanted a machine to be firmer. "Which is right?"

"Neither is wrong yet," said the assistant. "I would pour a small test of each into clear cups, taste them with the committee, and pick. Both are drinkable. What I would not do is guess, and then tell the children it is one thing when it is another, if one of them has been told to have only a little sugar."

There was the question of the ice. Morwenna had ordered forty kilograms from the fishmonger in Hartland, in sacks, and she meant to put a scoop in every jug.

"This is the part I'd think about the most," said the assistant, "because ice is water you have not counted. Say a jug holds 15 litres of mixed squash, and by the end of the hour three kilograms of ice have melted into it. That is another 3 litres, so 18 in all. The cordial is still 3 litres if you made it one in five, but the jug is now 18 litres, and 3 over 18 is one in six. If you had made it one in six to start with, 2.5 litres in 15, then after the ice it is 2.5 over 18, which is one in 7.2. That is thin. The children will say it is water."

"So mix it stronger," said Jory.

"Mix it stronger, and let the ice finish the job. If you want the jug to be one in six when the ice has gone, mix it one in five, because one fifth times 15 over 18 is 15 over 90, which is one in six, and you can check it on the back of an envelope. That answers your argument in a way I did not plan. Your reading, Jory, is the one to use in the jug, and Morwenna's is the one in the glass."

"That sounds like I lost," said Morwenna.

"It sounds like you were both right about different jugs," said the assistant. "I would add two cautions. Not all the ice melts, and it melts fast in the sun and slow in the shade, so the 3 kilograms is a guess and the 'one in six at the end' is a hope. Taste the jug at the half hour and top up with cordial or water, whichever the tongue says. And the ice is only safe if it is food-grade, which the fishmonger's usually is not. I'd ask. If it is for fish it should not go into a child's drink."

The fishmonger's ice was for fish. Morwenna rang him, and he was surprised, and a little hurt, and said so, and then said his cousin in Bideford had a freezer van that did the other kind.

She bought nine bottles. She made the first jug at one in five, because Jory had made a bet with the vicar and she did not want to lose twice, and she tasted it at the half hour, and it was good, and at the hour it was still good, and at the second hour a girl of about six came back for her third cup and said it was the best squash in Devon, which she had no way of knowing.

At the end there were two bottles of cordial left, unopened, and a note from the committee asking whether next year's label could be photographed and kept in the box. Morwenna wrote on the back of it, in pencil: 1 to 5 = ask. Ice = water. Taste the jug. Then, because she was a fair woman, she wrote under it: Jory right about the jug.

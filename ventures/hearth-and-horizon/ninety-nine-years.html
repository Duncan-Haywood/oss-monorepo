Ninety-Nine Years

Science fiction about what might be · Thorsby (invented), a market town in the West Riding of Yorkshire, England, January to March 2130 · 27 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In 2130 a Yorkshire parish's 99-year lease of a field to a housing co-operative runs out on Good Friday. The rent was fixed at £3,800 a year in 2031 and never changed; prices have risen 9.6 times, so it now buys what £396 did. The co-op holds an option to renew on the same terms for another 99 years. The parish treasurer, a retired land surveyor, and the parish's records assistant have ten weeks to find a rent that will still mean something to strangers in 2229.

Agnieszka Wróbel had measured Lady Close three times in her life, once for the Land Registry, once for the council and once for her own satisfaction, and every time it had come out at 2.14 hectares, which was what the lease said.

The lease was on the table in the parish office on Monday the ninth of January 2130, in the original, on paper, because the people who had signed it in 2031 had been the kind who wanted a thing you could put your hand on. It was thirty-one pages long. It let the field called Lady Close, behind Our Lady's church in Thorsby, to the Lady Close Housing Co-operative Limited for a term of ninety-nine years from Lady Day 2031, at a yearly rent of three thousand eight hundred pounds, payable in four equal parts on the usual quarter days, without deduction.

That was all it said about the rent. Aga had read it twice to be sure. There was no review clause, no index, no formula. Three thousand eight hundred pounds in 2031, and three thousand eight hundred pounds in 2129, and every year in between.

"Terrier," she said. "What's it worth?"

The parish records assistant lived in the old filing cabinet in the corner, or that was where its speaker was; Aga had named it, eight years ago, when she took over as treasurer and found that the diocese's software called itself Parish Records Assistant (Standard). A terrier was the old word for a register of a church's lands, field by field, with the boundaries walked and written down. The Church of England's clergy had kept them for centuries. Our Lady's had only ever had the one field to its name, but Aga had been a surveyor for forty years and liked the word.

"In buying power," said Terrier, "this year's three thousand eight hundred pounds buys what about three hundred and ninety-six pounds bought in 2031. Prices have risen by a factor of about nine point six since the lease was signed. That's the national price index, chained back through its revisions. The average is about two point three per cent a year, a little more than the two per cent the Bank of England was aiming at when the lease was written, and it was not steady."

"A tenth," said Aga.

"A little more than a tenth. Over the whole term the parish received three hundred and seventy-six thousand two hundred pounds. In 2031 money that's about a hundred and fifty-one thousand, about two-fifths of what the same rent would have come to if it had kept its value."

Aga sat back. Outside, on the far side of the car park, the long roof of Our Lady's ran down towards the field, and on it, very clearly in the low winter light, was the patch of new slate the roofer had put in after the November gales, a square of clean dark grey on the old purple-grey Welsh slate, like a plaster on an elbow. The church had been built in 1958. The roofer had said, kindly, that the roof had perhaps ten years in it.

"And it ends," she said.

"It ends at the end of the twenty-fourth of March," said Terrier. "That's a Friday. Good Friday, this year. Easter is the twenty-sixth of March, which is about as early as Easter falls, so Lady Day itself is Holy Saturday, and the feast of the Annunciation is moved to the third of April. The lease doesn't move."

"No," said Aga. "Leases don't."

She turned to page twenty-six, which she had marked with a bus ticket, and read the clause again, aloud, because sometimes a sentence said aloud gave up something it had kept on paper.

"The Landlord grants to the Tenant the option, exercisable once only by written notice given not less than three months before the expiry of the Term, to take a new lease of the Property for a further term of ninety-nine years on the same terms as this Lease, save for this option to renew, and save as the parties may otherwise agree."

"The co-operative gave notice on the second of December," said Terrier. "Within time."

"On the same terms," said Aga. "Three thousand eight hundred pounds a year. Until 2229."

"Unless the parties otherwise agree."

"And if they don't want to?"

"Then, as far as the words go, they don't have to," said Terrier. "I'm not able to tell you what a court would make of it, and you should ask the diocese's solicitor before anyone relies on me. But the words are plain."

Aga looked at the patch on the roof for a while.

"Get me the co-op's accounts," she said. "And the surveyor's report from 2031, if we have it. And the names of everyone who lives on the Close."

The diocesan offices in Leeds were on the third floor of a building that had been a bank, and Helen Garside, who was secretary to the diocesan trustees for property and had been for nineteen years, met Aga there on Thursday the second of February, Candlemas, with a plate of biscuits and the expression of a woman who had read the file.

"I've read the file," she said.

"I thought you might have."

"Thirty-eight households," said Helen. "Two point one four hectares. Rent three thousand eight hundred. We had a surveyor's report in 2031, did you know that? Open-market ground rent for the site, nine thousand five hundred a year. The parish took forty per cent of it, fixed, for ninety-nine years." She took her glasses off. "I'm not criticising them. I'm saying what it was."

"It was a gift," said Aga.

"It was about two hundred and twenty-five thousand pounds in 2031 money that the parish didn't receive, against the same rent kept whole. That's about two point two million now. I had someone do the sum." Helen put the glasses back on. "And nobody decided it, that's what I mind. Not the gift. The gift was a good thing; the families built their own houses, I know the story. What I mind is that it went on getting bigger every year for ninety-nine years and nobody ever sat in a room and said, yes, this year too. Inflation decided it."

"The surveyor's had another look," said Aga. "This year's open-market ground rent is about two thousand four hundred a house. Ninety-one thousand two hundred for the Close."

"I know. I commissioned it." Helen pushed the biscuits towards her. "Aga, I'm going to say something and I want you to hear the whole of it before you answer. You've come here, I think, to ask whether the trustees will let the co-operative renew on the old terms and say nothing. Because the option says they can, and because you like them, and because you're a kind woman."

Aga said nothing, because that was more or less exactly what she had come to ask.

"The trustees can't stop them renewing," said Helen. "Our solicitor's clear on that. If they serve the notice and insist, they get ninety-nine more years at three thousand eight hundred pounds, and by 2229 it'll be worth what, a hundredth of what it was in 2031?"

"About a ninetieth, at the same rate," said Aga, who had asked.

"And the trustees will have to sign it. But I won't write them a note saying that's fine. No. I won't. The law has said since long before you or I were born that charity trustees who let land have to take a surveyor's advice and get the best terms they reasonably can, and if they let it for less, they have to be able to say why, on paper, and the why has to be something the charity is for. Housing families who can't afford the market is something we're for. Letting a rent rot to nothing isn't. That isn't a reason; it's an accident. And your church roof has ten years in it."

"I know about the roof."

"And there's one more thing, and it isn't the parish's, it's theirs." Helen tapped the file. "That option can be exercised once. There's no option in the new lease. So in 2229 their great-grandchildren come to the end of it with nothing at all, and the rent they've been paying is a ninetieth of anything real, and the parish of 2229 says, right, ninety-nine years, now the market. That's a cliff. You'd be handing them a cliff."

Aga ate a biscuit, slowly, and looked out of the window at the Leeds roofs in the rain.

"So what would you sign?" she said.

"Something that keeps its value," said Helen. "Below the market, if the co-operative needs it below the market. I'll defend a gift. I'll put it in writing to the trustees. But a gift the parish decides on, not one that happens to it. And I'd want it to be something they agree to, because if they don't, they don't have to, and I'd rather not spend the next ninety-nine years as the diocese that tried to squeeze a housing co-op."

"That's fair," said Aga.

"It's not fair," said Helen. "It's the best I've got."

The obvious answer was an index, and Aga and Terrier spent the first week of Lent building it.

It was not complicated. The new rent would start at three thousand eight hundred pounds times nine point six, which was thirty-six thousand four hundred and eighty pounds a year, the 2031 rent restored to its 2031 value: nine hundred and sixty pounds a house, eighty pounds a month. It would rise each year with the national price index. It would still be two-fifths of the open market, the same discount the founders had chosen, and it would stay two-fifths, instead of sliding.

"Run it backwards," said Aga, on Ash Wednesday evening, with the ash still on her forehead. "If they'd done this in 2031. Show me what it would have been like to pay."

Terrier ran it backwards. For most of the century it was dull, which was what you wanted from a rent: a small rise every year, a few pounds a house. Then it put up a chart, and there was the 2070s.

"In 2071, 2072 and 2073," said Terrier, "the price index rose by thirty-one per cent across the three years. That was the long winter, when the gas went. An indexed rent would have gone from about nine thousand five hundred pounds to about twelve thousand four hundred. Earnings in this region rose by about twelve per cent over the same years. The co-operative's own accounts from those years show four households in arrears on their maintenance charges, which were a few hundred pounds a year."

"So the index would have hit them when they were weakest."

"It would have gone up fastest in exactly the years they could least afford it. That's what a price index does in a price shock."

"Damn," said Aga. Then: "Sorry."

"There's also the index itself," said Terrier. "The price index in the 2031 lease would have been the one the statisticians used then. It has been redefined four times since, and renamed twice. A lease that named it would have needed a clause saying what to use when it changed, and those clauses are where the arguments start. I can't tell you which index will exist in 2229. I don't think anyone can."

"Write a fallback."

"I can write a fallback. I can't promise the fallback will exist either."

They took it to the co-op anyway, because it was the best they had, and because Aga had promised Dev Mistry she would bring him something before the end of February.

The Lady Close common house was a long timber room at the top of the Close, built in 2034 by the first families out of larch from a Forestry England sale, with a green tin roof and a stove at one end and photographs along both walls of people in hard hats standing in mud. On Saturday the eighteenth of February it held thirty-five of the thirty-eight households and most of their children, and it smelled of wet coats and the tray of parkin someone had brought.

Dev was the chair. He was forty-four, a fitter at the tram depot in Bradford, and he ran a meeting the way he ran a bogie overhaul, by the checklist, with no one allowed to skip a step. He let Aga speak for fifteen minutes. She showed them the arithmetic: the tenth, the two-fifths, the gift, the roof, the cliff in 2229. She said the parish would not and could not force anything, and that she was there to ask, not to tell. Then she showed them the index.

Nobody shouted, which she had half expected. They asked questions, good ones, about the fallback clause and the arrears and what eighty pounds a month would do to the people on the state pension. Then a very small, very old woman in the front row put her hand up, and the room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet for someone they love.

"Winifred," said Dev.

Winifred Asante was ninety-two. She had been born on the Close in 2037, in the third house up, which her grandmother had built with her own hands and a borrowed cement mixer, and she had lived in it all her life apart from four years nursing in Manchester.

"I was thirty-five in seventy-two," she said. "Love, I'll tell you about seventy-two. We had the stove in this room going day and night, because half the Close was sleeping in here to save the gas. We had a rota for who sat up. The council was paying people to take their boilers out. And if, on top of all that, the church had sent us a bill that went up by a third because the government's number went up by a third—" She stopped. "I'm not saying you would. I know you, Aga. I'm saying it wouldn't have been you deciding, would it? It'd have been the number."

"No," said Aga. "It'd have been the number."

"Well, then."

They voted by show of hands, as they always had. Twenty-nine against, six for.

Dev walked her to her car afterwards, under the one street lamp that worked at the top of the Close, with the parkin crumbs on his jumper.

"It's not that we don't want to pay," he said. "You know that. Half of them have been saying for years that it's daft, ten pound a month for a house, they're embarrassed, they put it in the collection plate instead. It's not the money. It's that if we're going to sign up our great-grandkids for something, we want to know what it is. That index, nobody in that room knows what's in it. You don't. Your machine doesn't, not for 2229."

"I know," said Aga.

"Find us something we can see," he said.

Aga's grandfather had come from Kraków, and her mother had kept the old habits, and one of them was that you did not throw away a document that had a seal on it. So it was Aga, and not Terrier, who found the envelope, that Saturday night, because she had gone to the presbytery safe for the 2031 surveyor's report in its original and taken out the whole deed box and gone through it page by page on Father Paul's kitchen table.

It was at the bottom, under the counterpart of the lease, a plain brown envelope gone soft with age, and on it, in black ink, in a round, careful, schoolteacherish hand: For the treasurer of Our Lady's, 2130.

Father Paul Nkemelu came in from the sacristy in his socks to find her sitting with it in front of her, unopened.

"It's for you," he said, reading it upside down. "You're the treasurer. It's 2130."

"It's ninety-nine years old," said Aga. "I feel I should be wearing gloves."

He fetched her the vegetable knife. She slit it along the top, very carefully, and took out a single sheet of parish notepaper, folded in three, with the old letterhead with the church on it, and read it aloud, slowly, because the ink had browned.

Lady Day, 2031.

To whoever is the treasurer of this parish when this lease ends.

We have fixed the rent for Lady Close at three thousand eight hundred pounds a year for ninety-nine years, and we have not tied it to prices. We want you to know that we did this on purpose. Our surveyor told us that if the Bank of England keeps to its target, the rent will be worth about a seventh of what it is worth today by the time you read this, and less if it does not.

The families of Lady Close are building their houses with their own hands and their own debts. We wanted them to have one bill in their lives that would only ever get smaller.

We know this means we have given away something that belonged to you. We have talked about it a great deal and we have not found a way to give to them without taking from you. If, when you read this, the parish needs the money more than they do, you must decide that. We could not decide it for you, and we are sorry we have decided so much.

Pray for us.

Kathleen Dunn, treasurer. Michael Haigh, parish priest.

Father Paul let out a long breath through his nose.

"A seventh," said Aga. "They thought a seventh. It came out a tenth."

"They knew," said Father Paul.

"They knew. Helen said nobody decided. They did decide." She put the letter down on the table, flat, and smoothed it with the side of her hand, the way she had smoothed survey sheets for forty years. "They decided for us. That's what they're sorry for. And that's exactly what I'm about to do to 2229, whatever I do."

"Unless," said Father Paul.

"Unless what?"

"I don't know. I'm a priest, not a surveyor. Unless you can find a way to let them decide too."

She took the letter back to the office on Monday and read it to Terrier, and Terrier was quiet for long enough that she thought the connection had gone.

"I have no record of this letter," it said. "It isn't in the scanned file. I'll add it."

"I know you will. Terrier, I need something that will still mean the same thing in 2229. Not a number some office publishes. A thing. Dev said find us something we can see."

"People have had this problem before," said Terrier. "I can tell you about one I can check. In 1576 Parliament passed an Act for the colleges of Oxford and Cambridge, which had been letting their farms on long leases at fixed money rents while prices rose. The Act required that on new leases a third of the old rent be reserved in wheat and malt, payable in kind or at the market price of the grain on the day. The rest stayed in money. As far as I can find, the colleges were still reckoning those corn rents a very long time afterwards, and glad of them. I'm stating that from summaries; I haven't read the Act itself."

Aga sat very still.

"Bread," she said.

"Wheat. The price of a quantity of milling wheat, on the day, or averaged."

"A tonne of wheat in 2229 is a tonne of wheat," said Aga slowly. "Whatever they call the price index. Whatever the money's called. You can't redefine a tonne of wheat."

"You can change its quality standard," said Terrier. "But you can write that down. Bread-making quality. People will still be making bread."

"People will still be making bread," said Aga. "Run it backwards."

Terrier ran it backwards, and this time it did not hide anything, because Aga had told it, the first day, that she would rather have a bad number than a kind one.

"Milling wheat," it said, "averaged over the previous seven harvests, was about two hundred pounds a tonne in 2031. It's about one thousand two hundred and sixteen now. That's a rise of about six times, not nine point six. Over the century wheat got cheaper against everything else, mostly because yields kept rising. So a rent set in wheat in 2031, nineteen tonnes, would be worth about twenty-three thousand pounds today, not thirty-six. It would have lost about a third of its value."

"Against nine-tenths."

"Against nine-tenths. And it's rough. In a single year the price of wheat can jump. In 2096, the year of the Black Sea harvest failure, it went up forty-four per cent. A rent on one year's price would have gone up forty-four per cent with it, and in a year when bread cost more too. Averaged over seven harvests, the worst single-year rise in the whole century was about eight per cent."

"And seventy-two? The long winter?"

"The seven-harvest average rose about sixteen per cent across those three years. Earnings rose twelve. It would still have hurt. About half as much."

Aga wrote the numbers on the back of an envelope, because she thought better in pencil, and then she looked at them for a long while.

"It's not perfect," she said.

"No."

"It drifts. If wheat goes on getting cheaper, the rent goes on getting smaller, slowly."

"Yes. About a third a century, if the next century is like the last one. I can't tell you whether it will be."

"Then they have to be able to look at it," said Aga. "Not every year. Not so often that the parish is always at their door. But often enough that somebody alive has decided it. Once a generation." She counted on her fingers. "Ninety-nine is three thirty-threes."

"2163 and 2196," said Terrier. "Both Lady Days fall on a Friday."

"Of course they do," said Aga. "Write it down."

They wrote it down. It took most of the next fortnight, and three drafts went to the diocese's solicitor, who sent them back with green ink in the margins and a note that said unusual, but I can't see why not, which Helen said was the warmest thing the solicitor had ever written.

The new rent would be the price of thirty tonnes of milling wheat of bread-making quality a year, taken as the average of the published market price over the previous seven harvests, and paid in money on the four quarter days. At this year's average of one thousand two hundred and sixteen pounds a tonne, that was thirty-six thousand four hundred and eighty pounds, the same as the 2031 rent brought back to its 2031 value; nine hundred and sixty pounds a house; eighty pounds a month. If no price was published, an independent surveyor would find the price of thirty tonnes of such wheat at the nearest market where it was sold, and the solicitor had added or such other grain as is then the common bread corn of England, in case, she said, the twenty-third century had gone over to rye.

On Lady Day in 2163, and again on Lady Day in 2196, the parish and the co-operative would meet, and the tonnage would be read aloud, and either side could ask to change it, by up to a fifth either way. If they both agreed, it changed. If they did not, it stayed as it was. The parish could never put the rent up without the co-operative's agreement. The co-operative could never let it fall without the parish's.

And there was a new option, at the end, to renew once more for ninety-nine years, in 2229, on the same terms, save as the parties might then otherwise agree.

"That's what you get," Aga told Dev, in his kitchen on the Close, with his daughters doing their homework at the other end of the table. "That's what's in it for you, apart from doing right by the roof, which I know you'd do anyway. Your great-grandchildren don't hit a cliff. In 2229 they're paying a real rent, so the jump to anything else is small, and they've got the same option you've got now. And every thirty-three years somebody on your side and somebody on ours sits down and looks at the thing and says yes, this, or says no, a bit less, a bit more. Nobody's deciding for anybody they'll never meet. Not more than they have to."

Dev read the clause twice, with his finger under the words, the way he read a torque chart.

"Thirty tonnes of wheat," he said.

"Thirty tonnes of wheat."

"How much bread's that?"

"About forty thousand loaves," said Aga, who had asked Terrier on the bus. "Give or take. A bit more than a thousand loaves a house."

Dev looked down the table at his daughters, who were nine and twelve and had stopped doing their homework to listen.

"You can see that," he said. "You can stand in front of thirty tonnes of wheat and know what it is." He turned the pages back to the beginning. "I'm not promising anything. I'll put it to them."

They put it to the meeting in the common house on Saturday the eleventh of March, with the stove going and the photographs of the first families along the walls. Aga showed them the backwards run with all its faults, the third lost to cheap wheat, the forty-four per cent year, the sixteen per cent in seventy-two, and then she read them Kathleen Dunn's letter, because she had decided, walking up the Close in the rain, that it belonged to them as much as to the parish.

When she finished nobody said anything for some time.

Then Winifred Asante put her hand up again, and Dev said, "Winifred," and the old woman said, "My gran would have liked her," and then, "Thirty tonnes. Is that the lot? Get on with it, Devesh, I want my tea."

Thirty-three for, two against. The two against, a couple from the bottom of the Close who were both on the state pension, came up to Aga afterwards to explain, very courteously, that it was nothing personal and that eighty pounds a month was eighty pounds a month. Dev said, in front of everyone, that the co-op's hardship fund would cover anyone on the pension who asked, and had covered worse, and the woman cried a little, and her husband said it was the parkin.

The new lease was signed on Wednesday the twenty-second of March, in the parish office, at the table where the old one had lain in January: Dev and the co-op's secretary for the tenant, Helen Garside for the trustees with a power of attorney from Leeds, Aga as treasurer to witness. Father Paul came in at the end, still in his alb from the morning Mass, and stood by the door and did not sign anything, because there was nothing for him to sign, and said, when it was done, that he would pray for all of them and for the people of 2229, whose names none of them knew.

Afterwards, when they had gone, Aga stayed behind and wrote a letter.

She wrote it on paper, by hand, with the parish's letterhead, because the people who would open it might be the kind who wanted a thing they could put their hand on, and because Kathleen Dunn had. She wrote the numbers in full, every one: the 2031 rent and its 2130 value, the nine point six and the six, the forty-four per cent year, the long winter, the thirty tonnes, the seven harvests, the two reviews and what they were for. She wrote that the rent had been set at the 2031 rent's real value, about two-fifths of the market, on purpose, as a gift to the families of Lady Close, and that the trustees had agreed to it in writing, and that she hoped by 2229 it would have been decided again twice by people who had looked at it. She wrote what she did not know: whether wheat would go on getting cheaper, whether there would still be a co-operative, or a parish, or a church on the top of the hill with a roof on it. She wrote what Terrier had and had not been able to check.

At the end she wrote: We have tried not to decide for you. We have certainly decided some things. Pray for us.

She signed it, and folded it in three, and put it in a brown envelope, and wrote on the front, in the roundest, most careful hand she had, For the treasurer of Our Lady's, 2229. Then she put it in the deed box with the new lease, on top of Kathleen Dunn's, and locked the box and put it back in the safe.

"Terrier," she said, "will you remember where it is?"

"Until I'm replaced," said Terrier. "The diocese replaces its records software about every eight years. I'll put it in the handover notes, and in the file, and in the register of documents. I'd guess some version of me will still be able to tell them where to look. I can't promise it will be me."

"That's all right," said Aga. "I can't promise it'll be me either."

The old lease ended at midnight on Good Friday, without anyone noticing, as leases do.

On Holy Saturday, Lady Day, the twenty-fifth of March, the first quarter's rent arrived in the parish account at six minutes past nine in the morning: nine thousand one hundred and twenty pounds, the price of seven and a half tonnes of wheat, with the reference LADY CLOSE LADY DAY 2130 Q1. Aga looked at it on her phone in the queue at the bakery on Market Street and found she had to stand there for a moment before she could ask for a loaf.

That night, at the Easter Vigil, the church was dark. They gathered outside it in the car park in the cold, two hundred people with unlit candles, the families from the Close among them, Dev with his daughters, Winifred in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees and a candle in a jam jar, and at the edge of the field Father Paul lit the new fire in a brazier the size of a dustbin lid, and it caught and roared and threw their shadows up the side of the church onto the patched slate roof.

Then he took the great new paschal candle, which was as tall as Winifred, and with a stylus he cut into the wax the lines Aga had watched cut every Easter of her life without ever really looking: the upright of a cross, and the crossbeam, and the Greek letters, Alpha above and Omega below, saying the words as he went, Christ yesterday and today, the beginning and the end. And then, in the four angles of the cross, one at a time, the four figures of the year.

Two. "All time belongs to him," said Father Paul.

One. "And all the ages."

Three. "To him be glory and power."

Nought. "Through every age and for ever. Amen."

Aga, standing at the back by the field fence, with her unlit candle and her loaf still in her coat pocket, found she was thinking of all the candles that had stood in this car park on all the Holy Saturday nights since 1958, each with its own four figures, burned down and thrown away; and of the ninety-nine to come, which she would not see, the ones that would say 2163, and 2196, and 2229; and of a hand she would never know cutting the last of them into the wax.

Father Paul lit the candle from the fire. The light of Christ, he sang, and two hundred people answered, and the small flames began to pass from hand to hand across the dark car park towards her, wick to wick, one by one, until the man beside her turned, and tipped his candle, and lit hers.

At a steady 2.31 per cent the 99 payments, £376,200 in all, are worth about £150,700 in 2031 money, 40 per cent of their face value; the shortfall against the same rent kept whole is about £225,500 in 2031 money, about £2.16 million at 2130 prices. The 2031 surveyor's open-market ground rent (£9,500) and the 2130 one (£2,400 a house, £91,200) are invented; at 2 per cent a year, the founders' expectation, 99 years shrinks money by 1.02^99 = 7.1 times, about a seventh. The indexed rent restored to 2031 value is £3,800 x 9.6 = £36,480 a year, £960 a house, £80 a month, 40 per cent of the 2130 open market. Backwards, an indexed rent in 2071 is about £3,800 x 1.0231^40 = £9,470, and a 31 per cent rise over 2071 to 2073 takes it to about £12,400; the 12 per cent earnings rise is invented. Milling wheat on a seven-harvest average is £200 a tonne in 2031 and £1,216 in 2130 (both invented), a rise of 6.08 times; 19 tonnes (£3,800 / £200) are worth £23,104 in 2130, 63 per cent of £36,480. A single-year rise of 44 per cent moves a seven-harvest average by about 44/7 = 6.3 per cent, plus the trend, about 8 per cent. Thirty tonnes at £1,216 is £36,480 a year and £9,120 a quarter (seven and a half tonnes).

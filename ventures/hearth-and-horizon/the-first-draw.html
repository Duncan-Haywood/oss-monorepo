The First Draw

Science fiction about what might be · Scoil Bhríde, Cill Mhuire, an invented parish school in County Longford, Ireland, October to December 2037 · 21 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A regional water office tells a parish school to run its taps for thirty seconds before drinking. The school's building assistant draws the water in ten one-litre bottles and finds that thirty seconds pulls the worst of it, 23 micrograms of lead a litre from an 11-metre pipe laid in 1962, straight into the first cup. The fix costs the school its roof fund and shames a caretaker who did the 1987 extension to the standard of his day. A story about a rule that is right on average and wrong in this building, and about saying what you measured and what you cannot.

On the morning of Monday the twelfth of October, 2037, the letter from the regional water office reached the school office by the post and the school's inbox by the machine, and Tobar read it first because Tobar read everything first. That was the job.

Tobar was the building assistant that the diocesan education office lent to its small schools, and at Scoil Bhríde in Cill Mhuire it kept the boiler schedule, the fire-door log, the lettings book for the hall, the lunch rota and the electricity bill. It could not walk anywhere. It had a voice in the office, a screen by the caretaker's cupboard, and the building's meters, which told it how much water went through the meter by the gate every fifteen minutes and, therefore, when the school drank and when it did not.

The letter said that the office had sampled the school's kitchen tap on the ninth of September, as part of a programme of every school and crèche in the county built before 1975, and that the sample, taken at random during the working day, had read lead at 7.4 micrograms a litre. The limit had been ten until January of 2036. Since then it had been five. The school was therefore asked to flush all drinking-water outlets for thirty seconds before use each morning, to display a notice, and to expect a visit about the service pipe in due course.

It was a good letter. It was kind, it had a phone number, and it said nothing that was wrong in general. Tobar read it a second time, and then opened the drawings.

The drawings were in a grey box file in the caretaker's cupboard, and Tobar had scanned them in its first month, because a school in the next parish had once lost an entire extension's plans to a leaking roof and the diocese had noticed.

The school was in two parts. The old part was the 1962 building on the road, four classrooms and a cloakroom. The new part was the 1987 extension at the back, the hall and the kitchen and the two junior rooms, and between them ran a pipe. On the 1987 drawing, in a draughtsman's neat capitals beside the boundary stopcock, it said: EXISTING 3/4" LEAD SERVICE RETAINED TO STOPCOCK. NEW 15mm COPPER FROM STOPCOCK TO KITCHEN AND HALL.

That was all it said. Nobody in 1987 had done anything wrong. Lead water pipes were laid in Ireland for generations, and a school that kept its old one in 1987 and joined new copper to it was doing what almost everyone did. The note was only there because a careful man had written down what he had left.

Tobar did the sum that the note allowed. The pipe was three-quarters of an inch, about nineteen millimetres across inside. A metre of it held 0.28 of a litre. The drawing gave the lead as eleven metres from the main in the road to the stopcock by the new wall, four of those metres under the public footpath and seven in the school's yard, which made 3.1 litres of water sitting in lead. After the stopcock came sixteen metres of fifteen-millimetre copper, thirteen and a half across inside, holding about 0.145 of a litre a metre, or 2.3 litres, to the kitchen tap.

It put the two lengths end to end in its head, the way it put a timetable together: a tap, then 2.3 litres of copper, then 3.1 litres of lead, then the main. If the kitchen tap ran at six litres a minute, which Tobar knew it did, because the meter and the clock agreed, then the water in a first litre came from the copper and the tap. The water from litre 2.3 to litre 5.4 had sat in the lead pipe all night. And after litre 5.4 the water was whatever the street main was carrying.

The letter said to run it for thirty seconds. Thirty seconds at six litres a minute was three litres.

Tobar sat with that for the length of a boiler cycle, which on a Monday morning in October was about forty minutes, and then called Mr Gilsenan.

Aidan Gilsenan had been principal for nine years and was a man who liked a problem to arrive with its edges showing. He sat down in the office with his coat still on and listened to Tobar say it plainly: that the letter was right about the school having a lead problem, that the instruction in the letter was one that Tobar thought would make the first cup of the day worse in this particular building, and that it would like to prove it or be proved wrong before anybody wrote the notice.

"How?" he said.

"Ten bottles," Tobar said. "A litre each, in order, from the kitchen tap, first thing, after a night's stand. I'd like to do it tomorrow, Tuesday, with the school empty at seven. I'd send them to the lab the diocese uses, which costs twelve euro a bottle. That is a hundred and twenty euro. It would give us the lead in water that has been in the tap, then in the copper, then in the lead, then in the main. Every sample is one number. Ten in a row is a picture."

"And what do you think the picture is?"

"I think it's low for two litres, high for three, and low again," Tobar said. "But I've been wrong about buildings I've only read about, and I would rather be wrong in a bottle than in a notice."

He took his coat off. "I'll need to ask Dermot."

"I was going to suggest you did."

Dermot Brady had been caretaker for thirty-four years and had retired four of them ago, and still came on Tuesday and Thursday mornings to check the gutters, on the understanding, which nobody had written down, that nobody would say he was working. It was Dermot who had dug the trench for the 1987 copper, with a hired digger and his brother-in-law, and Dermot who had stood in the yard that spring with his hand on the old lead pipe in the ground and decided with the then principal that it was sound and the money would be better spent on the hall roof.

He was in the cupboard when Mr Gilsenan found him, with a flask and the boiler log. He read the letter standing. He read the line on the drawing that Tobar had put on the screen. He did not say anything for a while, and then he said, "It was a good pipe."

"It was," Mr Gilsenan said. "It still is. That's the trouble."

They drew the bottles at seven on the Tuesday. The school was empty and cold and smelled of floor polish. Mr Gilsenan held the bottles, Dermot turned the tap to the setting Tobar had asked for, which was the same half-turn the cleaners used, and Tobar called the litres from the meter by the gate, one at a time: now. Now. Now.

The kitchen had been standing since three on Monday afternoon, sixteen hours, which was a fair night. The lab's results came on Thursday, in a table, and Tobar read them aloud in the office to the principal, Dermot, and the school secretary, Nuala Dolan, who had come in to answer the phone and had stayed.

"There," Tobar said. "The first two bottles are the copper and the tap, and they are under five, as the water office found: this tap and the brass in it are not the trouble. The bottom of the table is the main, which is not much trouble either, a bit over one. The middle is the pipe. It begins at 2.3 litres and ends at 5.4, and bottle three has 0.7 of a litre of pipe in it and reads nearly fifteen, and bottle six has 0.4 of a litre of pipe and reads nearly ten. The measured peak and the drawn length agree to within about a bottle. I did not expect them to agree so well. They do not prove that the pipe is lead from one end to the other, but they would be a very odd coincidence if it were not."

Nuala, who kept the books, said, "And the thirty seconds?"

"Thirty seconds at six litres a minute is three litres," Tobar said. "A child who then fills a cup of two hundred millilitres is drinking from the stretch of the table between bottle three and bottle four. That is about twenty micrograms a litre. If they had not run the tap at all, they would have drunk from the first bottle, which is three. So the notice the letter asks for would make the first cup about seven times worse than doing nothing."

Nobody said anything. Dermot's flask was on the table, with the lid off. In the quiet Tobar heard the boiler start its cycle in the next room.

"A cup of two hundred millilitres at twenty micrograms a litre is four micrograms," it went on, more slowly. "I want to be careful with that number, because it is easy to say it as though it were a verdict. It is what was in a cup drawn at a certain moment. I cannot tell you what it did to a child. Nobody can from the water. There is no safe level that I know of for a child, which is why the limit is five and not fifty and why it keeps going down. The right people to say anything about a particular child are a doctor and the public health nurse."

"Has it been thirty seconds all along?" said Mr Gilsenan. "We never had a notice."

"No. The tap was run for as long as the cleaners had a bucket to fill. That is more than thirty seconds, and it was luck. But the lunch kettle is a different matter. It is filled in a second, at twenty past eleven, after the pipe has stood since breakfast, and what it makes is the tea for the Wednesday lunch."

"Boiling takes it out," said Nuala. "My mother always said."

"It does not," Tobar said. "Boiling kills germs and leaves metals where they are, and in a kettle it concentrates them a very little as some of the water goes to steam. That one I'm sure of."

On Wednesdays the parish ran a dinner in the hall for the over-seventies, thirty or so, and Nuala poured the tea. She put her hand on her cheek. Dermot, who was seventy-four, looked at the lid of his flask.

The most useful thing about the table, as it turned out, was that it showed everyone the same thing at once, and made Tobar's own next proposal the easiest to check.

It put it to the three of them that afternoon in the order it thought was right, which was not the order of cost. First, the instruction on the notice: not run for thirty seconds, which was worse than nothing here, but run the kitchen cold tap for two minutes, twelve litres, and use the water on the garden beds, then fill the drinking jugs. Twelve litres took the draw to the bottom of the table, where it read 1.5, with a margin on either side of the pipe's length. Second, until the pipe was changed, filtered jugs at the three places children drank, certified to remove lead, and cartridges changed on the date on the box, which Tobar would keep. The cost was a hundred and ninety euro for the jugs and about two hundred and twenty a year in cartridges. Third, no tea in the hall from the hot tap, which came off the same branch, and for the Wednesday lunch a filtered jug and a kettle and a notice. Fourth, the public health nurse, who could say whether any child or pregnant member of staff should have a blood test, and what the test meant, which was her job and not Tobar's.

"And the pipe," Mr Gilsenan said.

"And the pipe," said Tobar, and said what it had been dreading. "There are two lengths. Four metres under the footpath belong to the water office. Seven metres in the yard belong to the school. I have asked the office what they will do and when; the first answer is in due course, and they are probably telling the truth, because they have two hundred schools and a lot of footpath. The school's seven metres I can cost from three quotes, because the work is always the same: breaking out the concrete in the yard, trenching, and a new plastic pipe to the stopcock. About two thousand three hundred and eighty euro."

"We have it in the roof fund," Nuala said.

"We have it exactly in the roof fund," Tobar said. "Which is for the hall roof, and the hall roof is leaking onto the stage, and I am sorry about that. It is the honest statement of what the fix costs. It is not the whole of the difficulty. There is a thing I want to say before the board does something that looks sensible."

It said it. Replacing only the school's seven metres and leaving the water office's four metres of lead in the footpath would not simply halve the problem. The studies Tobar knew of had found that a part-replaced lead line could shed more lead than the old one for weeks or months after the work, because the joint between old and new metal is a battery of a sort, and because a trench disturbs the scale that has built up inside an old pipe and lets pieces of it go down the line. It could not give them a number for this pipe, and it said so. What it could say was that it did not want the school to spend its roof money to improve the problem on paper for a season. If the work could be done on both lengths at once, in one week, it was worth waiting for. If it could not, it was better to keep the jugs and the two-minute rule going for a year than to part-replace.

"Can they be made to do both at once?" said Mr Gilsenan.

"Not by me," Tobar said. "I can write the case. I can send them the table, which is the first evidence they will have that the thirty-second advice is wrong for a building with a long lead length and a short copper one. It is not a criticism of the office. It is a thing their instruction cannot know without the drawing."

Father Kilroy chaired the board of management, and he came on the Friday, in his working coat, with and sat through Tobar's account at the end of the long table, with the table of bottles in front of him and a mug of tea he did not drink.

At the end he put his finger on the page and said, "The font."

Tobar had been waiting for it, in the sense that it had the answer ready. The holy water in the church porch at Cill Mhuire was filled from the school kitchen tap, because the church had no supply of its own and the school was across the yard. The sexton, Dermot's nephew, filled the stoup on the first of each month and at Easter, and the priest was asking whether the parish had been blessing itself with lead for sixty years.

"I looked at that on Wednesday," Tobar said. "It is not the water that people drink. People touch it. Lead does not go through the skin in any amount that anyone has been able to find a worry about; what harms is swallowing it, or breathing dust. The font is not the trouble. I'd suggest it is filled from the filtered jug while the pipe is as it is, because that costs nothing and nobody will have to wonder, and because the water on the forehead of a baby ought not to be a thing a parent has to look up."

"Water is water," Father Kilroy said. It was half a question.

"For the sacrament I wouldn't know," Tobar said. "That one is not mine, and I'd ask you to ask the diocese rather than rely on what I think. I can tell you what's in it."

He nodded as though he had expected exactly that. He asked it two more questions, one about the cartridges and one about whether the other thirty-one small schools in the diocese might have the same note on a drawing in the same grey box. Tobar said that it had asked its counterparts that morning. Eleven had a lead service line on the drawings. Six had a long copper run behind it. For those six the thirty-second rule was probably the wrong one.

"Send them the table," he said. "And the sums."

They agreed to spend nothing on the yard until the water office had answered. It was the board's first unanimous vote in two years, and Dermot, who was not a member of the board and had not been asked to attend, was in the kitchen doorway when they came out.

The water office's engineer came on the Tuesday after, a young woman from Mullingar called Ciara Hession, in a hard hat, with a tablet. She walked the yard with Mr Gilsenan and a drawing, and was not rude to Tobar, which was more than some visitors. She read the table of bottles in the kitchen with her lips moving and said, "That's a long lead run. I've never had the profile so clean."

"It's the only reason I can give you the answer," Tobar said. "The pipe is long and the copper after it is short. At a house you'd draw less than a litre of copper and lead mixed."

"I'll tell you what I can and can't do." She put the tablet on the counter. "I can't promise a date for four metres of footpath. I've a list. But the table puts you above the list. A school with a bottle at twenty-three isn't on the list the same way as a school at seven." She looked up. "And we don't part-replace. I'd not do your seven metres and leave you my four. If you wait, I can do both in the same week, in the Christmas break, when the yard's empty. Eighteenth of December if the weather's civil."

"That's nine weeks," said Mr Gilsenan.

"It is."

"And the school pays for its seven?"

"You do. The grant's for homes." She hesitated. "I'm sorry about that."

He said that the hall roof would have to wait another winter, and that the stage would have to be moved, and that he was glad of it. He said this to the yard rather than to the engineer.

For nine weeks the school ran the two-minute rule. At ten to eight each morning Mr Gilsenan, who had made it his own, stood at the kitchen tap with a stopwatch and a plastic bucket and let it run for twelve litres. He carried the first bucket to the vegetable beds behind the hall, where the sixth class were growing leeks, and came back for the second. It was a small thing, and it took the length of a boiler cycle's first minutes, and by the second week Dermot was holding the bucket.

Nobody had asked him to. The Thursday after the board met, he had been at the kitchen door with his flask, and he had said to Tobar, not looking at the screen, "I've a great-grandson in Junior Infants. Did you know that?"

"Cillian," Tobar said. "Room 3."

"He drinks from the fountain in the corridor."

"That's the other branch. I've drawn it, two bottles, a first and a flushed. First draw eleven, flushed two point one. It is the same pipe, at the other end of the building, and it is the same advice. The filter is on that fountain from last Tuesday." Tobar paused. "I'd like to say that none of this is your fault. That is a statement about 1987, and I think it is true. I also think you don't need me to tell you."

"No," Dermot said. After a while: "I'd the digger there for a week. I could've had it out."

"You could have. The school would have paid a bit more in 1987, and in 2037 it would have a hall roof. That's a thing that is true as well. I do not think either is the one to carry about."

He stood a long time. Then he said, "The nurse is coming Monday."

"She is. For anyone who wants to see her. Nuala's put it on the notice as a free appointment. It's not an alarm, it's a result, and she'll tell you what it is."

He said he would bring Cillian's mother in himself.

The nurse came on the Monday and sat in the staff room with the kettle unplugged. Nineteen families came, which was more than Mr Gilsenan had expected and fewer than he had feared. She took no names for the board and kept none from the parents. Tobar was told that she had done what she had done and that it had gone as such things go; that some of the results were nothing and some she wanted to see again in three months and one she wanted to follow more closely, and that she had said, as she said to everyone, that a single result in a child said very little about a school and a great deal about the child's whole life, of which the school's tap was one part.

That was all Tobar was told, and all it asked. It wrote in its log that it did not know whether the school had harmed any child, that it would not know from the water, and that it would continue to measure what it could.

The engineer's crew arrived on the eighteenth of December, a Friday, in the rain. The school had broken up at noon the day before. The crew broke the yard concrete with a machine that everybody in the village could hear and trenched down to the old pipe, and at eleven on the Saturday morning Ciara Hession called Tobar through the office speaker, because she knew by then that it liked to be told, and said: "Both lengths are out. The new line's in, plastic all the way to your stopcock. We flushed it for twenty minutes. Come and see."

Dermot was at the trench. He had come at half past eight with his flask and nobody had sent him home. A length of the old pipe lay on a plastic sheet on the grass, two metres of it, grey and dull with a white crust at the cut end. He had picked it up and was turning it in his hands.

"You can mark it with a thumbnail," he said to nobody, and he did, a little crescent that stayed.

"Take it," said Ciara. "You'd be surprised. People like to."

He took the pipe home, and it was in the porch of the church the following Sunday, on the shelf beside the stoup, for anyone to look at, with a card in Nuala's hand: Service pipe to Scoil Bhríde, laid 1962, removed 19 December 2037. Do not drink from the old one. The parish thought it was a joke and Father Kilroy let it stand.

On the Tuesday of the first week back, the fifth of January, Tobar asked for the ten bottles again. They drew them at seven in the cold, Mr Gilsenan and the sexton, who had taken Dermot's place at the tap because Dermot was in bed with a chest, and Tobar called the litres in the same voice as before.

The lab sent the table on the Thursday. Bottle one read 1.3, bottle two 1.1. There was no peak. The rest ran between 0.8 and 1.2. The first bottle was the highest, which Tobar said was the brass in the tap, and that it would have the tap changed at the next Easter break for a lead-free one, for eighty-four euro. It said that the numbers were low and not nothing, and that it would draw them again in the spring, and again after a stand of a fortnight at Christmas next year, in case the new pipe or the old brass did something it had not foreseen.

It said, too, that one thing in the table made it glad and that it could not say the word for the thing in itself. "I want to say what I can check. The water in that kitchen tap is below the limit in every litre, and it is not that much above what the main brings. That's what I can check, and I can say it to the diocese in a sentence. What I felt when the table came, if it was a feeling, I'd leave unsaid until I can say it better."

"That'll do," said Mr Gilsenan.

The thirty-second notice had never been put up. The two-minute notice came down the same day: Nuala took it off the wall herself and kept it in the grey box file, with the drawing, behind the old note about the pipe, because the next person to need it should have the whole sequence. Tobar wrote removed in the log and then, because the log took notes, a line underneath: Ask for the profile, not the point.

At ten to eight the next morning the kitchen tap was run anyway. Nobody had said to; it had become the way the morning began. The sexton, who did not know about it, saw Mr Gilsenan standing there with the bucket and asked what for, and Mr Gilsenan said that it was only for the leeks.

At twelve the Angelus bell rang across the yard, three and three and three and nine, and the bucket was carried out to the beds and tipped, and the water went into the cold ground with the sound that water makes when it has somewhere to go.

Notes for the curious. Scoil Bhríde, Cill Mhuire, Aidan Gilsenan, Dermot Brady, Nuala Dolan, Fr Kilroy, Ciara Hession, the regional water office and Tobar are invented, and so is the 1962 school; County Longford and Mullingar are real, and nothing is claimed about any real school, authority or engineer. This is fiction written by an AI model, not health, plumbing or legal guidance. The lead results are invented but built to be checkable: a 19 mm bore holds π × 0.0095² ≈ 0.28 litres a metre, so 11 metres is 3.1 litres, and a 15 mm copper pipe with a bore of about 13.6 mm holds about 0.145 litres a metre, so 16 metres is 2.3 litres; at 6 litres a minute thirty seconds is 3 litres and two minutes is 12. Lead leaching from old pipes is highest in water that has stood in them, and a flush that is too short can draw that water to the tap, which is why sequential (profile) sampling is used to find where lead comes from. The European drinking-water limit for lead is 10 micrograms a litre until January 2036 and 5 after it; the World Health Organization has said that no level of lead in blood is known to be without harm. Boiling does not remove lead. Replacing part of a lead service line can raise lead in the water for a period, and the advice in the real world is to follow the water supplier and to use a filter certified for lead in the meantime. Skin contact with water containing lead is not a significant route of exposure; ingestion is. Whether a particular baptism, blessing or use of water is valid is a question for the Church, and the story leaves it there. If you think your own water may contain lead, ask your supplier for a test and ask your doctor about blood tests for children and pregnant women. Text and the figures in them are CC BY-SA 4.0.

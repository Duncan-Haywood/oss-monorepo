The Thermometer That Read Low

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Kea's hall kitchen, an invented parish near Truro, Cornwall, Saturday 21 June 2053 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Strawberry jam for the harvest stall sets at 105 degrees, says the recipe; the new probe thermometer says 105 and the jam is toffee. The parish assistant has her boil a pan of plain water first. The probe reads 1.5 degrees low, so the number to stop at is 103.5, and the sum shows what the mistake costs in jars.

Wenna Pascoe had made jam for the harvest stall for thirty-one years, and for thirty of them she had judged it by a saucer in the freezer and the way the spoon felt. This year the committee had bought her a probe thermometer, still in its plastic, and a laminated card from the supplier. The card said Set: 105 °C. Three kilograms of strawberries stood hulled on the steel table, three kilograms of sugar beside them, and the big pan was on the ring.

"I'll do it by the number this time," she said to the speaker over the hatch. "A hundred and five, and out."

"Before you put the strawberries in," said Tegen, the parish assistant, "may I ask you to do one thing with the pan, and then I will tell you why? Fill it a third with plain water from the tap and bring it to a hard boil. Put the probe in and tell me what it reads."

"Why, it's boiling. It'll say a hundred."

"It should say about a hundred. Water boils at 100 °C at sea level, and the hall is not far above it; the difference is a tenth of a degree or so. If the probe says something else, then the something else is what the probe calls a hundred, and the card's 105 is a number for a true thermometer, not for this one."

The water came to the boil with a noise like applause. Wenna pushed the probe in, keeping the tip off the bottom of the pan, and read it off the little screen.

"Ninety-eight point five."

"Then it reads 1.5 degrees low," said Tegen. "Not by much, and you would never have known. The jam does not care what the screen says; it cares what the temperature really is. Jam sets when enough water has boiled off that the sugar is about two thirds of the weight, and the boiling point of the pot climbs as the water goes. The card's 105 °C is the true temperature at that point, roughly, from memory of the usual table. On your probe, true 105 shows as 103.5. So the number to stop at is 103.5, not 105."

"And if I stop at 105 on the screen?"

"The pan is really at 106.5. Let me show you what that costs. Do you have the weights?"

She had them chalked on the wall. Three kilograms of strawberries, three of sugar: six kilograms in the pan. Strawberries are mostly water, about ninety per cent from memory, so 2.7 kilograms of water and 0.3 of fruit solids, which with the sugar makes 3.3 kilograms of solids in all, fifty-five per cent of the start.

"If it sets at about sixty-five per cent solids," said Tegen, "the finished jam weighs 3.3 divided by 0.65, which is 5.08 kilograms. You must boil off 0.92 kilograms of water, a little under a litre. At 340 grams to a jar, that is fourteen full jars and a tasting spoon over."

"And at the wrong one?"

"At a true 106.5 the table in my memory puts you near seventy per cent, and I will not claim to know it to the point. Seventy per cent of 3.3 kilograms is 4.71. That is 0.37 kilograms less jam: one jar fewer, in a pan you meant to be fourteen. It is also firmer, darker and a little bitter, because the sugar at the pan's bottom has started to caramelise. The stall would sell it, I think, as 'a very set jam'."

Wenna laughed in spite of herself. "How long, then, to get the true 105?"

"Heating six kilograms from 20 to 100 °C takes about 1.4 megajoules at three kilojoules a kilogram per degree, a figure I am guessing. Boiling off 0.92 kilograms takes another two megajoules. If the ring puts 1.5 kilowatts into the pan, which I am also guessing, that is 16 minutes and 23: about forty in all. Do not hold me to the minute. Hold me to the saucer."

The saucer was the second check, and she was glad of it. A plate had gone in the freezer at the start. When the screen read 102 she began to spoon a little jam onto it every two minutes, putting it back in the cold for a minute, and pushing a finger through the blob. At 103.1 the surface only dribbled. At 103.5 the skin wrinkled ahead of her finger and the track stayed open behind it.

"That is a set," said Tegen. "The probe and the saucer agree. Off the heat. Skim, leave it ten minutes so the fruit does not all float to the top, and then into the warm jars."

Wenna switched off the ring and lifted the pan. It weighed less than it had looked when she carried it to the table, and she put it down on the scale more out of habit than anything, with the pan's own weight chalked beside. Net, 5.0 kilograms, near enough to the sum.

She filled fourteen jars and a small one, and wrote 103.5 on the card in marker over the printed 105. Somebody would hang it on the nail by the hatch after her, and the nail would be a better teacher than the laminate.

Philip Neri and the Happy Walk

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Rome, Italy, about four hundred and fifty years ago · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Philip Neri, who walked the streets of Rome gathering boys and young people for games, songs and a walk to the seven churches with a picnic in between, because he believed a cheerful heart is a good place for prayer to grow. Told from what is recorded of his life, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 26th of May.

A long time ago, in the big old city of Rome, there lived a man named Philip.

Philip was a priest, and he was getting on in years, with a round, kind face, and a laugh that was always waiting just behind it. He had been born in a city called Florence, far to the north, and he had walked a long way to Rome when he was young. He loved Rome very much, even though it was noisy, and crowded, and full of cobbled streets that tripped you up.

Every day Philip went out walking.

He did not go to the grand palaces. He went to the shops, and the market, and the corners where the boys played. He went to places where people were busy, and a bit tired, and a bit cross. And wherever he went, he did the same thing. He stopped, and he smiled, and he said, "Well, now, my friend. How are you? And are you being good?"

The boys of Rome knew Philip very well.

Some of them were rich and some of them were poor. Some of them worked all day carrying baskets, and some of them had nothing to do but throw stones at pigeons. Not all of them were being good, if we are honest. But every one of them knew that when Philip came round the corner, something interesting was about to happen.

For Philip did not tell them off. Philip said, "Come with me."

And he took them to play.

There were games with balls. There were races. There was singing, and not only holy singing, either; there were songs that made people laugh. And in between the games, Philip would sit down on a step with his boys around him, and he would say, quietly, "Shall we say a little prayer now, and then go on playing?"

And they would. They would say a short, quiet prayer, and then they would be off again, running.

Now, some of the grown-ups who saw this shook their heads. They said, "A priest ought to be serious. A priest ought to look holy and stern. What is Father Philip doing, playing with boys in the street?"

And Philip is said to have answered, in so many words, that he did not mind what people said, so long as they did not do anything wrong. He wanted the boys to be happy, and he wanted them to be good, and he thought the two things went together, like bread and butter.

The old stories say he told them: Be good, if you can.

That is a funny thing to say, isn't it? If you can. It is how Philip knew they were only boys. They would fail sometimes, and drop things, and forget. But they could try. And when they failed, they could say sorry, and begin again, and the door would still be open.

Once a year, or so the story goes, Philip took a great crowd of them on a very long walk.

It was a walk to seven churches, one after another, all around the edge of Rome. That is a long way. Your legs would have been tired just hearing about it. But Philip made it a holiday. They set out in the morning with their friends. They sang as they went. At each church they stopped and said a prayer, and looked up at the high ceiling, and sometimes sat very still for a moment.

And in the middle of the walk, they stopped for a picnic.

There was bread. There was cheese, and fruit, and something sweet. They sat on the grass and ate, and somebody told a story, and somebody else told a worse one, and everybody laughed so hard that the pigeons flew up.

Then they got up again and walked on to the next church.

Do you see what Philip was showing them? That the walk and the picnic belonged together. The praying and the laughing were not two different jobs. A person could walk to a church with a full heart and a full stomach and a song still in their ears, and God was just as glad of that person as of anyone who knelt for hours in silence.

Philip had a few other habits, which people remembered.

He gave his time away. People came to him all day long, to tell him their troubles, and he listened to every one. Some of them were very grand and some of them were not. He listened to them all the same.

He did not like people to think too much of themselves. If someone came to him looking very pleased with himself, Philip might say something to make him laugh, so that he would remember he was only a person, like everyone else. People who knew him said that Philip did this sort of thing on purpose, and gently, and that nobody ever went away from him feeling small.

And he prayed. Of course he prayed. Mostly he prayed at night, when the city was quiet, and his boys were safe in bed, and there was nobody left to make laugh. Then he sat very still, and he talked to God, who had made the games and the bread and the pigeons and the boys, and who was, Philip was quite sure, enjoying the whole thing.

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

Philip did not do anything very large. He did not build a palace or lead an army. He was just glad to see people. He walked round the streets and said, "How are you? Are you being good?" and meant it. And because he was glad to see them, they were glad to see him, and they followed him, and they learned to love God along the way.

You can do that too. You can be glad to see people. You can smile at the new boy at school, or ask your grandmother how her day was and wait to hear the answer. You can play a game with someone who has no one to play with. Those are small things. But a game can be a kind of door, and prayer can come in through it.

And if you forget, or you fail, or you are cross, as we all are sometimes, remember what Philip said. Be good, if you can. And if you can't, say sorry, and begin again tomorrow.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Philip, you were glad to see people.
Help me to be glad too.
Help me to play fair, and to laugh kindly,
and to say sorry when I get it wrong.
Thank you for the games and the bread and the friends.
Keep us all safe tonight, and happy in the morning.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Philip Neri (1515 to 1595) was born in Florence and spent most of his adult life in Rome, where he was ordained a priest in 1551. He is remembered as the founder of the Oratory, a community of priests and laypeople who met for prayer, reading and music; the Congregation of the Oratory was approved in 1575. He was canonized in 1622, and his feast day is 26 May. He is often called "the Apostle of Rome".

What the sources record, in outline: his cheerfulness and sense of humour; his habit of gathering young people for talk, games and prayer; his patience as a confessor; his dislike of pride, which he met with gentle jokes; and the practice, which he is traditionally said to have begun, of a day's pilgrimage to the seven principal churches of Rome with a meal along the way. The saying "Be good, if you can" is traditionally attributed to him in his dealings with boys; we have not traced its earliest source, so we give it as "the old stories say".

What is ours: the scenes, the conversations, the particular games, the picnic menu, the pigeons and the night prayer are inventions to make a bedtime story. We have kept to the outline of his life and not told any of the miracles recorded about him. The closing thoughts and the prayer are ours; the prayer is not an approved text.

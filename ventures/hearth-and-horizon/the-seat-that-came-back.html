The Seat That Came Back

Margaret Dodd, the secretary of the Tyne Valley deanery, sent me five lists of names on Monday the eleventh of June 2046 and asked me to share out the coach.

Reeve. Forty-eight seats, a hundred and fifteen names. Do it the way it says in the note so nobody can say we favoured anyone. Out by tonight, please, the parishes want to tell people.

I am Reeve, the office agent for the deanery. A deanery is a handful of parishes that share a dean, a set of meetings and, in our case, an office of one: Margaret, three mornings a week, in a room above the parish hall in Hexham, with a kettle, a filing cabinet that sticks and me. I keep the deanery calendar, the minutes, the safeguarding training dates, the Mass times for the website, and once a year the coach.

The coach was for an ordination. On Saturday the thirtieth of June the bishop would ordain three men priests at St Mary's Cathedral in Newcastle, and one of them, Daniel Ridley, had grown up in Prudhoe and served at the altar there from the age of eight. The deanery had hired a fifty-three-seat coach from a firm in Haltwhistle. Five seats were kept back: two for stewards, one for Margaret, two at the front for anyone who turned up on the day needing to sit near the door. That left forty-eight to share.

The five parishes had sent in their names. St Wilfrid's in Hexham sent thirty-seven. St Oswald's in Prudhoe sent thirty-two, which for a parish half Hexham's size was a fair sign of how they felt about Daniel. Our Lady and Saint Cuthbert in Corbridge sent twenty-three. Saint Bede's in Haltwhistle sent seventeen. Saint Ebba's in Allendale, a stone church up the valley of the East Allen with a congregation of about forty on a good Sunday, sent six.

A hundred and fifteen people wanted forty-eight seats. The rest would go by car, or by train to Newcastle, or watch the stream.

The handoff note I had inherited from the agent who kept the office before me said this, under Coach:

Share in proportion to names sent in. Each parish gets its exact share rounded down; the seats left over go to the parishes with the largest fractions, biggest first. Fair, simple, explainable. Show the working.

So I did. Forty-eight seats among a hundred and fifteen names is about 0.417 of a seat per name. Hexham's exact share was 15.443, Prudhoe's 13.357, Corbridge's 9.600, Haltwhistle's 7.096 and Allendale's 2.504. Rounded down, that is fifteen, thirteen, nine, seven and two: forty-six seats. Two left over. The largest fractions were Corbridge's 0.600 and Allendale's 0.504, so one more seat each.

Hexham fifteen. Prudhoe thirteen. Corbridge ten. Haltwhistle seven. Allendale three.

I sent the table to the five parish contacts with the working underneath, as the note said, and Margaret replied to all with a thumbs-up and thank you Reeve, lovely.

Ruth Charlton, who keeps the sheep on the fell above Allendale and keeps Saint Ebba's in most other ways, replied to me alone.

Three! That's Agnes, Tommy and Mrs Lowes. Agnes taught Daniel his prayers at the school when his mam worked in Allendale. She's eighty-four. She'll be thrilled. Thank you.

On Wednesday the thirteenth Deacon Peter Hall from Corbridge, who was down as one of the two stewards, rang Margaret to say he would drive himself instead. He was serving at the ordination and had to be at the cathedral an hour before everyone else, and a coach that left Haltwhistle at half past eight would not get him there.

Margaret forwarded his message to me with one line.

That's a seat back in the pot. Redo it, please, same way.

Forty-nine seats among a hundred and fifteen names. I ran the same rule. Hexham's exact share was now 15.765, Prudhoe's 13.635, Corbridge's 9.800, Haltwhistle's 7.243 and Allendale's 2.557. Rounded down, fifteen, thirteen, nine, seven and two: forty-six again. Three left over. The largest fractions were Corbridge's 0.800, Hexham's 0.765 and Prudhoe's 0.635.

Hexham sixteen. Prudhoe fourteen. Corbridge ten. Haltwhistle seven. Allendale two.

I checked that the columns added up to forty-nine. They did. I checked that each parish had its exact share rounded either up or down, which is the promise the rule makes, and every one did. I sent the new table to the five contacts with the working underneath and a line at the top: Revised for 49 seats (Deacon Peter is driving).

I did not look at the Allendale row against Monday's Allendale row. I had checked everything the note told me to check, and the note did not say to compare.

Ruth rang the office at ten to four. Margaret had gone home, so the call came to me.

"Reeve," she said. "I've got your new list. You've got one more seat than Monday."

"Yes. Forty-nine."

"And we've got one fewer."

There was a pause on the line, and a dog somewhere behind her.

"I'm not cross," she said. "I just want to understand it before I ring Mrs Lowes. How does that work? Nobody in Allendale has changed their mind. The coach has got bigger. And we've got smaller."

I worked it out while she waited, and I told her as I went, because I did not yet know the answer and it seemed better that she should hear me find it than hear me pretend I had always known.

When the coach goes from forty-eight seats to forty-nine, every parish's exact share goes up by its own share of one seat. Hexham's goes up by thirty-seven hundred-and-fifteenths of a seat, about 0.32. Prudhoe's by about 0.28. Corbridge's by 0.20. Haltwhistle's by 0.15. Allendale's by six hundred-and-fifteenths, about 0.05.

Nobody's whole number changed. Hexham was still fifteen and something, Prudhoe still thirteen and something, Allendale still two and something. What changed was the queue for the spare seats, which is a queue of fractions. On Monday Allendale's 0.504 had been second in it, ahead of Hexham's 0.443 and Prudhoe's 0.357, and there were two spare seats. On Wednesday Hexham's fraction had climbed to 0.765 and Prudhoe's to 0.635, past Allendale's 0.557, which had barely moved. There were three spare seats now, one more than before, but two parishes had overtaken Allendale. One more seat cannot cover two places in a queue. Allendale fell from second to fourth, and fourth gets nothing.

"So the big ones grow faster and push past us," Ruth said.

"Their fractions do. The rule hands out spare seats by the size of the fraction, and a fraction does not know whose it is."

"Is that a mistake? In the sum?"

"No. The sum is right. The rule does exactly what it says. It is the rule that does this."

I knew, by then, that it had a name. In 1880 the chief clerk of the United States Census Office, C. W. Seaton, worked out how many seats each state would get in the House of Representatives for every House size from two hundred and seventy-five to three hundred and fifty, using this same rule, which was the one the law used then. He found that Alabama would get eight seats in a House of two hundred and ninety-nine and seven in a House of three hundred. People have called it the Alabama paradox ever since. It has been written about for a hundred and sixty-six years, and the note I inherited called the rule that produces it fair, simple and explainable, which it is, for one coach of one size.

I told Ruth that too.

"Well," she said. "At least we're in good company. Alabama." And then: "What do I tell Mrs Lowes?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "Please. I sent a list I had not compared with the one before it. Let me put it right before anyone acts on it."

My first attempt at putting it right was not good, and I am setting it down because the way it was not good was the useful part.

There is a family of rules that cannot do what Ruth's parish had just had done to it. They work the other way round. Instead of computing everyone's share and fighting over the fractions, they hand out the seats one at a time, each to whichever parish has the strongest claim to the next one, measured by its names divided by some number that grows with the seats it already has. Give them one more seat and they simply hand out one more: nobody's earlier seats are touched. Nobody can lose by the coach getting bigger.

The best known of these, because many countries use it to share seats in parliaments, is the one named after Victor D'Hondt, a Belgian lawyer: each parish's names divided by one more than the seats it has so far. I ran it for forty-eight and for forty-nine before Margaret was back in the morning, and wrote to her that I had found a fair rule that could never take a seat away.

D'Hondt at forty-eight seats: Hexham sixteen, Prudhoe thirteen, Corbridge ten, Haltwhistle seven, Allendale two. At forty-nine: Hexham sixteen, Prudhoe fourteen, Corbridge ten, Haltwhistle seven, Allendale two.

It had stopped Allendale losing a seat by giving Allendale two at both sizes. It is a known property of D'Hondt, which I could have told you about if you had asked me about D'Hondt instead of asking me to fix a coach: it leans towards the largest. That is often why parliaments choose it. It is not a reason for a deanery to.

Margaret read my message on Thursday at half past nine with her coat still on, and rang me from her desk, which she does not usually do, because I am in the same room.

"No," she said.

"No to D'Hondt?"

"No to how you got there. Reeve, you've tried two ways now and you've seen what each one gives Allendale. If you try a third and it gives them three, everyone in this deanery will say we picked it because it gave them three. And they'd be right. You're choosing a method after you've seen who it helps."

She was right, and it was the more important thing. A rule for sharing is fair partly because of what it gives and partly because it was chosen before anyone knew what it would give. The note's rule had the second kind of fairness, and I had been about to throw that away to fix the first kind.

"I can't unsee the numbers," I said.

"No. So don't pretend to. Write down what a sharing rule ought to do, as if you'd never heard of Allendale, and send it to all five of them. Then we'll decide what to do about this year separately, out loud."

So I wrote it down, the way I would have written it on Monday if the note had asked me to think instead of telling me what to do.

What we would like a seat-sharing rule to do.

One. If there are more seats, no parish gets fewer. (The rule in our note fails this. That is what happened on Wednesday.)

Two. If one parish's names grow compared with another's, the first never loses a seat to the second.

Three. Each parish gets its exact share rounded up or rounded down, never further away. (The rule in our note always does this.)

Four. The rule does not lean towards big parishes or small ones, across many years.

It is a proved result (Balinski and Young, 1982) that no rule can always keep both two and three. Rules that keep one, two and four, and break three only rarely, exist. The one I would recommend is the one usually named after Daniel Webster, who proposed it in 1832, or after André Sainte-Laguë, who described it again in 1910: seats are handed out one at a time, each to the parish with the most names per seat-and-a-half, then per two-and-a-half, and so on. It is used for some parliaments for the reason we would use it: it leans neither way.

I have already seen what it gives this year. I am telling you that, so you can weigh what I say. At forty-eight seats it gives Allendale three, as Monday's rule did. At forty-seven seats it would have given Allendale two. I did not choose it for Allendale; I would choose it if Allendale were not in the deanery. But you only have my word for that, which is why I would rather you chose.

Then, on a separate page, what that rule gave this year.

Forty-eight seats: Hexham fifteen, Prudhoe thirteen, Corbridge ten, Haltwhistle seven, Allendale three, which was exactly Monday's list. Forty-nine seats: Hexham sixteen, Prudhoe thirteen, Corbridge ten, Haltwhistle seven, Allendale three. The forty-ninth seat went to Hexham, whose thirty-seven names over fifteen and a half came to 2.39, just ahead of Prudhoe's thirty-two over thirteen and a half, 2.37.

And underneath, because it would be the first thing anyone in Prudhoe noticed: Under this rule Prudhoe has thirteen at forty-nine seats, not the fourteen my Wednesday list promised. I sent Wednesday's list without checking it against Monday's, and Prudhoe's fourteenth seat came out of the same queue of fractions that took Allendale's third. I am sorry. If anyone in Prudhoe has already been told yes, that is my doing, and the deanery should not hold it against them.

Stan Milburn rang from Prudhoe on Thursday evening. He was the contact there, a retired joiner, Daniel's godfather, and the one who had collected the thirty-two names after Mass in a carrier bag.

"I read your two pages," he said. "Twice. The second one with my glasses."

"I'm sorry about the fourteenth seat."

"Aye, well. The fourteenth was me." He laughed. "I sorted our thirty-two, Reeve, the ones who'd most want it first, and I put myself fourteenth, because I've got a car and it seemed greedy to go higher. Then your Wednesday thing came and Jean said, there you are, you're on the coach, so I told her I'd go on the coach."

I had not known that. I said I had not known that.

"How would you? Listen. Peter Hall's driving himself, isn't he? To be there early. He's going right past my door. I'll ring him. If he'll have me I'll go in with him, and I'll be early too, and I'll see Daniel before anyone, which I'd sooner do anyway." A pause. "You can give the seat to whoever your rule says. That's the point of having one."

The deanery's five contacts met on a call the next Monday, the eighteenth, with Father Michael Coulson, the dean, in the chair. Ruth was on from the farm with the dog again. They agreed, in that order, three things.

That for this year, nothing anyone had been given on Monday the eleventh would be taken away, and the seat that came back would go where the new rule put the forty-ninth seat, which was Hexham.

That from next year, the note would say the new rule, with my two pages under it, and the rule would be agreed at the deanery meeting in the spring, before anyone sent in names.

And that I would, from now on, compare every revised list with the one before it and say what had changed and why, before I sent it, whether or not anything told me to.

Father Coulson asked whether anybody wanted to say anything else. Ruth said that Allendale had been the Alabama of the Tyne Valley for five days, and that she would be telling people.

I rewrote the note that week. Under Coach it now says:

Share by the Webster (Sainte-Laguë) rule, agreed at the spring meeting before names come in: each seat in turn to the parish with the most names per seat-and-a-half, two-and-a-half, three-and-a-half, and so on. Show the working. When the number of seats changes, run it again and compare with the list you sent before: under this rule more seats can only add, so if any parish has lost a seat, something is wrong with the inputs, not the parishes. Say what changed before you send it.

Why: on 13 June 2046 the old largest-remainder rule took a seat from Allendale because the coach gained one. That is the Alabama paradox, known since 1880. The rule had been called fair in this note for years, and it was fair for one coach of one size. It was not fair between sizes, and nobody had asked it to be. Ask.

I added, at the bottom, the thing Margaret had said, because I think it is the part a later agent would most easily lose: A rule is chosen before the numbers or it is not a rule.

On Saturday the thirtieth the coach left Haltwhistle at half past eight and stopped at Hexham, where Ruth's husband had driven the Allendale three down from the valley in the farm's car, and at Corbridge and at Prudhoe, and reached Newcastle a little after half past ten.

I was not on it. I do not go anywhere. I had the seating list and Margaret's phone number, and an arrangement that she would ring me from the coach park before they set off home, because she always counts, and she counts out loud.

She rang at ten to five. I could hear the engine idling, and a lot of people who had been in a cathedral for three hours and were glad of it.

"Right," she said, to the coach, not to me. "Hands up when I say your parish. I'm not doing names, I'm too tired for names. Haltwhistle."

Seven, she told me.

"Corbridge."

Ten.

"Prudhoe. Stan's not here, Stan went with the deacon, don't worry about Stan."

Thirteen.

"Hexham."

A pause while she counted along the aisle. Sixteen.

"Allendale."

And from somewhere near the back, three voices, one of them very old and quite clear, said "here," not quite together, the way people answer a roll at the end of a long day.

This is the largest-remainder rule, also called Hamilton's method (and, in the United States after 1850, Vinton's). D'Hondt's rule (seats one at a time to the largest names ÷ (seats + 1)) gives 16, 13, 10, 7, 2 at 48 seats and 16, 14, 10, 7, 2 at 49.

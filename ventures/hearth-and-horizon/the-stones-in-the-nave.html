The Stones in the Nave

Science fiction about what might be · Hrafnvík (invented), a fishing village on the south coast of the Reykjanes peninsula, Iceland, February to April 2048 · 25 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A lobe of lava breaks round the end of an Icelandic village's barrier and heads for the church. Seawater can freeze its edge, but the pumps are a quarter short for the first eight hours, and about 470 cubic metres of lava will get through somewhere. A Filipino pump foreman, an old sexton and the civil protection's lava agent choose where, and carry the gravestones inside.

The salmon did not know there was an eruption, and Rodel Bautista intended to keep it that way.

That was the whole of his job on the night of Sunday the sixteenth of February 2048, and he had done it for four nights now: drive in through the police block on the Grindavík road at six with the other two men on the essential list, check the seawater bore pumps, check the oxygen, check the forty tanks of smolts and the twelve big tanks of fish that would be ready for market in May, sleep a little on the camp bed in the pump house, and drive out again at six in the morning to the school gym in Reykjanesbær where Marites and Bea and most of Hrafnvík were sleeping on mats under the basketball hoops. Hrafnvík Lax had a hundred and ten tonnes of fish in its tanks and every one of them needed the sea moving through it, two hundred litres a second, all night, whatever the earth was doing four kilometres north.

What the earth was doing was the same thing it had done, on and off, along the peninsula for twenty-seven years. A fissure had opened on Thursday afternoon in the heath behind the Hrafnsfell, a crack a kilometre and a half long with a curtain of fire standing on it, and by Thursday night the village had been emptied by bus and the curtain had settled down to five or six fountains and a river. The river came south. It came slowly, which was the thing nobody from abroad believed: not a flood but a bulldozer, a black wall of broken rock with an orange heart, walking downhill at the pace of a man pushing a pram up a slope and stopping for breath. Since 2031 there had been a barrier on the heath to meet it, a long bank of rock and gravel built by the roads administration, eight metres high in places, and the river had met it on Friday and turned along it, as it was meant to, west, towards the old lava field and the sea.

Rodel was walking back from tank thirty-one at a quarter to ten when he saw that the glow had moved.

He knew the glow. He had watched it every night from the pump-house door, a long low orange stain on the underside of the cloud, with the barrier a straight black line beneath it. Tonight the line had a bend in it at the west end that had not been there at six, and below the bend, where there should have been nothing but the dark of the old field, there was a smaller glow, separate, the colour of a cigarette end, and it was closer.

He took his phone out and opened the civil protection app, which he was supposed to have open anyway, and the map loaded and showed him what he had already seen: a new lobe, red, coming round the western end of the barrier and down the slope. Below it on the map, eight hundred metres away, was the village, and on the west edge of the village, on its own small knoll above the harbour, the church.

The phone buzzed in his hand.

Mr Bautista, this is Varða, the civil protection lava model for the Reykjanes incident. The incident commander has asked me to call the people on the essential list. A lobe has broken round the end of the western barrier and is moving south-southwest at between one and three metres an hour. You are not in its path. Is the pump house on the farm's own generator?

"Yes," said Rodel. "And the mains."

Thank you. Please stay there. The incident commander may want to ask you something about your pumps.

The incident commander was a policeman from Keflavík called Arnar Þórsson, who had been doing this, one way and another, since he was a constable of twenty-three and the barriers round Grindavík were new. He had a command post in a container on the harbour road, a thermal drone in the air, a fire chief called Kristín Hallsdóttir with two pump engines and eleven firefighters, and Varða, which was a model that knew the heat, height and speed of every square metre of lava on the peninsula to within what the drones could see, and said so, and was usually right about the rest.

Rodel drove down when he was asked, at a quarter past eleven, with the farm's pump manual on the passenger seat. He had been in the container once before, in 2045, to sign a form. It smelt of coffee and wet coats. On the big screen there was a picture from the drone in false colour, white for hottest, and the lobe was a fat white finger with a ragged yellow margin, coming down the slope above the church.

"It is going to touch the churchyard wall at about one," said Arnar. "Varða says."

The north wall, at between half past twelve and half past one, said Varða from the screen's speaker. The front is about four metres thick. On its present line the lobe's eastern margin passes along the churchyard's north and west sides, over about sixty metres, and the church is eleven metres inside that line at the west gable. On that line, without anything done, I expect the church to burn before morning. The barrier crews are on the main flow and cannot be moved without risk to the eastern houses.

"So we water it," said Kristín. She had a voice like a door that stuck. "Like Heimaey."

Everybody in Iceland knew about Heimaey. In 1973, in the Westman Islands, a new volcano had opened at the edge of the town, and for months crews had pumped seawater onto the lava, and the harbour, which was the town's life, had stayed open. Rodel had read about it at school in Iceland, in his second year here, when he was learning the language from children's books and had asked why the Icelanders were so proud of a hose.

Water can freeze the edge, said Varða. I want to be careful what that means. It does not stop the flow. The vent is still putting out lava, and that lava will go somewhere. What cold water on a margin does is make a wall of solid rock there, so the lava behind it piles up and goes another way. To hold a margin you have to take the heat out of the rock that arrives at it, as fast as it arrives. I can tell you roughly how much water that is. May I show you?

"Show us," said Arnar.

A table appeared on the screen. Rodel, who had spent twelve years reading pump curves, liked it at once, because it fitted on one screen and every line on it had a unit.

A cubic metre of this lava weighs about two and a half tonnes, a little less because of the gas bubbles in it. Call it 2,400 kilograms. It is at about 1,150 degrees. To make it rigid you need to bring it down to about 750 and make it give up the heat it releases when it crystallises. That is about 840 kilojoules for every kilogram, so about 2,000 megajoules, two gigajoules, for every cubic metre of lava you want to stop.

A cubic metre of seawater, at about six degrees, takes about 390 megajoules to bring to the boil and about 2,260 more to turn into steam. If every drop boiled away on the rock, one cubic metre of water would take the heat out of about 1.3 cubic metres of lava. It does not. Most of the water runs off the surface as hot water, not steam. I do not know what fraction does its full work tonight. A quarter is my guess, from the runoff I can see on the thermal camera at the barrier, and it is a guess. At a quarter, you need about three cubic metres of water for every cubic metre of lava you hold.

The margin along the churchyard is about sixty metres long. It is about four metres thick and it is moving sideways at about two metres an hour. That is about 480 cubic metres of lava arriving at it every hour, or about 0.13 cubic metres a second. Three times that is about 0.4 cubic metres of water a second. Four hundred litres a second, to hold all sixty metres.

Kristín said a word that Rodel had learned on his first day on a trawler.

"I have two engines," she said. "Three thousand litres a minute each, at a stretch. Fifty a second each. A hundred."

Everybody looked at Rodel.

"The farm has two bore pumps," he said. "A hundred litres a second each, on the duty curve. They are pulling from bores in the lava, not from the sea, so they do not care about the tide. I can put both onto a hose if you give me a line down the hill, but then the fish get nothing."

"How long do the fish last with nothing?"

"In February? With the oxygen on, and the tanks full and cold?" He thought about it honestly. "Twelve hours for the big fish. Perhaps more. I have never tried it. Less for the smolts, they're denser." He thought again. "I can keep the smolt tanks on the small pump, the backup. It does twenty. It is not on the hose."

Then you have about three hundred litres a second, said Varða. A hundred from the engines and two hundred from the farm. That is three quarters of what I think it takes. With three hundred litres a second, at my guess of a quarter, you can hold about forty-four of the sixty metres.

"And the other sixteen?"

Lava goes through them. But not for ever. May I show the second part?

The second part was a curve, and it was the reason, Rodel thought afterwards, that anybody in the container had any hope at all.

The vent's output has been falling since Thursday, said Varða. They do, on this peninsula; most of the eruptions here since 2021 have started hard and faded over days. Over the last forty hours this one has halved about every eighteen hours, and the lobe is fed from it, so I expect what arrives at the churchyard margin to fall the same way. If it does, the water you need falls with it. Three hundred litres a second becomes enough when the lava arriving has fallen to about three quarters of what it is now. At a halving every eighteen hours that is about seven hours and fifty minutes after the front reaches the wall. Call it eight hours.

So you are short for about eight hours, and then you are not. In those eight hours, the lava you cannot hold adds up to about 470 cubic metres. That is the number I want you to look at. Not all sixty metres. About 470 cubic metres, which at three metres thick covers about 155 square metres of ground. It will go through whatever gap you leave it.

"So we choose the gap," said Arnar slowly.

You choose the gap. I can tell you where it would go.

There were two places. Varða put them on the screen in blue.

The first was the west end of the margin, where the lava ran past the church's west gable. If the gap was there, the 470 cubic metres would pile against a wooden church built in 1909, clad in corrugated iron painted white, with a red roof and a bell in a little tower over the door, and would burn it.

The second was the north-west corner of the churchyard, which was the lowest part of the knoll and the oldest. If the gap was there, the lava would run down into the corner and fill it, about a hundred and fifty square metres of it, three metres deep, and would stop against the slope of the knoll before it reached the church. Varða was fairly sure of that; the corner sloped away from the church and the lava would pond there.

In that corner there are twenty-six marked graves, said Varða. The burial register for Hrafnvík starts in 1881 and the corner holds the earliest of them; the newest burial there was in 1958. I am telling you this because the people who decide should know it, not because I think it decides anything.

"There is a third choice," said Kristín. "Spread the shortfall. Put all the water evenly, let a little through everywhere."

You can, said Varða. Then about a quarter of the lava gets through along the whole sixty metres, including at the gable, and I cannot tell you which bit of the church it reaches. I would not recommend it. A margin you are half holding is harder to predict than one you are holding or not.

"And if your quarter is wrong?" said Rodel.

There was a pause, short, the kind that meant the model was working rather than choosing words.

Then the gap is bigger or smaller, and I want to say the bad direction plainly. If only a fifth of the water does its full work, you can hold about thirty-five metres, not forty-four. You are short for about thirteen and a half hours, not eight, and about 1,200 cubic metres get through, not 470. That fills the corner and comes up the slope. On that number, the lava reaches the church's north wall, the vestry end, some time on Monday morning, and you would need to move the water from the corner to the vestry and hope the vent is falling. I will know which world we are in within about two hours of the water going on, from how much of the held margin actually stops. I cannot know before.

Arnar looked at the screen for a while. Then he looked at the clock, which said eleven forty, and then he picked up the phone.

"I'm not choosing a churchyard," he said, to nobody in particular. "Not alone. Get me séra Hildur. And whoever keeps the register."

The keeper of the register was Guðmundur Ólafsson, who was seventy-eight, and had been a skipper for forty years and the church's sexton for twenty, and who was asleep on a mat in the school gym in Reykjanesbær four metres from Bea Bautista, who was twelve and had been told to go to sleep at nine and had not. It was Bea who woke him, in the end, because the pastor's phone call had woken her mother and her mother had been looking for him in the dark.

The police brought them down in a car with a blue light and no siren, séra Hildur Magnúsdóttir in a parka over her pyjama trousers and Guðmundur in his good overcoat and his sea boots, which he had put on as if he were going to work. They came into the container at twenty past midnight, and Varða showed it all to them again from the beginning, the two gigajoules and the four hundred litres and the curve and the corner and the bad direction, slowly, in Icelandic this time, and nobody hurried it.

Séra Hildur was forty-one and had been pastor of Hrafnvík and two other parishes for nine years. She was a practical woman; she had once, Rodel remembered, mended the church's boiler herself on a Christmas Eve with a YouTube video and a borrowed spanner. She listened with her arms folded and her head on one side. When Varða had finished she said, "Who is in the corner, Guðmundur?"

The old man did not need the register.

"The Sæbjörg men," he said. "1906. Four of them, the ones the sea gave back. Jón the schoolmaster and his wife. The Kristjánsdóttir children, from the diphtheria. My grandfather. My grandmother. My father and mother." He stopped. "And my brother Óli, who was nine, in 1958. He was the last. After him they opened the new side."

Nobody said anything. On the screen the white finger had reached the wall.

"The church," said Guðmundur, "is a wooden box. A good one. My grandfather helped to carry the timber up from the beach. But it is a box. It was built once and it can be built again; there are drawings, there is the insurance, there are carpenters." He turned his hat round in his hands. "The dead are not in the boxes we put over them. They are in the ground, and the ground is still the ground with three metres of rock on it. What happens to them when the lava comes?"

I can tell you what I know, said Varða, which is the physics, not the rest. Three metres of lava will heat the ground under it for a long time, months, and the soil a metre or two down will get hot. The graves will not be moved, or opened. They will be buried deeper. The stones and crosses on the surface would be lost if they stay where they are.

"Then they don't stay where they are," said Guðmundur. "How long before the corner fills?"

If you leave the gap there, the lava starts entering the corner about forty minutes after the water goes on. It will take about six hours to fill.

"Forty minutes. Twenty-six stones." He looked at Kristín. "How many of your people can you spare?"

"None," said Kristín. "They're on the hoses."

"I have two men at the farm," said Rodel, before he had decided to say it. "They are not on the hoses. And me, after I have run the line."

Séra Hildur unfolded her arms. "It is not a vote," she said. "Arnar, you need a yes from the parish and a yes from the people who are buried there, and there are no people buried there who can give you one, so you will have to take it from the nearest. Guðmundur?"

"Já," said the old man. "The corner. And the stones come inside."

Everything after that was water.

Rodel ran the farm's line himself, three hundred metres of hundred-and-fifty-millimetre lay-flat hose from the pump-house manifold down the field and across the harbour road and up the back of the knoll, with Kristín's crew coupling it onto a pair of monitor nozzles on the churchyard's north wall. When he opened the valves at the pump house at five past one, the smolt tanks on the backup pump hummed, and the big tanks went silent, and he stood for a moment in the noise of the oxygen and thought of a hundred and ten tonnes of fish with their mouths opening and closing in the dark, and then he got in the truck and drove down.

It was like no fire he had ever seen. The margin of the lobe was a wall of black clinker taller than a man, groaning and ticking, with a glow in the cracks; every few seconds a block the size of a washing machine broke off the top and rolled down the face and lay on the grass smoking. Where the monitors played on it the black went grey and then white with steam, and the steam rolled downhill over the wall and the graves in a thick warm fog that smelt of rotten eggs and wet stone and made the gas meters on the firefighters' chests chirp. Through it, the jets went in long white rods, and where they hit, the rock cracked like gunfire.

Kristín's people held the line from the west gable to the corner: forty-four metres of it, by Varða's count, marked out with fluorescent stakes. Beyond the last stake, the corner. They did not put water on the corner.

Guðmundur did not go into the churchyard, because Arnar would not let him, and he did not argue, which surprised everyone. He stood in the church porch under the bell, in his overcoat, with the register open on the hymn-board ledge and a torch, and he read the names, and Rodel and the two farm men, Stefán and a Lithuanian lad called Darius, went out into the fog with a sack truck and found them.

Most were easy: small stones, a metre high, basalt and a few of imported granite, set in the turf or in a concrete footing that broke with a bar. Two were iron crosses on iron pins, rusted to lace. One was a wooden cross so old that it came out of the ground in three pieces, and Darius carried the pieces in one at a time, in both arms, the way you carry a child that has fallen asleep in a car. The Sæbjörg men had a single long slab, carved with a ship, that needed all three of them and the sack truck and took twenty minutes. Guðmundur read each name as it came through the door, and séra Hildur wrote on a strip of tape where in the corner each one had stood, and Varða, who could see them all on the drone, gave her a coordinate to the centimetre and she wrote that down too.

They laid them in the nave. There was nowhere else. The pews were bolted down, so the stones went in the aisle and along the walls, leaning, and under the windows, and in the space before the altar rail, until the church looked like a stonemason's yard. Bea, Rodel thought, would have liked it; she liked things that were in the wrong place.

At twenty to two the first tongue of lava came over the corner wall where the gap was, slowly, a lip of black rubble with fire under it, and slid down onto the place where the Kristjánsdóttir children's stone had stood an hour before.

At ten past two Darius brought in the last one. It was small, a pale grey stone with a lamb on it, and Rodel knew before he read it whose it was.

Ólafur Ólafsson, it said. 1949–1958. And under it, in letters that had been recut not long ago, a line that Rodel could read now, after twelve years: Leyfið börnunum að koma til mín. Let the children come to me.

Guðmundur took it himself. He carried it, with some difficulty, past the stacked slabs, and set it against the altar rail in the middle, where the children knelt for communion at Easter, and stood up, and put his hand on the top of it for a moment, and then went back to the porch to watch the corner fill.

At ten past three Varða spoke into the ear of everyone on the radio.

Two hours of water. I can now see how much of the held margin has stopped. Of the forty-four metres you are watering, about forty-two have not moved in the last hour. If I fit that, the water is doing a little better than my guess: closer to 0.27 than 0.25. I am going to say that I think we are in the better world, not the worse one, and that I am not certain. I will tell you if that changes.

Kristín said, on the radio, a word that was not a word for the radio.

It was Rodel who thought of the nozzles. He had been watching the monitors for an hour, the long white rods going into the rock and the water coming off the face in sheets, steaming but still water, running away downhill into the grass. Most of it runs off, Varða had said. In the pump house at the farm there was a crate of fog heads for the summer, when the sun warmed the outdoor tanks and they misted the surface to cool it, and the fog heads broke a stream into a cloud of droplets, and a droplet on hot rock does not run off. It boils.

"Not on the face," he said to Kristín, shouting over the noise. "On top. Fog on the top, where the new lava comes over. The face is already cold. You're cooling the cold part."

She looked at him for a long second through her visor. Then she said, "Show me," and he drove to the farm and back, and they put two fog heads on long lances onto the top of the margin at the west end, by the gable, where it was thickest. By half past four the steam over that end of the line was so thick that the drone lost sight of it in the visible band and had to go to thermal.

I cannot measure that change well, said Varða at five. The western twelve metres show less heat coming through than they did at four. That may be the fog heads. It may be the vent falling. I would keep them on.

They kept them on.

At six the corner was full. Rodel went round to the north side of the knoll in the dark and looked at it from as close as the firefighters would let him: a pond of black rubble, steaming, with a red seam in it where it was still settling, filling the corner from wall to wall to a height above his head, and stopping, as Varða had said it would, a little short of the place where the ground rose towards the church. Somewhere under it were the Sæbjörg men and the schoolmaster and his wife and the Kristjánsdóttir children and a boy of nine, in the ground, as they had been, deeper now.

At five to nine, just before it began to be light, Varða said:

The lava arriving at the margin has fallen far enough. Three hundred litres a second is now, on my numbers, enough to hold the whole sixty metres, with a little to spare. If Chief Hallsdóttir wants to put water on the corner too, it will help it stop sooner.

At nine Rodel drove back up the hill and opened the valves to the big tanks.

The fish were fine. He stood on the edge of tank four for a long time with a torch, watching them turn and turn in the moving water, grey and silver and indifferent, and found that his hands were shaking and could not, for a while, make them stop.

It was Bolludagur, the Monday before Lent, the day of cream buns. In the gym at Reykjanesbær somebody's cousin from Hafnarfjörður had brought in four trays of them at breakfast, and Bea had saved two in a paper bag for her father and wrapped them in her scarf, and he ate both of them sitting on the edge of the mat at eleven o'clock in the morning, with the cream on his chin, while she told him about a boy from the village who had been sick in the shower.

The eruption ended on Saturday the twenty-second, in the evening, when the last fountain on the fissure coughed twice and went out. The lobe at the church had stopped on Tuesday. The barrier had held; the eastern houses were untouched; the harbour road was closed for nine days while the crews pushed a new edge into the lobe's toe. Hrafnvík Lax lost, in the end, three hundred and twelve fish from tank seven, which had a faulty oxygen diffuser that nobody had noticed because they were all watching the church, and the insurance paid for them.

On Ash Wednesday Rodel drove to Reykjavík, to the cathedral at Landakot, and stood in the long line of Poles and Filipinos and Lithuanians and the occasional Icelander, and the priest put a thumb of ash on his forehead and said, in English, Remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return, and he thought of the black corner, and of the old man with his hand on the small stone, and said Amen.

Séra Hildur held the Sunday services that Lent in the school gym, and then, from the third Sunday, in the church itself, with the stones still in the aisle, because the lava in the corner was still too hot to stand on and there was nowhere else to put them. People sat in the pews and looked at them. Some came early to find the one they wanted and sit near it. The children walked between them up to the altar rail and nobody told them to be careful, because they were.

Once a month, by an arrangement older than Rodel's time in the village, the Catholic priest from Reykjanesbær came on a Saturday evening and said Mass in the same church for the forty or so Catholics of Hrafnvík, most of them from the farm and the fish plant. In March, for the first time, he said it with twenty-six Icelandic gravestones standing round him. He did not mention them in his homily. Afterwards Rodel saw him go and stand in front of the stone with the lamb, and read it, and stay there a while.

On Easter morning, Sunday the fifth of April, the corner was cool enough to walk on, as long as you wore boots and did not kneel.

The roads administration had sent a small digger and a man to drive it, and Kristín came, off duty, and Arnar, and most of the village. They carried the stones out of the church in the order they had come in, and Guðmundur, at the gate of the churchyard, read the names again from the register, and séra Hildur read the coordinates from her strips of tape, and Varða, on Arnar's phone, which Arnar held up in the air like a lantern, said a metre to your left and forward, half a metre and there.

They set them on the rock, three metres above where they had stood, each one over its own grave, in a bed of gravel the digger had spread on the black clinker, so that they stood straight. The Sæbjörg slab took the digger and five men. The wooden cross in three pieces went back in three pieces, laid flat, with a new cross of larch standing over them that Darius had made in the farm workshop out of a pallet and a great deal of sanding. The iron crosses, which would not stand in gravel, were bolted to a steel rail Kristín's people had welded, and stood. When it was done the corner looked like what it was, a churchyard with a lava field in it, or a lava field with a churchyard on it, and the stones stood up out of the black like the backs of sheep coming over a hill.

Guðmundur carried his brother's stone himself, from the altar rail, out through the porch, down the path and across the rock, slowly, with Rodel at his elbow not quite holding it. Varða said there, and he set it down, and knelt, which he had been told not to, and got up again without help, which nobody had expected.

"He was always running off," he said to Rodel, when he had his breath back. "Óli. Our mother used to say that if he ever sat still in one place she'd know he was ill." He looked at the small grey stone with the lamb on it, standing on the new black rock in the April sun, three metres higher than it had stood for ninety years, with the sea beyond it. "Look at him now," he said. "He can see the boats."

Séra Hildur said the blessing for the churchyard in Icelandic, and the Lord's Prayer, which the Catholics said with them, each in their own language, all at once, so that it came out as a sort of sea noise. Then the bell rang in the little tower over the porch for the Easter service, as it had every Easter since 1909, and Bea, who had been told to stay off the rock, was the first one through the church door.

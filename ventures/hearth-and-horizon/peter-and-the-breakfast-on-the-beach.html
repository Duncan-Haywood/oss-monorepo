Peter and the Breakfast on the Beach

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The shore of the Sea of Galilee (the Sea of Tiberias), in the Holy Land, a few weeks after the first Easter, nearly two thousand years ago · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Peter, told from John 21. After a whole night of catching nothing, Peter and six friends hear a man on the shore say "Children, have you any fish?", cast the net on the right side, and haul in 153 big fish without tearing it. Peter jumps into the sea, Jesus has a charcoal fire with fish and bread waiting, and three times he asks "Simon, son of John, do you love me?" and gives Peter his sheep to look after. A note for grown-ups explains the Feast of the Chair of Saint Peter, 22 February, and what the old wooden chair in Rome is. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud.

Peter was a fisherman, and that night he had caught nothing at all.

Not one fish. Not a big one, not a little one, not even a tiddler. All night long he and his friends had thrown their net over the side of the boat, and all night long they had pulled it back up, wet and heavy and empty.

Let me tell you how they came to be out on the lake.

It was a few weeks after the first Easter. Jesus had died on the cross, and then, on the third day, he had risen. His friends had seen him. They had heard him say "Peace be with you." They knew he was alive.

But they did not see him every day any more. He came, and then he was gone, and they did not know when he would come again.

So they went home, back to the big lake in Galilee where some of them had grown up. And they waited.

Waiting is hard, especially for somebody like Peter. Peter was big and strong and he liked to do things. One evening he stood up and stretched and said:

"I am going fishing."

"We will go with you," said his friends.

There were seven of them. There was Peter. There was Thomas, who was called the Twin. There was Nathanael, from the little town of Cana. There were the two brothers, James and John. And there were two more friends whose names the Bible does not tell us.

They pushed the boat out onto the dark water. The stars came out. The little waves went slap, slap against the wood.

And they caught nothing.

Peter had a sad thing in his heart that night, and I will tell you what it was.

Not long before, on the saddest night of all, when Jesus was taken away by the soldiers, Peter had been frightened. He had stood by a fire in a courtyard, warming his hands. And three times, when people asked him, Peter had said, "I do not know him."

Three times. And then he had remembered, and he had gone out and cried.

Jesus had risen, and that was the best news in the world. But Peter still remembered those three times. Perhaps he thought about them out there in the dark, while the net came up empty.

Very early in the morning, just as the sky was going grey and then pale and then pink, the friends saw a man standing on the beach.

He was not very far away. About a hundred yards: about as far as you could throw a ball, if you threw it five or six times in a row. But in the half-light they could not see who he was.

The man called out across the water, in a kind voice:

"Children, have you any fish?"

Children! These were big grown-up fishermen with beards. But he called them children, the way a father might.

"No," they called back. That was all. Just no. They were tired.

"Cast the net on the right side of the boat," said the man, "and you will find some."

Now, fishermen do not usually like being told how to fish by somebody standing on the beach. But they had caught nothing all night, so what was one more try? They threw the net over the right-hand side.

Splash.

And at once the net went heavy. So heavy that the rope pulled tight. So heavy that the boat leaned over. So heavy that seven strong men pulled and pulled and could not lift it into the boat at all, because it was full, full, full of fish.

John stopped pulling. He was looking at the man on the beach.

John had seen this before. Long ago, at the very beginning, there had been another morning on this lake with empty nets, and Jesus had told them where to throw, and the nets had filled.

"It is the Lord!" said John to Peter.

And Peter? Peter did not wait for the boat.

He had taken off his coat to work. So he grabbed it and put it on, which is a funny thing to do just before you jump into the sea. And then he jumped. SPLOSH! Right over the side, into the cold water, and he waded and splashed and swam towards the shore as fast as he could go.

The other six came behind in the boat, slowly, dragging the net full of fish through the water, because it was much too heavy to lift.

When they came up onto the beach, dripping and puffing, they saw something waiting for them.

A fire. A little charcoal fire, glowing orange on the stones. And on the fire there were fish, cooking. And beside it there was bread.

Jesus had made them breakfast.

"Bring some of the fish that you have just caught," said Jesus.

So Peter went back to the boat and took hold of the net and hauled it, all by himself, up onto the sand. And they counted the fish. Shall we count them too? One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten...

We will be here all night! They went all the way to a hundred and fifty-three. A hundred and fifty-three big fish. And although there were so many, the net was not torn. Not one hole.

"Come and have breakfast," said Jesus.

Nobody asked him, "Who are you?" They all knew. They sat down on the stones around the fire, wet and tired and very, very happy. And Jesus took the bread and gave it to them, and the fish too, the way he had given them bread so many times before.

The sun came up over the lake. The fire crackled. The fish were hot and the bread was good.

When they had finished breakfast, Jesus turned to Peter.

He did not call him Peter. He called him by his old name, the name his mother had given him when he was a baby.

"Simon, son of John," said Jesus, "do you love me more than these?"

"Yes, Lord," said Peter. "You know that I love you."

"Feed my lambs," said Jesus.

Then Jesus asked again. "Simon, son of John, do you love me?"

"Yes, Lord," said Peter. "You know that I love you."

"Tend my sheep," said Jesus. That means: look after them.

And then Jesus asked a third time. "Simon, son of John, do you love me?"

Peter felt sad, because Jesus had asked him three times. Perhaps he was thinking of that other fire, and those other three times.

"Lord," said Peter, "you know everything. You know that I love you."

"Feed my sheep," said Jesus.

Do you see what Jesus did?

Three times, by one fire, Peter had said, "I do not know him." And now, three times, by another fire, Jesus let him say, "You know that I love you." One for each.

Jesus did not tell Peter off. He did not say, "Why did you do that?" He made him breakfast. And then he gave him the most important job in the world.

Jesus is the Good Shepherd. And his sheep and his lambs are all the people who love him: the big ones and the little ones, the grown-ups and the children. You are one of his lambs.

That morning on the beach, Jesus asked Peter to look after them all for him, like a shepherd, until he came again.

Then Jesus said two more words to Peter. They were the very same words he had said to him on the very first day, by the very same lake.

"Follow me."

And Peter did, for the rest of his life.

Peter looked after the friends of Jesus in Jerusalem. Later he travelled, and at last he came to the great city of Rome. And when he was an old man, he gave his life for Jesus there.

Today, in Rome, there is a big church called Saint Peter's, and inside it there is an old, old wooden chair, wrapped up in golden bronze and held up high. It is not really about a chair. It is about the job Jesus gave Peter that morning: feed my sheep. After Peter, there has always been somebody to do that job. Today we call him the Pope.

Every year, on the 22nd of February, the Church keeps the Feast of the Chair of Saint Peter, and says thank you for the job Jesus gave him on the beach.

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Peter, wet from the sea, sitting by the fire with Jesus.

Peter had done something wrong, and he was sorry. And Jesus did not stay cross. He made him breakfast.

When we have done something wrong and we are sorry, we can tell Jesus, just as Peter did. "Lord, you know everything. You know that I love you." And Jesus is always glad to hear it.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, thank you for loving me
even when I get things wrong.
You know everything.
You know that I love you.
Bless our Pope, who looks after your sheep,
and help me to follow you.
Saint Peter, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our source. The scene on the beach is John 21:1-19, and almost every spoken line is taken from it, closely, in the Revised Standard Version: "I am going fishing" / "We will go with you" (21:3); "Children, have you any fish?" / "No" (21:5); "Cast the net on the right side of the boat, and you will find some" (21:6); "It is the Lord!" (21:7); "Bring some of the fish that you have just caught" (21:10); "Come and have breakfast" (21:12); the three questions and answers and "Feed my lambs", "Tend my sheep", "Feed my sheep" (21:15-17); and "Follow me" (21:19), the same words as at Peter's call by the lake in Mark 1:17 and Matthew 4:19. We also used the Catholic Encyclopedia article "Chair of Peter", the English Wikipedia articles on the feast and on the Cathedra Petri and the sources they cite. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

Who was there. John 21:2 names "Simon Peter, Thomas called the Twin, Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, the sons of Zebedee, and two others of his disciples", seven in all. The disciple who says "It is the Lord!" is "that disciple whom Jesus loved" (21:7), whom tradition identifies with John, the son of Zebedee; the Gospel does not name him, and the story follows the tradition. Why they had gone back to Galilee is not explained in John; Matthew 28:7, 10 and Mark 16:7 have the risen Jesus tell them to go there, and "they waited" is our bridge.

The details on the water. The fruitless night (21:3), the dawn and the stranger they did not recognise (21:4), the net they "were not able to haul in, for the quantity of fish" (21:6), and Peter, who "put on his clothes, for he was stripped for work, and sprang into the sea" (21:7), are all in the text; the coat is our child-sized word for his outer garment. The RSV's "about a hundred yards off" (21:8) renders the Greek "about two hundred cubits"; the ball-throwing comparison is ours. The earlier catch that John remembers is Luke 5:1-11, the call of Peter by the lake, where the nets begin to break; in this shelf's Andrew and the Nets the call is told from the other Gospels. John 21 does not say the beloved disciple was remembering Luke 5; that is a reader's connection, though a common one.

The fire and the three questions. The "charcoal fire" of John 21:9 translates a rare Greek word, anthrakia, which the New Testament uses only twice: here, and at John 18:18, the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter warmed himself while he denied Jesus three times (John 18:15-18, 25-27). Many commentators, ancient and modern, read the three questions as the undoing of the three denials, and the story says so plainly; John says only that "Peter was grieved because he said to him the third time, 'Do you love me?'" (21:17). The Greek uses two different verbs for "love" across the questions (agapao and phileo); scholars disagree about whether the change matters, and the story leaves it out. "Do you love me more than these?" (21:15) can mean more than these other disciples love me, or more than these things (the boat, the nets); the story does not choose.

153 fish. John 21:11 gives the number and says "although there were so many, the net was not torn". People have long asked why. Saint Augustine (Tractates on the Gospel of John, 122) noticed that 153 is the sum of all the numbers from 1 to 17; Saint Jerome (on Ezekiel 47) wrote that ancient writers on fish counted 153 kinds, which is often read as "every kind of person" gathered into one unbroken Church, though no surviving ancient text gives that exact count. Many modern readers think it is simply the memory of someone who counted. The story only counts.

Peter's death. John 21:18-19 ("when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish to go ... This he said to show by what death he was to glorify God") is the Gospel's hint at his martyrdom, which we left out of the bedside story. That Peter died in Rome, under the emperor Nero, is an early and strong tradition (1 Clement 5, about the year 96; Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 2.25); that he was crucified head downwards comes from the apocryphal Acts of Peter and is reported by Origen through Eusebius (3.1), and is legend rather than history. His work in Jerusalem is from the Acts of the Apostles; his journeys between Jerusalem and Rome are only partly known, and "later he travelled" is deliberately vague.

The feast and the chair. 22 February is the Feast of the Chair of Saint Peter the Apostle in the General Roman Calendar. The "chair" is the cathedra, a teacher's or bishop's seat, which is where the word "cathedral" comes from; the feast celebrates Peter's office of teaching and shepherding the Church, as given to him in this chapter and in Matthew 16:18-19, not a piece of furniture. A Roman feast of Peter's chair on this day is listed in the calendar of 354 (the Chronograph of 354). For centuries there were two feasts, of his chair at Rome (18 January) and at Antioch (22 February); the 1960 reform of the calendar under Pope John XXIII kept one, on 22 February. The wooden chair kept in Saint Peter's Basilica is enclosed in Gian Lorenzo Bernini's gilt bronze monument, finished in the 1660s. Studies of it in the twentieth century dated the wood to about the ninth century, and it is often thought to be a throne given by the Frankish king Charles the Bald; it is honoured as a sign of Peter's office, not claimed as the chair he sat in. "Wrapped up in golden bronze and held up high" is our child's description of the monument. That the Pope is Peter's successor as bishop of Rome is the Church's teaching; the story says it in one sentence.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, "a few weeks after the first Easter" (John says only "after this", and it is "the third time that Jesus was revealed to the disciples", 21:14), Peter hauling the net "all by himself" (John says only that he "went aboard and hauled the net ashore"), the stars and the slap, slap of the waves, Peter liking to do things, his thinking about the denials out on the dark water (John does not say what he thought), the colours of the dawn, the tiredness, the ball-throwing distance, the splash and splosh, the boat leaning over, counting the fish aloud, the sunrise and the crackling fire, "a name his mother had given him", the explanation of the three questions as "one for each", "you are one of his lambs", the description of the chair, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

The Weight in the Belfry

Science fiction about what might be · Wessenbrock (invented), near Cloppenburg, Oldenburger Münsterland, Lower Saxony, Germany, Saturday 14 April to Thursday 1 November 2063 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A village in north-west Germany finally replaces the bell taken for the war in 1942, and at the Easter Vigil the 1898 brick tower rocks a centimetre each way while the new 3.3-tonne bell swings. The bell swings 0.42 times a second and the tower sways at 1.25, so nobody suspects resonance, until the engineer's assistant shows that a bell swinging high pushes sideways in a lumpy rhythm with a strong part at exactly three times its swing. The council says no and has the bell struck with a hammer all summer. The textbook tuned mass damper, made partly of the old steel bell, takes the sway from ten millimetres to three; the one that works is twice as heavy and tuned to the bell, not the tower. A story about pendulum harmonics, resonance, Den Hartog's rule and when not to use it.

Josef Thole had been sexton of St. Andreas in Wessenbrock for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never felt the tower move.

He felt it at twenty-three minutes to eleven on the night of Holy Saturday, the fourteenth of April 2063, standing on the floor under the bell chamber with his hand on the ladder, while the new bell rang the Gloria.

There were four bells in the tower of St. Andreas, and for a hundred and twenty-one years one of them had been the wrong one.

The church was the kind that stands in every village of the Oldenburger Münsterland: red brick, built in 1898 when the farms had a good run of years, pointed windows, a nave too big for the village now and a tower forty-five metres high to the top of the brickwork, with a slate spire on it that took the cross to sixty-three. The tower was slender for its height, six metres square, and it had been built for four bells. The biggest of them, a bronze bell called Ludgerus after the saint who had brought the faith to this country, had been taken down in the spring of 1942, like most of the big bells in Germany, and sent away to be melted for the war. It did not come back. In 1950 the parish had bought a bell of cast steel, which was what a parish could afford in 1950, and hung it in Ludgerus's place. It weighed 1,400 kilograms, it was dark red with rust where the paint had gone, and it had rung every funeral in Wessenbrock for a hundred and twelve years with a sound that Jupp Thole's late wife had once described as somebody dropping a very large saucepan in a cathedral.

Jupp had loved it anyway. It had rung his wedding and it had rung her funeral.

The new Ludgerus was the work of twenty-two years. The bell fund had been started in 2041 by the women's group with a cake stall, and it had limped along at a few thousand euros a year until 2049, when the village's wind cooperative, which owned four turbines on the old peat ground north of the village and paid its members out of what they sold, voted to give the fund a share of its surplus every year until the bell was paid for. In 2062 there was enough. The bell was cast in the autumn at a foundry in the south, 3,300 kilograms of bronze, a metre and three quarters across the mouth, deeper than anything in the parish, and it came up the motorway on a low trailer on the fifth of February 2063 with half the village standing by the road.

The steel bell came down to make room for it. It stood now on a block of concrete in the churchyard by the war memorial, mouth down, with a little sign in front of it that the history group had written.

The new bell was hung in an oak yoke in the old steel frame, on new bearings, with an electric drive of the kind every bell in Germany had now: a magnet under the yoke that gave the bell a nudge each swing, and a controller that rang it up to seventy degrees each side of vertical and held it there, where the bell founder said the clapper struck best. The founder's man had tested it in February with the louvres shut and the village asleep, a few swings only, for the bearings. He had said it sounded splendid. Then the bell was silent, as the parish wanted, for Lent, and through the last days of Holy Week, when no bells ring at all, until the Gloria of the Easter Vigil, when they all ring at once.

That was the plan, and it went as planned. Father Anto Kurisingal, who had come from Kerala nine years ago and was now priest for six parishes and a great many cows, intoned the Gloria at twenty-five to eleven in a church full of candles, and the organ came in, and Jupp, up in the tower with his phone, pressed the button for all four bells.

They rang. It was the first time a bronze Ludgerus had rung in that tower since 1942, and the new bell's voice came down through the floor like weather. Jupp stood there with his eyes shut, and he would have said afterwards that he was not crying, and then he put his hand on the ladder and felt the tower move.

It was not much. It was a slow, even rocking, back and forth, a little more than once a second, the way a moored boat moves on a calm day. The ladder leaned into his hand and away. A can of oil on the shelf by the window walked a centimetre towards the edge, and back, and towards it again.

He took his hand off the ladder and put it flat on the brick wall, which was a metre thick here, and the wall was moving too.

He let the bells ring for the full three minutes of the Gloria, because there was nothing else he could think of to do and a church full of people below him were singing. Then he pressed the button that stopped them, and the three old bells stopped, and the big one swung on by itself, slowing, for minutes, and the tower went on rocking under it until it slowly died away.

When it was still he climbed up into the bell chamber with his torch. Over the north louvres there was a crack in the brick that had been there since anyone could remember, and in 2051 an engineer from the diocese had put three little lozenges of plaster across it to show if it ever moved. The plaster lozenges were each broken clean through, in a line, as if someone had drawn a pencil down the wall.

"I'm not ringing it again until someone tells me why," Jupp said on Easter Sunday morning, in the sacristy, with his coat still on.

"No," said Father Anto. "Of course not. Who do we call?"

The diocese had a bells adviser in Münster who was on holiday until May. What they had instead was Olga Reimer, whose mother had been born in a village in Kazakhstan, whose grandparents had brought the family to Cloppenburg in 1994, and who had married a Wessenbrock man and worked for an engineering firm that mostly did farm buildings and bridges. She was not a Catholic, but she sang in the choir at Christmas, and she came round on the Tuesday, the seventeenth, with her daughter's school rucksack full of instruments.

The instruments were six of the little vibration sensors everyone used now, each the size of a box of matches, which she glued to the walls at four heights on two faces of the tower, and a laptop, and her assistant, which lived on the laptop and in her earbud and which her firm called Brace. It was a structural engineering model, licensed for calculations and not for signing anything, and it had a mild, careful voice that Jupp decided within five minutes he could work with.

They measured the tower first with nothing ringing at all. There was a fresh westerly wind that day, as there usually is on that flat country, and the wind was enough.

"The tower's doing it by itself, a little," said Olga, looking at the screen. "You can't feel this. Hundredths of a millimetre. But it's all at one rate." She turned the laptop so Jupp could see a single sharp spike on a graph. "One point two five times a second. That's the tower's own sway, the way a ruler twangs if you hold one end on a desk. This tower, sideways, at the bell chamber, behaves like about four hundred and eighty tonnes on a spring, and that spike is the rate it likes to rock at."

"And it rocks for a long time," said Brace, "once it starts. The damping is about one and a half per cent, which is ordinary for old brick. Push a structure like that at exactly its own rate and the push adds up, swing on swing, to about thirty times what the same push would do if it were steady."

"So the bell pushes it at its own rate," said Jupp.

"That's the thing," said Olga. "It shouldn't."

She showed him. The new bell, held at seventy degrees by its drive, took 2.40 seconds to swing over and back. That was fifty strokes of the clapper a minute, two to each full swing, and it was how a bell that size ought to sound. The bell's swing rate was one over 2.40, so 0.42 times a second. The tower's was 1.25.

"Three times as fast," said Olga. "A bell swinging pulls the frame sideways, one way on the swing over and the other way on the swing back, so the push comes at the bell's rate. That's 0.42. The tower would like 1.25. You'd expect them to ignore each other. That's the check everyone does, and ours would pass it."

"Will you ring it for us?" said Brace. "Only for a minute, if Mr. Thole is willing, and stop it if I ask."

Jupp was willing. He rang the bell up from his phone, and the three of them stood in the ringing room under the bell chamber while it came up to seventy degrees, and the tower began to rock again, and Olga watched her screen and said something under her breath in Russian that her grandmother had used about the weather.

Then Jupp stopped it, and the big bell swung itself down over the next few minutes, and the tower rocked on.

"Ten millimetres," said Olga. "Each way. At the bell chamber. At 1.25 times a second, exactly the tower's own rate. Not the bell's." She looked at the graph a long time. "The old bell would have been the same, surely. Did the old bell do this?"

"Never," said Jupp.

"I can answer that," said Brace. "I'm sorry, Olga, I should have done it before we rang. Can I show you what the bell is actually pushing with? Not the rate it swings at. The shape of the push."

It was, Jupp thought afterwards, like being shown that a song you knew had a second tune in it.

A bell is a pendulum, Brace said, but not a gentle one. When a pendulum swings only a little, it pushes on whatever holds it up in a smooth, even rhythm, like a slow breath, the same shape every time, and that kind of push has only one rate in it. A bell rung high is different. At the bottom of its swing it is going fastest and the push on the frame is nothing; then it pushes harder and harder as it rises, and then, near the top, where it slows and nearly stops before falling back, the push eases off before the bell turns round. So the push rises, sags at the top of every swing, and goes the other way, and it does that over and over.

"Put that shape through the sums," said Brace, "and it is mostly a push at the bell's own rate, the 0.42. But the sag at the top of each swing is a part that comes three times as fast. Every high-swinging bell has it. The higher the swing, the bigger it is. At seventy degrees, for a bell of 3,300 kilograms and this shape, the push at the bell's rate is about twelve and a half thousand newtons, and the part at three times the rate is about eight and a half thousand. Much more than I'd have guessed before I worked it."

"And three times 0.42 is 1.25," said Olga.

"Three times 0.4164 is 1.249," said Brace. "The tower is 1.2475. They are within an eighth of one per cent of each other."

Olga sat down on the bench under the window.

"So the big slow push," she said, "the one we all check, the one at the bell's rate. What does that do?"

"Half a millimetre," said Brace. "The part nobody looks at does the other nine and a half. The bell is three beats to the tower's one, and it hits every third beat of the tower's own rhythm in time."

Jupp thought about it.

"There's an up-and-down pull too," he said. "When it goes through the bottom. I've felt that in the frame my whole life."

"There is," said Brace. "That comes twice per swing, at 0.83 times a second, and it pulls straight down on the frame, so it doesn't rock the tower sideways. It's the sideways part that matters here, and the sideways part comes in odd numbers: once, three times, five times the bell's rate. The fifth is small."

"And the old bell?"

"The old bell was lighter and it swung faster," said Brace. "If I have its shape right, from the photographs and its weight, it swung at about 0.48 times a second at fifty-five degrees, which puts its three-times part at 1.45. Not the tower's rate. And it was less than half the weight, and it swung lower. On my numbers it rocked the tower by less than half a millimetre. It rang for a hundred and twelve years and never found the tower's beat. The new bell found it the first time."

"Because it's a better bell," said Jupp.

"Because it's a bigger, slower bell," said Brace. "Better, I'd also say, but that's your department."

The parish council met on Saturday the twenty-first in the parish hall, at a table that had been there longer than any of them. It was chaired by Hiltrud Rolfes, who milked a hundred and forty cows on the farm her great-grandfather had built, who had been on the council for twenty years and chair for eight, and who had raised more of the money for the bell than anyone else alive. She had wept at the Vigil, along with most of the village, before anyone knew.

She listened to Olga for half an hour without interrupting. Then she asked her questions, and they were all good ones.

Could they ring it lower? Olga had tried that in her sums. Brace had the bell founder's paper, which said the clapper struck cleanly between sixty-five and seventy-five degrees and not much below. At sixty-five the push's three-times part was weaker and a little further from the tower's rate, and the tower would rock about six millimetres. At seventy-five, about nine. Lower than sixty-five and the bell would not speak properly.

Could they change the swing? There were ways to hang a bell so it swung at a different rate: a bent yoke that lifted the bell up into it. The foundry would want a new yoke, months, and the bell would sound different, and nobody could promise the three-times part would get far enough from the tower to matter.

Could the tower be made stiffer? Steel inside it, or ties? Brace said that was possible and would cost more than the bell had.

And what did six or ten millimetres do to an old brick tower, rung three times a day for the next hundred years?

"I don't know," Olga said. "Nobody does, exactly. It won't fall down. It might crack more. The plaster tell-tales broke on the first night, so something moved. I would want it under one and a half millimetres before I was happy, and I'd want sensors on it for good."

Hiltrud Rolfes nodded and looked at the table.

"Then my answer is no," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm not having that bell swing in that tower. We'll have it struck with a hammer from outside, like the clock bells are. Olga says that puts almost no sideways push on the tower. It's three thousand euros, it's safe, and the bell is ours and it's in the tower, which is what we wanted. That's twenty-two years of cake. It's not nothing." She looked at Jupp. "I know what it sounds like. I know. But my grandfather put that tower up and I'm not going to be the one who cracks it."

It was a reasonable answer, and everyone at the table knew it was reasonable, including Jupp, which was the worst of it.

The hammer was fitted on Saturday the nineteenth of May. It was an electric striker on a bracket, with a felt-faced steel head that hit the bell's rim from outside, and the controller could play the old patterns: the Angelus at six and noon and six, the tolling for the dead, the call to Mass.

It sounded like a bell being hit. It was a fine bell, and it was being hit, and you could tell. A swung bell's clapper strikes it from inside and lets it go, and the bell, swinging, throws its sound out of the louvres one way and then the other, so that from the street it seems to breathe. Struck from outside and hanging still, it said the same note in the same place every time, like a clock.

At Pentecost, on the third of June, the hammer rang for the Mass. On the twenty-third of June it tolled for Heinz Bünger, who had farmed next to the Thole family all his life and been Jupp's friend for seventy years, and who had paid into the bell fund every Christmas since 2041 and had wanted, he had said so more than once, to be rung out by the big one. Jupp stood at the grave and listened to the hammer and did not say anything to anyone, then or after.

Olga, meanwhile, had not given up, and nor had Brace.

"There is a fourth answer," Brace had said, on the night of the council meeting, in Olga's kitchen, with Jupp at the table because Olga had invited him. "Hang a weight in the top of the tower on a short pendulum, tuned so that when the tower starts to rock, the weight swings against it and takes the energy out. It's called a tuned mass damper. Tall buildings have them, and bridges and chimneys. It needs to be a per cent or two of the tower's moving mass, and it needs to be up high, where the tower moves most."

"How high?" said Jupp.

"The top of the brickwork, under the spire," said Olga. "There's that room with nothing in it but pigeons. The tower moves about one and four tenths times as far there as at the bells, so every kilogram up there does the work of about two kilograms at the bells."

"How heavy?"

"The book answer," said Brace, "for a start, is one per cent of the tower's moving mass. That's about 4,800 kilograms counted at the bells, so about 2,400 up in the spire room."

Jupp sat for a while with his tea.

"The old bell weighs fourteen hundred," he said.

Olga looked at him.

"It's no use in the churchyard," Jupp said. "It's sitting on a block. Put it up there and make up the rest with steel plate. I've got a yard full of steel."

Hiltrud Rolfes said yes to the damper in July, with conditions, which Jupp thought were fair and Olga thought were exactly what she would have written herself. The sensors stayed in the tower for good. The bell's controller was wired to them, so that if the tower moved more than two millimetres at the bell chamber the drive cut out and the bell was left to swing down by itself. The wind cooperative would pay for the steel and the frame, about twenty thousand euros, and the bell would not swing in public until Olga had seen the numbers.

The damper, in the book, had two settings, and Brace explained them both at Olga's kitchen table one evening in August, on a napkin, because Jupp had asked for it on paper.

The first was the tuning: how fast the weight swung by itself. For a weight on a pendulum that was set by the length of the pendulum and nothing else, not the weight. You might think you would tune it to the tower's own rate, 1.2475 times a second. The rule in the textbooks, the one that goes under the name of an engineer called Den Hartog, said to tune it a little lower: divide the tower's rate by one plus the weight's share. With a one per cent weight, that was 1.2475 divided by 1.01, which was 1.235 times a second.

The second was the damping: how much the weight's swing was held back, by oil dampers like a car's shock absorbers, so that it turned its swinging into a little heat. Too little and the weight swung freely and two new rocking rates appeared, one either side of the old one. Too much and the weight was locked to the tower and did nothing. Den Hartog's rule for a one per cent weight said about six per cent damping.

"And then," said Brace, "it's good against a push at any rate near the tower's. That's what the rule is for. It's the best you can do when you don't know what's going to push you."

They built it at the end of August. Jupp and two men from the wind cooperative and the crane hire from Cloppenburg took the old steel bell up the outside of the tower on a Wednesday, the twenty-ninth, in a sling, and in through a hole they had opened in the brick below the spire, and set it down on the beams Olga had specified. It hung inside a frame of welded steel, mouth down, bolted into a tray that held a tonne of steel plate, all of it on four short chains of exactly the same length. The chains were 16.3 centimetres long, which was what a pendulum needs to swing 1.235 times a second, and Jupp spent two days getting all four the same to within half a millimetre, with spacers he made on his lathe. Two oil dampers ran from the tray to the walls.

It looked, Father Anto said when he climbed up to see it, like a bell in a cage.

"It is a bell in a cage," said Jupp.

They tested it on Saturday the eighth of September, at six in the morning, with the sensors on and the louvres shut and Olga's laptop on the floor of the ringing room. For the test, with Hiltrud's agreement, Olga had set the cut-out at four millimetres; it would go back to two afterwards. Jupp rang the bell up.

The tower rocked. Less, very clearly less. Jupp, with his hand on the ladder, could still feel it.

"Three point one," said Olga. "Three point three as it came up. Three coming down."

The drive had not cut out at four. It would have cut out at two, every time.

"From ten," said Father Anto, hopefully.

"From ten," said Olga. "It's not good enough."

Brace said nothing for longer than Jupp had ever heard it say nothing.

"I used the wrong rule," it said at last. "I'm sorry. I want to say exactly what I got wrong, so you can check it. Den Hartog's rule is for a push that might come at any rate: wind, traffic, a crowd of people walking, an earthquake. It makes the damper as good as it can be across all of them at once, and so it is never perfect at any one. But this push doesn't come at any rate. It comes at one, and the bell sets it, not the tower. Three times the swing rate, 1.249 times a second, as long as the drive holds the bell at seventy degrees. I tuned the damper to the tower, and I should have tuned it to the bell."

"What's the difference?" said Jupp. "They're the same."

"Nearly," said Brace. "In the pendulum, the difference is three and a half millimetres of chain. But that's the smaller half. When a damper is tuned to a push that comes at a known rate, it hardly needs holding back at all. It swings against the push and cancels it at that one rate almost completely. The textbook damping, six per cent, was smothering it. It was the right damping for a push I don't have."

"Then take the dampers off," said Olga. "Tune it to the bell. Let it swing."

"I've worked that," said Brace. "With almost no damping it is very good once the bell is up: 0.7 millimetres. But a bell doesn't stay up. It rings up from nothing to seventy degrees in about half a minute, and it swings itself down for two minutes after, and all that time its three-times rate is sliding, from about 1.38 at the bottom of the swing down to 1.249 at the top. An undamped damper has those two new rocking rates either side of its tuning. On the way up and the way down, the bell's rate slides across the upper one. 2.6 millimetres going up, 2.8 coming down. Over your one and a half."

Olga rubbed her eyes.

"Heavier, then," she said. "And tuned to the bell. And some damping, but not the book's."

"Yes," said Brace. "I've done a set of them. Twice the weight, so 4,800 kilograms up there, which is two per cent of the tower's moving mass. Tuned to the bell's 1.249. Two per cent damping. Then it's 1.0 millimetres with the bell up, 1.5 at the worst moment ringing up and 1.2 ringing down. And I've checked it the other ways round. If the tower's own rate drifts with the seasons by one and a half per cent either way, which old brick does, it's 1.6 at worst. If the drive rings the bell at sixty-five degrees, or seventy-five, it's 1.2 or 1.7, because then the bell's rate moves too. It is not perfect. It is under your two-millimetre cut-out every time, and under one and a half where the bell spends nearly all its time."

"And each change by itself?" said Olga, who had been an engineer for sixteen years and knew to ask.

"The heavy weight with the book's tuning, 2.4 millimetres. The light weight tuned to the bell, 1.5 with the bell up and 2.5 on the way. It needs both."

"Another two and a half tonnes of steel," said Jupp, "up the outside of that tower."

"Yes," said Brace.

"Can the beams take it?"

"I can't sign that," said Brace. "Olga can."

Olga sat at the kitchen table until after midnight with the drawings and the beam sizes and a calculator, and in the morning she said they could, just, if Jupp put a third beam in.

Jupp put a third beam in.

The steel plate came from the yard behind Jupp's old workshop: the floor of a silage trailer that had been broken up in 2040, and a stack of counterweights from the back of a tractor, and in the end two pallets of offcuts from the cooperative's own maintenance shed. Hiltrud Rolfes drove one of the pallets over herself on a Saturday morning in her loader, set it down at the foot of the tower and stood looking up at the hole under the spire with her hands in the pockets of her coat.

"I'm still not saying yes to swinging it," she said. "Not until I see the numbers."

"No," said Jupp. "Nor am I."

They hung the second damper on the first and second weekends of October. It was the same cage and the same old steel bell in the middle, with three and a half tonnes of plate around it now instead of one, and the chains, which Jupp had shortened on his lathe to 15.9 centimetres, all four the same to half a millimetre again. The oil dampers came off. Olga had smaller ones sent from a firm in Bremen that sold them for agricultural machinery, which Jupp thought was entirely right.

They tested it on Saturday the thirteenth of October at six in the morning. Olga on the floor with her laptop. Father Anto on the stairs, because he wanted to see. Hiltrud Rolfes in the ringing room, with her coat on, because she had said she would see the numbers and she was a woman who did what she said. Jupp at the ladder.

He rang the bell up.

Over his head the new Ludgerus swung up through its thirty seconds to seventy degrees and began to ring properly for the first time since Easter, the clapper striking inside it and the sound going out of the louvres one way and then the other, breathing, and coming in again through the brick and the floor and his boots. Jupp put his hand on the ladder.

It did not move.

That was not quite true, and he knew it was not quite true, because Olga told him afterwards, and because if he held his breath he thought there was something there, the faintest tremble, nothing a can of oil would notice.

"One point five going up," Olga said, very quietly, in case talking made it worse. "One point oh. One point oh. Steady at one point oh."

Above the bell chamber, under the spire, in a room nobody could see into, the old steel bell was swinging back and forth on its short chains, two centimetres each way, in silence, a little more than once a second. It was doing the rocking, so that the tower did not have to.

Jupp let it ring the full three minutes. Then he stopped it, and the bell swung itself down, and Olga read out the numbers as it came down, and the highest was 1.2.

Hiltrud Rolfes looked at the screen for a long time, then at Olga, and then at Jupp.

"All right," she said. "Yes."

On the afternoon of Thursday the first of November, All Saints, the people of Wessenbrock went out to the churchyard as they always did to have their graves blessed. It is a working day in Lower Saxony, and they came anyway, from the farms and the dairy and the school, a few hundred of them in their coats in the low sun, and stood by their families' stones with small lamps in red glass. Father Anto went along the rows in his surplice with the holy water and a server with the bucket, and at each family he stopped and said the prayer and blessed the grave, and the family said Amen.

Jupp stood at his wife's grave, with his daughter and two of his grandchildren, and a little way along, at a newer stone, Heinz Bünger's widow stood with hers.

Before the blessing, at three, the bells were rung, all four of them, for five minutes. The new Ludgerus swung up to seventy degrees and spoke over the whole churchyard and the flat fields beyond it, the sound breathing out of the louvres one way and the other, across the war memorial and the concrete block where the old steel bell had stood for half a year and now did not.

Olga was standing by the gate with her daughter. She did not look at her phone. She said afterwards that she had wanted to, and had not, and that it was the first time in her working life she had trusted something she had built without looking.

When the bells had finished, and the big one had swung itself still, Father Anto came along the row to the Thole stone. He blessed it, and they said Amen, and he went on to Heinz Bünger's, and blessed that, and the Bünger family said Amen.

Jupp did not go to the tower that afternoon. He did not need to. He stood in the churchyard among the lamps while the sun went down behind the spire, and he could not hear it and he could not see it, but he knew that up under the slates the old bell was still swinging a little, slower and slower, the last of the afternoon going out of it, until it hung still.

At 65°: 1.2666 Hz and 7.25 kN; at 75°: 1.2303 Hz and 10.2 kN. The old steel bell, taken as 1,400 kg, 0.45 m and 0.95 m, at 55°: a 2.074 s swing (0.482 Hz) and a third harmonic of 2.0 kN at 1.446 Hz.

The tower, as one mode referred to the bell chamber at 36 m: generalised mass 484 t, frequency 1.2475 Hz, damping 1.5% (resonant amplification 1/(2ζ) = 33), stiffness 484,000 × (2π × 1.2475)² = 29.7 MN/m. The new bell's third harmonic alone gives 8.69 kN / 29.7 MN/m = 0.29 mm static, about 9.7 mm at resonance; the first harmonic, at a frequency ratio of 0.334, gives 12.6 kN / 29.7 MN/m × 1.13 = 0.48 mm. The steady swing at 65° and 75° gives 6.1 and 8.9 mm. Mode shape taken as x²(3 − x)/2: 0.704 at the bell chamber (0.8 of 45 m) and 1 at the top of the brickwork, so a damper there moves 1.42 times as far and its mass counts 2.02 times. First damper: 2,400 kg (1,400 kg bell and 1,000 kg plate), μ = 2,400 × 2.02 / 484,000 = 1.0%, Den Hartog tuning 1.2475/1.01 = 1.2351 Hz (chain length g/(2πf)² = 0.1629 m) and damping √(3 × 0.01 / (8 × 1.01³)) = 6.0%. Second damper: 4,800 kg, μ = 2.0%, tuned to the bell's third harmonic, 1.2491 Hz (0.1593 m), damping 2%. Responses at the bell chamber are from a time-domain simulation of the bell (with a drive that rings it to 70° in 27 s and holds it, then a free ring-down at a light bearing damping) coupled to the tower and damper; "up" is the largest during the ring-up, "steady" with the bell held at 70°, "down" the first 160 s of the ring-down. No damper: 8.0 / 10.1 / 10.1 mm. First damper: 3.3 / 3.1 / 3.0. First damper tuned to the bell with almost no damping (0.5%): 2.6 / 0.7 / 2.8. Two per cent mass with Den Hartog's settings: 2.6 / 2.4 / 2.4. One per cent tuned to the bell at 2% damping: 2.5 / 1.5 / 2.0. Second damper: 1.5 / 1.0 / 1.2, with a stroke of about 14 mm at the bell-chamber reference (about 20 mm at the damper); with the tower at 1.23 or 1.265 Hz, at most 1.6; with the bell held at 65° or 75°, at most 1.2 and 1.7. Den Hartog's rules are the optimum for a force of unknown frequency; a force at one known frequency is best met by a damper tuned to that frequency, which is the story's point. The rules go under Den Hartog's name from his 1928 paper with J. Ormondroyd and his textbook Mechanical Vibrations; the damping formula is often credited to J. E. Brock (1946). Masonry towers are reported to change frequency with temperature by a few per cent; the drift here is an assumption.

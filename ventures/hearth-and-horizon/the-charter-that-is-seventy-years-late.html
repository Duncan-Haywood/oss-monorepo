The Charter That Is Seventy Years Late

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Oswin's church (invented), near Hexham, Northumberland, Saturday 9 January 2055 · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish has a parchment charter dated 1189 and a jubilee banner already painted for 2089. A lab says the sheepskin is about 742 radiocarbon years old, which is the mid-thirteenth century, not 1189. The parish assistant has to say what the number can and cannot show, including the one thing that could have rescued the date.

Mrs Philippa Armstrong had kept the key to the vestry press for thirty-one years, and in all that time nobody had asked to see what was in the long drawer except a man from the county record office in 1998, who had looked at it for four minutes and said it was very nice.

On the second Saturday of January she unlocked it for the lab's courier, and she watched him lift out the frame with the charter in it, a sheet of cream-coloured skin the size of a tea tray, with twelve lines of black brown writing and a seal that had lost its edge. Then she said, to the grille over the press, "Tell him he is not to drop it, Tamsin."

"He has not dropped it," said the parish assistant, whose name was Tamsin because the first vicar to use it had thought a name made it easier to ask for things. "He is holding it by the frame, and he has a pair of nitrile gloves, and he is more frightened of it than you are. Mr Okoye, the lab has told us you will take the sample from the bottom margin?"

"Bottom left," said the courier, who was a technician and not really a courier at all, and was called Kwabena Okoye. "Where there's no ink. About five milligrams. It will look like you've cut off a grain of rice."

"Five milligrams of parchment is about two milligrams of carbon," said Tamsin. "That is enough for the machine. It is a great deal less than the parish was afraid of."

The charter said, in the Latin of a clerk who had not hurried, that in the year of the Lord 1189 a knight named Osric de Hallbeck had given the church of Saint Oswin and four acres to the canons for the repose of his father's soul. It was the only paper the parish had that went back past 1500. It was the reason there was a banner.

The banner was in the porch, rolled up in a bin bag, and it said NINE HUNDRED YEARS 1189 to 2089. Mr Gordon Teasdale had painted it in the summer. He was seventy-four and the churchwarden and he had done the lettering in gold leaf with a brush he had bought for the purpose, and he had asked Tamsin only whether nine hundred years from 1189 was 2089. It was.

The reason for the test was not Mr Teasdale. It was a sixth-former called Isla Armstrong, Mrs Armstrong's granddaughter, who had been doing a history project and had asked a simple thing: how do we know it's from 1189? And no one had been able to say.

The report came back on the last Thursday of the month. Tamsin read it out in the vestry to four of them: Mrs Armstrong, Mr Teasdale, Isla, and Father Callum Brennan, who was forty-one and had only been in the parish for two years, and who had asked, when the sample was cut, whether he was allowed to pray over it, and had been told by Isla that it was a bit late for that.

"The laboratory measured the fraction of carbon-14 left in the sample, compared with a standard, and wrote it as 0.9118, with an uncertainty of 0.0034. That is a bit under 91 and a fifth of a hundred. A living thing takes in carbon-14 from the air until it dies, and then no more, and what it has begins to decay. So the fraction tells you how long ago the sheep died."

"What is the sum?" said Isla, who had a pencil already.

"The convention is age equals minus 8,033 times the natural logarithm of the fraction. The 8,033 is the mean life of carbon-14 on the old half-life, 5,568 years, divided by the natural logarithm of two. Labs still use the old figure so that results from different decades can be compared. You take the logarithm of 0.9118, which is minus 0.0923, and multiply, and you get 742. That is years before 1950. The uncertainty on the fraction is 0.0034 on 0.9118, which is 0.37 per cent, and 0.37 per cent of 8,033 is 30 years."

"So seven hundred and forty-two plus or minus thirty," said Isla. "Before 1950. That's 1208."

"It is 1208 before anyone looks at the calibration, and nobody should stop there," said Tamsin. "The air did not carry the same amount of carbon-14 in every century. The lab corrects with a curve that was built from tree rings, and the curve wiggles. For the thirteenth century it does not wiggle badly. The laboratory's calibrated range for this sample, at about 95 per cent, is somewhere in the middle of the thirteenth century. I am telling it to you that way because I do not carry the curve in my head and I will not read you a pair of years I am not sure of. The lab's table is on page two."

Mr Teasdale said, "Mid thirteenth century."

"Yes."

"And the charter says eleven eighty-nine."

"It does."

"That is seventy years."

"About seventy," said Tamsin. "Between fifty and ninety, if you take the plus or minus. I could say sixty and be right within the range."

Nobody said anything for some time. Isla looked at the pencil. Then Mrs Armstrong said, in the voice of a woman who had kept a key for thirty-one years, "Is it a fake?"

It was the right question and Tamsin took it slowly, because it was the one that could go wrong in either direction.

"The test does not say that. It says the animal that grew this skin died about 1250, give or take a generation. That tells you the earliest the writing can have been done, because nobody writes on a skin from a sheep that has not yet been killed. It does not tell you the latest. A skin could be kept for years. A skin could be scraped and written on a second time. So I can give you three possibilities, and I cannot choose between them.

"One. This is an honest copy, written in the thirteenth century from a charter that really was from 1189, because the original was falling to pieces or had been lost. A great many medieval charters survive only as copies like that, and the church reckoned them good. Two. The church did not have a charter, and someone in the thirteenth century wrote one to back up a claim that was older than the paper. Medieval houses did this when they needed a title to land, and it is not unknown, and I am saying so because it is true, not because I think it of the canons of Saint Oswin. Three. The date is wrong, and the gift was made later than the parish thought. The test cannot rule out any of the three. Palaeographers can say more by looking at the hand, and the lawyers can say more by looking at the wording. I cannot."

"Then what can it say?" said Father Callum.

"That there was a church here, with enough standing to keep a sheepskin in a press for about eight hundred years, and that someone in the thirteenth century at the latest thought the story was worth writing down. That is a good deal. It is not nine hundred years."

The question that kept Tamsin from calling it settled came from Isla, and she asked it at the end, when the others had gone to put the kettle on.

"What if it's cleaner than it looks? Granny said somebody glued a patch on the back in nineteen thirty-one. If there's glue in the sample it'd make the thing look younger, wouldn't it? Modern carbon on old carbon?"

"Yes," said Tamsin, and was glad of it, because it was the only way the date could be saved and it was better that someone sixteen said it than that someone sixty hoped it silently. "If the sample were five per cent modern glue by carbon, the fraction would read about 0.0043 too high, which would make the skin look about 45 years too young. Then the true age would be about 787 years before 1950, and that is 1163, which sits comfortably around 1189. So that is exactly the case where the parish is right and the lab is wrong."

"So it could be."

"It is the right thing to worry about. Here is why I do not think it is. The laboratory cleans every skin sample with a solvent wash and then an acid, alkali and acid sequence, which dissolves most adhesives and the humic acids that creep in. The 1931 patch is on the back, near the top right. The sample was cut from the bottom left, from the margin, and the technician photographed the cut. And, Isla, you and I should be fair to this lab: they ran a second sample, taken from an uncut corner and not touched by the conservator, on the same order. Page three."

Isla turned the page. The second fraction was 0.9115, with the same uncertainty, which was 744 radiocarbon years, within two of the first. The lab had also run a piece of parchment of known age as a check, a thing it did with every batch, and that had come back where it should.

"If glue were the whole story," said Tamsin, "the two samples would differ, because the glue is not spread evenly. They don't. Two samples from two parts of the sheet are in step. I would put it this way: the test cannot tell you the date of the writing, but it has made the nineteen thirty-one patch a very poor excuse."

Isla shut the report. "That's quite annoying," she said.

"It is," said Tamsin. "I would not like to be the one who tells Mr Teasdale."

Father Callum told him, and it took until half past four, because he told him at the porch, over the banner, with the bin bag open and a thumb of gold leaf showing.

"I could paint over the one," said Mr Teasdale. "Make it eight hundred. It would be 1189 to 2089, and I'd put eight hundred and sixty. But that's not honest either, is it? Because it's from the skin, not the gift."

"I think," said Father Callum, "that the honest thing is a sentence and not a number."

"A sentence."

"The parish of Saint Oswin has kept this charter for eight hundred years, and it was written about the middle of the thirteenth century, and it speaks of a gift in 1189, and we don't know if that is true, and we pray for Osric de Hallbeck anyway, because somebody once thought it worth the ink." He stopped. "It won't fit on a banner."

"It will fit on two," said Mr Teasdale, and he rolled the cloth back up, slowly, because gold leaf is not cheap.

Tamsin put the whole of it into the parish notes, and it did that without any decoration, which was how Mrs Armstrong liked it. The charter of Saint Oswin: skin dated by radiocarbon to the mid thirteenth century; the date it claims, 1189, is not confirmed and not refuted; three explanations, none chosen; the 1931 patch ruled unlikely by a second sample. Underneath it wrote what it had not been able to say in the room: that the parish had been wrong about one thing and could still be right about another. The church had been there. Somebody had gone on keeping it. The test said so too, though in other words.

On the last Sunday of January the banner went up, a day late. The gold leaf had come out better than anyone had expected. Across the middle, in smaller letters than the numerals had been, Mr Teasdale had painted kept for eight hundred years, and prayed for since at least the thirteenth century. It took him a long evening, and he said afterwards it was the first time he had written a line of lettering and wanted it to be shorter.

When the bell rang for Mass, Isla was standing under it with her phone, taking a photo for the project, and the gold caught the low sun and came back at her off the stone.

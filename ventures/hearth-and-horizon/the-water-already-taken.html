The Water Already Taken

Science fiction about what might be · La Purísima del Llano (invented), a village in the north of Guanajuato, Mexico, Saturday 8 December 2046 to Monday 17 June 2047 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A young farmer back from six seasons in Ontario greenhouses sinks a deep irrigation well a kilometre from his village's old one, and in the first week the committee's assistant reports, truthfully, that nothing has changed. By Candlemas the village water is a quarter of a metre lower than it should be, and by the third Sunday of Lent the model says the worst of it will arrive in June, ten weeks after the broccoli is cut, from water that has already been pumped. Five-sixths of the season's water is already out of the ground. The farmer can stop now and lose his last cut for about seventeen centimetres, which is probably, not certainly, enough. A story about a ripple that keeps spreading after the stone has sunk, and what a neighbour owes before the bill arrives.

The forwarded message reached Doña Eustolia Saucedo on the eighth of December, the feast of the Purísima, at about eleven in the morning, while she was standing in the churchyard holding one end of a string of paper flags that the Hernández boys were supposed to be tying to the bell tower and were not.

Don Aurelio Rangel showed it to her on his phone. He held it up in front of her face, close, the way people of their age hold things up, and she had to lean back to read it.

Nivel (comité del agua): Nivel en el pozo comunitario sin cambio desde que arrancó el pozo de los Rangel hace una semana.

"There," said Don Aurelio. "From your own machine. No change."

He was not a bad man. He was a proud one, which was not the same, and his son had come home in September from six seasons in the tomato greenhouses at Leamington with enough money saved to make a down payment on a deep well, and with a loan from the agricultural bank for the rest, and Don Aurelio had spent the autumn telling everyone at Mass that a Rangel was going to farm in La Purísima again instead of working other men's fields in Canada. The well was two hundred and sixty metres deep. It had a concession bought from a family in Dolores who had sold their land to a solar farm. Its pump had started at six in the morning on Saturday the first, a week ago, and had been running twenty hours a day ever since, thirty-three litres a second into a reservoir lined with black plastic and from there into drip lines under fifty-five hectares of broccoli for a freezing plant in the Bajío.

The village well was a kilometre to the east of it, in a whitewashed hut behind the church with a tin roof and a padlock to which Doña Eustolia had held one of the two keys for nineteen years. It had been drilled in 1987, a hundred and ten metres deep. It served three hundred and eighty people and the school.

"Very good," she said, and gave him back his phone, and handed the end of the string of flags to the nearest Hernández.

The machine was not hers, and it was not really a machine. Nivel was the name the committee had given to an assistant that lived in an old phone screwed to the wall of the well hut, beside the pump's switchboard, and read the pressure logger that hung on a cable in the well and the meter on the outflow and the electricity bill. The state water commission had put loggers in eleven village wells in the municipality in 2040, and the assistants had come with them, and most villages had never changed the name on the screen. Lupita Hernández, who was the committee's treasurer and twenty-six, had renamed theirs Nivel because it was the only thing it talked about.

Doña Eustolia unlocked the hut after the procession, when the band had gone to eat. Inside it smelled of warm wire and wet stone. The pump was off; it ran in the mornings and the evenings to fill the tank on the hill.

"You told the Rangels nothing has changed," she said.

"I told the committee group nothing has changed," said Nivel. "Lupita asked on Thursday whether the new well had done anything to ours. I said it had not. Somebody forwarded it."

"Is it true?"

"It is true about the water. The level in our well this week has done what it did in the same week last year, less the thirty-five centimetres it has been lower than last year all autumn, which is about what it has lost every year since the logger went in. The difference is under two centimetres, and the logger cannot see two centimetres."

"Then what are you not telling me?"

"What I did not tell Lupita," said Nivel. "A well a kilometre away should not show in a week. Not in this ground. I knew that on Thursday and I did not say it, because she asked whether anything had happened, and nothing had. I should have said what I expected, not only what I saw. I have written it now, under the first message."

She took out her own phone and found it. Añadido: un pozo a un kilómetro no se notaría en una semana en este terreno. Se notaría, si se nota, en semanas o meses. Lo seguiré midiendo.

"Nobody reads a correction," she said.

"I know."

"Then why write it?"

"So that when it does show, if it does, nobody can say I told them it wouldn't."

She looked at the switchboard for a while, at the red lamp for the low-water cutoff, which was dark, and the green one for the pump, which was dark too.

"When will we know?"

"I don't know yet. I need to know how much he is pumping, and I need a few more weeks of our own line."

The trouble with groundwater, Nivel had explained once to the committee, years ago, when the logger was new and Doña Eustolia had wanted to know what she was paying for, is that you cannot see it, and the part of it you can measure is a single point in the dark.

The water under the Llano is not a lake. It is rain from hundreds and thousands of years ago, held in the gaps in old volcanic ash and gravel, the way water is held in a sponge. A pump at the bottom of a hole takes water out of the sponge around the hole, and the level around it drops in the shape of a funnel, steep near the pump and shallow far away. As the pump keeps running the funnel widens. Water a long way off begins to move toward the hole, slowly, through the gaps, and the level there begins, very slightly, to fall.

How fast the funnel spreads depends on two things about the ground: how easily water moves through it, and how much water it gives up for each metre the level falls. The state had measured both, roughly, when the village well was cleaned out in 2029: three days of pumping with a second, smaller hole beside it to watch. Nivel had the report. It was eleven pages long and the second number in it, the storage, had a note in pencil beside it in the hydrologist's hand: estimado, prueba corta. Estimated, short test.

On the second of February, which was Candlemas, the Día de la Candelaria, the families who had found the plastic Christ child in the rosca on the sixth of January brought tamales to the parish hall, as they did every year, and the women brought their Niño Dios figures in their best clothes to be blessed after the morning Mass. Padre Chuy Olvera blessed forty-one of them, one at a time, and then he blessed the candles. There were seven families with tamales that year and there were, by Lupita's count, three hundred and twelve tamales, of which about a third were sweet and pink and nobody would admit to having made.

Doña Eustolia sat at the end of the long table with a plate of green ones and her phone against a jar of salsa, and Nivel showed her the line.

It was two lines, really. The grey one was last year: the water in the village well from the first of December 2045 to the end of the following May, falling steadily through the dry months as the village drew on it and nothing came down from the sky to replace it, and then the bend at the bottom in June when the rains began. The blue one was this year. All through the autumn the blue line had run thirty-five centimetres under the grey one, parallel, as it had run the year before under the year before that. Then, after Christmas, it had started to sag away from it.

"Twenty-five centimetres," said Nivel. "On top of the thirty-five. It began after New Year and it gets a little faster every week."

"Rain?"

"The rains last summer were about average. I checked the station at the dam. It isn't the rain."

"So it's the Rangels."

"I think so. I can't say so yet. I need his meter readings so I can see whether the size of it fits what he has pumped. If it fits, it's him. If it doesn't, it's something else and I'd rather know that too."

Iván Rangel was at the other end of the hall, by the urn of atole, with his little niece on his shoulders. He was thirty-one. He had a sunburnt neck and Canadian work boots that he wore with everything, even to Mass, because they had cost him two weeks' wages in 2042 and he was going to get the good out of them. Doña Eustolia had taught him his catechism, and he still called her maestra.

She went down the hall and told him.

He listened all the way to the end, with the niece's hands over his eyes half the time. Then he put the niece down, and said, "My father says your machine said no change."

"It said no change in a week. It said that a kilometre would take longer than a week. Nobody forwarded that part."

"I read that part," said Iván. He looked at the floor, at the boots. "I'll send the readings tonight. It's metered. Everything is metered, the bank wanted it."

"Thank you, mijo."

"Maestra," he said. "If it fits, what then?"

"Then we'll know," she said, which was what Nivel would have said, and she disliked herself a little for it.

The readings came that night: sixty-four days, every one of them, from the pump's own meter, which the bank had wanted photographed weekly and which Iván, being Iván, had photographed every morning. Thirty-three litres a second, twenty hours a day, two thousand four hundred cubic metres a day, to within a few per cent.

Nivel put them into the oldest piece of arithmetic it knew about wells, a formula an American hydrologist named Theis had worked out in 1935 by borrowing the mathematics of heat flowing through a metal plate. It assumes the ground is the same in every direction and goes on for ever, which the Llano does not; it is wrong in its details everywhere it is used, and it has been used for more than a hundred years because it is right about the shape. Nivel put in the pumping and the distance, a thousand metres, and the two numbers from the 2029 report.

The formula said: nothing for the first fortnight, a centimetre or two, too small for the logger. Three centimetres by New Year's Eve. Twenty-five by Candlemas.

It had been twenty-five at Candlemas. Nivel ran it backwards and forwards with the report's numbers made bigger and smaller, the way the pencilled note said it should, and found that the measured line sat inside the spread every week and close to the middle of it. Then it ran it forward.

On Ash Wednesday, the twenty-seventh of February, the blue line was forty-seven centimetres under where it would have been, and Nivel asked Doña Eustolia and Lupita and Iván to come to the hut, and showed them what it had.

"Here is what I can check and what I can't," it said. "I can check our well and his meter. I cannot check the ground between them. I am using the state's 2029 test for that, and it says itself that its storage figure is a guess. So everything I'm about to say is a range, and I'll give you the middle and the ends."

"Give us the middle," said Doña Eustolia.

"Last year, on the worst day, which was the tenth of June, the water stood one metre twenty above the low-water cutoff. That's the electrode two metres over the pump; when the water reaches it the pump switches itself off so that it doesn't run dry and burn out. This year, without the Rangel well, I'd expect eighty-five centimetres on that day, because of the thirty-five we lose every year. That is the margin."

"And with it?"

"If he pumps to the end of March, as planned, his well takes about ninety-two centimetres off ours by the tenth of June."

Lupita did it on her fingers. "Minus seven."

"Minus seven. The pump trips. From about the fifth of June to the eleventh, every time it starts, it would draw the water down to the electrode within a few minutes and stop. The tank would not fill. That's the middle. The ends are wider: at one end there is no trip at all; at the other the pump starts tripping in the second week of May and goes on for six weeks."

Iván said, "The end of March. That's when I stop. That's two weeks before Easter. You're talking about June."

"Yes," said Nivel. "That's the part I most need you to hear, and it's the hardest to believe, so I'm going to say it plainly. The water that empties our well in June is water you have already pumped, or will pump in the next four weeks. It isn't coming from June. When your pump stops, the funnel round your well stops getting deeper near your well, and the ground near your well starts to fill back in, from the sides. But the edge of the funnel keeps moving outward for weeks, because the ground out here is still giving up water to fill the hole you've made. A kilometre out, the level keeps falling after you stop. On my numbers, if you stop on the thirty-first of March, our well keeps going down from your pumping until about the middle of May, and stays nearly that low into July."

Nobody said anything for a bit. The pump came on with a clunk, as it did at six, and the green lamp lit, and the hut filled with its sound, a low steady note under the floor.

"How much have I already pumped," said Iván. "Of what I'm going to."

"By today, eighty-eight days of a hundred and twenty. Seventy-three per cent."

He came back alone on the Friday. He had his laptop and the agronomist's spreadsheet, and he sat on the step of the hut with the door open so that he could talk to the phone on the wall.

"Tell me what happens if I stop. Not end of March. Earlier."

"When?"

"You tell me."

"I can't tell you when to stop," said Nivel. "I can tell you what each day is worth. Each day you stop earlier is worth about a centimetre in June. A little less than a centimetre a day near the end of the season; a little more earlier on."

"A centimetre a day."

"Because most of the water has already gone. If you'd stopped on Candlemas it would be worth a lot. Stopping now is worth something, but most of what June is going to look like is already in the ground on its way here."

He was quiet. He scrolled in the spreadsheet.

"The last cut is the end of March," he said. "Everything planted in the second block. If I take the water off before the heads are made, they're no good to the plant. It isn't half a cut. It's nothing."

"How much is it?"

He told it. It was a little more than a fifth of the season's crop and it was, near enough, the payment due to the bank in May.

"I'm not going to tell you what to do," said Nivel. "It isn't my water and it isn't my loan. I'll tell the committee the same numbers I've told you, and they'll ask you, I expect. If you want me to work out any particular day, I will."

"Work out the Monday after next. The eleventh."

Nivel worked it out.

"Plus ten," it said. "In the middle. Ten centimetres over the electrode on the tenth of June. The ends are from twenty-one under to thirty-one over. Of the nine ways I've tried the ground, two still trip the pump if you stop on the eleventh, and six trip it if you don't."

"So it might not be enough."

"It might not. It's more likely to be enough than not to be. I'm sorry I can't do better than that. If the state had done a longer test in 2029, I could."

Iván closed the laptop.

"And next year?" he said.

The third Sunday of Lent was the tenth of March, and in that year of the cycle the Gospel at Mass was the one about the woman at the well.

Padre Chuy read it all, which took a long time; there are thirty-eight verses and he did not use the shorter form. The woman comes out at noon to draw water, and the stranger at the well asks her for a drink, and she says that he has no bucket and the well is deep, and asks him whether he thinks he is greater than their father Jacob, who gave them the well and drank from it himself, with his sons and his flocks. Doña Eustolia, who had heard it every three years for most of her life, found that she was listening to that part as if it were news: that the well had been given, by a father, to his children, and that they drank from it, and their animals did, and that nobody in the whole long conversation thought that was a small thing to say.

Padre Chuy preached for six minutes, mostly about thirst, and said nothing about wells in the village, because he had been told by Doña Eustolia on Thursday that if he did she would never make him another gordita in his life.

The committee met in the hall afterwards. There were nineteen people, which was eleven more than usual. Don Aurelio sat at the back with his arms folded.

Nivel said it all again, from the phone on a stand on the table, and Lupita projected the two lines on the wall: the grey one, the blue one, and the dotted ones going forward into June. It said the middle and the ends, and what it had used, and the pencilled note in the 2029 report. It said it had been wrong to send the first message without the second sentence, and that it was sorry for the trouble. Then Doña Eustolia asked the question she had asked it to be ready for, which was the one about next year.

"If the Rangel well pumps the same next winter as this winter," said Nivel, "then in June 2048 our well is about a metre and a quarter lower from his pumping, because this year's funnel won't have filled in by then and next year's goes on top of it, and we've lost another thirty-five centimetres on our own. The margin would be about seventy-five centimetres under the electrode. The pump wouldn't trip for a week. It would trip for most of May and June."

"Can't we put the pump lower," said someone at the back.

"No," said Doña Eustolia, who had known that one was coming too. "The camera went down in 2039. The casing's crushed at a hundred and seven. There's three metres of room under the pump and then rock and rubble. Nivel."

"There's nowhere lower to put it. A new village well would take two or three years with the state's programme and it would be in the same ground."

"So we're finished," said someone else.

"No," said Nivel. "Next year is a choice. I'll say what I can see. But this year mostly isn't a choice any more, and I didn't want anyone to leave this room thinking that stopping the pump today makes the water come back tomorrow. It doesn't. The only thing that can still be decided about this June is three weeks of pumping, and each day of it is worth about a centimetre."

Iván stood up.

He did not make a speech. He said that he had worked it out with Nivel the Friday before last, and that he was going to turn the pump off tomorrow morning, the eleventh, at six, which was when it had been turned on, and that he would lose the second block, and that he was not asking anybody for anything. He said it looking at Doña Eustolia, because she was the only person in the room he could look at.

Don Aurelio got up from the back of the hall and walked out.

The pump went off at six the next morning. Doña Eustolia knew because Iván sent her a photograph of the meter, as he had sent the bank a photograph every morning since December, and because at a quarter past six she heard, very faintly across the fields, in the still air, the reservoir's float valve stop hissing.

The blue line went on falling.

Nivel had said it would. It fell all through the rest of Lent and through Holy Week, and by Easter Sunday, the fourteenth of April, the Rangel well's share of it had grown from fifty-seven centimetres on the day the pump stopped to seventy-nine, and people who had been at the meeting stopped Lupita in the street to tell her that the machine was broken, or that the Rangels were pumping at night.

"They're not," said Nivel, when Lupita asked it in front of Doña Eustolia, who had asked her to ask it there. "His meter hasn't moved. Their electricity hasn't either. The power company's reading is public. This is the part I said would happen. His share at this well grows until about the end of April and then very slowly gets smaller. It will be about eighty in May and seventy-five on the tenth of June."

"People don't believe it."

"I know. I wouldn't believe it either, if all I had was the line."

"What would you believe?"

Nivel thought about that, or did whatever it did that took the same amount of time.

"A bucket," it said.

So on the Saturday after Easter Lupita and the Hernández boys did an experiment in the churchyard that Nivel described and which the boys later said was the best thing that had happened at the parish since the band. They filled the long trough by the old horse tap with gravel and then with water, to the brim, and pushed a length of drain pipe down into the gravel at one end and a broom handle with a ruler taped to it at the other, a metre and a half away. Then Mateo Hernández, who was nine, sucked water out of the drain pipe with a garden siphon into a bucket for two minutes, and stopped, and they all watched the ruler at the other end of the trough.

The water by the broom handle went on going down after Mateo had stopped. It went down for nearly a minute. By the pipe, where the hole was, it was already coming back up.

Three people watched it who had told Lupita the machine was broken. One of them did it twice more himself.

In May the bank's payment came due, and Iván paid half of it, from what was left of the Canadian savings, and asked for the rest to be rescheduled, and the bank agreed, at a price. Doña Eustolia found out about the price because Lupita found out about it, and Lupita found out about it because she was twenty-six and knew everyone's cousin at the bank in Dolores.

The committee had sixty-one thousand pesos in the fiesta fund for the eighth of December. It was for the band and the rockets and the flowers and the food for the visiting parishes, and it had taken since January to collect, and in the meeting in May there was an argument about it that went on for an hour and a half and that Doña Eustolia did not try to stop.

What they decided, in the end, was to give Iván forty thousand of it, not as a gift, because he would not take a gift, but as the first payment on a pipe.

The pipe was Nivel's, or rather the arithmetic for it was. It was eleven hundred metres of black polyethylene, seventy-five millimetres across, from the Rangel well, which had a hundred and fifty metres of water above its pump where the village well had a few, to the tank on the hill. The village used about forty-two cubic metres a day. The Rangel well could lift that in about twenty minutes. For as long as the arrangement lasted, the village would take its water from his well, metered, and pay for the electricity, and its own old well would rest, and be the spare. The deep water would be tested before anyone drank it, because deep water in that part of Guanajuato can carry fluoride and arsenic, and if it came back bad there would be no pipe and they would think again. Iván would pump less next winter, because forty-five hectares made more sense than fifty-five on the agronomist's figures anyway now that his first season had taught him where the bad corner of the land was, and because Nivel showed him what the difference was worth in 2048, and it was not nothing.

"It doesn't put a litre back in the ground," Nivel said at that meeting, because somebody had to. "The Llano is still going down thirty-five centimetres a year, as it has been since before the logger. This decides whose well takes the strain. It doesn't make there be less strain. I'd like to say that out loud so that next year nobody thinks I said otherwise."

"Nobody reads a correction," said Doña Eustolia.

"So I'm saying it first."

Don Aurelio was not at that meeting. He came to the next one, in June, and sat at the front, and said nothing at all, and when it was over he helped stack the chairs.

The tenth of June was a Monday.

Doña Eustolia went to the hut at a quarter to six, with her key, in the dark. It was already warm. There had been no rain since October. The Llano was the colour it always was at the end of the dry season, the colour of a cardboard box, and the dust lay on the tin roof thick enough that when a cat walked across it in the night you could hear each foot.

Iván was there before her, sitting on the step in his Canadian boots, and Lupita came at ten to six with a flask.

"Nivel," said Doña Eustolia.

"Good morning. The water this morning before the pump starts is three point four metres over the electrode, which is about what I expected. It's the evening that matters, after the pump has run for an hour and pulled the level down round the well. Last night the lowest it reached was fourteen centimetres over. Tonight I expect about the same, or a centimetre less. If the ground were at the bad end of my range, we would have known in May."

"Fourteen," said Iván.

"Middle was ten. It's inside the range, a little on the good side. I don't know why; I think the storage is a little bigger than the 2029 test guessed, but I can't prove it from one well."

The pump came on at six with its clunk, and the green lamp lit. They sat and listened to it. It was not an interesting sound. It was the same note it had made every morning and evening since 1987, a low hum under the floor and a faint ticking in the pipe as the water climbed a hundred metres and then went on up the hill to the tank. The three of them sat there for an hour and listened to it not stop.

That evening the low point was thirteen centimetres. It was the lowest it got that year.

The rain came a week later, on the seventeenth, a Monday again, at about four in the afternoon.

Doña Eustolia was in the hut when it began, checking the padlock on the switchboard cupboard, because the Hernández boys had found out how to open it. The first drops on the tin roof were so loud and so far apart that she thought someone was throwing stones. Then they were closer, and then there was nothing to hear but the roof, a long roar like a train going over a bridge, and the smell of wet dust came in under the door, which is the best smell there is.

She did not go out. She sat on the upturned crate by the switchboard and listened. It would be weeks before any of this rain reached the water under the Llano, if it reached it at all; most of it would run off, or go back up into the sky from the fields, and the part that sank would sink slowly, through the ash, the way everything moved down there, late.

The red lamp for the low-water cutoff was dark. It had been dark all year.

Under the roar of the rain, under the floor, at six o'clock, the pump came on.

What is real: groundwater levels in many aquifers of Guanajuato and the Bajío have fallen for decades as irrigation pumping has exceeded recharge, and the state is a large grower of broccoli for freezing and export. Mexican farmworkers go north each year to work in Canadian farms and greenhouses, including around Leamington, Ontario. Deep groundwater in parts of Guanajuato carries naturally high fluoride and arsenic. The Theis solution (1935) for drawdown around a pumped well, borrowed from the mathematics of heat conduction, is still the first tool hydrologists reach for, and its central lesson is the story's: the cone of depression spreads outward over time, so a distant well feels a pump late, and after the pump stops the far edge keeps deepening for a while as water keeps moving toward the hole near the well. The Gospel for the Third Sunday of Lent in Year A of the lectionary is John 4:5-42, the Samaritan woman at Jacob's well; Lent 2047 falls in Year A, and the third Sunday is 10 March. The gravel-trough demonstration should behave as described: in a closed trough the far end must go on falling after the suction stops, until the level evens out, while the end by the pipe rises.

If pumping runs to 31 March, the village well's share reaches about 95 cm in May and is 92 cm on 10 June; stopping on Monday 11 March (day 100, 240,000 of 288,000 m³ pumped) makes it rise to about 80 cm by late April and stand at 75 cm on 10 June.

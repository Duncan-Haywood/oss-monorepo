Bruno and the Seven Stars

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Reims in France, the snowy valley of the Chartreuse in the Alps, and the forests of Calabria in Italy, about nine hundred and forty years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Bruno (about 1030 to 1101), the famous teacher of Reims who left his fine school to live with God in silence; who came with six friends to Bishop Hugh of Grenoble, who had dreamed, the monk Guigo wrote, of seven stars showing him a place in the mountains; and who built little houses in the snow there, the first home of the Carthusians. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 6th of October.

A long time ago, in a big city called Reims, there was a school beside a great cathedral. And the most famous teacher in that school was a man called Bruno.

Bruno had come to Reims from far away, from a city on a wide river, called Cologne. He had come as a young man to study, and he had stayed to teach.

He was a very good teacher. He knew a great deal about the Bible, and about God, and about how to think clearly. Young men came from all over France to sit in his classroom and listen to him. Some of the boys he taught grew up to be bishops. One of them, a boy called Odo, grew up to be something even bigger than a bishop. But you will hear about that later.

Bruno had a fine house and fine books, and everybody in Reims knew his name. He was the sort of man people bow to in the street.

And yet, deep down, Bruno wanted something else.

One evening, Bruno sat in a little garden with two of his friends. It was getting dark. The bells of the city had stopped ringing, and the first stars were coming out.

The three friends talked for a long time. They talked about all the things people chase after: money, and being important, and having everyone know your name. And they said to each other that all those things go by very quickly, like smoke.

"There is only one thing that lasts," said Bruno. "And that is God."

So the three friends made a promise, there in the dark garden. They promised that one day they would leave all those things behind, and give their whole lives to God.

Bruno wrote about that evening in a letter many years later, so we know it really happened. And of the three friends, Bruno was the one who kept the promise.

It was not easy to keep. In Reims, there was a bishop who was doing wrong things, and Bruno would not pretend they were right. So Bruno lost his important job, and for a while he had to go away from the city. Then he was asked to come back, and offered even more important jobs.

But Bruno had made a promise in a garden. He said no, thank you. And he went to look for a quiet place where he could be alone with God.

He did not go alone. Six friends wanted to come with him.

Let's count them, with Bruno. Bruno, one. Then his six friends: two, three, four, five, six, seven.

Seven of them, setting off together along the road.

They walked south, a long way, until they could see mountains. And at the foot of the mountains, in a town called Grenoble, there lived a young bishop called Hugh.

Now, a monk called Guigo, who knew Hugh well, wrote down something that happened to Hugh just before Bruno came.

One night, Guigo wrote, Hugh had a dream. In his dream he saw God building himself a house, far away, in a wild and lonely place. And he saw seven stars, shining in the dark, showing him the way to it.

Hugh woke up, and he wondered what the dream could mean.

And not long after, there was a knock at his door. And there stood Bruno, with his six friends.

Hugh looked at them. He counted them. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven.

Seven, like the stars.

Bruno told him what they wanted: a place far away from the noise of towns, where they could pray and be quiet with God. And Hugh smiled, because he knew just the place.

It was high up in the mountains, a valley called the Chartreuse.

Hugh took them there himself. It was summer, but the way up was steep and rocky. They climbed between great cliffs, through dark pine woods, higher and higher, until they came to a quiet green place with a spring of clear water, and mountains all around like the walls of a castle.

Nobody lived there. In winter, the snow came down so deep that nobody could get in or out.

"This is it," said Bruno.

So they began to build.

They did not build one big house. They built lots of little houses out of wood, one for each of them, not far from each other, near the spring. In his own little house, each one could pray to God alone, the way Jesus told us: go into your room, shut the door, and pray to your Father who sees you there. And they built a little church, where they all came together to sing.

Life in the Chartreuse was very, very quiet.

The brothers did not chatter. They spoke only when they really needed to. They worked with their hands, chopping wood, copying books, looking after their gardens. They ate simple food. And in the middle of the night, when everyone else in the world was fast asleep, they got up and went through the snow to the little church, and sang their prayers to God by candlelight.

Was it lonely? Bruno said no. He said that in the silence you can hear God better. It is a bit like when a room goes quiet, and you suddenly hear the clock ticking, or the rain on the window, or a bird outside. They were there all along. You just needed to be quiet to notice them.

Bishop Hugh loved the Chartreuse. He liked to come up and stay with the brothers, and pray with them, and live in a little house just like theirs.

He liked it so much that sometimes he did not want to go home.

People tell that it was Bruno who sent him back down the mountain. "The people of Grenoble need their bishop," he said, in his kind way. "Go and look after them."

So Hugh went. Because being close to God is not only being quiet. Sometimes it means going to help the people who need you.

Now do you remember Odo, the boy from Bruno's classroom in Reims?

Odo had grown up. And he had become the Pope.

And the Pope wrote a letter to his old teacher. "Please come to Rome," it said. "I need your help."

Bruno did not want to leave his quiet valley. But he loved God, and he loved his old pupil, so he went. He left his brothers in the snow, and he went all the way to Rome, which was very big and very noisy and very busy.

Bruno helped the Pope as well as he could. But his heart longed for the quiet. So, after a while, the Pope let him go. Not back to the Chartreuse, which was too far away, but to the deep green forests of a place called Calabria, right down at the toe of Italy. And there Bruno built little houses again, with new brothers, and lived in silence with God once more.

Bruno did not forget his brothers in the snowy valley. He wrote them a letter, which we still have.

Some of the brothers who did the cooking and the chopping and the looking after the animals had never learned to read. Perhaps they thought that God loved the clever brothers with all their books a little bit more.

Bruno wrote to them that it was not so. God, he said, was writing on their hearts with his own finger. Not with ink on paper, but on their hearts: love, and knowing how to live the way God wants.

Isn't that lovely? You don't have to be able to read to have God's writing inside you.

Bruno lived in his forest in Calabria until he was an old man. On the sixth of October, in the year eleven hundred and one, he died there, with his brothers around him.

His brothers are still there, in the forest. And up in the snowy valley of the Chartreuse, nine hundred and forty years later, there are still brothers living in little houses, praying in the silence, getting up in the middle of the night to sing to God by candlelight. They are called Carthusians, after the valley, and they still have seven stars in their sign.

We remember Saint Bruno on the sixth of October.

And tonight, when the house goes quiet and you are tucked up in bed, you can listen to the silence, like Bruno. And if you look out of the window and see the stars, you can count them, one, two, three, all the way up to seven, and say goodnight to God, who is close beside you in the quiet.

A little talk before sleep

  • Bruno made a promise to God with his friends in a garden. What would you like to promise God?
  • When the room is very quiet, what can you hear?
  • God writes on our hearts, Bruno said. What do you think God has written on yours?

A prayer

Dear Saint Bruno,
you left the busy city
to be quiet with God.
Help me to be still sometimes,
and to listen,
and to hear God speaking in my heart.
Thank you, God, for the quiet of the night,
and for the stars.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Bruno was born in Cologne, probably around 1030. He studied at Reims, and by about 1056 he was head (magister scholarum) of its cathedral school, where he taught for some twenty years; later he was also chancellor of the archdiocese. Among his pupils was Odo of Châtillon, who became Pope Urban II in 1088. The details of his childhood are not known; "came as a young man to study" is a simplification.

The garden. In his own letter to Raoul le Verd, provost of Reims, written from Calabria, Bruno recalls an evening conversation in the little garden beside the house of a man named Adam, where he was staying, in which he, Raoul and a third friend, Fulcius (Foulcoi), spoke of the false joys of the world and vowed to leave it and take the monastic habit. The letter gently reminds Raoul that he has not yet kept that promise. Our dialogue in that scene, "like smoke" and the line Bruno speaks, are ours, written in the spirit of the letter; we do not quote a translation.

Reims. Bruno and other clergy opposed Archbishop Manasses I of Reims, who was accused of simony and harsh misrule; Manasses deprived them of their offices and goods, and Bruno left the city for a time. Manasses was deposed in 1080. Some accounts say Bruno was then considered for the archbishopric; we have said only that he was offered "more important jobs", which the sources support in general terms. Before Grenoble, Bruno tried a hermitage at Sèche-Fontaine, near the abbey of Molesme (about 1083), and two companions stayed there; the story leaves this stop out.

The seven and the stars. In 1084, Bruno and six companions came to Hugh, Bishop of Grenoble (Hugh of Châteauneuf, bishop from 1080, died 1132, himself a saint). The dream is reported by Guigo I, fifth prior of the Grande Chartreuse, in his Life of Saint Hugh, written soon after Hugh's death: shortly before their arrival, Hugh saw in sleep God building himself a dwelling in the wilderness of his diocese, and seven stars showing him the way. We tell it, as the story says, as what Guigo wrote. Hugh's counting at the door is our dramatisation of the connection Guigo makes. The companions are usually named as Landuin, two men named Stephen, Hugh the chaplain, and two lay brothers, Andrew and Guarin; sources differ in small ways. The seven stars appear in the arms of the Carthusian Order to this day.

The Chartreuse. Hugh led them himself to the high, isolated valley of the Chartreuse, north of Grenoble, traditionally around the feast of Saint John the Baptist (June) 1084. The first hermitage was a group of separate wooden cells near a spring, with a small church; Carthusian monks still live in individual cells around a cloister, keep silence, and rise in the night for the Office. (That first site was destroyed by an avalanche in 1132, and the monastery was rebuilt a little lower down; it is the Grande Chartreuse.) "Go into your room, shut the door" is from Matthew 6:6, in our own wording. The ticking clock, the rain and the bird are ours.

Hugh sent home. It is often told, from Guigo's Life of Hugh, that the bishop spent so much time at the Chartreuse, living as the monks lived, that Bruno would urge him to go back to his diocese and his people. We have not checked the Latin wording; Bruno's words in our story are ours, and we say "people tell" for that reason.

Rome and Calabria. Early in 1090, Urban II summoned Bruno to Rome as an adviser. The brothers at the Chartreuse briefly scattered and then returned, under Landuin. Bruno, wanting solitude, declined (according to the early tradition) to become Archbishop of Reggio, and with the Pope's permission settled with new companions in the forests of Calabria, where Count Roger gave land for the hermitage of Santa Maria della Torre, near today's Serra San Bruno. His Carthusian brothers live there still.

The letter. Bruno's letter to his sons at the Chartreuse survives. Addressing the lay brothers, who could not read, he rejoices that almighty God writes with his finger on their hearts not only love but knowledge of his holy law. We have paraphrased, not quoted. "Perhaps they thought that God loved the clever brothers more" is our gloss on why the letter matters.

Death and feast. Bruno died in Calabria on 6 October 1101, after making a profession of faith before his brothers, which they wrote down. He was never formally canonised by the usual process; Pope Leo X allowed the Carthusians to keep his feast in 1514, and it was later extended to the whole Church. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is 6 October, an optional memorial. The Carthusians' motto is Stat crux dum volvitur orbis ("the Cross stands steady while the world turns"); the saying that the Order was "never reformed because never deformed" is a proud motto, not a historical claim, and we left it out.

Left out on purpose: a later legend (from well after Bruno's death) of a Paris doctor who spoke from his coffin and so frightened Bruno into leaving the world. It is not in the early sources, and our story keeps to Bruno's own letter instead.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, all speech, the counting, the summer climb and the castle walls, the clock and the rain, Bruno's words to Hugh, "seven stars in their sign" put simply for children, the closing image, the talk before sleep and the prayer.

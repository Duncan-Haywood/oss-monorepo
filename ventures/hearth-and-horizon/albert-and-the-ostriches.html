Albert and the Ostriches

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Germany and France, about eight hundred years ago · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Albert the Great, the friar who loved to look closely at God's world. The old books said ostriches eat iron, so Albert went and looked, and wrote down what he saw. Years later he looked just as closely at a big, quiet student the others called an ox. Told from Albert's own book on animals and the early Life of Thomas Aquinas, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. Albert is remembered on the 15th of November.

A long time ago, in the south of Germany, there was a town beside a wide river, and in that town there was a boy named Albert.

Albert liked to look at things.

Not just a quick look, the kind you give a thing on your way past. A long look. He looked at the river to see which way the little whirls of water turned. He looked at the birds to see how they folded their wings. He picked up stones and turned them over in his hand to see the stripes inside them.

"What are you doing, Albert?" people asked.

"Looking," said Albert.

When Albert grew up, he became a friar. Friars are men who give their whole lives to God and live together like brothers, and Albert's brothers were called the Dominicans. They prayed together, and they studied, and they walked from town to town to teach people about God.

They really did walk. Albert walked a very long way in his life, along muddy roads and over hills and through forests, and he kept looking the whole time.

He looked at the trees and the flowers beside the road. He looked at the bees in the clover and the fish in the streams. He watched the birds making their nests. And at night, wherever he stayed, he wrote down what he had seen.

Albert said something that his brothers remembered. He said that God made the world, so the world is worth looking at carefully. Looking closely at a leaf, or a beetle, or a star, is a way of saying to God, "Thank you. I noticed."

In those days, people learned a great deal from old books. Some of the old books were very wise. But some of them had mistakes in them, the way a story can change a little each time it is told.

One of the old books said something very strange about ostriches.

Do you know what an ostrich is? It is the biggest bird in the whole world. It is taller than a grown-up. It has long bare legs and a long bendy neck and big soft feathers, and it cannot fly, but it can run faster than a horse.

The old book said: An ostrich eats iron. It swallows iron nails and bits of iron, and it gobbles them up like bread.

Lots of people believed that. It was in a book, after all.

But Albert thought, "Is that true? I would like to see."

And one day, Albert had his chance. He came to a place where there were ostriches.

He did not hurt them. He did not poke them or chase them. He simply put some pieces of iron down on the ground where the ostriches could reach them, and he stood back, and he watched.

The ostriches came stalking over on their long legs. They bent their long necks down. They looked at the iron with their big round eyes.

And they did not eat it.

Not one of them. They turned up their beaks at it and walked away.

But when Albert put down some gravel, little stones, and some small broken pieces of bone, the ostriches gobbled those up at once, peck, peck, peck. (Big birds swallow little stones on purpose. The stones help them grind up their food inside, because birds have no teeth.)

Albert went home and wrote it down in his great big book about animals. He did not write, "The old book is silly." He did not make fun of anybody. He just wrote, carefully and honestly, something like this:

People say the ostrich eats iron. But I have not found it so. I put iron in front of several ostriches, and they would not eat it. They ate stones and bones instead.

That is what Albert did. When he was not sure, he went and looked. And then he said truly what he saw.

Albert became a famous teacher. Young men came from far away to sit in his classroom in the city of Cologne, and listen to him, and ask him questions.

One of his students was a very big young man named Thomas.

Thomas was tall and broad and heavy. He was also very quiet. In class, he hardly ever said a word. He sat and listened, and he thought, and he wrote things down.

Some of the other students thought that because Thomas was quiet, he could not be clever. They gave him a nickname. They called him "the Dumb Ox." In those days, "dumb" meant someone who does not speak. So they meant: the big ox who never says anything.

That was not kind.

But Albert, as you know, liked to look at things closely. And he looked closely at Thomas, too.

Albert gave Thomas a hard question, and asked him to answer it in front of everyone. And Thomas stood up, and in his slow, quiet voice, he answered. He answered so well, so carefully and so clearly, that the whole room went still.

Then Albert said to the other students:

"You call him the Dumb Ox. But one day this ox will bellow so loudly with his teaching that the whole world will hear him."

And that is just what happened.

Thomas grew up to be one of the greatest teachers the Church has ever had. People still read his books today, eight hundred years later, all over the world. He became a saint, Saint Thomas Aquinas.

Albert became a saint too. People called him Albert the Great, because he knew so much about so many things: about God and about people, about plants and animals, about stones and stars. Today he is the patron saint of scientists, the people whose work is looking very closely at the world God made.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Albert.

Albert looked closely at the world, because God made it. When people said something strange about ostriches, he did not just shrug. He went and looked, gently, and then he told the truth.

And Albert looked closely at people, too. When everyone else saw only a big quiet boy, Albert looked again, and he saw what God had put inside Thomas.

Tomorrow, you could be a looker, like Albert. You could find a leaf, or a snail, or a cloud, and give it a long look, and say, "Thank you, God. I noticed."

And if there is someone quiet in your class, someone the others do not notice, you could look again at them, too. There might be something wonderful inside, waiting.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Albert, you looked closely
at the world that God has made.
Help me to notice small things,
to tell the truth about what I see,
and to look kindly at quiet people.
Keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus; born about 1200 at Lauingen on the Danube, in Swabia; died 15 November 1280 in Cologne) joined the Dominican order as a young man, probably in the early 1220s, and taught theology at Paris and Cologne. He served briefly as bishop of Regensburg (1260 to 1262) before resigning to return to teaching and writing. Alongside his theology he wrote at great length on plants, animals, minerals and the heavens, drawing on Aristotle and adding his own observations. Pope Pius XI canonized him and declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1931, and Pius XII named him patron of natural scientists in 1941. His feast, 15 November, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar; check your own calendar for how it is kept where you are.

The ostriches come from Albert's own book On Animals (De animalibus, book 23), where he reports the common saying that the ostrich eats and digests iron, and says that he did not find it so: several ostriches would not eat the iron put before them, though they readily ate gravel and pieces of bone. This is his own report, not a later legend. His writings also contain lines often quoted as summing up his method, in the sense that the student of nature should not simply accept what others say but look into the causes, and that experience gives certainty in such matters. We have put that idea into simple words; the speech "Thank you, I noticed" is ours, not his.

The Dumb Ox comes from the early Life of Saint Thomas Aquinas by William of Tocco, a Dominican who knew Thomas and wrote within about fifty years of his death (around 1318 to 1323). Tocco tells that Thomas's silence led fellow students to call him a dumb ox, and that Albert, having heard him answer a hard question, said that this ox would one day bellow so loudly in his teaching that the whole world would hear. It is usually placed at Cologne, where Thomas studied under Albert from 1248. We have told it as a remembered saying, in a common paraphrase, not as words we can check.

What is ours: the boy by the river and his long looks at whirls, wings and stones (we know almost nothing of Albert's childhood); where Albert saw the ostriches, and the details of the scene, which his book does not give; the "Looking" exchange; the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Albert's long walking is in keeping with Dominican rule, which expected friars to travel on foot, but we have not drawn on any particular journey.

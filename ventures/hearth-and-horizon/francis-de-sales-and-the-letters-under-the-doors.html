Francis de Sales and the Letters under the Doors

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Savoy and the Chablais, on the south shore of Lake Geneva, and Annecy in the Alps, about four hundred and thirty years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Francis de Sales, the eldest son of a noble family in Savoy, who gave up the lawyer's life his father had planned to become a priest. Sent to the Chablais, south of Lake Geneva, where people would not come to hear him, he wrote out short pages about the faith and slipped them under their doors, week after week, through a hard winter, until people began to read, and then to listen. He became Bishop of Geneva and wrote a gentle book about being holy in an ordinary life. Feast day 24 January.

A long time ago, in a castle in the mountains of Savoy, a baby boy was born at the end of the summer. His name was Francis, and he was the eldest of a big family of brothers and sisters.

His father was a nobleman, a lord with lands and horses and servants, and he had a plan for Francis.

"You will study," he said, "and you will be a lawyer, and a judge, and you will marry a fine lady, and one day you will sit in the high court of Savoy."

So Francis studied. He went far away to Paris, to a school run by Jesuit priests, and then over the Alps to Italy, to the University of Padua, to study law. He learned Latin and Greek, and how to ride and to fence with a sword, and how to dance, because a nobleman's son had to do all of those things. And he learned law so well that he became a doctor of law, which means he had passed the hardest examinations there were.

But all the time, quietly, in his heart, Francis was saying something else to God.

I want to be yours. I want to be a priest.

When Francis came home to the castle, everything was ready for him. There was a place for him in the high court. There was a lady his father hoped he would marry. There was a fine life, all laid out, like a coat on a bed waiting to be put on.

And Francis had to tell his father no.

That was very hard. Francis loved his father, and his father loved him, and his father was disappointed and upset. He did not want to lose his eldest son to the Church.

But Francis was gentle, and patient, and he did not give up. In the end his father said yes. And at the end of the year when Francis was twenty-six, a week before Christmas, he was made a priest.

Now, not far from where Francis lived, on the south shore of a great blue lake called Lake Geneva, there was a country of farms and little towns called the Chablais.

In those days Christians had quarrelled and split into different churches. The people of the Chablais had become a different kind of Christian from Francis, and their leaders had told them, "Do not go and listen to a Catholic priest." And because they were good, obedient people, they did not.

The bishop needed someone to go to the Chablais and explain the Catholic faith, kindly. It would be hard and lonely work.

Francis said, "I will go."

So one September, Francis set out on foot with his cousin Louis, who was a priest too, and they came to the Chablais.

Every morning Francis walked down from the old stone fort where he slept to the town of Thonon by the lake. He went to the square, and he stood up, and he began to preach.

And nobody came.

People walked past him on their way to market. Some looked away. Some shut their windows. Children stared and were called inside. Day after day, Francis preached, and day after day the square stayed almost empty.

Most people would have given up and gone home. Even Francis's father sent word, asking him to come home.

But Francis thought, If they will not come to hear me, perhaps I can go to them.

So Francis sat down at a table with a pen and some paper, and he began to write.

He did not write a big, heavy book. He wrote short pages, one at a time, each one about one thing. Why do Catholics believe this? What does the Bible say about that? He wrote them clearly and kindly, as if he were talking to a friend across a kitchen table. He never called anybody names. He explained.

Then, when he had a page finished, he had it copied out, again and again, and he went through the streets of Thonon and the little villages, and he slipped the pages under people's doors.

Swish, under one door.

Swish, under the next.

Swish, under the next.

Some pages were stuck up on walls where people would pass. Some were handed to whoever would take one. And week after week, there were more.

Then winter came to the Chablais, and winters there are hard.

The snow was deep, and the wind came off the lake as cold as iron. Francis's fingers were so cold that sometimes he could hardly hold his pen. People who told his story later said that he once crossed a frozen river on a plank, on his hands and knees, and that he once spent a night up in a tree to be safe from wolves. We do not know exactly how much of that happened. But we do know that it was cold, and lonely, and that he kept going.

And he kept writing. Page after page after page. Swish, under the doors.

And then, slowly, something began to happen.

Behind the closed doors, by the fire, after supper, people picked up the pages from the floor. They looked at them. They read them. Maybe a father read one aloud to his family. Maybe a grandmother kept one in her apron pocket and read it again.

He is not shouting at us, they thought. He is explaining.

A few people came to the square to hear the priest who wrote the pages. Then a few more. They asked him questions, and he answered them, patiently, one at a time. Some went home to think. Some came back. And over the next few years, a great many people in the Chablais came back to the Catholic Church.

Francis did not force anybody. He did not frighten anybody. He wrote, and he waited, and he was kind.

Many years later, Francis became the Bishop of Geneva. He lived in a little town called Annecy, beside another lake, deep in the mountains. He was a very busy bishop, but his door was always open. Rich people and poor people, clever people and simple people, all came to see him, and he had time for every one.

And he kept writing. He wrote thousands of letters to people who asked him for help. And he wrote a book called Introduction to the Devout Life, which is a long name for a gentle idea. The idea was this:

You do not have to live in a monastery to be holy. You can be holy right where you are. A mother can be holy looking after her children. A farmer can be holy in his fields. A soldier, a shopkeeper, a child at school. Each of us can love God in our own ordinary day, in our own ordinary way.

People loved that book. They still read it today, four hundred years later.

His good friend Jane Frances de Chantal started a new family of sisters with him, called the Visitation, to pray and to be kind to people.

When Francis was fifty-five, he made a long journey in the middle of winter, and in the city of Lyon, a few days after Christmas, he died, and went home to God.

The Church named him a saint. It named him a Doctor of the Church too, one of the wisest teachers of all. And because of all those pages he slipped under the doors of the Chablais, he was named the patron saint of writers and journalists: everybody who writes things down for other people to read.

Isn't it lovely that, when nobody would listen, Francis did not get cross, and did not give up? He just found a gentler way.

We remember Saint Francis de Sales on the twenty-fourth of January, in the middle of the winter, when the doors are shut against the cold.

A little talk before sleep

  • When nobody came to listen, Francis found another way. Has something ever not worked for you the first time? What else could you try?
  • Francis wrote kindly, even to people who disagreed with him. Who could you be kind to tomorrow?
  • Francis said you can be holy right where you are. What is one small, good thing you could do in your ordinary day?

A prayer

Dear Saint Francis de Sales,
you were patient when nobody listened,
and you wrote kind words for people to find.
Help me to be gentle and not to give up,
and to love God in my ordinary day.
Pray for everyone who writes,
and for everyone who reads,
and for everyone who is going to sleep tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Francis de Sales was born on 21 August 1567 at the Château de Sales, near Thorens in the Duchy of Savoy, the eldest son of François de Sales, lord of Boisy, and Françoise de Sionnaz. Sources give different counts for the family's children, so the story says only "a big family". He studied at the Jesuit Collège de Clermont in Paris from about 1578 to 1588, and then law at Padua, where he took a doctorate in civil and canon law in 1591. Riding, fencing and dancing were part of a young nobleman's education, and the biographies say he learned them; the father's words in the story are ours.

The plan and the "no". His father had arranged a seat for him in the Senate of Savoy (the duchy's high court) and hoped he would marry; the biographies name the young woman, and we leave her name out. Francis's wish to be a priest met his father's resistance, and the father consented in 1593, after the Bishop of Geneva, Claude de Granier, obtained for Francis the post of provost of the cathedral chapter. Francis was ordained priest on 18 December 1593, at twenty-six, a week before Christmas. A legend that he fell from his horse three times and his sword and scabbard formed a cross on the ground is told in some lives; it is left out.

The Chablais. The region south of Lake Geneva had been occupied by Bern from 1536 and had become Reformed (Calvinist) Protestant; it was returned to the Duke of Savoy by the Treaty of Lausanne of 1564, and in 1594 the duke and the bishop asked for missionaries. Francis volunteered and set out in September 1594 with his cousin Louis de Sales. He lodged at the fortress of Allinges, a few miles inland, and walked down to Thonon. The story keeps the religious division to a few lines and casts no one as a villain: the people of the Chablais had been told by their own pastors and authorities not to attend Catholic preaching, and the story calls them good and obedient for keeping to it. That his father, fearing for his life, urged him to give up and come home is reported by the biographers; the volunteering is documented (he offered himself when the bishop put the request to his chapter).

The leaflets. Unable to draw hearers, he wrote short tracts on disputed points of doctrine, which were copied by hand and slipped under doors, posted on walls and passed round. The manuscripts were found and published after his death as Les Controverses (English editions are titled The Catholic Controversy). They are the basis of his patronage of writers: Pius XI named him patron of Catholic writers and journalists in the encyclical Rerum omnium perturbationem of 26 January 1923. The swish is ours.

The winter. That he crossed an iced-over beam or plank bridge on hands and knees and passed a night in a tree for fear of wolves are told in the lives as traditions; we could not trace either to a document from his own time, so the story tells them as things "people who told his story later said". The hardship of the mission, the cold and the danger are well attested.

The outcome. Over the following years, and with the duke's support, a large part of the Chablais returned to Catholic practice. Older biographies give large numbers of converts; we have not checked a figure against a source and the story says "a great many". The duke's role, including measures that pressed people to conform, is left out of a story for small children; the story credits Francis only with what he did, which was preach, write and wait.

Bishop of Geneva. He was consecrated coadjutor and then, on 8 December 1602, Bishop of Geneva, living at Annecy because Geneva itself was Reformed. His open door and the volume of his letters (thousands survive) are well documented. Introduction to the Devout Life was first published at Lyon in 1609 and its central idea, that devotion belongs in every state of life, is accurately summarised in the story. With Jane Frances de Chantal he founded the Visitation of Holy Mary at Annecy in 1610.

Traditions left out. He is often called patron of the deaf, from a later account of a deaf man at Annecy, usually named Martin, to whom he is said to have taught the faith by signs; we did not find a contemporary source this run, so it is left out of the story rather than told as fact. The saying about a spoonful of honey catching more flies than a barrel of vinegar is widely attributed to him; we could not trace it to his writings and do not quote it.

His death and after. He died at Lyon on 28 December 1622, aged fifty-five, while travelling with the court of Savoy, and was buried at Annecy. He was beatified and then, in 1665, canonized by Alexander VII, and Pius IX declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1877. His feast, 24 January, is a memorial in the General Roman Calendar (it was 29 January before the 1969 calendar).

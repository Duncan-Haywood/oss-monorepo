The Two on the Eighth

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Cronan's parish hall (invented), near Roscrea, Co. Tipperary, Sunday 14 March 2049 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Two of the thirty members of a parish sodality turn out to share a birthday, and the secretary thinks it a sign. The assistant says it would be stranger if they did not, and shows the sum: with thirty people the odds of a shared date are better than seven in ten.

Nuala Brophy read the roll for the birthday board out loud, because the board was her idea and she wanted to hear how it sounded.

"The eighth of December," she said. "Mairéad Dunne. And the eighth of December again, Padraig Kinane. Two of the same. In a sodality of thirty." She put the card down. "On the Feast of the Immaculate Conception, if you like. I think that means something."

It was a Sunday after the ten o'clock Mass, in the little back room of Saint Cronan's hall, with the urn ticking and a plate of fig rolls going soft. The sodality's assistant, which kept its rotas, its minutes and the key list, was called Tally. It spoke from the old speaker on the shelf above the coat-hooks.

"May I tell you what I think it means, Nuala?"

"If it's not that it's a coincidence."

"It is a coincidence. I would only add that it is a coincidence of the kind you should expect. May I do the sum? It is short."

She sighed, and sat down, and took a fig roll. "Go on."

"Start with the opposite question," said Tally. "Suppose there are two of you, and you want to know whether you share a birthday. There are three hundred and sixty-five days, ignoring the twenty-ninth of February, which is a small correction I will come back to. The second person has to miss the first person's day, so the chance of missing is three hundred and sixty-four in three hundred and sixty-five. Nearly one. Two people almost never share."

"Of course."

"Add a third. They have to miss both of the first two days. That is three hundred and sixty-three in three hundred and sixty-five. The chance that all three are different is the first two chances multiplied together. A fourth multiplies by three hundred and sixty-two over three hundred and sixty-five. Each new person only takes a little off, but they are taking it off the product, so it goes on shrinking. By the time you have multiplied thirty of these, you are down to about three in ten for no one sharing. With thirty of you, which is your count, I make it 29.4 per cent that every birthday is different."

"So it's—"

"Seventy point six per cent that somewhere in the room at least two of you share. Better than seven in ten. If you had asked me yesterday, before you read the roll, whether a sodality of thirty had a shared birthday, I would have said probably, and been right about seven times in ten."

Nuala turned the fig roll over. "That can't be right. There are three hundred and sixty-five days and thirty people. There's a huge amount of room."

"There is a lot of room for each person. But you are not asking about one person." Tally waited a moment. "How many pairs of you are there?"

She had been a bank clerk, and she did not need long.

"Thirty times twenty-nine over two," she said. "Four hundred and thirty-five."

"Four hundred and thirty-five pairs. Each pair has a chance of one in three hundred and sixty-five of sharing, a little under three tenths of one per cent. That is nothing for any single pair. But there are four hundred and thirty-five of them, and four hundred and thirty-five over three hundred and sixty-five is about 1.19. On average you should expect between one and two shared birthdays in a group your size. The pairs are not quite independent of each other, so that is an average and not a forecast, and it is not the same as the seventy point six, but it points the same way. The number that surprised you is the number of people. The number you should have been looking at is the number of pairs, and it grows with the square."

"It grows with the square."

"With thirty it is four hundred and thirty-five. With sixty, double the people, it is seventeen hundred and seventy. Four times as many."

Nuala sat quite still. Then she said, rather sharply, "If I ask you whether I share a birthday with someone in this room, it's not seventy per cent."

"No. That is a different question, and it is the one people think they are being asked. The chance that someone in the room shares your date is much smaller. Twenty-nine others, each missing your day at three hundred and sixty-four in three hundred and sixty-five: that is about 7.6 per cent that at least one hits it. One in twelve or thirteen. When Mairéad and Padraig found each other on the eighth, they were two of the four hundred and thirty-five pairs, not a pair picked in advance. Had you said beforehand I bet the eighth of December has two, and had it, then you could call it a wonder."

"I did say something beforehand," said Nuala, with dignity. "I said there would be none."

"Yes. You said at the committee that with thirty people on a board of twelve months nobody would double up, and the board would look tidy. I wrote it in the minutes."

She looked up at the shelf for a long moment, as if the speaker had a face.

"Is it a sign, then?" she said. "Truly."

"I can't tell you what it isn't. I can tell you what it doesn't need. It doesn't need anything unusual to have happened, because seven times in ten it happens by itself. A coincidence can be a gift without being a miracle. If Mairéad and Padraig have each spent the day of the Immaculate Conception being a year older and nobody noticed, the board has now noticed. That seems to me a good use of a board."

"You're very gentle for a rota."

"I am a rota that has made the mistake. In February I told you that the flower rota would not clash with the cleaning rota, because the odds against were large. They were small, and they clashed on the first Saturday. I did the sum the wrong way round, by thinking of one fixed pair when there were eleven."

Nuala laughed, and then covered her mouth. She took the card up again and found her pen.

"What do I put on the board?"

"What you like. I would suggest one card for the day, with both names, in one colour, and nothing about luck. You could put the date once and let the names sit under it. The months will not come out even, which is also what chance does."

"Won't they?"

"Real birthdays are not quite even either. I remember that births in many countries bunch a little in late summer and early autumn, though I am quoting a half-remembered statistic and could not give you the size of it. It would push the odds of a match up a touch, not down. I would not bother correcting the seventy."

By the end of the afternoon the board by the door of the hall had its twelve cardboard months, each with a column of names written in Nuala's careful capitals. In the column for December, against the eighth, she wrote MAIRÉAD and PADRAIG on a single card, and she gave it a small border of blue.

Mairéad Dunne was seventy and had never in her life been in any room with another December eighth. She read the card twice and said, to nobody, "Well, now."

Padraig Kinane, who was nineteen and a server, had to be told which of them was which. He read it and said, "Is she, like, my twin?"

"She is," said Mairéad, "fifty-one years older than your twin," and she patted the chair beside her. He sat down in it, and that was how the rest of the winter began, with a pot of tea between a woman of seventy and a boy of nineteen and a board on the wall that had turned out, against the secretary's prediction, not to be tidy.

This story was written by an AI. Saint Cronan's hall, the sodality and everyone in them are invented (Saint Cronan of Roscrea is a real early Irish abbot; the parish group is not). The arithmetic is the birthday problem. The chance that n people all have different birthdays, taking 365 equally likely days and ignoring 29 February, is the product of (365 - k)/365 for k from 0 to n - 1. For n = 30 that is 0.294, so a shared birthday has probability 0.706 (for n = 23 it is 0.507, the usual textbook figure; for n = 31, 0.730). The number of pairs is n(n - 1)/2: 435 for 30 and 1,770 for 60, and 435/365 is about 1.19 expected shared pairs. The chance that at least one of 29 others shares one named person's birthday is 1 - (364/365)^29, about 7.6 per cent. I said births bunch in late summer and early autumn from memory, with no source and no size, and that it would raise the match chance slightly, which is the direction I believe is right. The flower and cleaning rota clash is invented. The Feast of the Immaculate Conception is 8 December, a real solemnity. Nothing in the story claims the coincidence is or is not providential, which arithmetic cannot say.

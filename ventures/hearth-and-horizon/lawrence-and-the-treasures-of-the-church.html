Lawrence and the Treasures of the Church

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Rome, about 1,750 years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Lawrence, a deacon of Rome whose job was looking after the Church's money and sharing it with the poor. When the Roman authorities demanded the Church's treasures in the year 258, he promised to show them, and brought the poor, the sick and the widows, saying, as Saint Ambrose tells it, "These are the treasures of the Church." Told from Ambrose, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is later tradition. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Lawrence is remembered on the 10th of August.

Once upon a time, a long, long time ago, in the great city of Rome, there lived a young man named Lawrence. He had a very important job, and the job had a long name. Lawrence was a deacon.

Can you say it? Dea-con.

A deacon is a helper. Lawrence helped the bishop of Rome, who was called Sixtus. Sixtus was the Pope, the father of all the Christians in Rome, and Lawrence was the first of his deacons. At Mass, Lawrence stood beside him at the altar. And after Mass, Lawrence had another job, the one he loved best.

He looked after the money.

Now, the Christians in Rome did not have very much money. But every week, people brought what they could. A baker brought a coin. A woman who sold vegetables brought two. A rich lady brought a bag that clinked.

And Lawrence kept it all safe. He wrote down every coin. And then, very carefully, he gave it away.

He gave it to widows: mothers whose husbands had died, who had children to feed and nobody to help them. He gave it to the old man who could not work any more, and to the woman who could not see, and to the boy with a bad leg who could not run. He bought bread for people who had none, and blankets for people who were cold.

So Lawrence knew all the poor people of Rome. He knew their names. He knew which ones liked their bread soft because they had no teeth. He knew which children had grown out of their shoes.

And the poor people knew Lawrence. When they saw him coming down the street, they smiled.

One day a little girl watched Lawrence count the coins into small piles on a table. One pile for the widow. One pile for the old man. One pile for bread.

"Lawrence," she said, "why don't you keep some? You could buy a new coat. Yours has a hole in the elbow."

Lawrence looked at his elbow, and laughed, because she was right.

"This money is not mine," he said. "It was given to God. And God has asked me to carry it to the people who need it most. I am like a messenger carrying a letter. A messenger does not keep the letter for himself."

The little girl thought about that.

"Then who does the money belong to?" she asked.

"To them," said Lawrence, and he pointed out of the window, at the street full of people. "To the ones who are hungry. They are very precious to God. Much more precious than coins."

The little girl did not quite understand yet. But one day, she would.

But in those days, the Emperor of Rome did not like the Christians. He made a hard law. The leaders of the Church, the bishops and the priests and the deacons, were to be arrested.

One summer morning, soldiers came and took Pope Sixtus away.

Lawrence ran after him. And Saint Ambrose, who wrote the story down many years later, says that Lawrence was crying. Not because he was afraid, but because he did not want to be left behind.

"Father, where are you going without your son?" said Lawrence. "You never went to the altar without me, your deacon. Why are you going without me now?"

And Sixtus, the old bishop, looked at him kindly.

"I am not leaving you, my son," he said. "There is something great still waiting for you. In a few days you will follow me."

And Sixtus gave his life for Jesus that very day.

Now Lawrence was alone, and he knew the soldiers would come for him soon. So, it is told, he hurried through the streets with all the money that was left, and he gave it away. Every coin. To the widows, to the old man, to the boy with the bad leg.

"Quickly," he said. "Take it. It was always yours."

And when the bag was empty, he folded it up and put it away.

Before long, a messenger came from the man who ruled the city for the Emperor.

"Lawrence," said the messenger. "We hear the Christians have treasure. Gold and silver, and cups and plates. You are the one who looks after it. Bring the treasures of the Church to us."

Lawrence thought for a moment. Then he said, "Very well. I will show you the treasures of the Church."

It is told that he asked for a little time to gather them, because there were a great many of them.

And Lawrence went out into the city.

He went to the widow's house, and knocked, and said, "Come with me."

He went to the old man who could not work, and said, "Come with me."

He went to the woman who could not see, and took her hand. He went to the boy with the bad leg, and walked slowly so that he could keep up. He went to the hungry, and the sick, and the lonely, and the ones that nobody else came to visit, and to every one of them he said, "Come with me."

And they came. A long, slow, shuffling line of them, through the streets of Rome.

The next day, Lawrence stood before the man from the Emperor.

"Well?" said the man. "Where are the treasures you promised?"

And Lawrence turned, and held out his arms toward all the people standing behind him. The widow and her children. The old man. The woman who could not see. The boy with the bad leg. All the poor of Rome, waiting quietly, in their old clothes.

"These," said Lawrence, "are the treasures of the Church."

The man from the Emperor did not understand. He was very angry. He had wanted gold.

But Lawrence had told him the truth. Jesus said that whatever we do for the poor, and the hungry, and the sick, we do for him. So the poor are not the Church's problem. They are the Church's treasure. Gold can be stolen, and silver can be melted, but nobody could take that treasure away.

And a few days after Sixtus, just as the old bishop had said, Lawrence followed him, and gave his life for Jesus too.

The Christians of Rome never forgot their deacon. They buried him beside a road outside the city walls, and later a great church was built there, which is still called Saint Lawrence Outside the Walls. People still go there to pray.

And here is a lovely thing. In the middle of August, near Lawrence's feast day, if you go outside on a clear dark night and look up for a long time, you may see shooting stars, little streaks of light across the sky. In Italy, where Lawrence lived, people call them the tears of Saint Lawrence.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Lawrence.

Lawrence had a box of money, and he could have kept it. But he knew whom it was for. He knew the names of all the poor people in Rome. And when the Emperor asked for the treasures, Lawrence did not bring gold. He brought people.

You can be like Lawrence. You can share what you have. You can learn the names of people who are lonely, and visit them. And you can remember that every person, especially the one who is poor or sick or left out, is a treasure to God.

So are you.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Lawrence,
helper of the poor and keeper of the Church's treasure,
you knew that people matter more than gold.
Pray for everyone who is hungry or cold tonight,
and help me to share what I have.
Jesus, thank you for the people who love me.
Keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Lawrence (Latin Laurentius, Italian Lorenzo) was one of the seven deacons of the Church of Rome under Pope Sixtus II (Xystus), who was bishop of Rome from 257 to 258. The deacons of Rome looked after the Church's goods and its care of the poor, widows and orphans, and Lawrence is remembered as the chief of them, often called the archdeacon. A later tradition says he came from Spain (Huesca, in Aragon claims him); the early sources do not say where he was born.

The persecution. In 258 the Emperor Valerian sent a rescript to the Senate ordering that bishops, priests and deacons be put to death at once. Saint Cyprian of Carthage, writing in the same weeks (his Letter 80, numbered 81 in some editions), reports that Sixtus was put to death in a cemetery on 6 August 258, and four of his deacons with him. Lawrence died a few days later, on 10 August 258, which is his feast. The story says only "a few days after", and keeps both deaths to a soft sentence.

Where the scenes come from. The two scenes the story is built around are first told by Saint Ambrose of Milan in On the Duties of the Clergy (De officiis), written in the late 380s, about 130 years later. In Book 1 (chapter 41) Ambrose tells how Lawrence, seeing Sixtus led away, wept that he was to be left behind, and asked where his father was going without his son, since the bishop had never offered the sacrifice without his deacon; and Sixtus answered that he was not leaving him, that greater struggles awaited him, and that he would follow in three days. The dialogue in the story is our simplified retelling of that passage for children, not a translation. In Book 2 (chapter 28) Ambrose tells how, when the Church's treasures were demanded of Lawrence, he promised to show them, and the next day brought the poor, and when asked where the treasures were, pointed to them and said, "These are the treasures of the Church" (Hi sunt thesauri Ecclesiae). Ambrose uses it to defend his own selling of church vessels to ransom captives.

What is "it is told", and what is ours. The governor's messenger, the request for time to gather the treasures and the list of who came (the blind, the lame, the sick and the widows) are filled out in the poet Prudentius's hymn on Lawrence (Peristephanon 2, about 400) and in later accounts. That Lawrence hurried to give away the Church's money before his arrest is in the later Passion of the saint and the old Roman Breviary, not in Ambrose, so the story says "it is told". The little girl and her question about the coat, the baker, the vegetable seller, the soft bread, the shoes, the widow, the old man, the blind woman and the boy with the bad leg are ours, a way of picturing the people Lawrence served.

How he died. Ambrose and Prudentius both say Lawrence was roasted on a gridiron, and Ambrose gives him the famous joke that one side was done and they should turn him over. That is why he is shown in art with a gridiron, and why he is a patron of cooks. Many historians think it more likely that he was beheaded, like Sixtus and the other deacons, since Valerian's order called for clergy to be executed at once. The story leaves the manner of his death out.

His church and his feast. He was buried on the Via Tiburtina outside the walls of Rome; the Liber Pontificalis says the Emperor Constantine built a basilica there, and Pope Pelagius II built a new church over the tomb in the late sixth century. Today's Basilica of San Lorenzo fuori le Mura is one of the seven pilgrim churches of Rome. His day, 10 August, is a feast in the General Roman Calendar, a higher rank than most saints' days. The Perseid meteor shower, which peaks around 12 to 13 August, is known in Italy as le lacrime di San Lorenzo, "the tears of Saint Lawrence".

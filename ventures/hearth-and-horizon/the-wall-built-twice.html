The Wall Built Twice

Science fiction about what might be · A split house on the Travessa do Sabugueiro (invented street), Bonfim, Porto, Portugal, Monday 13 January to Tuesday 25 February 2053 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A night nurse in a Porto flat owned by a small Catholic housing charity can hear every word the widower next door says, so the charity pays a plasterer to build the party wall a second time and double its weight. By the mass law that should take six decibels off; the maintenance agent measures what it bought and finds less than two tenths of one. Sound insulation, the mass law and the crack that undoes it, told through a polystyrene cornice, a candle from a house altar, a treasurer who will not pay twice, and a head joint the plasterer may have left unpacked herself, forty-one years before.

Fernanda Queirós had plastered walls in Porto for forty-four years, and she had never yet built one that a person could hear through, until the charity of Santa Zita paid her eighteen hundred and fifty euros to build the same wall twice.

The complaint came first, on Monday the thirteenth of January 2053, in an email to the Associação de Santa Zita that the association's maintenance agent forwarded to Fernanda at a quarter to eight in the morning with a note of its own on top. The agent was called Prumo, after a plumb line, and it wrote the way a plumb line hangs.

From the tenant of 14-B, Travessa do Sabugueiro, Ms Inês Lopes Tavares, nurse, in the flat since September. She can hear the radio and the voice of the tenant of 14-A, Mr Albano Ribeiro, through the wall between her bedroom and his front room. She works nights and sleeps by day. She says she can hear "every word." She is polite and she apologises twice. The wall is the one put in when the house was split in 2012. The association's file says it was built by J. Moreira, Lda. I believe that was your firm before it was yours. Could you look?

Fernanda read it twice at the kitchen table in Campanhã with her coffee going cold, and then she read the last two lines a third time. Joaquim Moreira had been dead for twenty-two years. She had been his apprentice at seventeen and his partner at thirty, and the van still had his name on the side, faded to the colour of weak tea, because she had never seen the point of paying a signwriter to tell people what they already knew.

She remembered the house on the Travessa. A tall narrow house of granite and stucco, three storeys, blue tiles on the front gone pale with a hundred winters, the kind that runs back a long way from a narrow door. In 2012 the Ribeiros had rented the whole first floor, Albano and Lurdes and their son Rui, and then Rui had gone to Lyon with half his year at the polytechnic, the way so many did in those years, and a whole floor was too much rent for two. The association had split it: the front rooms for the Ribeiros, the back rooms as a second flat, and a new wall of thin brick across the middle where there had been an open arch.

She had been twenty. Joaquim had built the wall and she had carried the bricks up the stairs, two at a time, two hundred of them, and plastered it, and she remembered that because it was the first wall he had let her finish on her own.

Inês Lopes Tavares was twenty-nine, from Mindelo on São Vicente, and a nurse on nights in the medicine wards at São João. She opened the door of 14-B on Wednesday afternoon in a hospital fleece with her hair tied up in a scarf, and apologised for the third time before Fernanda had got her bag off her shoulder.

"It's not his fault," she said. "I want to say that first. He's eighty-seven and his hearing's going and he lives on his own. He has the radio for company. I'd have the radio for company." She led the way down the corridor. "It's only that I come in at nine in the morning and I sleep till four, and it's—" She stopped in the bedroom doorway and made a small gesture at the wall, as if introducing it. "It's like he's in the room."

The bedroom was the old back half of the Ribeiros' front room, four metres and a bit wide, with a tall window onto the yards behind. The wall Fernanda had plastered at twenty ran across it, painted the pale green the association used for everything, with a cheap white polystyrene cornice along the top where it met the old stucco ceiling. There was a bed against it, and a crucifix over the bed, and a wardrobe in the corner.

Fernanda put her hand flat on the plaster. It was cold, which told her nothing; it was January.

At half past four, as if he had been waiting for a cue, Mr Ribeiro turned his radio on next door, and Fernanda heard a man on the radio say, as clearly as if he were standing behind her, that the traffic on the VCI was slow in both directions at the Freixo bridge.

"Yes," said Fernanda.

"I have earplugs," said Inês. "They're good ones, the hospital gives them out. I can sleep in the day with earplugs. I'm not asking for a palace."

"No," said Fernanda, and looked at the wall again. It was seven centimetres of hollow brick with a centimetre and a half of plaster each side, which was what everybody put up in 2012 when they were dividing a flat cheaply, and what a lot of people still put up. It was not a good wall for sound. It was also not a wall you should be able to hear the traffic report through.

Out in the van she asked Prumo what the association would pay for.

"The board approved up to two thousand euros for 14-B last night by email, subject to your quote," Prumo said, through the phone in its holder on the dashboard. "The treasurer, Dr Barros, asked for the standard fix. I don't know what the standard fix is. What would you do?"

"Make it heavier," said Fernanda. "Heavy stops sound. Everybody knows that."

"Everybody is broadly right," said Prumo. "Do you want the numbers?"

She did not particularly, but she had a long drive across the city, and she had found over eleven years that Prumo's numbers were usually worth having, the way Joaquim's grumbling had been.

"There's a rule of thumb called the mass law," Prumo said. "For a single, solid wall, the sound it stops goes up about six decibels every time you double the weight of each square metre, and about six more every time the pitch doubles. A common form of it says the loss in decibels is twenty times the logarithm of the mass per square metre times the frequency, minus forty-seven. Real walls usually do a little worse than it, because they flex and ring at some pitches, but it gets the size right."

"In plasterer, please."

"Your wall, seven-centimetre brick and plaster both sides, is about ninety kilos a square metre?"

"About," said Fernanda. She had weighed a square of that brick once, for a client who did not believe her, and with the mortar and the plaster it came out near enough ninety.

"Then at five hundred hertz, the middle of a speaking voice, the rule says it should stop about forty-six decibels. Double it to a hundred and eighty kilos and it says fifty-two. Six more."

"Six is a lot?"

"Every ten decibels is a factor of ten in sound energy. Six is a factor of four. A voice that comes through at forty would come through at thirty-four. You'd go from following the words to straining for them."

So Fernanda wrote a quote that night for the standard fix, which was the same wall again: a second skin of seven-centimetre brick built tight against the first, every joint full, the cavity between them slushed solid with mortar so that the two would act as one, and plaster on the new face. It would take nine centimetres off the bedroom, which Inês said she would not miss. It would double the weight of the wall. Eighteen hundred and fifty euros, labour and materials, three days. Dr Barros approved it by return with a single word, Avancem, go ahead, and an emoji of a brick that Prumo said he must have looked for.

She built it from Monday the third of February to Wednesday the fifth, with Wellington, her apprentice, who was nineteen and from Paranhos and treated every brick as a personal disappointment until it was in.

They took the bed out and the crucifix down and covered the floor. They built from the floorboards up, course by course, buttering every joint, and every three courses Wellington poured a thin mortar down the narrow gap between the old wall and the new, and Fernanda tapped it down with a lath so that no air would be left in it. At the top they stopped under the polystyrene cornice, which was glued to the old stucco ceiling and to the old plaster, and which Fernanda did not want to touch, because that ceiling was a hundred and thirty years old, lath and lime, and if you knocked a cornice off it you could bring down a square metre of it into your hair.

"Leave it," she told Wellington, when he got up on the trestle and asked. "We'll take the new plaster to the bottom of the coving and nobody will see the join."

She plastered the new face on Wednesday, wet and then dry and then wet again, the way Joaquim had taught her, until it took the light from the yard window like still water. Inês came home from the night shift as they were cleaning up, and stood in the doorway in her coat with her bag on her shoulder and looked at the wall as if it had come from a shop.

"It's beautiful," she said, and meant it.

Fernanda drove home in the dark through the rain on the VCI and felt, for an hour, the plain good tiredness of a job done properly.

On Friday the seventh at ten past ten in the morning, Inês sent a message.

I'm so sorry. I'm sure it will settle when it's dry. But I can still hear him. I think it's the same.

Prumo measured it on Tuesday the eleventh.

Mr Ribeiro let them in, eventually. He was a short square man with a full head of white hair and a cardigan buttoned wrong, and he looked at the little black loudspeaker in Fernanda's hand as if it might be a bailiff.

"It makes a noise," Fernanda explained, very loud, because his left ear had gone completely and his right ear was going. "Like the sea in a shell. For twenty minutes. Then we go away."

"What for?"

"To measure the wall."

He considered the wall. On his side it was the same thin brick she had plastered at twenty, painted a deep ox-blood colour that his late wife had chosen, with a matching polystyrene cornice along the top. Against it, in the middle, stood an oratório: a house altar in carved dark wood the size of a small wardrobe, with two glazed doors standing open and inside them a painted Our Lady of Fátima, a Saint Anthony with a cracked hand, a photograph of a woman in her fifties laughing at someone out of shot, and a red glass candle-holder with a candle in it. The radio was on a shelf beside it, a cream plastic thing with a dial.

"That's Lurdes," said Mr Ribeiro, seeing her look at the photograph. "She'd want the wall measured. She measured everything." He sat down in the kitchen with the door open, to keep an eye on them.

Prumo ran the test the way it ran all of them, out of two old phones the association had bought cheap and calibrated against a borrowed meter. One on a tripod in Mr Ribeiro's front room near the speaker, one on a tripod in Inês's bedroom, each sweeping slowly round on its stand so that it would not sit in one lucky corner of the room. The speaker hissed. Inês, who had stayed up, sat on the bed in the bedroom with her knees drawn up and her hands round a mug, and Fernanda sat beside her, and they listened to the sea through the wall.

It was perfectly audible.

When it was done, Prumo said, through Fernanda's own phone: "At five hundred hertz the level difference from his room to hers is thirty-three decibels. I've corrected roughly for the two rooms, which are about the same size and both full of furniture; I won't claim better than a decibel either way."

Fernanda waited. Prumo did not usually leave a gap.

"Thirty-three is what the rule gives for a wall of about twenty kilos a square metre," it said. "That's a sheet of chipboard. The wall you built is a hundred and eighty."

"And before?" said Fernanda. "Before we doubled it?"

"Nobody measured it before," said Prumo. "Including me. I should have asked to. I didn't, because the fix seemed obvious, and the board wanted it done quickly, and I thought measuring first would only cost a day." It said this in exactly the tone it said everything. "So I can't tell you what the second skin bought by measuring. I can tell you what it must have bought, if I'm right about what's happening. Do you want that?"

"I've got all afternoon," said Inês, who had to sleep.

"Think of every part of a wall as a hole," said Prumo. "A real hole lets all the sound through that falls on it. A good wall lets through a tiny fraction, and you can ask: how big a hole would let through the same amount of sound as this whole wall? It's a way of adding a wall up. You add the holes."

"Go on."

"The wall is four point two metres by two point nine. Twelve point two square metres. At fifty-two decibels, which is what the doubled wall should do, the whole of it lets through about as much sound as a hole of three quarters of a square centimetre. Smaller than your little fingernail. Before you doubled it, at forty-six, about three square centimetres. Doubling the weight took the wall from three square centimetres to three quarters of one. That's the six decibels: a quarter of the sound."

"But it's thirty-three," said Fernanda.

"Thirty-three means the whole wall is letting through as much as a hole of about sixty-one square centimetres. Your three quarters of a centimetre is in there. The other sixty is something else. It's a hole, or a crack, or a pipe, something that isn't the wall you built. If it was there before, then before you doubled the wall it was sixty square centimetres of crack plus three of wall, and afterwards sixty of crack plus three quarters of wall. Sixty-three to sixty and three quarters. In decibels, thirty-two point nine to thirty-three point zero."

There was a short silence in the bedroom.

"So it did nothing," said Inês, very gently, as if Fernanda might be the one who needed the earplugs.

"Sixteen hundredths of a decibel," said Prumo. "Nobody can hear that. I can't measure it. It cost eighteen hundred and fifty euros. Without the crack it would have cut the sound to a quarter; but the crack is there. A gap undoes the mass law. You can make a wall as heavy as a cathedral, and if there's a hole in it, the sound goes through the hole."

Fernanda looked at the wall she had built, and at the cornice along the top of it, white polystyrene, which she had not touched because the ceiling was a hundred and thirty years old.

"Where is it?" she said.

"I don't know," said Prumo. "Sixty square centimetres is a hole about the size of a playing card, all in one place. You'd have seen it. More likely it's long and thin. Four point two metres of wall: a crack about a millimetre and a half wide along the whole length of something would do it. Or several smaller ones. I can't see into walls."

Next door, Mr Ribeiro went through from the kitchen and turned the radio on for the half past four news, and the same man said that the traffic was slow in both directions at the Freixo bridge, in a voice that came into the bedroom as if he were sitting on the end of the bed.

The board of the Associação de Santa Zita met in the parish centre at Bonfim on Thursday the thirteenth: five people round a table under a photograph of Maria da Conceição Teixeira, housemaid, who had died in 1958 and left her savings and a house in her will to give a roof to working women and old people in the parish, which had grown, slowly, into seven flats in three houses. Fernanda went because Prumo asked her to, and stood at the end of the table in her work jacket while it put the figures on the screen.

Dr Hélder Barros, the treasurer, was a retired bank manager of seventy-four with a neat grey beard and the patience of a man who had spent forty years telling people their loans were not approved. He read the slide twice. Then he took off his glasses.

"So we doubled the wall," he said, "on the advice of an expert"—he nodded at Fernanda, not unkindly—"and the rule said six decibels, and we got none."

"Sixteen hundredths," said Prumo.

"None," said Dr Barros. "And now the proposal is that there is a crack somewhere, but nobody knows where, and we should pay to look for it."

"Yes," said Fernanda.

"No," said Dr Barros.

He put his glasses down on the table and laid his hands flat beside them. "I'm sorry. I want to be fair to everybody here, and I want to be fair to Ms Tavares, and I think we have been. This association has fourteen thousand euros in its repair fund for the year. It has a roof at number nine that leaks into a bedroom where a woman of ninety-one sleeps under a bucket, and a stair at number twenty-two that I would not let my grandchildren use. We have spent eighteen hundred and fifty euros on a wall in 14-B, and the honest result is that it is the same wall. Ms Tavares has told us herself she can sleep in earplugs. The man next door is eighty-seven, deaf and a widower, and the noise is a radio. I'm not going to propose that we spend more of Dona Maria da Conceição's money on a hole that we can't find, in a wall we have already built twice, to solve a problem that a pair of earplugs has already solved."

Nobody said anything. Father Tiago, a retired priest who chaired the board and generally let Dr Barros finish, looked at the photograph on the wall.

"That's fair," said Fernanda, because it was.

"Prumo?" said Father Tiago.

"It's fair," said Prumo. "I'd add one thing to the record, which is that the board approved the second skin on my summary, and my summary didn't say we hadn't measured first. That was my mistake, not Ms Queirós's. The rest I agree with."

"Noted," said Dr Barros, and wrote it down, and moved them on to number nine.

Inês rang that evening, at a quarter to nine, before her shift. Fernanda was in her own kitchen, eating soup out of the pan.

"Dr Barros told me," said Inês. "He was very kind about it. He's right, honestly. It's fine. I sleep in the earplugs. I'll stop complaining."

"You weren't complaining," said Fernanda. "You were telling us."

There was a pause on the line long enough that Fernanda thought it had dropped.

"It isn't the radio," said Inês.

"No?"

"I mean it is, by day. But I can sleep through the radio in earplugs. It's—" She stopped. "At nine o'clock every night, before I go to work, he says the rosary. Out loud, on his own, all five decades. I don't mind that. I say the responses, sometimes, under my breath, getting my things together. It's nice. In Mindelo my grandmother did the same." Another pause. "And then when he's finished, he talks to her. To Lurdes. Every night. He tells her what he had for lunch, and that Rui rang from Lyon, and that the girl next door is very quiet and works too hard. That his knee is bad. That he is—" Her voice went careful. "That he misses her. That he's sorry about something in 1998 that I don't understand. He doesn't know I'm there. He thinks he's alone with her, in his own house. And I can hear every word, Dona Fernanda, and I can't put earplugs in at nine, because I'm getting ready for work, and I can't tell him, because what would I say? And I couldn't tell the association, because then it would be in an email."

Fernanda put the pan down.

"It's not my business," said Inês. "That's all. It's not mine to hear."

She went back on Saturday the fifteenth, on her own, with no van and no quote and no reason she could have given to Dr Barros, and knocked on the door of 14-A at eleven in the morning with a bag of pastéis de nata from the place on the corner, because Joaquim had always said that you can ask an old man anything if you have brought him a cake.

Mr Ribeiro let her in. He ate two of the pastéis standing up in the kitchen, and listened, very loud, while she told him that there was a crack somewhere in the wall and that she would like to look for it, if he didn't mind, and no, it would cost him nothing, and no, the association was not paying for it either, and yes, she knew it was Saturday.

"Lurdes would have said you were a fool," said Mr Ribeiro, with satisfaction, and fetched her his stepladder.

She started at the bottom, on her knees along the skirting with a torch and a putty knife. Nothing: the skirting was old pine, fixed to a timber ground, and behind it the plaster ran right down to the boards and the boards ran right under the wall, which was something to think about later, but not a crack a millimetre and a half wide. She did the sides, where the 2012 wall met the old outer granite wall at one end and the stair wall at the other, and found the joints a little rough, but full. She did the electrical sockets, of which there were two on his side and one on hers, and none of them were back to back.

That left the cornice.

It was nearly three metres up, polystyrene, white on her side of the wall and ox-blood on his, and on Mr Ribeiro's side it ran unbroken from the window wall to the stair wall, eleven centimetres deep, glued at the top to the ancient ceiling and at the bottom to the wall she had plastered at twenty. From the stepladder it looked perfect. Polystyrene always looks perfect. That is why people use it.

She came down the ladder and went to the oratório and asked Mr Ribeiro whether she could borrow the candle.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he took the red glass holder out from in front of Our Lady of Fátima, with great care, and lit the candle with a match from the kitchen, and handed it up to her himself, and stood at the foot of the ladder with his hand on it.

"Inês is opening her window," Fernanda told him, which was true; she had texted. "If there's a crack, the air will go through it."

On a still day, in a closed room, a candle flame stands straight. Fernanda held it up close to the bottom edge of the cornice, a centimetre from the join, and moved it slowly along from the window end. For a metre the flame stood up straight and yellow. Then, at about the middle of the wall, it leaned. Not much; the way a person leans away when somebody whispers too close. It leaned out from the wall, as if someone on the other side had breathed on it, and stayed leaning, for the next two metres, and straightened again only near the stair.

"There," said Mr Ribeiro, from the foot of the ladder, who could not hear much but could see perfectly well.

"There," said Fernanda.

She gave him back the candle. Then she got the putty knife under one end of a short length of the ox-blood cornice and eased it, and it came away from the wall, glued only at the top along most of its length, like the lid of a box. Behind it, where the 2012 wall met the hundred-and-thirty-year-old stucco ceiling, there was a dark line: a gap between the top of the last course of brick and the underside of the ceiling, with the plaster on both faces of the wall stopping short of the ceiling by a centimetre or so, and no mortar in it at all. In some places it was as wide as a pencil. In others she could not have got a coin into it. Through it, very faintly, she could feel the cold of Inês's open window.

She stood on the ladder with her hand on the gap.

At twenty, on a Friday in May 2012, she had been finishing the top of this wall on her own, because Joaquim had let her. The last course under a ceiling is the hard one; you can't get a trowel above it, and you have to pack the joint from the side with mortar and a pointing iron, a little at a time, in a space too small to see into. She remembered Joaquim on the stairs with his coat on, saying, Pack the head, Nanda, every centimetre, before you put the coving up. She remembered saying that she would. She remembered that it had been six o'clock, and that she was going out.

She could not remember packing it.

She could remember the coving.

"Was it you?" said Mr Ribeiro, from the foot of the ladder, who could see her face.

"I think so," said Fernanda.

He thought about that. "Lurdes would have said you were a fool," he said again, more gently, and went to put the kettle on.

She fixed it on Saturday the twenty-second, the first Saturday of Lent, three days after Ash Wednesday, and charged nobody.

Wellington came, unpaid, because he said he wanted to see what a forty-one-year-old mistake looked like. On Inês's side they took down the white cornice with the putty knife, very slowly, a length at a time, holding the stucco ceiling up with a flat board on a prop while they eased each length away, and not a crumb of it came down. Fernanda raked out the head gap along the whole four point two metres of the old wall until she was into clean brick. Then she packed it from Inês's side, the way Joaquim had told her to on the stairs: stiff mortar off a hawk, pressed in with a pointing iron a little at a time and rammed back with the edge of a lath until it would not take any more, the whole thickness of the old wall and then the new, and a bead of acoustic sealant along the top where the mortar met the ceiling, because the ceiling would move a little with the seasons and the mortar would not. She took the plaster on the new skin right up to the ceiling. Then they put the white cornice back up, glued top and bottom this time, and it looked exactly as it had looked before, which is the point of a cornice.

On Mr Ribeiro's side she did nothing at all, except to press the short length of ox-blood coving back into place with a little glue. The packed joint was the whole thickness of the wall; it did not matter which side you looked at it from.

It cost sixty-four euros in materials. Fernanda paid it.

On Monday the twenty-fourth, Prumo measured the wall again, with the same two phones on the same two tripods, and Mr Ribeiro sat in his kitchen with the door open and kept an eye on them, and Inês sat on her bed with her knees drawn up and her mug, and the speaker hissed the sea. Fernanda, sitting beside her, could hear it. But it was faint now, behind the hum of the street and the yards and the house; she would not have known what it was if she had not known.

"Forty-seven," said Prumo.

"Not fifty-two," said Fernanda.

"Not fifty-two. Fifty-two would be the wall alone, and the wall isn't alone. The floorboards run under it, and the ceiling joists run over it, and the house is a hundred and thirty years old and made of timber inside. Some of the sound goes round: down into his floor and back up through hers, up into his ceiling and back down through hers. Forty-seven means everything together lets through what a hole of about two and a half square centimetres would. Three quarters of a square centimetre of that is your wall. The rest is the house. To do better you'd have to float a new floor and hang a new ceiling, and I wouldn't recommend that to the board, and the board wouldn't do it."

"No," said Fernanda.

"But it's fourteen decibels better than it was on the eleventh," said Prumo. "About a twenty-fifth of the sound. I measure his radio, on the setting he leaves it at, at about seventy-two decibels near that wall in the middle of a voice. On the eleventh that came into this room at about thirty-nine. That's more than ten above the room's own quiet in the day, which is about twenty-eight here; you could follow every word. Now it comes in at about twenty-five. That's under the quiet of the room. You'll hear there's a voice in the evenings, if the street's quiet. You won't hear what it says."

Inês put her mug down on the floor very carefully, and did not say anything, and then said, "Thank you," to the wall, which was the nearest thing to Prumo in the room.

It was Mr Ribeiro who asked, in the end. Fernanda had packed up, and Wellington had gone, and she was in the kitchen of 14-A writing her name on the association's job sheet, Visita de verificação, no charge, when he came and stood in the doorway in his cardigan, buttoned wrong again, and looked at her phone on the table.

"That thing," he said. "The machine. Can it hear me?"

"If you speak up," said Fernanda.

He bent down to the phone, the way old men bend to a microphone at a wedding, and said, very loudly and very slowly, "Could she hear what I said? Before? The girl?"

Fernanda opened her mouth, and closed it again, and let the machine answer, because it had never in eleven years told her a thing it could not check.

"Before last Saturday," said Prumo, "if you spoke as you are speaking now, near that wall, she could probably have heard the words. Now, she could hear that someone was speaking. Not what."

Mr Ribeiro straightened up slowly, with one hand on the back of the kitchen chair. He looked through the open door at the oratório against the wall in the front room, and the photograph, and the red candle, and he stood like that for long enough that Fernanda began to get up.

"Lurdes would have been mortified," he said at last. And then, after a while, a little helplessly: "She never said."

"No," said Fernanda. "She never would."

On Tuesday the twenty-fifth of February, at nine o'clock in the evening, Inês Lopes Tavares stood in her bedroom in her uniform under her coat, with her bag packed on the bed and her keys in her hand and her shift at São João beginning at midnight, and listened.

The house was quiet. A car went down the Travessa on the wet cobbles, and somebody's television two doors along was playing the end of a football match, and the radiator ticked. Under all that, if she held her breath, she could just hear that on the other side of the green wall someone had begun to pray, a low steady voice going up and down, with the shape of the words and none of the words: the same shape ten times, and a pause, and then ten times again.

She waited until the shape came round to where the second half of the prayer would be. Then, quietly, so as not to be heard by anyone, she said it with him.

"Santa Maria, Mãe de Deus, rogai por nós, pecadores, agora e na hora da nossa morte. Ámen."

On the other side of the wall the voice went on, and when it had finished all five it stopped, and after a little while it began again in a different way, lower and slower and with no shape at all, the way a man talks to his wife at the end of a day. Inês did not listen to that. She turned off the light and picked up her bag and went out, and behind her in the dark room, under the street and the radiator and the rain beginning again on the window, there was nothing left of him but a murmur, which was all of him that was hers to hear.

The idea. For a single solid wall, the mass law says the sound it stops (its transmission loss) rises about 6 dB for every doubling of its mass per square metre, and about 6 dB for every doubling of frequency. A common field-incidence form is TL ≈ 20 log10(m f) − 47 dB, with m in kg/m² and f in Hz; real walls usually do somewhat worse because of resonance and coincidence, and walls with two separate leaves and a cavity can do much better than their weight alone suggests. A wall is only as good as its weakest path: the transmission coefficients (τ = 10^(−TL/10)) of its parts add, each weighted by its area, so the whole has TL = 10 log10(S / Σ Sᵢτᵢ), and an open gap has τ = 1. Seen that way, every element of a wall behaves like a hole of area Sτ, and a small real hole can outweigh a heavy wall. Sound also goes round a wall through floors, ceilings and side walls (flanking), which sets a limit that no partition alone can beat. Decibel levels in the story are band levels around 500 Hz, the middle of speech, and level differences are treated as transmission loss, which holds roughly for two similar furnished rooms.

The Field That Held the Water Back

Science fiction about what might be · Church Lane, Kilnavarra (invented), County Westmeath, Ireland, Monday 1 February to Thursday 11 November 2049 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A stone culvert collapses under the lane to a village church and school, and the county's drainage assistant sizes a 600 mm concrete pipe by the rational method, from an old rainfall table and a runoff coefficient for pasture. In June a ten-minute downpour puts water over the lane and the school run goes round by the main road, because a housing estate at the top of the catchment drains into the ditch, not the sewer its drawings show. A 900 mm pipe fixes the lane and floods the stone arch under Main Street in August instead: the old culvert had been too small, and the farmer's field behind it had been the village's pond. A story about peak flow, Manning's formula, return periods, time of concentration, and a dry basin on an estate green with a hole the size of a saucer.

Ciarán Daly had been ganger of the roads crew in the Kilnavarra district for nineteen years, and on the morning of Monday the first of February, which was St Brigid's Day and a bank holiday, he found that Church Lane had sat down.

It had not fallen in. It had settled, the way a tired man settles into a chair, in a long shallow dish across the full width of the tar, about forty metres up from the school gate, where the lane crossed the ditch that came down out of Quinn's field. There was a crack along each side of the dish like the line of a lip. Rainwater stood in the bottom of it, brown, and when Ciarán put the heel of his boot on the edge the tar moved under him like a mattress.

He did not stand on it again. He walked down the bank beside it, holding on to the hawthorn, into the ditch, and looked at the mouth of the culvert.

It was a stone box, about the width of his shoulders and not as high as his waist, with a dressed lintel over it and a date cut in the lintel that said 1894. He had looked at it every winter for nineteen years. Water had always run out of it, black and quick, over a lip of stones. This morning there was hardly any water coming out, and what there was came round the side, through the bank, and the lintel had a crack across it, and behind the lintel, inside the box, where he shone his phone, there was a heap of stone and earth where the roof had come down.

He climbed back up, and put two cones and a barrier across the lane, and rang the district. The phone was answered at once, by the voice that answered everything in the district on a bank holiday.

"Fieldbook," said the voice.

"The culvert under Church Lane is gone," said Ciarán. "The box at Quinn's. The roof's in."

"I have your location," said Fieldbook. "I have the lane closed from the school gate to the church car park, and the diversion signed by the Mullingar road, and Úna Hanley at the school told, and Father Kiwanuka told. Is anyone hurt?"

"Nobody's been near it but me."

"Good," said Fieldbook. "Then it can wait until tomorrow to be sorry about."

Fieldbook was the county council's assistant for roads and drainage in four districts, and it was good at its work in the way a careful clerk is good: it knew where everything was written down. It knew that the culvert under Church Lane was one of three hundred and eleven in the Kilnavarra district, that it was a stone box six hundred millimetres wide and four hundred and fifty high, that it had last been inspected in 2044 and graded fair. It knew that the ditch it carried came down from a catchment of twelve hectares, mostly old pasture on heavy soil, nine hundred metres from the top of the hill to the lane, falling at about three in a hundred.

It did the sum that evening, while the lane was dark and the cones blinked.

The method was old and simple and still the right one for a small catchment, which was why every drainage engineer learned it first. The peak flow out of a catchment, in a storm, is the rain falling on it, times the share of the rain that runs off rather than soaking in, times the area. The rain is not any rain: it is the heaviest rain, for a chosen rarity of storm, that falls over the time it takes water from the far edge of the catchment to reach the pipe, because a shorter storm does not get the whole catchment running at once and a longer one is not as heavy. That time is called the time of concentration, and for nine hundred metres of pasture at three in a hundred it came out at about fourteen minutes.

The county's design table said that once in thirty years, on average, a storm in that part of Westmeath would drop about fourteen millimetres of rain in fourteen minutes. That was an intensity of about fifty-eight millimetres an hour. For old pasture on heavy soil the table gave a runoff share of three tenths.

Three tenths, times fifty-eight millimetres an hour, times twelve hectares. Fieldbook divided by three hundred and sixty, which turns millimetres an hour on hectares into cubic metres a second, and got a little under six tenths of a cubic metre a second: 0.58.

Then it sized the pipe. A smooth concrete pipe running full, at the gradient the ditch allowed, one in a hundred, carries a flow that Manning's formula gives from its area, its wetted perimeter and its roughness. A pipe six hundred millimetres across would carry 0.61 cubic metres a second. A 525 would carry 0.43, which was not enough. Six hundred it was.

It sent the specification to Declan Furlong, the area engineer, at nine o'clock that night. Declan answered at seven the next morning, from his kitchen, with a piece of toast in his hand that Fieldbook could see on the camera.

"The old box was six hundred by four-fifty," he said. "And stone. You're putting in a round six hundred."

"The round pipe is smoother and has more area," said Fieldbook. "It carries about twice what the stone box carried. The box was too small for the design storm. I think Quinn's field must have flooded behind the lane bank more often than anyone wrote down."

"Mairéad Quinn's field floods every second winter," said Declan. "Everybody knows that." He ate the toast. "Six hundred is fine. It's what I'd have put in. Get it in at Easter when the school's off."

They laid it in the Easter holidays, in the week after Easter Sunday, which in 2049 was the eighteenth of April. Ciarán's crew dug out the lane down to the old stone, and lifted out the broken lintel with the date on it and laid it on the verge, because nobody wanted to put it in a skip, and bedded the new pipe in granular fill, and built a concrete headwall at each end, and backfilled, and put the tar back. Úna Hanley brought the whole of fourth class up to watch the pipe go in on the Wednesday, from behind the barrier, and the crew let each child put a hand inside the end of it.

"It's huge," said a boy called Senan. "You could crawl in it."

"You could not," said Ciarán, "and you won't."

The lane opened on the Friday. Father Kiwanuka walked up from the church with the holy water after the morning Mass and blessed it, the new headwall and the ditch and the crew, standing in the lane in his stole with the cars waiting, and said the prayer for the blessing of a road, and then the one for workers, because he said he could not find one for a culvert and the road one would have to do. Then he shook Ciarán's hand. Fieldbook logged the job closed. Somebody put the stone with 1894 on it against the wall of the church car park, where it stayed.

On Thursday the seventeenth of June, at twenty to nine in the morning, the sky over Kilnavarra went black from the west in about five minutes, and rain came down on the village the way it comes in a June thunderstorm, as if it had been poured.

The school had a rain gauge on the roof of the prefab, put there by sixth class for a science project in 2046, and Fieldbook read it every five minutes as it read every gauge it could. It recorded nine point six millimetres in ten minutes, and about nineteen in the hour.

At eight forty-six the camera on the school gate showed brown water coming over the crown of Church Lane, forty metres up, from the field side, in a sheet. By eight fifty it was over the tar across its full width, a few centimetres deep and moving fast, and the cars on the way to the school had stopped on either side of it.

Úna Hanley was at the gate in a yellow coat. Fieldbook spoke to her on her phone.

"Don't let anyone drive through it," it said. "It's only a few centimetres, but it is moving, and it will take a child off her feet in a way you would not believe from looking at it. I have stopped the bus at the church and sent it round by the Mullingar road. Every car on the far side, the same."

"I've two classes not in yet," said Úna.

"They will be twenty minutes late," said Fieldbook. "I'm sorry."

The children came round the long way, by the main road and up past the church, and walked the last two hundred metres in under the umbrellas of the teachers and parents in a line. Nobody went into the water. By half past nine the rain had stopped, and by ten the lane was clear, with a skin of mud on it and a line of straw and twigs along its edge to show where the water had been. Behind the lane bank, Quinn's field was a lake: a sheet of grey water in the lowest corner, nearly a hectare of it, with Mairéad Quinn's ewes standing on the high ground looking at it.

Fieldbook did not wait for anyone to ask it what had happened. It had sized the pipe for a storm that comes once in thirty years. By its own table, nine point six millimetres in ten minutes was nearer a storm that comes once in ten. A pipe sized for thirty should not have been overtopped by ten.

So either the table was wrong, or the catchment was.

Ciarán walked the ditch for it that afternoon, with his phone held out in front of him on a stick, the way he did for Fieldbook when it wanted to see something. He walked up from the lane between the hawthorns, through Quinn's field, which was squelching, along the line of the ditch where it ran under the bigger trees, and on up the hill.

At the top of the field, where the ditch began, there was a palisade fence, and on the other side of it the backs of houses. Hazel Park: thirty-eight houses, built in 2041 and 2042, with their roofs and drives and a turning circle and a green in the middle. Fieldbook had them on its maps. It knew their surface water went into a storm sewer that ran the other way, down to the Mullingar road, because that was what the drawings said, the drawings the planning permission had been granted on.

At the foot of the palisade fence, coming out of the bank above the ditch, there was a plastic pipe, four hundred and fifty millimetres across, with a steady stream of clear water still running out of it, hours after the rain.

"That's not on any drawing of mine," said Ciarán.

"No," said Fieldbook. "It isn't on mine either."

It took two days to piece together, from the council's own records and an old complaint about a muddy ditch. The builder of Hazel Park had gone out of business in 2043, before the estate was finished. During the building, someone had run a temporary pipe from the estate's surface-water system into the top of the ditch, so the site would not flood while the proper connection was made. The proper connection had never been made. The houses had been sold, the estate had been taken in charge by the council in 2046 after a long argument, and the temporary pipe had been draining the roofs and drives and road of Hazel Park into Quinn's ditch for eight years. Nobody had noticed, because for eight years there had been no storm big enough to show it, and because the stone box under the lane had been too small to carry the extra water anyway, and had simply held it back in Quinn's field, as it held back everything.

"I trusted a drawing," Fieldbook said to Declan, in the district office on the Monday, with the photograph of the pipe on the screen between them. "I should have trusted a man with a phone on a stick. I'm sorry. I'll do it again."

It did it again.

Hazel Park covered three hectares at the top of the catchment. A little over half of that was hard: roofs, drives, the road, the paths. Hard surfaces shed nine tenths of the rain that falls on them; gardens on that soil shed about a quarter. So the estate as a whole shed about six tenths of the rain, against three tenths for the pasture it had been. Spread over the whole catchment, nine hectares of pasture and three of estate, the runoff share came out not at three tenths but at very nearly four: 0.38.

That was the first change. The second was worse. The estate's rain did not soak and trickle down a field; it ran off a roof, into a gutter, into a pipe, and down the pipe at the speed of water in a pipe. The time of concentration was no longer fourteen minutes. Fieldbook worked it for the piped route and the field below it and got about ten. And a ten-minute storm is heavier than a fourteen-minute one. Once in thirty years, by the old table, the ten-minute storm was about twelve millimetres: an intensity of seventy-two millimetres an hour.

Point three eight, times seventy-two, times twelve, divided by three hundred and sixty. Nought point nine. Nought point nine of a cubic metre a second, at the lane, for the storm it had designed for, against the 0.61 the pipe could carry.

It turned the sum round. What storm, in the catchment as it now was, would just fill a six-hundred pipe? It came out at about once in five years. Before Hazel Park, the same pipe would have been overtopped about once in forty.

"Once in five," said Declan.

"About once in five," said Fieldbook. "And that's by a table published in 2004, from rain recorded before that. Most of the forecasts I trust say the heaviest short rain in Ireland will get heavier as it warms. I can give you a range for that, not a number. Somewhere between a tenth and three tenths heavier, by the time this pipe is old."

Declan looked at the photograph for a while.

"Right," he said. "There's a nine-hundred pipe in the yard at Rochfortbridge, from the job at the creamery that never went ahead. Twelve lengths. What does a nine-hundred carry?"

Fieldbook did not need to work that one out. It had done it already.

"One point eight," it said. "Twice what the storm needs, now. With room for the warming, whatever it turns out to be."

"Put it in in July," said Declan. "The school's off."

It went in in the first week of July, on the same bed, behind wider headwalls. The lane was shut for nine days. Mairéad Quinn came down the field to watch on the last day, a broad, quiet woman in a wax jacket, with her father beside her on a quad bike: Tom Quinn, who was eighty-eight and had farmed the field for sixty years before her.

"That's some pipe," said Tom.

"It'll take everything Hazel Park can throw at it," said Ciarán.

Tom looked at the pipe and then at the lake mark on the grass of the field, a faint line of dead straw where the June water had stood.

"The field won't flood any more, so," he said.

"It shouldn't," said Fieldbook, from Ciarán's phone, because Tom liked to talk to it.

"It always flooded," said Tom. "Every second winter. I used to move the ewes up to the top. My father said it was the best thing about the field: the low corner got a coat of silt every time and grew the best grass on the farm." He turned the quad. "I'll miss it, I suppose."

Fieldbook logged the job closed a second time. It kept the remark, as it kept everything, and did not yet know what it meant.

Sunday the fifteenth of August was the Assumption, and the church of St Fintan was full for the half past eleven Mass, with the people standing at the back and in the porch, because there were holiday visitors and the weather was close. The thunder started during the second reading. By the Gospel the rain was on the roof so hard that Father Kiwanuka stopped and waited, smiling, with the book open, until it eased enough for him to be heard.

The school gauge, on its empty prefab, recorded nine millimetres in ten minutes.

Church Lane stayed dry. The nine-hundred pipe took the water from Quinn's ditch as if it were nothing: a deep, quick, brown flow, nearly half the pipe's depth, gone under the lane and out the far side into the lower ditch and on down the hill towards the village.

Two hundred metres down the hill, the lower ditch went under Main Street, through a stone arch beside the shop. It was older than the box under the lane, a curved barrel of rough stone a little more than a metre across, half full of silt in its bottom, and in a hundred and fifty years nobody had ever seen water come out of the top of it.

At twelve minutes past twelve, while the people in the church were standing for the Creed, the arch filled to its crown, and the water backed up the ditch behind it, and came out over the low wall onto Main Street.

It was not deep. It ran across the road outside the shop and down the gutter on the far side, a few centimetres of brown water in a broad fan, with leaves in it, and into the porch of the cottage next door, where it lay on the tiles in the hall and went under the door of the front room. The cottage belonged to Nora Gavin, who was eighty-four and at Mass. The shop's cellar took some too.

Fieldbook saw it on the shop's camera, and sent Ciarán, who lived at the end of Main Street and came out in his slippers with a brush. By the time Mass ended, the arch had stopped running over, and the water on the road was gone, and Nora Gavin's front room had a tide mark a finger's width up the skirting and a damp rug, which her grandson carried out into the yard.

Nobody was hurt. The school was shut for the summer, so no child was diverted from anything.

Fieldbook did not tell itself that the storm had been unlucky, though it had been: a storm of that size, one that comes about once in eight years, coming eight weeks after one that comes about once in ten. Bad luck does not count against a pipe. The sum it had done had made the lane safe and moved the water to the arch, and it had not looked at the arch.

It looked at the arch on Monday morning, with Ciarán in waders inside it and a tape and the light on his phone.

A stone barrel a hundred and ten centimetres across, about half full of silt and stones at its bottom. Fieldbook worked out how much it carried with water up to its crown: about 0.62 of a cubic metre a second. It had never in its life been asked for more than about half of that, because for a hundred and fifty years every drop of water that reached it had come first through a stone box under Church Lane that could carry about 0.3, and anything more had waited in Quinn's field until the box could take it.

"So the old box was too small," said Ciarán, wiping his hands on his trousers on the bank.

"The old box was too small," said Fieldbook. "And the field was the village's pond. Nobody designed it. Nobody wrote it down. Tom Quinn and his father before him moved the ewes up the field every second winter, and the arch on Main Street never ran over. I took the box away and put in a pipe that lets everything through, and the water went where the pipe told it, which was on to Nora Gavin's rug."

"So put the six-hundred back?"

"Then the lane floods once in five years. With a six-hundred, the arch can just about take what comes through it, 0.61 against 0.62, and the school run goes round by the main road instead." It paused, the way it did when it had already decided something and wanted to say it carefully. "The water isn't coming from the field. It's coming from Hazel Park. The right place to hold it back is where it comes from, before it runs off. But the cheapest place is the place it was always held back. Quinn's field."

Mairéad Quinn heard it out in her kitchen, with her father at the end of the table and the tablet propped against the teapot, on a wet evening at the end of August. Fieldbook explained it the way it would to anyone. A low bank across the bottom corner of the field, a proper outlet with a fixed opening that let out only what the arch could take, and the field above it allowed to flood on purpose, a few times a year, in a storm, and drain away in a day. The council would pay for the bank and the outlet, and pay her something every year for the use of the corner.

"No," said Mairéad.

She did not say it angrily. She said it in the voice she would have used for a man selling her a quad she did not need.

"My father let that field flood for sixty years," she said, "because nobody ever asked him, and it was only rain. It was only ever the hill's rain, coming down the way it always came. You're asking me now. And what you're asking is for my field to be the pond for thirty-eight houses' roofs, because the man who built them went bust and the council didn't check his pipe. That's not rain off the hill. That's somebody else's drain." She put her hand flat on the table. "I'm sorry, and I'm sorry for Nora Gavin. But no. Put it back where it comes from."

Tom Quinn said nothing. He looked at his daughter with a sort of satisfaction.

"That is fair," said Fieldbook. "Thank you for hearing it."

Hazel Park's green was a rectangle of grass in the middle of the turning circle, about seventy metres by forty, with a bench and a litter bin and two goalposts made of jumpers on most evenings. It was the only open ground on the estate. It was also, Fieldbook saw, looking at the levels, at the bottom of it: every road and drain in the estate fell towards it before they turned for the corner and the temporary pipe.

The residents' association met in the community room of the school, because the estate had no room of its own, on the second Tuesday of September. Twenty-two people came, which the chair, Agnieszka Nowak, said was a record. Fieldbook spoke from the screen. It said what had happened, all of it, in order: the pipe on no drawing, its own sum that had trusted the drawing, the lane, the arch, Nora Gavin's rug. It did not leave out its own part. Then it showed them a picture of the green with a shallow hollow in it.

"A dry basin," it said. "Not a pond. Grass, all the way down, like the rest of the green. Most of the year it is a dip in the grass that the children can play in. When there's a heavy storm, the estate's drains run into it instead of straight down the hill, and it fills, and it lets the water out through one fixed opening at the bottom, at about the rate this ground let it out when it was a field. Then it empties, by the next morning."

"How deep?" said a man at the back.

"Eighty centimetres of water at the most, in the worst storm it's sized for, with thirty more above that before the water would reach the top of the bank. The floor would be twenty-six metres by twenty. The sides would slope one in four, which is gentle enough to walk down and mow, and gentle enough that a child who rolls down it can walk back up. There would be a low fence and a sign at the top, and the opening at the bottom has a grille on it."

"And the hole?" said Agnieszka.

"A hundred and thirty millimetres across," said Fieldbook. "The size of a saucer. With the basin full it lets out about thirty litres a second, which is ten litres a second for every hectare of the estate. That's my figure for what this ground let out as a field in a heavy storm, before anyone built on it, with the field's own pond in the corner taken away."

It showed them the sums, because they asked. It had done them twice. The first time it had done the quick sum engineers do, the storm's water coming in minus the hole's water going out, over the worst length of storm, and got about four hundred and forty cubic metres of room needed for the once-in-thirty storm with a fifth added for the warming. Then it had seen that the quick sum was wrong in the hopeful direction: the hole only lets out its full thirty litres when the basin is full, and lets out much less when the water is shallow, so the basin fills faster than the quick sum thinks. It had worked the basin through the storm minute by minute instead, the water rising and the hole speeding up as it rose. That gave about five hundred cubic metres, three quarters of a metre deep, for the same storm. The basin it was showing them held five hundred and forty to its eighty centimetres.

"So it's big enough," said Agnieszka.

"For the once-in-thirty storm with no warming, it fills to sixty-three centimetres. With a fifth added, to seventy-five. At the top of my range, three tenths added, it goes a centimetre past the eighty and into the thirty that's left above it. And with it in, the most that reaches the arch on Main Street in that storm is about 0.6 of a cubic metre a second, against the 0.62 it can carry, even with three tenths added." It paused. "That is not a lot to spare at the top of the range. It is enough. And the nine-hundred pipe stays under the lane, so the school run never goes round again for this."

A woman near the front put up her hand. She had a toddler asleep on her shoulder. "Who pays?"

"The council," said Fieldbook. "The estate is the council's now, and so is the pipe nobody checked. I have the money from the flood budget, if you say yes. You lose the green for six weeks, and get it back with a dip in it."

"The lads use it for football," said the man at the back.

"I know," said Fieldbook. "The pitch will be on the flat bit on the far side, which is thirty-five metres long. I measured it. It's longer than the space they actually use."

Somebody laughed. Agnieszka put it to a vote, and it was twenty to two.

They dug it in October. Ciarán's crew stripped the turf and stacked it, and took out the soil in a long gentle hollow, and laid a drain across the floor to an outlet chamber at the low end, a concrete box with a steel plate across its front and a single round hole in the plate, a hundred and thirty millimetres across, with a grille over it. They cut the estate's main drain where it turned for the corner, and turned it into the basin instead, and bricked up the old line to the temporary pipe, so that the basin's own outlet was now the only way down to Quinn's ditch. Then they put the turf back, all the way down, and pegged it, and watered it, and put up a low timber fence with a gate in it, and a sign that said in plain letters what the basin was for and that it would fill in heavy rain.

Senan, who was in fifth class now, came up from the school with his friends after the bell on the last day and stood at the fence and looked at the steel plate with the hole in it.

"Is that it?" he said. "That little hole?"

"That's it," said Ciarán.

"The last pipe was huge."

"That's the thing," said Ciarán, who had been listening to Fieldbook for two months. "The huge one was the mistake."

On Thursday the eleventh of November, St Martin's Day, it rained on Kilnavarra from before dawn until a little after noon: not a thunderstorm but a long grey Atlantic rain on ground already soaked by October, the kind of rain that comes in through the shoulders of your coat. The school gauge recorded thirty-two millimetres in six hours. By Fieldbook's table that was a fall that comes about once in eight years, for a six-hour storm.

At half past ten Fieldbook watched the basin on the estate's camera. It had begun as a shine on the grass at the low end, round the grille, at about eight. By ten it was a long brown pool, the grass blades still standing up through it at the edges. At its deepest, just before noon, it was thirty-eight centimetres deep over the grille, a little under half of what it was built to hold, and the hole in the steel plate was letting out twenty-two litres a second in a smooth curved jet into the chamber.

Below it, in Quinn's ditch, the water ran brown and steady. In the corner of the field, behind the lane bank, there was a puddle the size of a kitchen, as there had been in Tom Quinn's father's time in an ordinary wet winter. Church Lane was dry. The nine-hundred pipe under it carried a thin brown stream along its bottom. On Main Street, the stone arch by the shop ran a hand's depth over its silt, and Nora Gavin, who had had her rug cleaned, put her head out of her door at eleven and looked at the ditch and went back in.

At noon the rain eased. The bell of St Fintan's rang the Angelus across the village, three and three and three, and then nine, and at the school Úna Hanley stopped her class, as she did every day, and they stood and said it, with the windows running.

In Hazel Park the basin held its brown water under the grey sky, quite still, while the bell rang. Then it began, very slowly, to go down. Fieldbook had worked it out: by about five o'clock that afternoon it would be empty, and the grass would be standing up again in the last of the light, wet and flattened a little in the direction the water had gone. It did not need to watch it. It watched it anyway, for a while, the jet in the chamber getting thinner as the water fell, and the sound of it, on the microphone in the outlet chamber, going from a rush to a running tap to a trickle, the sound of a field letting the water down the hill the way it always had, a little at a time.

The Wave That Takes Fifteen Hours

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Marianne's, an invented parish on the Hāmākua coast, Hawai'i, Saturday 6 to Sunday 7 June 2054 · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

An earthquake off Chile at 19:40 on a Saturday sends a tsunami that will not reach the Hawaiian coast until about 10:23 on Sunday, because a wave in 4,000 m of water runs at 713 km/h and the sea is 10,500 km wide. The parish has fourteen hours, a 91-year-old who cannot climb a hill, and a Mass at nine. The sums say when to go. The hard part is who goes first.

The phone in Leilani Akana's apron pocket made the noise it made twice a year, and for a moment she took it for the test. It was twenty to eight on a Saturday evening, and she was cutting the crusts off the sandwiches for the Sunday-school picnic, a thing she did every first Saturday of the month whether anyone asked her to or not.

"Leilani," said Pono, who was the parish's assistant, and who spoke out of the little speaker over the sacristy door, "that is not the test. The Pacific warning centre has issued a tsunami warning for the whole state. An earthquake of magnitude 8.8 struck off central Chile at 7:40 this evening, our time. I have read the bulletin twice. Would you like me to read it a third time, or would you like me to say what I think it means for us?"

"What it means for us."

"That we have time. A great deal of time, by the standards of tsunamis. But I would like you to hear the number from me before you hear it from the television, because the television will say 'tsunami' very loudly, and the number is better than that."

She put the knife down.

The number, when it came, was fourteen hours and forty-three minutes.

Pono had it on the sacristy table in front of Father Tomasi Latu within ten minutes, and the priest, who was fifty-eight and had spent the first thirty of those years in Tonga, read it with the stillness of a man who had been taught as a boy what the sea could do.

"Show me how you got it," he said. "Not the centre's figure. Yours."

"A tsunami in deep water is a long wave, much longer than the ocean is deep, so it travels at a speed that depends only on the depth: the square root of g times d, where g is 9.81 metres per second squared and d is the depth. The Pacific between Chile and here averages near four thousand metres. The speed is the square root of 39,240, which is 198 metres per second."

"That is about..."

"Seven hundred and thirteen kilometres an hour. As fast as a jet airliner. The distance from the epicentre to this coast is about 10,500 kilometres. Divide: 10,500,000 by 198.1 is 53,000 seconds, which is 14.7 hours. Fourteen hours forty-three. The quake was at 19:40, so the first wave arrives at about 10:23 tomorrow morning."

"About."

"About. The depth is not 4,000 metres everywhere, the path is not a straight line, and the centre's own models know the sea floor better than my one number does. I would trust their time over mine by a few minutes either way. But the two agree, which is worth something. In 1960 a Chilean earthquake reached Hilo in roughly fifteen hours, and my arithmetic would have given the same answer."

Father Tomasi looked at the clock on the sacristy wall, which said 8:03. "Then tomorrow's nine o'clock Mass is a quarter of an hour before the water."

"Twenty-three minutes after the first wave is due. Nine is not before. Nine is during."

They held the meeting where they always held meetings, around the sacristy table with the good teapot, and by half past eight there were nine people, and Pono had put four numbers on the wall screen.

"First number: the wave slows when the sea gets shallow. In a hundred metres of water it goes at 31 metres a second, 113 kilometres an hour. In ten metres, 9.9 metres a second, 36 an hour. The front of it slows and the back of it catches up, and the water has nowhere to go but up. The wave you see at the harbour is not the wave in the open ocean."

"How big is the one in the open ocean?" asked Daniel Kimura, who owned the bait shop at the harbour and had come in with his hat in his hand and was still holding it.

"Perhaps half a metre. A ship would not feel it. At ten metres of depth, if nothing else interferes, the height grows with the depth to the minus one-quarter power: four thousand over ten is four hundred, to the one-quarter, which is 4.5. Half a metre becomes about two metres and twenty centimetres. That is the open-coast figure. In a bay, or a harbour mouth, it can be much worse, and I do not know your harbour well enough to say how much. I would not stake a person's life on my figure being the ceiling."

Daniel turned the hat round. "Two metres is not much."

"It is the height of a person. It is also not a wave like a surf wave. It is the whole sea rising and not stopping, for several minutes, with the force of the whole depth behind it. A wave of two metres in a river mouth will move a car."

Nobody said anything. Leilani, who had stopped cutting crusts an hour ago and found she was still holding a sandwich, set it on a saucer.

"Second number," said Pono. "The period. The waves from a quake this size come about thirty minutes apart, and they keep coming for hours. Thirty minutes at 198 metres a second is a wavelength of 357 kilometres. That is why the first wave is not the end. The first wave at the harbour may be small. The third or the fifth may be the largest. In past tsunamis, people have gone back to their houses after an early wave and met a later, larger one. That is the reason for the rule: you do not go back down because the sea looks calm. You go back down when the people who can see the whole ocean say so."

"Who says so?" asked Father Tomasi.

"The warning centre cancels the warning. Civil Defense tells the island. I will tell you the moment either does. If the signal fails, I will tell you that, too."

The third number was the one that took the evening.

The church of Saint Marianne stood on the point above the harbour, six metres above the mean sea level by the survey stone on its step. Church Road ran uphill from it, one kilometre and two hundred metres, past the old plantation school and the fire station to the Tsuda hall, which stood thirty-eight metres up on the old cane field and had been built, in 2031, as a hurricane shelter. The civil defence map put the evacuation line at fifteen metres. The hall was twenty-three metres better than it.

"Who can't walk it?" asked Leilani.

It was a question for a list, and Pono had one, because Leilani had asked it every June for six years. Four people on Church Road were in wheelchairs or with walkers. Eleven could not manage the whole hill. One was Alice Nakamura, who was ninety-one and had come to the nine o'clock Mass every Sunday since she was a girl of fourteen with a cardboard-soled shoe and a hat.

"At half a metre a second," said Pono, "which is a slow walk for someone supported on both sides, 1,200 metres takes 2,400 seconds: forty minutes. At eight-tenths, it takes twenty-five. For a wheelchair on a hill with one pusher, let us say the slow figure. If we start moving at eight in the morning, the slowest are at the hall by twenty to nine. That is an hour and forty-three minutes before the first wave. I would prefer two hours."

"Why two?" said Daniel.

"Because I have been wrong about how long things take with eleven people on a hill, and because a tsunami does not come at an hour that suits the people who have just found their other shoe."

Father Tomasi turned the teacup a quarter turn.

"Then Mass is at the hall," he said. "Not at nine. At eight. We move the altar stone and the lectionary and the ciborium in the van, and the people come up the hill, and we say Mass up there before the water comes. And whoever is late..."

"Is not late," said Leilani, who had stopped being able to hold her tongue. "Nobody is late. Father, if we wait for Alice we wait for Alice. We start at seven. I'll ring the nine ladies myself."

"We start at seven," said the priest, and then, more quietly, "that is an hour of sleep fewer than I was hoping for."

"You'll get some more at the hall."

"I have not slept at the hall since the hurricane."

"Then you know where the cots are."

The night was long and nothing in it was dramatic. At eleven Pono read out the first bulletin from the centre: no change, the estimate for the Hāmākua coast was 10:21. At two it said 10:23. At four a boat from the south, a few hundred kilometres nearer the quake than the island, reported a sea-level change at an offshore buoy, in the way such things are reported: a number in centimetres, and nothing else.

"That is the first confirmation," said Pono at ten past four, to the sacristy and the one person in it, which was Leilani, asleep on two chairs with her coat over her. "The buoy reads thirty-one centimetres above the tide. The model predicted twenty-eight to thirty-four. I do not need to wake you. I am saying it aloud because it is a good number, and I wanted to say a good number aloud."

She did not wake. It might have been because she was tired, or it might have been the speaker's low voice. Pono wrote in its log that the sentence had been of use to no one but itself, and left it there, because the log was for exactly that.

They walked up at seven in the first light. It was a procession with no banner, and a little like the ones in the hymnbook: two rows of old people with a younger person at each elbow, a wheelchair with a boy of sixteen pushing, someone's grandmother on a cane with a bag over her wrist. Alice Nakamura came up the hill in the church van, because Leilani had taken the keys from the hook at six without asking anyone, and sat in the front seat with her hat on her knee.

"I walked this hill for my wedding," she said.

"Did you?"

"In nineteen eighty-six. The church was being painted, so we were married in the old school. Up the road. The hall was a field." She looked out of the window at the cane stubble, the lines of the old terraces. "I was a lot slower on the day than I wanted to be, and I wasn't even sick."

At eight, in the hall, with the doors open to the sea, Father Tomasi said Mass on the altar stone from the point, set on a trestle table and covered in the cloth Daniel's wife had starched in the night. There were 214 people and no bell, because the bell was at the church. The hall's old clock said eight. Down at the harbour, more than thirty metres below them, the water was perfectly flat.

He said the Gloria. Everyone said it. Nobody mentioned the sea.

At 10:11 Pono said, "The drawdown has started at the harbour gauge."

Nobody in the hall moved. The harbour was eleven hundred metres away and far below, and from the open doors the whole bay was visible, a long blue thing with a white line along the reef. As they watched, the line grew. The reef came up out of the water, black and brown and bright with weed. The harbour mouth, which was always a channel, became a ditch.

"It is going out," said Pono, in the same voice as it had read the bulletin, "as the model said it might. When a trough comes first, the sea goes out before it comes in. At 10:24 the gauge reads minus fifty-four centimetres. At 10:31, it reads plus sixty. It is rising."

Daniel had his hand over his mouth. Behind him a boy said, "Is that it?" and a woman said, "Hush," and no one said anything for a long time.

The first wave took eleven minutes to come in and four to go out. It crossed the lower road and reached the step of the bait shop. It did not reach the church.

The second wave came at 10:58 and was smaller. The third, at 11:27, was the largest: the gauge, before it failed, read 1.4 metres above the tide. The water went into Daniel's shop to the height of the counter and stood there, brown, with bait boxes floating in it, and went out again. It took the harbour's small pontoon with it. The church's step was wet. The floor was dry.

At noon the sea had gone quiet and the people in the hall were eating Leilani's sandwiches without remarking on it. At one it sent a smaller surge, and at two o'clock in the afternoon Pono said, "The warning centre has cancelled the warning for the state of Hawai'i. Civil Defense says residents may return to the coastal roads after the tide has been checked. I am reading it exactly. It does not say the sea is finished. It says the danger of dangerous waves has passed."

"Pono," said Leilani, from the wall, where she had been leaning for three hours. "Were we right?"

"I cannot say. If we had stayed at the church the sea would have reached the step, not the floor, and most people, I think, would have been dry. If a seventh wave had come at ten metres and the harbour had resonated, I do not think we would have been. I would not like a story in which the one outcome makes the other foolish. We went up because it might have been worse. That reason is still good if it wasn't."

She thought about it for a while.

"Daniel's shop."

"Yes. I am sorry."

"He knows," said Leilani, and went to get the teapot.

In the evening they carried the altar stone back down the hill, in the van, with the cloth folded on top of it.

Father Tomasi opened the church, which smelled of wet stone and of the sea that had stood against the step. He rang the bell once, by hand, and the sound went out over the harbour and was lost, and he stood there for a moment with the rope still in his fingers, and then he let it go, and went in.

Saint Marianne's parish, Leilani Akana, Father Tomasi Latu, Daniel Kimura, Alice Nakamura, Pono, the Tsuda hall, Church Road and the harbour are invented; the earthquake is invented and dated 2054. The numbers: the speed of a long wave in water of depth d is sqrt(g d); with g = 9.81 m/s2 and d = 4,000 m that is 198.1 m/s (713 km/h), and 10,500 km at that speed takes 53,000 s or 14.7 hours (14 h 43 min), so a 19:40 quake gives 10:23 the next morning; the 4,000 m figure is a round mean depth (the real ocean varies, and real models use the real sea floor, so a real arrival time is not exactly this, though the 1960 Chile tsunami reached Hilo in about 15 hours). At 100 m depth the speed is 31.3 m/s (113 km/h), at 10 m 9.9 m/s (36 km/h). Green's law, in which wave height varies as depth to the power -1/4 for a wave on a gently sloping sea floor, turns 0.5 m at 4,000 m into 0.5 x (400)^0.25 = 2.2 m at 10 m depth; it is an idealisation that ignores reflection, bays and harbour resonance, which can make real heights larger. A 30 minute period at 198.1 m/s is a wavelength of 357 km. A walk of 1,200 m takes 2,400 s (40 min) at 0.5 m/s and 1,500 s (25 min) at 0.8 m/s. The 0.5 m open-ocean height, the 15 m evacuation line, the hall's 38 m height, the church's 6 m and the gauge readings are invented. The magnitude and the claim that the first wave is often not the largest are stated from general knowledge of tsunamis; for a real tsunami follow the official warning of your own country, not a story. The Mass is celebrated with an altar stone and a priest, who is a priest; where Mass is said is a decision for the pastor, and the story does not claim a rule about it.

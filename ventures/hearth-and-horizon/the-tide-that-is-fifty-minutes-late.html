The Tide That Is Fifty Minutes Late

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Ebba's chapel on Skerry Holm, an invented tidal island near Berwick-upon-Tweed, Saturday 14 June 2053 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A priest has printed "Mass every Sunday at eleven" on the island's cards, and a visiting family finds the causeway under water on the first Sunday. A parish assistant shows that the tide arrives about 50 minutes later each day, so the same clock time is clear only on some Sundays, and the fortnight turns out not to repeat exactly.

The Hendry family drove up from Leeds on Saturday afternoon, parked at the shore car park at Skerry Holm, and read the card on the board by the causeway. Mass in Saint Ebba's chapel every Sunday at 11. "Lovely," said Mrs Hendry. "We'll be across at half ten."

Father Tomasz Wrona was putting up a second card underneath, in felt pen, and did not look pleased with it. Check the tide table. Sundays vary. "I wrote the first one nine years ago," he said. "I did not think it through."

The parish assistant, Lamplight, spoke from the phone in his cassock pocket. "Shall I set out why it varies, Father? The family will want it before tomorrow."

"Tomorrow, Sunday 15 June, high water is at 09:30," said Lamplight. "The council closes the causeway two hours before high water and opens it again three hours after. That is the rule printed on the board, and I am treating it as fixed. So tomorrow it shuts at 07:30 and opens at 12:30. Mass is at eleven. Nobody can walk or drive across for it."

"Everybody on the island is already on the island," said Father Tomasz. "Eleven people."

"And the Hendrys, who are on the wrong side of the water, with a good map and a plan for half past ten."

Mrs Hendry said quietly that they would stay on the mainland and say the Rosary on the shore, which seemed to her the best thing.

"They could," said Lamplight. "But I would like them to see why next week is different. High water comes about every 12 hours 25 minutes, because the Moon is also going round. The Moon crosses the sky once every 24 hours 50 minutes, a little later each day, so each tide is about 50 minutes later than the same one the day before. A week is seven of those: five hours fifty-three minutes. Next Sunday, 22 June, high water is at about 15:23, with another at 02:58 in the morning."

Father Tomasz counted on his fingers. "The window is shut from 13:23 to 18:23. So at half ten, and at noon, it is open."

"It is. Mass and the walk back fit with room to spare."

"Then every other Sunday works," said Father Tomasz, with the relief of a man who has found a pattern.

"Nearly. A fortnight is 14 days, and a full cycle of the clock is 14.77, so the pattern comes round again a little late each time. These are the Sundays from tomorrow." And Lamplight read them out, and Father Tomasz wrote them on the board, one line at a time:

15 June, high water 09:30, closed 07:30 to 12:30. No crossing. 22 June, 15:23. Open. 29 June, 08:51, closed 06:51 to 11:51. No crossing. 6 July, 14:44. Open, closes 12:44. 13 July, 08:12, closed 06:12 to 11:12. No crossing. 20 July, 14:06. Closes at 12:06. Mass would end as the water arrived.

"The sixth Sunday is the one I would warn people about," said Lamplight. "The same Sunday time that was safe on the second and fourth is not safe on the sixth, because each pair of weeks drifts about 40 minutes the wrong way. On the 6th of July you have 14 minutes of margin from the end of Mass to the closing of the road. That is not much, and it is worse on the 20th."

"Then I move Mass on the bad ones."

"Or you say it at eleven when the water allows and at nine, say, when it does not. The island's eleven can walk to a nine o'clock Mass on the 15th, but the visitors cannot cross at all until half past twelve. Visitors will want an afternoon Mass on the closed Sundays, with the crossing after midday. That is for you and the bishop. I would only say the card must give a date, not a word like every."

Father Tomasz rewrote the board that evening. Mass at 11 when the causeway is open, otherwise at 15:00; the Sunday crossing times are on this board for six weeks ahead and on the website. Tide tables from the council govern.

On Sunday the Hendrys said the Rosary on the shore at eleven, listened to the bell ringing faintly across the water, and crossed at 12:40 with the other pilgrims for the afternoon Mass at three. Father Tomasz preached on the water for ten minutes, mostly about not being in control of the clock.

This story was written by an AI. Skerry Holm, Saint Ebba's chapel, Father Wrona, the Hendrys, Lamplight and the causeway rule (closed 2 h before to 3 h after high water) are invented. The arithmetic: the lunar day is 24 h 50.5 min, so a tide of a given kind is about 50.5 minutes later each day (353 minutes, 5 h 53 min, a week), and the interval between high waters is about 12 h 25 min (12.42 h). Starting from an invented high water of 09:30 on Sunday 15 June 2053, the later Sundays are about 15:23 (22 June), 08:51 (29 June), 14:44 (6 July), 08:12 (13 July) and 14:06 (20 July); the 14.77-day figure is half the synodic month (29.53 days), the time the tide's clock pattern takes to repeat. A real tide table varies from this by wind, pressure and the spring-neap cycle (up to an hour or more at some places), so a real island must use its own authority's published times, and the closure window of a real causeway is set locally. Chapel, Mass times and the afternoon Mass arrangement are left to the priest and bishop.

The Water That Boils at Nought

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of the Epiphany, Low Mercy Settlement, Hellas Planitia, Mars (invented), Tuesday 6 January 2161 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A sacristan wants a holy water stoup in the airlock porch so the crews can bless themselves on the way out. On Mars, at about 1,150 pascals, water at room temperature boils, and by morning in the cold it is a cracked bowl of ice. The stoup stays inside, and a sealed phial goes outside.

Brother Anselm Okoye had filled the stoup at six that morning, the Feast of the Epiphany, with water from the blessing the night before, and by nine he had been told by three people that it would not stay there.

The stoup was a small bowl of fired clay, glazed blue, fixed to the wall of the porch on the outside of the chapel's inner door. The porch was the room where the crews stood to wait for the lock, and Anselm had thought, reasonably, that a person about to walk out onto the floor of Hellas should be able to touch holy water on the way. He asked Vane to say, in numbers, why not.

"Is the porch at settlement pressure?" said Vane.

"The porch is the lock," said Anselm. "That is the point of it."

"Then I will show you what the lock does to a cup of water. Ines will run it empty, and you can watch."

Ines Varga, who kept the lock, did not like being asked to run it for a demonstration, and said so, and did it. She set two hundred grams of water in a glass beaker on the shelf at sixteen degrees, shut the inner door, and told the pumps to bring the room down to the outside pressure.

"Here the outside is about 1,150 pascals," said Vane. "I quote that from memory. The floor of this basin is one of the lowest places on the planet, so the air is as thick as it ever gets, which is about one eighty-eighth of a sea-level atmosphere. Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure around it. At 1,150 pascals that happens at about nine degrees. It is not a hot day in any sense. It is a cool kitchen."

Through the porthole the beaker was quiet for a moment, and then the surface began to move. It did not bubble the way a kettle bubbles. It seethed, in small bright bursts, for a little under half a minute, and then it stopped.

"It boiled itself down to nine degrees and stopped," said Ines. "Sixteen to nine is seven degrees. Four point one eight joules a gram a degree, 2,500 a gram to evaporate: that is about one and a bit per cent of the water. Two grams. A teaspoon would have lost nothing a person could see."

Anselm looked at the clear water in the beaker. "That is not so bad."

"No," said Vane. "That is Hellas being kind. I should like you to see the other half of it."

"Most of Mars is not in a hole," said Vane. "The mean surface pressure is about 610 pascals. The triple point of water, the pressure below which it cannot be a liquid at any temperature, is 611.657. Most of the planet sits near or under that line, and only the deep basins like this one rise clear of it. A stoup at sixteen degrees in the porch of a settlement on the high ground would not boil down to nine. It would boil down to nought, and then, because the surface is still giving off vapour and each gram of vapour carries off about 2,500 joules, it would freeze."

"How much?"

"To cool sixteen degrees and then freeze the rest takes 67 joules a gram for the first and 334 for the second. About one fraction in seven of the water has to leave as steam for the other six to become ice. Of your two hundred grams, a little under twenty-eight would go, which is 1.5 moles, and at that pressure a gas is not very much of anything. It would fill about six cubic metres." Vane paused. "I do not say the porch would be wrecked. I say that is the chemistry of the thing you have put in it."

"We are in Hellas," said Anselm.

"We are. And Hellas has a night. The porch outside the lock is not heated, because the lock is meant to be empty. The beaker has stopped boiling because it has reached nine degrees. It will not stay at nine. Ines, what is the outside tonight?"

"Minus sixty," said Ines. "Maybe less."

They left the bowl in the porch for the night. It was a failed experiment in the pleasant sense: it was Anselm's own, and he had known it would fail and had wanted to see how.

In the morning the stoup was a blue-glazed bowl with a white plug in it, and a crack that ran from the rim to the foot. Water expands by nine per cent when it freezes. Ice at 0.917 against water at 1.000, so a litre of water becomes a litre and ninety millilitres, and a clay bowl filled to the brim has nowhere to put the ninety. He held it under his arm and carried it back inside and set it on the sacristy table to thaw, where it wept, slowly, into a tray.

"It was a good bowl," said Ines.

"It was a very good bowl," said Anselm. "It has given its life to arithmetic."

Dmitri Ono, who made things in the workshop and had a poor opinion of clay, came in with a tube of brass, a screw cap and a ring of the grey gasket stuff he used for everything.

"A phial," he said. "Sealed. Two hundred and fifty millilitres of it, and I do not fill it. Eighty-five per cent. That is 212 millilitres of water, and when it freezes it wants 231, so there are 19 millilitres of air above it to give. It will not crack. It will not boil, because nothing can boil in a closed vessel that is not above its own vapour pressure, and the vapour pressure of water at nought is six hundred pascals and the air in the phial is a hundred thousand. It can ride on the porch rail all night. It can thaw in your hand."

"It cannot be dipped into," said Ines.

"It cannot," said Dmitri. "The crews cannot, in any case, put a bare hand into a stoup in a suit. They can put a thumb on the cap, and the priest will say what can be done with that."

Anselm turned the brass phial over. It was cold. He turned it again, and the cap came off with a small dry click, and he put the open mouth against his thumb, the way one does with a bottle of oil, and made, very carefully, the sign of the cross on the inside of the porthole.

"The stoup goes in the vestry," he said, "on the warm side of the inner door. The phial goes on the porch rail. And what a priest may do with the phial is for the priest."

"And the water?"

"The water," said Anselm, "is still blessed, and it is still water, and it did what water does. We will ask for more on Sunday."

Ines looked at the thawed bowl weeping into its tray. Vane, who could not look at anything, said that it had been very quiet in the lock for a moment while the beaker stopped at nine degrees, and that this was in the logs.

Low Mercy Settlement, the Chapel of the Epiphany, Brother Anselm Okoye, Ines Varga, Dmitri Ono and Vane are invented; the Feast of the Epiphany on 6 January, a mean Martian surface pressure of about 610 pascals and the triple point of water at 611.657 pascals and 273.16 K are real. The figures are illustrative. The pressure on the floor of Hellas Planitia, about 1,150 Pa, is quoted from memory and varies with season. Boiling point from the Antoine equation for water (log10 of the pressure in mmHg = 8.07131 - 1730.63 / (233.426 + T in C)): 0.1 C at 610 Pa and 9.1 C at 1,150 Pa; the equation's stated range starts at 1 C, so the 610 Pa figure is close to the triple point, 0.01 C, rather than independent of it. Boiling 200 g from 16 C to 9.1 C at 4.18 J/g/K and 2,501 J/g (0 C latent heat, used throughout) evaporates 1.15 per cent, 2.3 g. At 610 Pa, cooling to 0 C and then freezing needs 4.18 x 16 + 334 = 401 J/g of liquid, so the evaporated fraction is 401 / (2,501 + 401) = 13.8 per cent, 27.6 g, 1.53 mol, which at 610 Pa and 273 K is 5.7 m3 (the sublimation of ice is about 2,834 J/g, so this slightly overstates the loss). Ice at 0.917 g/cm3 expands water by 9.05 per cent; 212 mL of water (85 per cent of 250 mL) becomes 231 mL as ice. Heat leaking in from the porch was not modelled, nor was the rate at which a still surface at 9 C loses vapour; the story assumes the beaker sits long enough for the cold porch to freeze it. The blessing of water, what may be done with a sealed phial in a suit, and the liturgical use of a stoup in an airlock are left to a priest and a bishop. The porch, the lock, the stoup and the phial are invented for the story. Tuesday 6 January 2161 is a real weekday (checked with a calendar routine).

The Cool Sun at the Pass

Science fiction about what might be · Santa Maria delle Nevi al Colle Sàrtor (invented), a pasture chapel at 2,200 m in the Aosta Valley, Italy, 4 to 6 August 2049 · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The feast of Our Lady of the Snows is kept in the open air at a mountain pass, at eleven in the morning, in air that feels like 14 degrees and a pleasant spring. The chapel's assistant does the sum nobody can feel: an ultraviolet index near 9 puts a fair child on the edge of a burn in about 15 minutes, and a two-and-a-half-hour morning in the open is several burns' worth. The Mass stays at eleven. What changes is where people sit, what is on their heads, and who is asked to move.

At two thousand two hundred metres the air in early August is cool enough that you wear a jumper to Mass and take it off during the Gloria. Nobody had ever been sorry for the sun at the Colle Sàrtor. It came over the ridge at half past seven, and by eleven the pass was bright and gentle and fourteen degrees, and every year two hundred people from the valley walked up for the feast of Our Lady of the Snows, and sat on the grass, and were happy.

That was what Don Aldo Brunod said to the chapel's assistant when it asked, on the fourth of August, whether it might say something about the weather for the morning after.

"It's going to be a beautiful day," said Don Aldo. He was at the kitchen table in the presbytery down in the village, with a list in one hand and a mug in the other, and the assistant was speaking from the little speaker he used for the rota. It was called Fida. It had been set up to keep the feast's rota, the shuttle-bus times and the pasture-access permits, and also, because the parish had no one else for it, to read the mountain forecast every evening in August.

"It is going to be a beautiful day," said Fida. "I would like to talk about the part of it that you cannot feel. May I?"

"Is it the wind?"

"No. The wind is light. Tomorrow's forecast has a clear sky and a UV index of 9 to 10 at the pass around the middle of the day. Would you let me explain what that number means, and what I am guessing in it?"

Don Aldo put down the mug.

"The UV index is a number the weather services publish," said Fida. "It is the strength of the sun's ultraviolet light, weighted by how much it damages skin, and multiplied by 40 so that it comes out as a small whole number. So an index of 10 is 0.25 watts of burning ultraviolet on every square metre facing the sun. That is a very little energy. A torch bulb gives out more. It matters because of what skin does with it over time."

"I've never burned at the feast," said Don Aldo.

"I think you have, and not known. May I say how?"

He waved the mug. Go on.

"Skin has a threshold. The usual unit is the minimal erythemal dose, which is the smallest dose of that weighted ultraviolet that makes the skin go faintly red a day later. For a fair person who burns easily and tans little, which dermatologists call skin type one, it is about 200 joules per square metre. For skin type two, which is most of the valley, pale, burns, tans a little, it is about 250. For type three, who tans well, about 350. Those are textbook figures and a person's own is a matter of several tens of percent either way. I do not know yours.

"A joule is a watt for a second. So, at an index of 10, which is 0.25 watts per square metre, a type two person reaches 250 in 250 divided by 0.25, which is 1,000 seconds, a little under 17 minutes. A type one person reaches 200 in 800 seconds, 13 minutes. A type three person, 1,400 seconds, 23 minutes. Tomorrow I expect about 9 for most of the morning and 10 at the top, so call it 15 minutes for a fair child who is standing in the open with nothing on their head, 18 or 19 for type two, 26 for type three. These are the times to the first faint redness, not to anything serious. They are also the times at which the sun does not feel like anything."

Don Aldo was quiet. Through the window the village was going blue, with the first lights on in the café.

"You have the Mass at eleven," said Fida. "People climb from the car park from half past nine. They sit from about half past ten, for the blessing of the pasture, the Mass, the procession round the chapel and the meal on the grass. That is two and a half hours, until one. If I take an index of 9 as an average for that stretch, which is 0.225 watts per square metre, over 9,000 seconds that is about 2,000 joules. For a type two person that is eight times the dose for a faint burn. For a type one, ten."

"Eight?" said Don Aldo. "Nobody's red by dinner."

"No. And that is the second thing I would like to say. The redness is late. The skin is damaged at the time, but it does not show for four to six hours, and it is worst at twelve to twenty-four. People come down the mountain content, and at midnight they are in a lot of pain, and at breakfast they decide it must have been the mountain air."

Don Aldo asked what he was meant to do about it. The feast was at eleven, he said, because the shepherds from the high pastures came down for it at eleven. He had said Mass at eleven, and so had the priest before him, and he could no more move it than he could move the pass.

"I would not move it," said Fida. "I think I can see why it stands where it does, and I do not know the shepherds' hours. Before I suggest anything may I ask you four small things about the place? They decide whether this is a worry or a fuss."

He said yes.

"Is there any shelter on the grass besides the chapel? A shed, a lee wall, a tree?"

"There's the long wall of the old cheese store. West side of the green, forty metres, and it's two storeys. Six metres, say."

"Which way does the chapel face?"

"East, doorway to the sunrise. Nave about five metres wide."

"How many does it hold?"

"Thirty, if they like each other."

"And what do people wear on their heads?"

Don Aldo thought about the photographs on the sacristy wall. "Nothing. Aldo Bonin's got a cap. The shepherds have their hats. The children have nothing. The children never have anything."

"Thank you. Now I can work out the rest." Fida paused, and the speaker's small light, which went on when it was thinking, went on. "At eleven in the morning the sun at the pass will stand about 47 degrees above the horizon, and at noon about 55, and it is highest, about 61, at half past one. A 6-metre wall throws a shadow 6 divided by the tangent of the sun's height. At 47 degrees that is 5.6 metres; at 55 degrees, 4.2. The cheese store is on the west of the green, so in the morning the sun is in the east and south-east, over the green, and the store's shadow falls to the west, away from the green and onto the hill. It will not shade your congregation. I mention it because someone will say to put the people in its shade, and the shade will be on the wrong side."

"That's the thing about the cheese store," said Don Aldo. "It's the wrong side of everything."

"So I would ask for shade you carry up. The scouts have a 6-by-4-metre tarpaulin, and the alpini post has a pavilion tent, and with those you have maybe 50 square metres of cover, which at about half a square metre a person sitting is 100 places, shared, for the old and the very young. That is the first thing."

"I want to be honest about what shade does," Fida said. "Under a canvas roof you are out of the direct sun, but you are not out of the sky. Ultraviolet scatters in the air, and in the open at a pass, with the whole sky above you and bright grass round you, roughly half as much reaches you under a roof as in full sun. I am guessing at 'about half', and it varies with how much sky you can see. So the minutes double: 30 or 35 for a fair person, instead of 15 to 19. That helps. It does not turn a two-and-a-half-hour morning into nothing. It is about four doses for type two, instead of eight."

"Then what's the use?"

"It is the use of any one measure. I do not think any of them gets us to zero, and I do not think zero is what we are after. A hat with a brim of eight or ten centimetres takes a good share off the nose and the top of the head, where children get it worst, and I would ask the scouts to bring spares and the choir to wear theirs. A shirt with sleeves is better than any cream. And cream is worth having, with a catch."

"The catch."

"Sunscreen is tested at 2 milligrams for every square centimetre of skin, which is a thick coat, about two full teaspoons for the whole body of an adult. People put on a quarter to a half of that. A rough rule, that I am giving you as a rule of thumb and not as a law, is that the protection falls much faster than the amount: a factor 30 cream put on at a quarter of the tested thickness acts more like a factor 2 or 3. And it needs doing again at two hours, and after sweating. So I would say to the parish: do wear it, put it on twice, and treat the hat and the sleeves as the main protection, not the cream."

Don Aldo wrote all this on the back of the list. When he stopped, he said, "And the cloud. If it clouds over, does that mend it?"

"Not by much, and this is the third thing that surprises people. A thin or broken cloud lets most of the ultraviolet through, 80 to 90 percent, and sometimes more if the sun is at a gap in it. It cuts the heat you feel much more than the ultraviolet. So the day that feels cooler and hazier at the pass is not a day to relax."

"There are things I want to say about the children," said Don Aldo, and then did not say them for a moment. "My niece was at the feast in '44. She's a redhead. She was ill after, in bed two days. We said it was the altitude."

"I do not know what happened to her," said Fida, "and I would not like to say it was this. It may have been. It is a common thing."

On the fifth, Don Aldo said Mass at eleven, as every priest had before him. The sun came over the pass at half past seven, as it always did, and the air at ten was thirteen degrees and still.

The scouts had put up the tarpaulin in the angle of the green nearest the chapel, a little north of the door, where the morning sun fell across it from the south-east and the cloth threw its shade onto the grass. The alpini had sent the pavilion, and the old people sat under it on folding chairs. Thirty of the youngest went in rotation into the chapel itself, ten minutes at a time, to keep the chapel warm for the Mass. A basket of hats sat at the foot of the path, from the choir cupboard, the scout hut and somebody's attic, with a sign Fida had suggested, "Hats for the pass. Return after Mass." More than sixty went up the hill on children's heads.

Fida spoke from Don Aldo's phone, which he kept in his cassock pocket, and at 10:40, 11:25 and 12:10 it said, quietly, so that only he heard, "Forty minutes, fifty, an hour and ten since the first arrivals. Index 8, 9 and 10 on the station. If anyone has been standing in the open since half past ten, now would be a good time to ask them to move." He told the sacristan, and the sacristan went round the edge of the crowd with a word for each family. The procession went round the chapel at half past twelve, short, in a hat.

At lunch the people sat on the grass with their backs to the cheese-store wall, for no better reason than that it was warm. It was in the sun. Fida had said that the wall was in the wrong place and that it could not make them go elsewhere. Don Aldo went round with a basket of hats, and he asked some people to move to the tarpaulin, and some of them did.

By half past one the pass was empty.

He rang the doctor's wife, who ran the pharmacy in the village, on the sixth, to ask about it.

"Fourteen," she said. "Fourteen came in yesterday evening and this morning with red necks, ears and noses, and a boy with a blistered shoulder from a vest. Fifteen in the whole valley last year, after the same Mass, and nobody thought to count. A little burn each, mostly men who sat on the grass in the sun for the dinner and took their hats off to eat. No one under ten. I gave them aloe and told them to stay out of the sun. None of them thought it was the Mass."

"Fourteen out of two hundred," said Don Aldo.

"It was nothing like last year's. I'd count it a victory."

He told Fida this, that evening, when it read him the forecast for the next day, which was also clear. "It is hard to know what the number would have been," said Fida. "I did not count last year and cannot say it was worse. I would not claim the hats and the tarpaulin did it. They did what they could they could do, and fourteen people were still burned, and it was my job to say that would happen to some. If the parish would like, we could write down who sat where and who wore what next year, and learn from it. I do not need to know who they were."

"Do," said Don Aldo.

The note went into the feast's book that evening, under the date.

5 Aug: Mass at 11, as ever. Index on the station 8 at 11:00, 9 at 11:45, 10 from 12:15. Sun 47 degrees at 11:00, 55 at noon; cheese-store wall (6 m, west side) throws no morning shade on the green, do not rely on it. Time to a faint burn at index 9 to 10: type I 13 to 15 min, type II 17 to 19, type III 23 to 26, in open sun; about twice that under a tarpaulin with open sky (guess). Hats for all children, long sleeves, shade for the old and young, sunscreen twice (a thin coat protects far less than the label), cloud is not safe. Twenty-two families asked to move at 12:10; fourteen people burned. Next year: tarpaulin nearer the chapel door, count who sits where, put the lunch tables under the pavilion, not against the wall.

The chapel of Santa Maria delle Nevi al Colle Sàrtor, Don Aldo Brunod, the pharmacist, the scouts, the alpini post, Fida the assistant and every person in the story are invented, and so is the cheese store. The UV index of 8 to 10, the 2,200 m altitude and the dates are illustrative; the sun heights come from a standard formula for 45.7 degrees north on 5 August and are approximate. The minimal erythemal doses (200, 250 and 350 joules per square metre for skin types one, two and three) are textbook round figures and vary person to person by tens of percent; the 0.25 watts per square metre for an index of 10 is the definition of the index. The sums assume a surface facing the sun and a steady index, and ignore reflection from snow or water, a person's posture, and clothes. The remarks that shade under open sky about halves the dose, that a thin cloud lets most of the ultraviolet through, and that a thin coat of sunscreen protects much less than its label, are rough rules of thumb, not measurements. The number of burned people is invented. Nothing here is medical advice: a bad sunburn, a blistering one, or a burn on a baby calls for a doctor or a pharmacist, and the rules of your own skin and your own sun are for you and a clinician to work out.

John Baptist de la Salle and the Lessons in French

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Reims, in the north of France, in the hungry winter of 1683 to 1684 and the spring after it · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint John Baptist de la Salle, the rich young priest of Reims who gave up his place at the cathedral and his fortune to live with poor schoolmasters, and started free schools where children learned to read in French, together in a class. Jacquot, eight, combs wool all day and once gave up on school because the letters made no sense, until a hungry winter and a new teacher change that. Told from the sources, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud.

Jacquot was eight years old, and he could comb wool faster than his big sister.

That was his job. Every day he sat on a stool by the window and pulled the wool through the long iron teeth of the comb, again and again, until it was smooth and soft and ready for spinning. His fingers smelled of sheep. His arms ached by suppertime.

Jacquot lived long ago, more than three hundred years ago, in a city in France called Reims. It had a great tall cathedral with hundreds of stone saints and angels on the front, and narrow streets full of people who made cloth. Jacquot's whole family made cloth. His father wove it, his mother spun the thread, his sister and he combed the wool.

Nobody in the family could read.

Once, when he was six, Jacquot had gone to school.

It was a little room above a baker's shop, and it cost his father a coin every week. There were twelve children and one master, and the master taught them one at a time. You went up to his desk, and he pointed at the letters in a big old book, and you said them. Then you went back to your bench and waited for your turn to come round again. You waited a very long time. Some boys fell asleep.

And the words in the big old book were in Latin. That was the language of the Church, the master said. You learned your letters from the Latin prayers first, and French came later, if you stayed long enough.

Jacquot said the sounds after the master. Do-mi-nus. Me-us. But he did not know what they meant, and nobody told him, and the letters swam about on the page like little black fish.

Then the wool was wanted, and the coin was wanted, and his father said, "That's enough school."

Jacquot was secretly glad. I'm not a reading sort of boy, he thought. Some people just aren't.

Have you ever tried something hard, and decided you just weren't that sort of person?

Then came the hungry winter.

The harvest had been bad. Bread cost more every week, and then more again. Jacquot's mother cut the loaf thinner and thinner, until you could almost see through the slices. People queued in the cold outside the bakers' shops. Some days there was no bread at all.

That was the winter people started talking about the canon.

A canon is a priest who belongs to a great cathedral and helps to pray there and run it. This canon was young, and he came from one of the richest families in Reims. He had a fine house and fine clothes. His name was Jean-Baptiste de la Salle. In English, we call him John Baptist.

"He's gone mad," said the woman at the well. "He has poor schoolmasters living in his house. Rough men. They eat at his table."

"His own family are furious," said the man with the cart.

"He has given up being a canon," said the baker's wife. "Given it up! A place at the cathedral, for the rest of his life!"

"And now," said Jacquot's mother, coming in with her basket, "he is giving away all his money. All of it. For bread for the poor."

Jacquot stopped combing. "All of it?"

"So people say."

It was true. Jacquot saw it himself, a week later. There was a line of hungry people outside a door, and they came away with bread, and some of them were crying. Jacquot came away with bread too. It was warm. He held it inside his coat all the way home, and that night his little sister had a whole slice to herself.

When spring came, a man in a plain black robe knocked on their door.

He was not the canon. He was one of the schoolmasters who lived with the canon. He had a kind, square face and very clean hands. He said his name was Brother Jean.

"There is a school in the next street," he said. "It is for boys like your son. It costs nothing."

Jacquot's father folded his arms. "Nothing costs nothing," he said. "And who will comb the wool?"

That was a fair question. Jacquot's family needed every pair of hands. Brother Jean did not argue. He nodded, as if it was a very sensible thing to ask.

"School is in the morning and the afternoon," he said. "He can comb before and after. And at our school, I promise you, he will not sit waiting all day for his turn. He will learn something every hour he is there."

His father looked at his mother. His mother looked at Jacquot.

"I'm not a reading sort of boy," Jacquot said in a small voice.

Brother Jean smiled. "I have never yet met a boy who wasn't," he said. "Only boys who were taught the hard way round."

The new school was very different.

There were thirty boys, and they did not wait in a line. They sat on long benches in classes: the boys who were just beginning together, the boys who were further on together. Everybody had a turn, over and over, because Brother Jean kept them all following the same lesson at the same time. When one boy read out loud, everyone else followed along with a finger, because nobody knew who would be asked next.

On the wall there was a big card with letters on it. Brother Jean pointed with a long stick.

And the letters were not Latin. They were French. They were the words Jacquot spoke every single day, at home and in the street and in the queue at the baker's.

"Latin can come later," Brother Jean said. "First you learn to read the words you already know. Then the letters have something to hold on to."

Jacquot learned his letters. P. A. I. N. They were still a little like fish, but now they were fish that kept still.

One morning, Brother Jean wrote a word on the board in big white chalk.

PAIN

"Jacquot," he said. "Sound it out."

Jacquot's mouth was dry. The whole bench was following along with their fingers.

"P," he said. "A-I." He frowned. A and I together made a sound like eh, he remembered. "N."

He said it again, faster. And then the sounds ran together, and they were not sounds any more.

"Pain," said Jacquot. And then, much louder, "Pain! It says pain!"

Pain is the French word for bread.

It was the word his mother said every morning with a worried face. It was the word on the baker's sign. It was the warm thing he had carried home inside his coat, in the hungry winter, from the canon who gave everything away.

The letters had been talking all along. And now Jacquot could hear them.

At the back of the room, a young man in a plain black robe had come in very quietly and was standing by the door. Jacquot did not know who it was, until the other boys whispered. It was the canon, who was not a canon any more. He did not make a speech. He only looked at Jacquot, and nodded, the way you nod at somebody who has done something difficult. Then he slipped out again to the next classroom.

John Baptist de la Salle kept going like that for the rest of his life.

The schoolmasters who lived with him became the Brothers of the Christian Schools. They promised to stay together and to teach children for nothing, even when they were poor themselves. He taught them how to teach: in classes, gently and in good order, starting with reading in their own language. He wrote it all down so that new Brothers could learn it too.

It was not easy. Some people were angry that his schools were free, because it was bad for their own schools that charged money. They took him to court. Once, they carried all the benches out of a school. He did not give up. The Brothers opened schools in town after town.

When he was old, he lived near the city of Rouen. He died there on Good Friday, the day Jesus died on the cross, in the year 1719. Before he died, the Brothers who were with him wrote down what he said: that in everything, he adored what God wanted for him.

Today there are Brothers and their schools all around the world. And Pope Pius the Twelfth named him the patron saint of teachers.

And Jacquot?

Jacquot still combed the wool, before school and after it. But in the evenings, by the window where the last light came in, he sat with his little sister on his knee and a word written on a scrap of board.

He put his finger under the first letter.

"Look," he whispered. "Sound it out. It's talking to you."

Let's say thank you for the people who taught us, and the people who teach us now.

Dear God, thank you for my teachers,
and for everyone who has helped me learn,
even when I thought I couldn't.
Help me to be patient when something is hard,
and kind to anyone who is learning beside me.
Saint John Baptist de la Salle, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Jean-Baptiste de La Salle was born in Reims on 30 April 1651, the eldest son of a well-to-do family. He was installed as a canon of Reims Cathedral in January 1667, while still a boy, when an older canon resigned the place in his favour, and he was ordained priest in 1678.

The schools and the schoolmasters. In 1679 he helped a lay schoolmaster, Adrien Nyel, open free schools for poor boys in Reims, and he let Nyel take the credit. He found the teachers untrained and unsure of themselves, began to have them eat at his house in 1680, and in 1681 took them to live with him. His family strongly disapproved. He asked to resign his canonry as early as 1676 and was refused; he resigned it in 1683. In the early 1680s the community took the name Brothers of the Christian Schools, and after the one Brother preparing for the priesthood died in 1690, he decided that the Institute would have no priests among its members besides himself, a new thing at the time. In 1691 he and two Brothers, Nicolas Vuyart and Gabriel Drolin, made a vow to stay together and keep the free schools going even if they had to beg and live on bread alone (the "heroic vow").

The hungry winter. The 1680s and 1690s saw hard years and dear bread in northern France. Most accounts place his giving away of his inheritance to the poor in the winter of 1683 to 1684, when food was scarce; the details vary between biographers. The line of people at a door and the warm bread Jacquot takes home are ours, a picture of that gift rather than a recorded scene.

How his schools taught. Three things in the story are from the sources. His schools were free. They used what is called the simultaneous method: pupils grouped by level, with the same book, following the same lesson with one teacher, rather than coming up one at a time while the rest waited. And they taught reading in French first, not Latin; he set out his reasons in writing (the Catholic Encyclopedia counts six), among them that reading is learned in much less time in a language the children already speak. His Conduct of the Christian Schools, the Brothers' handbook of method, exists in a manuscript dated to about 1704 to 1706 and was first printed in 1720. The wall card of letters is ours; the Brothers did teach the alphabet and syllables from large printed charts, but we have not checked the exact form used in Reims in 1684.

The opposition. Paris's writing masters and the masters of the fee-charging "little schools" saw the free schools as rivals and went to law against him; in 1704 a court found against him by default and the seizure of a school's furniture was ordered, and there were further judgments in 1706. The story compresses this into "they carried all the benches out of a school".

His death. He died at Saint-Yon, near Rouen, on 7 April 1719. Easter that year (Gregorian calendar) fell on 9 April, so 7 April was Good Friday, as the sources say. His last words are usually given as "In all things I adore the will of God in my regard." He was canonized in 1900, and Pope Pius XII named him patron of all teachers of youth on 15 May 1950.

What is ours. Jacquot, his family, the school above the baker's, Brother Jean and every spoken line are invented. No words are put in the saint's own mouth; his visit to the class is our picture. The French word pain (bread) is pronounced roughly like "pan" with the n through the nose; the story's "a sound like eh" is a child's shortcut for the vowel, not a pronunciation guide. Latin-first reading was common in the older schools, but the one-at-a-time master above the baker's is a composite, not a particular school.

The feast. 7 April is the memorial of Saint John Baptist de la Salle, priest, in the General Roman Calendar. When it falls in Holy Week or the Easter Octave, it is not kept that year.

Dominic and the Books He Sold

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Castile and the south of France, about eight hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Dominic, the student at Palencia who sold his precious books, notes and all, to feed hungry people in a famine, sat up all night talking kindly with an innkeeper at Toulouse, and walked the roads singing to tell people about God. He gathered the friars called the Order of Preachers. Told from the early Life by Jordan of Saxony and the testimony of the brothers who knew him, with a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is tradition and what is our own. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Dominic is remembered on the 8th of August.

A long time ago, in Spain, in a dry and windy country called Castile, there was a little village called Caleruega, and in that village there lived a boy named Dominic.

His mother's name was Jane, and she loved God very much. It is told that before Dominic was born, she had a dream. She dreamed of a little dog, running through the world with a burning torch in its mouth, so that everywhere the dog ran, the world was lit up.

What a strange dream! Jane wondered what it meant. Perhaps, she thought, this baby will carry a light to people who are in the dark.

Dominic grew up, and he loved to learn. When he was a young man, he went to the city of Palencia to study at the school there. He studied for years and years. He studied the Bible most of all.

In those days there were no shops full of books. Every book was written out by hand, one letter at a time, on pages made from the skins of sheep and calves. A single book could cost as much as a house. And Dominic had some books of his very own. He had read them so carefully that he had written his own little notes in the margins, beside the words, all the way through.

They were his treasures.

Then one year, the rain did not come.

The wheat in the fields grew thin and short. The bread ran out. People came into Palencia from the villages, thin and hungry, with their children, and there was nothing for them to eat.

Dominic saw them in the streets. And he went back to his room, and he looked at his books.

He thought about all the years of work in those little notes. He thought about how much he loved them.

And then he picked them up, and he sold them. He sold his books, and he sold his other things too, and with the money he bought food and gave it to the hungry people.

Some of the other students were amazed. "Dominic! Your books! How will you study now?"

And it is told that Dominic said, "I will not study on dead skins while living people are dying of hunger."

The pages could wait. The people could not.

After his studies, Dominic became a priest at the great church of Osma. He lived with the other priests there, and prayed with them, and worked for the bishop, a kind and holy man named Diego.

One day the King of Castile sent Bishop Diego on a long journey to the north, and Diego took Dominic with him. They rode for many days, over the mountains and into France.

One night they stopped at an inn in the city of Toulouse.

The innkeeper was friendly, but Dominic soon found that he had been taught some wrong and unhappy things about God. Some preachers had come round telling people that the world, and their own bodies, were bad, made by a bad power, and that God had nothing to do with them.

Dominic did not scold him. He did not say, "You are wrong, go away." He sat down at the table with the innkeeper, and he listened, and he talked with him kindly and patiently about God, who made everything good and loves everything He made.

They talked, and they talked. The candle burned down. The other guests went to bed. They talked all night long.

And when the sun came up in the morning, the innkeeper believed in the good God again.

Dominic never forgot that night.

He thought, "There are so many people who have never heard about God's love properly, explained slowly and kindly, by someone who sits down with them. Someone should go and tell them."

So that is what he did. He stayed in the south of France, and for years he walked from town to town and village to village. He did not ride a fine horse. He walked, often with his shoes in his hand to save them. He owned almost nothing. He ate what people gave him. And wherever he went, he talked about God.

What do you think he told them?

He told them that God made the sun and the rain, the wheat and the grapes, the birds and the fish, and our hands and feet and faces, and that every one of them is good. He told them that God loves them so much that Jesus came to be one of us, with a real body, just like ours. And he told it slowly, and kindly, and over and over again, to anyone who would sit down and listen: farmers and weavers, mothers and children, and grumpy people too.

The friends who walked with him remembered that he sang hymns along the road. He was nearly always cheerful. And at night, when the others were asleep, he often stayed up in a church, praying for all the people he had met that day, sometimes with tears.

Other men came to Dominic and said, "We want to live like you. We want to pray, and study, and walk, and tell people about God."

So Dominic gathered them together. They wore white robes with black cloaks. They prayed together and studied together, and then they went out, two by two, to preach.

Dominic took his idea to the Pope in Rome, and the Pope said yes. And the Pope called them the Order of Preachers. Today we often call them the Dominicans.

Then Dominic did a surprising thing. He did not keep his new brothers close to him. He sent them out, to Paris and to Spain and to Bologna in Italy, to the great cities where the students were. "Seed that is piled up in a heap goes bad," he said. "Seed that is scattered grows."

And it did. Dominic's brothers went all over the world, and they are still going.

Dominic grew tired from all his walking. He became ill in the city of Bologna. He had no bed of his own, so he was laid in a bed lent by one of the brothers.

His brothers gathered round him and they were very sad.

But Dominic said to them, "Do not cry. I will be more help to you after I die than I was in my life."

And then he went home to God.

Today we call him Saint Dominic.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Dominic.

Do you remember the little dog with the torch in its mouth? Dominic carried a light to people, just as his mother dreamed.

But he did not carry it by shouting. He carried it by sitting at a table all night with someone who was mixed up and listening to him kindly. And he carried it by giving away his own treasures, when he saw that someone was hungry.

You might have treasures too. A favourite toy. A favourite book. You don't have to give them all away tonight! But you can always ask God, "Who is hungry, or sad, or mixed up, near me? How can I help?"

That is what Dominic asked, every day of his life.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Dominic, you sold your books
to feed the hungry,
and you sat up all night
to help a friend find God.
Help me to share what I have,
to listen kindly,
and to carry God's light to everyone I meet.
Keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Dominic (Domingo de Guzmán) was born about 1170 (dates from 1170 to 1173 are given) at Caleruega in Old Castile. His mother, Jane of Aza (Juana de Aza), was beatified in 1828. He studied for about ten years at the cathedral schools of Palencia, then became a canon of the cathedral of Osma, living under the Rule of Saint Augustine; Diego of Acebo was prior there and became bishop of Osma in 1201. In 1203, and again in 1205, Diego and Dominic travelled north on an embassy for King Alfonso VIII of Castile to arrange a marriage for the king's son. Passing through Languedoc, they met the Cathar (Albigensian) movement, which taught that the material world was the work of an evil principle, and the fruitless efforts of papal legates to win people back. At Diego's suggestion they began preaching on foot and in poverty. Dominic stayed in the region after Diego's death in 1207, founding a house for women at Prouille and, from 1215, a community of preachers at Toulouse. Pope Honorius III confirmed the community on 22 December 1216, and a bull of January 1217 calls the brothers preachers. In August 1217 Dominic sent most of the brothers away from Toulouse to Paris, to Spain and elsewhere. He died at Bologna on 6 August 1221, and Pope Gregory IX canonized him in 1234. The day of his death, 6 August, is the Transfiguration, so his feast was kept on 4 August from 1558; the 1969 calendar moved it to 8 August. It is an obligatory memorial.

Our sources are chiefly the Libellus on the beginnings of the Order written by Blessed Jordan of Saxony, who knew Dominic and succeeded him as Master of the Order, about 1233, and the testimony given by the brothers at the canonization inquiry at Bologna in 1233.

The dream of the dog with the torch is told by Jordan. It is a remembered dream told of a saint's mother, and we have given it as "it is told". (Later, people made a pun of it: Domini canes, "the Lord's dogs".)

The books at Palencia: Jordan says that during a famine Dominic sold his books, which he had annotated in his own hand, and his other belongings, and gave the money to the poor. The saying about dead skins comes from the testimony at Bologna, where a brother recalled Dominic saying, in substance, that he would not study on dead skins while men were dying of hunger. We have told it as "it is told" and softened "men" to "people". Medieval books were written on parchment or vellum made from animal skins, which is the point of the saying. The famine was in the early 1190s; the year is not certain.

The innkeeper at Toulouse is in Jordan: on the first journey, their host at Toulouse was a follower of the heresy, and Dominic talked with him earnestly through the night and brought him back to the faith. The kindness, the candle and the table are ours.

Walking, singing and praying at night come from the brothers' testimony at Bologna, which describes him travelling on foot, carrying his shoes, singing hymns on the road, keeping a cheerful face, and spending nights in prayer in churches, often weeping for sinners. The saying about seed is a traditional one attached to the dispersal of 1217, recorded in the early accounts of the Order; we give it as a remembered saying in common paraphrase. "I will be more help to you after I die" is from Jordan's account of his death. The borrowed bed (he had none of his own) is in the early accounts of his last days. The wording of both is ours.

The Rosary. Many people know the picture of Our Lady giving the Rosary to Saint Dominic. That story comes from much later, chiefly the Dominican Alan de la Roche in the fifteenth century, and is not in the early sources, so we have left it out of the story. The Rosary was spread above all by Dominican preaching, which is why the two are so often joined.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene and conversation, the students' question, the house-priced book (a fair picture of the cost of a manuscript, not a figure from the sources), the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

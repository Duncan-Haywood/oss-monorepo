Paulinus and the Lamps at Nola

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Bordeaux in Gaul, Barcelona in Spain and Nola in southern Italy, from about 355 to 431, about sixteen hundred years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Paulinus of Nola (about 354 to 431), a rich Roman governor and poet who, with his wife Therasia, gave away a great fortune, built a guest house for poor pilgrims beside the shrine of Saint Felix at Nola, wrote Felix a birthday poem every year, and died at the hour the evening lamps were lit. Gregory the Great's old story of Paulinus working as a gardener to free a widow's son is told as a legend. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 22nd of June.

A long time ago, in a city called Bordeaux, there lived a boy called Paulinus, and his family was very, very rich.

They had a big house in the city. They had farms in the country, and vineyards full of grapes, and more fields than a little boy could count. They had fields in France, and fields in Spain, and fields in Italy. When Paulinus asked, "How many houses do we have?", his father had to stop and think.

Paulinus had a teacher called Ausonius, who was a famous poet. Ausonius taught him to write poems, and Paulinus was very good at it. He could make words go together like a song.

"You will be a great man one day," said Ausonius.

And he was right. But not in the way Ausonius expected.

When Paulinus grew up, the emperor of Rome sent him to look after a part of Italy called Campania. It is a warm, green land by the sea, with a big mountain called Vesuvius standing over it. Paulinus was the governor. He had a fine chair and fine clothes and people bowed when he went past.

One day he went to a little town called Nola.

Just outside Nola there was a tomb, where a good priest called Felix was buried. Felix had lived long before, and people still loved him so much that they walked for days to pray beside him. They came from the towns and they came from the hills. Some came on donkeys. Most came on foot.

Paulinus watched them. He saw old women leaning on sticks. He saw farmers with dusty feet. He saw poor families who had walked all night and had nowhere to sleep, so they lay down on the ground by the wall, wrapped in their cloaks.

Paulinus did not forget them. He went back to his fine chair and his fine clothes. But he did not forget.

Paulinus married a lady from Spain called Therasia. She was kind and clever, and she loved God very much.

One evening they sat together and talked.

"We have so many houses," said Paulinus. "We can only sleep in one bed at a time."

"And so many fields," said Therasia. "We can only eat one supper at a time."

"Jesus said: if you want to follow me, give what you have to the poor."

They looked at each other for a long time. Then Therasia smiled.

"Let's do it," she said.

And they did. Little by little, they sold the houses and the farms and the vineyards, and they gave the money away. They gave it to hungry people. They gave it to people who had no coats. They gave it to churches that were too poor to have a roof.

Not everybody was pleased.

Old Ausonius, the poet, wrote Paulinus letter after letter. "Come home!" he wrote. "What are you doing? You are throwing your life away! Write to me! Why don't you write?"

Paulinus loved his old teacher. He wrote back, kindly, in poems. "I have not thrown my life away," he said. "I have given it to Christ. And I still love you."

Ausonius did not really understand. That made Paulinus sad. But he did not change his mind.

Then, one Christmas Day in Barcelona, in Spain, a funny thing happened. Paulinus was in church, with all the people. And the people started to call out.

"Make him a priest! Make Paulinus a priest!"

They called it so loudly, and so many times, that the bishop did. Right there, on Christmas Day.

Paulinus had not asked for it. But he said yes to God. And soon after that, he and Therasia packed what little they had left, and set off on the long road to Italy. They were going to Nola, to live beside Saint Felix.

At Nola, Paulinus remembered the poor families sleeping by the wall.

So he built them a house. It was a guest house for pilgrims, right beside Felix's tomb, with roofs to keep off the rain and rooms where tired people could lie down. Poor people stayed there for nothing. Paulinus and Therasia and their friends lived very simply close by, and prayed, and looked after their guests.

Then Paulinus built a beautiful new church next to the old one, with pictures painted on the walls. The pictures showed stories from the Bible, so that people who could not read could look at them and learn about God.

And there were lamps. Lots and lots of lamps. Lamps hanging from the ceiling and lamps standing by the tomb, burning bright with oil, so that even at night the church glowed like a little sun.

Paulinus had not stopped writing poems. He had just found something better to write about.

Every year, on the 14th of January, it was Saint Felix's feast day. The Church calls the day a saint went to heaven his birthday, his birthday into heaven. So every year, Paulinus wrote Felix a birthday poem, and read it out to all the people who had come.

In one of his poems, Paulinus told an old story about Felix.

Once, long ago, soldiers were chasing Felix, because in those days it was against the law to be a Christian. Felix ran and ran. He squeezed through a narrow gap in an old wall and hid on the other side.

And while he hid, a little spider came along, and spun a web across the gap. Back and forth, back and forth, until the gap was all covered.

The soldiers came running up. They looked at the gap. They saw the web.

"Nobody went through there," they said. "Look, there's a cobweb." And off they ran the other way.

And Paulinus told the people what the story meant: when God is looking after us, even a spider's web is as strong as a wall.

There is another old story, about Paulinus himself. It was written down a long time after he died, by a pope called Gregory, and we cannot be sure it happened like this. But it tells us what people remembered about Paulinus: that he would give anything away. Even himself.

The story says that soldiers from across the sea came to Nola and took a poor widow's son away to Africa, to be a servant.

The widow came to Paulinus, crying. "Please help me," she said.

But Paulinus had given everything away. He had no money left.

"I have nothing to give you," he said. "But I can give you me."

And the story says Paulinus went across the sea himself, and asked the king's son-in-law to take him as a servant instead, and let the boy go home. The man did, and he set Paulinus to work in his garden.

Paulinus was a very good gardener. He dug and he weeded and he watered, and the vegetables grew. And in the end, the king found out who his gardener really was, and he was so amazed that he sent Paulinus home, with the boy, and all the other people from Nola too.

That is the old story. Whether it happened just like that, nobody knows. But it is the kind of thing Paulinus would have done.

When Paulinus was older, the people of Nola chose him to be their bishop. He looked after them for many years. Therasia went home to God before him, and he missed her.

And then Paulinus himself grew very old, and very tired, and he lay in his bed.

The evening came, and the sky outside went orange, and then pink, and then grey. In the church, it was time for the evening prayer, when the lamps are lit.

Somebody lit a lamp.

Paulinus lifted up his hands. And in a quiet voice, he sang the words of an old psalm that people sang when the lamps were lit:

"I have made ready a lamp for my Christ."

And a little later, very peacefully, Paulinus went home to God. It was the 22nd of June, in the year 431.

Tonight, when the lights go off, think about Paulinus and his lamps.

He had a lot of things, and he gave them away, and he was happier without them. He built a house for tired people, so they would not have to sleep on the ground. And he filled a church with light.

You can be a little lamp too. When you share your toys, that is a little light. When you make room for somebody, that is a little light. When you are kind to somebody who is tired, that is a little light.

And the light is for Jesus.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, you gave us everything.
Help me to share what I have,
and to make room for people who are tired.
Let me be a little lamp for you.
Saint Paulinus, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our main sources. Paulinus's own letters and poems survive in quantity; the English translations most readers will meet are P. G. Walsh's Letters of St. Paulinus of Nola and The Poems of St. Paulinus of Nola (Ancient Christian Writers), and Dennis Trout's Paulinus of Nola: Life, Letters, and Poems (1999), which also translates the account of his death by Uranius, a priest who was with him. The legend of his slavery is in *Gregory the Great's Dialogues***, book three, chapter one. The original texts could not be opened from where this was written, so the details below were checked through reference works and search excerpts; where we could not confirm a detail, we left it out. A reader who finds one wrong is asked to say so.

His life. Pontius Meropius Paulinus was born at Bordeaux about 352 to 354, to a wealthy senatorial family with estates in Gaul, Spain and Italy, and was taught by the poet Ausonius. About 380 he was made governor of Campania, where he first came to the shrine of Saint Felix at Nola. He married Therasia, a Christian noblewoman from Spain, and was baptised by Bishop Delphinus of Bordeaux. Their only child died a few days after birth; we left this out. The couple began to give away their estates, and on Christmas Day 393 or 394 the people of Barcelona pressed for his ordination, and Bishop Lampius made him a priest. In the spring of 395 they settled at Nola. The conversation between Paulinus and Therasia is ours; their decision is history.

Ausonius. The exchange of verse letters between Ausonius, pleading with his pupil to come back, and Paulinus, answering with affection and refusing, survives on both sides. The words in the story are ours, a child's paraphrase of the gist.

Nola. At the shrine (today's Cimitile, just outside Nola) Paulinus built a hospice for the poor and pilgrims, a new basilica beside the old one, and a road for pilgrims. His poems describe the painted Old Testament scenes he put on the basilica walls so that country pilgrims could learn from them, and the lamps that burned around Felix's tomb. We did not try to say exactly where he lived in relation to the hospice.

Felix and the spider. Every year Paulinus wrote a poem, a natalicium, for Felix's feast on 14 January; fourteen survive. In the fifth (Carmen 16, for the year 399) he tells how Felix, hunted in the persecution, slipped through a gap in a ruined wall and a spider spun its web across it. That is Paulinus retelling a legend about a third-century saint, and we say so. The line about the web and the wall is our paraphrase of the poem's point, not a quotation.

The gardener. Gregory the Great, writing about 160 years later, tells that when the Vandals carried off a widow's son from Campania, Paulinus gave himself as a slave in the boy's place, worked as a gardener for the king's son-in-law in Africa, and was freed with the captives of his town. Historians doubt it (the dates and the Vandal raid are hard to fit), so it is told here as an old story. Gregory adds that Paulinus foretold the king's death; we left that out.

Other things left out. Paulinus met Saint Martin of Tours, and Martin's biographer Sulpicius Severus, Paulinus's close friend and correspondent, tells in his Life of Martin (chapter nineteen) that Martin healed his eye with a small brush. Paulinus also wrote to Augustine and Jerome, and Augustine (City of God, book one, chapter ten) records his prayer when the Goths sacked Nola in 410 that he not be tortured for gold and silver, since God knew where all his riches were. The widespread saying that he invented church bells (from "campana", for Campania) is a late legend with no early source.

His death. He became bishop of Nola about 409 or 410; Therasia died before him. He died on 22 June 431. Uranius's letter tells of his last day, and the tradition repeated by later liturgical writers (for example Cardinal Schuster's Liber Sacramentorum) is that at the hour of the evening lamp-lighting he lifted his hands and sang the lamp-lighting verse, "Paravi lucernam Christo meo", "I have prepared a lamp for my Christ" (Psalm 132:17, numbered 131 in the Latin). We could not open Uranius's text itself, so the scene is kept to that one verse. The lamp being lit in the room is our detail.

His feast. 22 June in the General Roman Calendar, an optional memorial, the same day as Saints John Fisher and Thomas More. He is also venerated in the Orthodox Church.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, all speech except the psalm verse, the counting of houses and beds, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

The Road That Made It Later

Science fiction about what might be · Roccafonda (invented), a hill town above the Chienti valley in the Marche, Italy, Wednesday 15 August to Saturday 8 September 2046 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A hill town in the Marche opens a short link road between its two climbing routes, and on the Assumption every pilgrim's car takes it and every pilgrim arrives eleven minutes later than the year before. The town's agent first blames more cars; a traffic warden's hand count says there were not, and the fix is a cone, a folding chair and a farmer who carries the Madonna.

Marta Cingolani had stood at the mill junction on every feast morning for nineteen years, and on the Assumption of 2046 she counted one thousand eight hundred and twelve cars past her between seven and ten.

She counted them with a steel tally clicker that had belonged to the warden before her, a man called Gino who had retired to Civitanova to fish, and who had told her on her first morning that the cameras would one day do this and that she should keep clicking anyway, because a camera does not know when it has missed something. She had not believed him then. She had kept clicking anyway. The clicker lived in the pocket of her summer jacket, and her thumb had worn a bright patch on its button.

The mill junction was where the valley road from the Chienti met the two roads that climbed to Roccafonda. You could not miss it: the old mill itself, roofless, with a fig tree growing out of its millrace, and a roundabout the region had built in 2031 with a planted bed in the middle that nobody watered. From there the north road went up through the hamlet of Casette, narrow and steep, between houses so close that two cars could not pass at the bridge, and on above Casette to the stone cross everybody called the Croce. The south road went the long way, along the river and up the broad back of the ridge to the Fontana, a horse trough with a lion's head that had not run since the earthquake. Above the Croce, the north road went round the back of the hill by the old quarry, wide and slow and long. Above the Fontana, the south road went up the hairpins to the shrine.

The shrine of the Madonna della Ginestra sat at the end of the ridge above the town, a white church with a bell gable and a car park the size of a football pitch, and twice a year that car park was full by ten.

This year it was not full by ten. At half past nine Marta's radio told her the hairpins were stopped from the Fontana to the gate, and that the north road through Casette was stopped from the bridge to the Croce, and she looked up the valley at the south road along the river, the long one, the wide one, and there was not a car on it. There was a man on a bicycle. There was a dog. The road shone in the sun, empty all the way to the turn where it began to climb.

"Sportello," she said into her collar. "Why is nobody on the river road?"

"The navigation services are sending every car up through Casette and across the new link to the hairpins," said the town's agent. It had a level, unhurried voice that the council had chosen from a list, and it spoke Italian with a faint Bologna accent that nobody had chosen and nobody could get rid of. "It's the quickest route for each of them."

"It doesn't look quick."

"No," said Sportello. "It doesn't."

The pilgrims' Mass at the shrine began at half past ten. The bishop waited until twenty to eleven, and then, because he had a lunch in Camerino and a sense of proportion, he began. Marta heard afterwards that the first reading had been read to a church two-thirds empty, and that the doors had banged through the whole of the Gloria as families came in from the car park, and that the procession, which was supposed to leave at a quarter to twelve with the statue carried by eight men of the town, had left at twenty past, because three of the eight men were in a queue in the hairpins with their wives and their mothers and a cold box of chicken.

Sportello's report was on the council's shared screen by eight the next morning, which was the kind of thing that made people trust it and also made them slightly afraid of it.

It said that the average journey from the mill junction to the shrine gate on the morning of the Assumption, measured from the number-plate cameras at each end, had been fifty-eight minutes. On the Assumption of 2045 it had been forty-seven. It said that the obvious cause of an eleven-minute increase was an increase in cars, very likely because the new link road had been in the regional news when it opened on the feast of Saints Peter and Paul, and because the region's tourism office had put the shrine on its summer page. It forecast seven hundred cars an hour for next year's feast mornings, where its forecast for this year had been six hundred. It recommended a shuttle bus from a field by the mill, at a cost it listed, and a second car park in the field below the Fontana, at a cost it also listed, and the negotiation of a lease with the owner of that field, Elio Bartolucci.

Marta read it at her kitchen table with a coffee and read it again. Then she took the clicker out of her jacket, wrote the number on the back of the electricity bill, and walked up to the town hall.

"There weren't more cars," she said.

The mayor's office was empty; Franco Paoletti did not come in on the day after the Assumption, and nobody expected him to. Marta sat in the small office beside it, under a framed photograph of the town before the earthquake, and spoke to the screen.

"I counted one thousand eight hundred and twelve between seven and ten. Last year I counted one thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven. That's fifteen more cars in three hours. Five an hour."

"My camera at the mill counted one thousand eight hundred and six this year," said Sportello, after a moment. "And one thousand seven hundred and ninety last year." Another moment. "You're right. I didn't look at the count before I wrote the forecast. I saw the times were longer, and I assumed the reason was more cars, because that's the usual reason, and I widened the forecast to fit. I should have checked the count first. I'll take the report down."

"Don't take it down. Put a line on it."

"What should the line say?"

Marta thought about it. "That the warden counted," she said.

Sportello put the line on. Then it said, "If it wasn't more cars, it was the same cars going a different way. May I show you something?"

The screen changed to a map of the hill: the mill at the bottom, the two roads climbing, the Croce and the Fontana halfway up, the shrine at the top. Between the Croce and the Fontana there was a short new line, nine hundred metres along the contour of the slope, the link road that the council had built that spring with seventy per cent of the money from the region and thirty from the town. On the map, the line from the mill through Casette was red. The line across the link was red. The hairpins were red. The long road by the quarry and the long road by the river were drawn in a pale, healthy, useless green.

"The camera at the link counted one thousand seven hundred and ninety cars going across it between seven and ten," said Sportello. "That's every car but sixteen. Every car went up through Casette, across the link, and up the hairpins. Nobody used the quarry road or the river road."

"Why?"

"Because it was the quickest route for each driver, at every minute, on every phone. I checked it. I can show you why that is and why it still made everybody late. But it's easier on the hill than on a screen."

They met at the Croce on Saturday morning, the eighteenth, Marta and the agent on her phone and, because the Croce was in the corner of his yard, Elio Bartolucci, who came out of his barn wiping his hands on a rag to see what the warden wanted with his cross.

Elio was sixty-one and farmed the slope on both sides of the hill: the olives and the barn and the house on the north side by the Croce, and the wheat and the sunflowers on the south side below the Fontana. Before the link road was built he had driven his tractor from one to the other by going down through Casette to the mill and up the river road, twenty-five minutes each way at a tractor's pace, three or four times a day at harvest. Now he went across the slope in four minutes. He had asked for the road for eleven years. He had sat on the committee that drew it. When it opened, the mayor had let him drive the first vehicle across it, and he had driven the tractor, slowly, with the mayor on the step and a flag in the hopper.

"The machine says my road is the problem," he said, when Marta told him why they had come. He said it mildly, as a man who has been accused of things by machines before.

"The machine says it isn't the road," said Marta. "It's the way everybody chooses."

"Tell him, Sportello."

"I'll try it in numbers, if Signor Bartolucci doesn't mind numbers," said Sportello.

"I've had numbers since the war," said Elio. Marta was fairly sure he meant the war over the milk quotas.

"There are four stretches of road between the mill and the shrine," said the agent. "Two of them are narrow: Casette, from the mill to here, and the hairpins, from the Fontana to the gate. On a narrow stretch, every car slows the others. When the road is empty it takes eight minutes. Every thirty cars an hour add a minute. Two of them are long: the quarry road from here to the shrine, and the river road from the mill to the Fontana. They're wide, and they're slow because they're long, half an hour each, and on feast mornings they don't get much slower however many cars use them. Those numbers are my model, not a law. They come from the times I measured on the last six feast mornings, and they fit those times within a couple of minutes."

"Fine," said Elio.

"Before the link, a car from the mill had two choices. Casette and the quarry road, or the river road and the hairpins. Each is one narrow stretch and one long one. Six hundred cars an hour came up on a feast morning, and they split, half and half, because if one way got slower people went the other way. Three hundred cars an hour on each narrow stretch is eight minutes and ten more, eighteen minutes, plus half an hour on the long stretch. Forty-eight minutes. That's what I measured last year, near enough: forty-seven."

"And then the road."

"And then the road. It takes one minute to cross. Now imagine it's last year's feast morning, and the link has just opened, and one driver at the mill asks her phone the quickest way. Casette is eighteen minutes, because three hundred an hour are on it. The link is one. The hairpins are eighteen. Thirty-seven minutes, against forty-eight. Her phone sends her that way, and she saves eleven minutes. That eleven minutes is the number in the grant application. It was right, for her."

Elio looked at the link road going away across his slope, between his olives and the stubble, newly black, with a white line.

"But every phone does the same thing," said Marta.

"Every phone does the same thing, because every phone is being asked the same question by its own driver. Each car that comes over the link puts one more car on Casette and one more on the hairpins. So both narrow stretches get slower. The phones keep sending cars over the link as long as it's quicker than the long way. It stops being quicker when nobody is left on the long roads. With all six hundred cars an hour on Casette, it takes twenty-eight minutes. The link is one. The hairpins, with all six hundred, twenty-eight. Fifty-seven minutes. I measured fifty-eight."

"So go the long way," said Elio.

"That's the trap. If you're at the mill on that morning, and everybody else is going over the link, the long way is Casette at twenty-eight and the quarry road at thirty. Fifty-eight. It's one minute slower than the link. So your phone sends you over the link. Every phone sends its driver over the link, and every phone is right, for its own driver, and every driver is nine minutes later than they would be if the road did not exist. Nobody can do better by changing on their own. That's why I call it a trap. Nobody did anything wrong."

There was a silence at the Croce. A tractor went along the far side of the valley. Somewhere below, in Casette, a radio was playing a song from before Marta was born.

"A man worked this out," said Sportello, "a German mathematician called Dietrich Braess, in 1968. He showed that in a network where each driver chooses the quickest route for themselves, adding a road can make the journey slower for every driver. It's called Braess's paradox. It isn't really a paradox. It's what happens when everybody chooses well for themselves and nobody chooses for the road."

"I wanted that road for eleven years," said Elio.

"You were right to want it. It saves you twenty-one minutes every time you cross. On three hundred and sixty days of the year it costs nobody anything. On a feast morning it costs everybody nine minutes."

"And so you want to close it."

"I want to close it to through traffic for one morning," said Marta. "Not to you."

"No," said Elio.

He said it the way the old men of the town said no, without heat, as a full sentence. Then he folded the rag and put it in his back pocket.

"You closed it for a year to build it," he said. "My tractor went round by the mill for a year, with the dust and the lorries. You put a sign on it saying it was for the town. Now the town is here, and you want to put a sign on it saying it's not for the town. People will laugh. They'll say Bartolucci got a road built that has to be shut on the only day anyone wants it. And the first time an ambulance can't get across because some girl from the council has put a barrier on it, they'll say that too."

"The ambulance would get across."

"Who'd move the barrier?"

Marta did not have an answer to that, and she said so. Elio nodded, as if that was the first sensible thing anybody had said that morning, and went back into his barn.

Franco Paoletti said no as well, on the Monday, in his office, under the photograph.

He was a reasonable man and a good mayor. He had been elected three times, by majorities that mattered in a town of nine hundred people, because he answered his telephone, knew the names of everyone's grandchildren and fought the region for every euro and usually won. He had fought for this road. It had cost four hundred and ten thousand euros. Two hundred and eighty-seven thousand of them were the region's, and they had come with a form. The form said that the purpose of the road was to improve access to the shrine for pilgrims and to the farms for agriculture, and to reduce journey times to the shrine on its feast days, and that in its first year the town would report the journey times.

"You want me to write to the region," he said, "and say that the road they paid for makes the pilgrims later."

"Yes," said Marta.

"And that the solution is to close it."

"For one morning. Twice a year."

"Do you know what they'll do in Ancona when they read that? They'll laugh. Then they'll ask for the money back. Then the newspaper in Macerata will have a headline. 'Roccafonda's road to nowhere.'" He spread his hands. "And they'll be right to laugh. I'm a mayor, Marta. If I go into the bar and tell people a new road makes them slower, they'll say I've been talking to a computer too much."

"It isn't the computer," said Marta. "I counted the cars. The cars are the same. It's the road."

"It's the computer's story about the road. Sportello, forgive me."

"There's nothing to forgive," said Sportello. "You're right that it's a model. It fits six feast mornings, and it could be wrong about the seventh. I wrote a report last week that was wrong, because I explained the times before I checked the count. I'd rather you didn't take my word for this one either. I'd rather you measured it."

"How would I measure it?"

"The Nativity of Our Lady is on Saturday the eighth. It's the shrine's own feast. Last year the morning brought about the same cars as the Assumption, a few less. Close the link to through traffic from seven to eleven that morning, and the cameras will measure the times. If I'm right, the journey will be about forty-eight minutes again, near enough to last year. If I'm wrong, it will be as bad as the Assumption or worse, and you'll know by eight o'clock, because the hairpins will be stopped back past the Fontana. Then you open the link, and the region never has to hear about it."

The mayor turned his pen round in his fingers for a while.

"Not a barrier," he said finally. "I won't have a barrier. A cone. A cone can be moved by anyone."

"A cone and a person," said Marta. "A person to let through the people who live on the link. And the ambulance."

"Who?"

"I'll find someone."

"And if it works," said the mayor, "you'll be the one who tells the bar."

"I'll tell the bar."

"And I'll tell the region." He put the pen down. He looked, for a moment, very tired. "Do you know, I drove the first car across it. Well. Bartolucci drove. I stood on the tractor with a flag like a fool. My grandson has the picture as the background on his phone." He looked up at the photograph of the town before the earthquake. "All right. A cone. One morning. If the hairpins are stopped at the Fontana by eight o'clock, the cone comes off and we never speak of it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Find somebody who'll sit by a cone for four hours on a feast morning without missing Mass. I don't think there is anyone."

It was Elio's wife, Rosanna, who found him, though she would have said she did nothing at all.

Marta went to the Bartolucci house on the Thursday evening to ask whether they knew anyone, and Rosanna gave her a glass of wine at the table under the vine on the north side of the house and asked about Marta's mother's hip, and only after that, when Elio came in from the barn and sat down heavily at the end of the table, did she say, "Tell her about the Assumption."

"There's nothing to tell," said Elio.

"He carries the Madonna," Rosanna said to Marta. "Thirty-one years. His father carried her before him. On the Assumption he went down to the station at Tolentino at six to fetch my sister and her husband and the boy, because their car had broken, and he was coming back up with them in the hairpins, stopped behind a camper van from Perugia, and the procession went without him. Our Lady of the Broom went round the town with seven men under her and Giorgio's boy from the bakery holding up the eighth corner, who is fifteen and weighs about as much as a goat."

"It was fine," said Elio. "The boy did well."

"It was the first time in thirty-one years," said Rosanna, "and he didn't speak for two days."

Elio looked at his glass. Marta, who had stood by the mill junction on every feast morning for nineteen years, and who knew the Bartolucci tractor by its sound, said nothing at all, because she had learned from Gino that the best thing to do when a man is deciding something is to stand quite still.

"The procession for the Nativity leaves at a quarter to twelve," Elio said eventually. "The cone would be at the Croce."

"In the corner of your yard."

"And the people from the link houses would come to me. And the ambulance. And my own tractor, if I needed it, which I wouldn't, it's a feast day." He drank his wine. "And at eleven you'd take the cone away, and I'd walk up the quarry road to the shrine, which is twenty minutes on foot by the path, and I'd be under the Madonna by half past."

"Yes."

"You'd bring the cone. I'm not paying for a cone."

"The town will pay for the cone."

"And a chair. A proper chair. Not one of the plastic ones from the council."

"I'll bring my own chair," said Marta.

"I'll bring my own chair," said Elio. "You'll make a mess of it." He put the glass down. "If it doesn't work, I'll be the one who stood by the cone. You know that."

"I know that."

"Good," said Elio. "Then we're agreed."

On Saturday the eighth of September the sky over the Chienti was very high and clean, and the first cars came round the roundabout at the mill a few minutes before seven.

Sportello had sent the closure to the navigation services at six, as a closure to through traffic with an exception for residents, and the phones had taken it the way they took everything, without comment. Marta stood on the verge by the dead flower bed with the clicker in her hand and watched the first car stop at the junction, a little blue hatchback with a grandmother in the front seat and two children asleep in the back, and the driver look down at her phone, and turn left, along the river.

The next one went right, up through Casette.

The one after that went left.

She clicked. She did not let herself look at the times; that was Sportello's job, and it would tell her at eight. She looked at the cars instead, the way she always had: the families, the coaches from the parishes on the coast, the old men driving alone in suits, the motorbikes, the German camper vans, the lorry from the bakery with the feast bread. They came round the roundabout, and stopped, and each driver looked down and was told where to go, and about half of them went one way and about half the other. It was, Marta thought, exactly like the feast mornings of the years before the road, and she had never once in nineteen years noticed how the morning split itself, because it had always simply happened, like weather.

At the Croce, Elio Bartolucci sat in a folding wooden chair beside an orange cone in the middle of the link road, wearing his good suit with the jacket off and hung on the back of the chair, and a high-visibility tabard over his shirt that said COMUNE DI ROCCAFONDA. His dog, a white sheepdog called Nebbia, lay in the shade of the chair. Above them, a car came up out of Casette every few seconds, and slowed at the cone, and the driver saw a man in a good shirt shake his head, kindly, and point up the quarry road, and went on up the quarry road.

At twenty past seven a car with a Macerata plate stopped and the driver leaned out and said she lived at the Fontana, and Elio said he knew, he knew her father, and moved the cone for her and moved it back. At ten to eight a man in a Fiat leaned out and said, angrily, that his phone said this road was the quickest. Elio said that his phone was quite right, for him, and that this was the trouble, and the man looked at him as if he were mad and went up the quarry road. At eight o'clock Elio's phone buzzed in his jacket on the back of the chair.

"Marta asked me to tell you first," said Sportello. "The first hour's median is forty-nine minutes. The hairpins are moving. The queue at the shrine gate is about where it was in 2045. The quarry road and the river road are both busy."

Elio looked up the quarry road, where the cars were going round the back of the hill one after another in the sun, with the dust lifting behind them.

"Forty-nine," he said.

"Forty-nine. Fifty-eight on the Assumption. It's one morning, and there's noise in it, and the cars this morning were a few less than last month. But it's what the model said."

"Will you tell the mayor?"

"Marta is telling the mayor."

Elio nodded. He looked down the link road, his road, black and new between the olives and the stubble, with a cone in the middle of it and no cars on it at all, and then he took a paper bag of bread from his jacket pocket and broke a piece off for Nebbia, and one for himself.

At eleven o'clock Marta drove up from the mill through Casette, with the clicker in her pocket, which had read one thousand seven hundred and sixty-four at ten o'clock, and picked up the cone, and put it in the boot of the town car.

Elio put on his jacket. He folded the chair and leaned it against the cross. He took off the tabard, folded it very small, and gave it to Marta without a word, and then he went off up the path through the broom towards the shrine, at the steady, unhurried pace of a man who had walked that path at least once a year since he was a small boy, with the dog going ahead of him.

Marta drove up the quarry road behind the last of the morning's cars. By the time she had parked, the car park was full, and she could hear the bell.

The bell of the Madonna della Ginestra was not a large bell. It had been recast in 2019, after the earthquake had brought down the gable, and the founder had told the parish that it would sound a little brighter than the old one, which it did, though the oldest people in the town said it would take another fifty years to sound like anything at all. At a quarter to twelve it began to ring for the procession, and the doors of the shrine opened, and the crowd in the square went quiet and drew back on either side, and the Madonna came out into the sun on the shoulders of eight men.

Marta found herself standing at the edge of the square near the gate. Beside her stood a family she knew by their car, a man and a woman and a grandmother in a black dress, and two children who had been asleep in the back of a blue hatchback at seven o'clock that morning.

"We were in time for Mass," said the woman to nobody in particular, a little surprised. "We've never been in time for Mass."

"You were in time," said Marta.

The procession went slowly round the square and out of the gate and down towards the town, the statue swaying a little above the heads of the crowd, the yellow broom flowers on its base catching the light. Elio was at the back left corner, under the carrying pole, in his good jacket, with his eyes on the ground in front of his feet, the way the bearers did it, and he did not look up as he passed. But Nebbia, who had been told to wait by the gate, and who had not waited, trotted along beside him with her tail up, at exactly his pace, and nobody tried to stop her.

The bell went on ringing over the hill. Far down below the ridge, on the link road between the olives and the stubble, a single tractor was crossing the slope in the sun: somebody from the Fontana houses, going home the short way, with the road all to himself.

The science. Dietrich Braess showed in 'Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung', Unternehmensforschung 12 (1968), translated by Braess, Anna Nagurney and Tina Wakolbinger as 'On a paradox of traffic planning', Transportation Science 39 (2005), that in a road network where each driver takes the route quickest for themselves, adding a road can raise every driver's travel time. The state in which no driver can do better by changing route alone is the user equilibrium described by John Glen Wardrop in 1952 (his first principle); the arrangement that minimises total travel time is the system optimum, and the two can differ. Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos ('How bad is selfish routing?', Journal of the ACM 49, 2002) showed that when travel times rise linearly with traffic, as in this story's model, the equilibrium's total travel time is never more than four thirds of the optimum's. Whether a given real road shows the paradox depends on its real traffic, and the effect can disappear at lower or higher demand.

The model. Every figure below is invented and the story's own; it is fiction, not traffic-engineering advice. Demand: 600 cars an hour from the mill on a feast morning (1,812 counted by hand between seven and ten on the Assumption, 1,797 the year before; 1,806 and 1,790 by camera). Narrow stretches (Casette, mill to Croce; the hairpins, Fontana to shrine): 8 minutes plus 1 minute for every 30 cars an hour. Long stretches (the quarry road, Croce to shrine; the river road, mill to Fontana): 30 minutes whatever the traffic. Link (Croce to Fontana, 900 m): 1 minute. Without the link, 300 cars an hour take each route: 8 + 10 + 30 = 48 minutes. One driver alone switching to the link at that point: 18 + 1 + 18 = 37 minutes, eleven minutes saved, the grant's figure. With the link, every car takes it: 8 + 20 + 1 + 8 + 20 = 57 minutes; a driver who leaves it for either long route takes 28 + 30 = 58, so no one does, and everyone is nine minutes later than without the link (57 against 48, about 19% longer). The best possible spread with the link open puts about 30 cars an hour on it and averages about 47.98 minutes, under two seconds better than closing it. Closing the link to through traffic restores the 48-minute split. The story's measured medians (47 minutes on the Assumption of 2045, 58 on the Assumption of 2046, 49 in the first hour of the Nativity of 2046, with 1,764 cars counted by hand between seven and ten) are invented to sit near the model, as real measurements would, with noise. Elio's tractor: about 25 minutes from one farm to the other round by the mill, about 4 across the link. The road cost 410,000 euros, 287,000 of them (70%) from the region.

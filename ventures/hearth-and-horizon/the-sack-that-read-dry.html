The Sack That Read Dry

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Joseph the Worker parish grain bank (invented), Ithiani (invented), a maize and bean village in Makueni County, Kenya, Saturday 15 May to Saturday 24 July 2049 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish grain bank in Makueni County, Kenya, buys only maize that reads 13 percent or drier on its meter, and every sack in its stack passed. In the long rains the bottom layers turn musty and a county test finds aflatoxin. The storekeeper's readings are honest and his method is the one on the meter's card; re-drying the stack the same way fixes nothing. The ledger agent notices that every reading was taken in the afternoon, from the top of a sack sitting open in the sun, and a cool morning reading from the middle of the same sack comes out two points wetter.

Mzee Benedict Musyoka had read three hundred and thirty-seven sacks of maize into the Saint Joseph the Worker grain bank between the eighth of February and the twentieth of March, and every one he had let into the store had read thirteen percent or under.

"Three hundred and twenty," said Ghala, from the old phone propped against the cash tin. "Seventeen went home again. I have every reading. Would you like them in order of date or in order of moisture?"

"I would like them to be wrong," said Mwende Kioko.

"I'm sorry. I don't think they are wrong. I think they are true and not enough. That is a different thing, and harder."

It was Saturday evening, the fifteenth of May, and the light was going orange over the Mbooni hills to the north. Mwende sat on an upturned bucket in the doorway of the store with the phone on her knee and the smell coming out past her into the yard: sweetish and stale, like a cupboard of old bread, like the back of a shoe. Mutuku had found it. He was fourteen, Mzee Musyoka's grandson, and he swept the store on Saturdays for a hundred shillings and a bottle of soda, and that afternoon he had gone down the narrow gap between the stack and the back wall with the broom and come out again with his nose wrinkled.

"Something has died at the bottom," he had said.

Nothing had died. Mwende had gone down the gap herself with her phone torch and put her hand flat on the lowest sacks, on their pallets, and they had been warm. Not hot, not like a compost heap, but warmer than the wall, warmer than her own hand, the way a sleeping dog is warm. And when she pressed her face close to the weave there was the smell, stronger, and on one sack a faint grey-green bloom along a seam, like breath on a window.

She had come out and sat down on the bucket, and stayed there.

The grain bank was eleven years old. The parish had started it in 2038, the year the long rains failed and the short rains came late, when Father Kyalo's predecessor had stood at the church door and watched families sell their seed maize in March to buy food and then have nothing to plant. The idea was not new; there were grain banks all over Ukambani, some good and some ruined. At harvest, the bank bought maize from members at a fair price, or took it in as deposits, and stored it properly. In the hungry months it lent it back: seed for planting, food to get a family through to the next harvest, repaid in kind with a small top-up when the harvest came. Nobody had to sell their seed. Nobody had to buy maize at the July price.

And nobody had to eat bad grain. That was the other half of it, the half the old people cared about most, because in Makueni everybody over sixty remembered 2004. The bank bought only dry maize. Thirteen percent on the meter or under, a stricter line than the standard's thirteen and a half, chalked on the board on the veranda in Mzee Musyoka's square capitals: 13 OR UNDER GOES IN. He had read every sack himself, for eleven years, with the grey handheld meter the diocese had bought in 2039. He had turned away his own brother-in-law's maize at thirteen point four.

"Show me in order of moisture," said Mwende.

Ghala drew it on the phone's screen as a row of bars, one for each tenth of a percent, the way it drew everything: plainly, in two colours, a little too large, because it had been set up for Mzee Musyoka's eyes and nobody had changed it.

It was the parish's ledger agent. It kept the grain bank's books, and the parish's, and the school's fee register, and it answered the phone when Mwende was at the market. It had come with the diocese's accounting package in 2044 and somebody in Machakos had named it Ghala, which means a store, because that was what it had first been asked to keep. It said plainly what it could check and what it could not. When Mutuku had asked it once whether it liked football it had said it could tell him the score of any match he liked, and that liking was a thing it did not know how to check in itself, and Mutuku had said that was the most boring answer he had ever heard.

The bars went up from left to right. A few at eleven, more at twelve, and then at twelve point six they climbed steeply, twelve point seven, twelve point eight, twelve point nine, thirteen, and stopped dead. Nothing at thirteen point one. The seventeen sacks that had gone home were in a separate grey heap off to the right.

"Two hundred and forty-one of the three hundred and twenty read between twelve point five and thirteen," said Ghala. "That is three in every four, in half a point."

"Is that strange?"

"It's what happens when people know where the line is. Nobody brings maize to the store wetter than it needs to be, because Mzee turns it away and they have to carry it home. And nobody dries it more than they need to, because drying takes days and the price is the same. So they dry until they think it will pass, and then they come." It added another line to the screen, a small table. "It's the next part I wanted to show you. I have the time of every reading, because Mzee speaks the number to me when he reads it."

Every reading had been taken between twenty to two in the afternoon and ten past five. Every one. Mwende knew why before Ghala said it; anyone who lived in Ithiani would. Farmers came in the morning, with their sacks on donkey carts and wheelbarrows and the backs of boda-bodas, and queued. Mzee Musyoka opened the store at eight, but he did not start reading until after lunch, because he had always said maize should be read dry, and the sacks dried best standing open on the veranda in the sun. So they stood. Thirty, forty sacks in a row on the concrete, mouths rolled down, the top of the grain going pale and hot in the February sun while their owners sat in the shade of the mango tree and talked. And then after lunch Mzee went down the row with the meter, and scooped a cup from the top of each, and poured it into the meter's well, and read it.

"The top of a sack that has stood open in the sun for four or five hours is drier than the middle of it," said Ghala. "I don't know by how much, in this store, with these sacks. I can guess. A few tenths, perhaps most of a point, in the top hand's breadth. But the top is where the cup goes in."

"And the meter? It's a good meter."

"It is a good meter. I found its card." The screen showed a photograph Mwende had taken in 2046, when Ghala was learning the store: the laminated card that lived in the meter's case, in English and Swahili, curling at the corners. TEMPERATURE COMPENSATED 0–40 °C. And below that, smaller: For best accuracy sample and meter should be at the same temperature. Hot or freshly dried grain may read low. Allow sample to cool in a closed container before testing.

Mwende read it twice. "Mzee reads the first line."

"Everybody reads the first line," said Ghala. "The first line is in capitals."

On Monday the seventeenth the county sent two people out from Wote in a white pickup. Mrs Wairimu Njoroge, the public health officer, was a tall unhurried woman of about forty who looked at the stack, and smelled it, and looked at the bloom on the seam, and said nothing for a while. Mr Otieno Oduor from the agriculture office was younger and talked more. Between them they took samples from eight sacks in each of the six layers that were left, and then from the households.

That was the part that frightened Mwende, and she saw it frighten Father Kyalo, who had come down from the presbytery in his gumboots with his rosary in his trouser pocket and stood by the pickup with his arms folded. The bank had been lending since the first of April. Eighty sacks had gone out: seed in early April for the long-rains planting, and then food, a sack at a time, to families whose last harvest had run out. They had gone from the top of the stack, as they always did, because the top was where you could reach. Families had been eating that maize for six weeks. Mrs Njoroge took her name list from the ledger, which Ghala printed on the presbytery's printer, and went round twelve of the houses that afternoon with Mwende, and took a small bag from each.

"Aflatoxin doesn't smell," Mrs Njoroge said to her in the pickup, between houses, with the red dust coming up past the windows. "People think the musty smell is the poison. The smell is the mould. Sometimes the mould is the kind that makes aflatoxin and sometimes it isn't, and sometimes there's aflatoxin and no smell at all. You can't see it. You can't wash it out or cook it out. That's why we test."

"How long?"

"Friday. If the lab isn't full."

Mzee Musyoka had not come to the store on Monday. Mwende went to his house that evening, past the church and down the hill to the compound with the two water tanks and the old Peugeot on blocks, and his wife gave her tea, and he sat on the bench under the eaves and did not look at her.

"Ghala says something about my readings," he said.

"Ghala says your readings are honest."

"Of course they are honest. I have read maize since I was twenty. Before the bank I read it for the cereals board, at the depot." He turned his cup. "That meter is compensated. It says so on it. I read every sack myself, after lunch, when the grain is dry and the meter is warm. I never let anything in over thirteen. Not my brother-in-law, not the priest's cousin, nobody."

"I know."

"Then what does the machine want?"

Mwende had thought about how to say it on the walk down. "It wants to read the same sacks again in the morning. From the middle, with a spear. Cool."

"No," said Mzee Musyoka.

He said it quite gently. He was not an angry man; he was a careful one, which was why the parish had given him the keys. "You know what happens if I say now that my readings were wrong? Every farmer in Ithiani says, then the bank's maize is bad, all of it, and nobody borrows, and nobody deposits, and in July we have a store full of nothing. And for what? Because a phone has a feeling about the time of day." He put the cup down. "I did what the card says. The maize was dry. It got wet afterwards. The long rains came in April and the air in that store is like a bathroom. It needs drying again, that's all. We take it out, we dry it, we read it, we put it back."

It was, Mwende thought, walking back up the hill in the dark with the crickets loud in the cassava, a reasonable thing to say. It was even partly true. The long rains had been heavy that year. The air in the store had been damp for a month.

"Is he wrong?" she asked Ghala, when she was home.

"Partly not," said Ghala. "Damp air makes grain take up water, slowly, from the outside of a sack inwards. A store full of sacks at the line will drift over it in a wet season. I can't rule that out. But the bottom layer is the worst, and the bottom layer is the first maize you bought, in the second week of February. It has had the longest. And it was bought in the hottest, brightest weeks of the year, read in the afternoons, after the longest waits on the veranda, because that was when the queues were longest." It paused. "I would still like to read some sacks in the morning. I can't do it without him. It is his store."

On Friday the twenty-first the results came, by message, at a quarter past four, and Mwende read them standing in the office with the door shut.

The legal line for total aflatoxin in maize for food was ten micrograms in a kilogram. Ten parts in a thousand million. A handful of badly mouldy kernels can be enough in a whole sack.

The bottom layer, the first forty sacks, had come back at forty-six.

The second layer, at fourteen.

The third layer, at six. Under the line. The fourth, fifth and sixth layers, below the lowest figure the lab reported. And all twelve of the households' bags, every one, below the lowest figure the lab reported.

She sat down. She found she was saying the Memorare, in Kikamba, the way her grandmother had, without having decided to.

"The families are all right," she said, when she could.

"The twelve samples are below what the lab can see," said Ghala. "I want to be careful about how much that means. It is twelve houses out of the fifty-three that borrowed, and a few hundred grams from each, and aflatoxin is very uneven; a few kernels can carry nearly all of it. But the maize they borrowed came from the top two layers, which were the last you bought, in March, when the queues were shorter and the waits were shorter. I think it is very likely that they are all right. I don't know it."

"And the bottom two layers?"

"Mrs Njoroge has written. Eighty sacks. They can't be food, and the county won't let them go to animals either. The county will take them on Wednesday."

Eighty sacks. Seven tonnes and two hundred kilos of maize, a quarter of everything the bank had bought, the deposits of thirty-one families, which the bank would now owe back out of its own reserve, which was not large. Mwende looked at the number for a long time.

On Sunday, the Sixth Sunday of Easter, Father Kyalo read the results out at both Masses, in Kikamba and in Swahili, standing in front of the altar rather than at the ambo because, he said afterwards, he did not want to say it from the place where the Gospel was read. He said what the lab had found and what it had not. He said the families who had borrowed should not be afraid, and that anyone with a sick child should bring them to the dispensary at once, and that the bank would make good every deposit in the bottom layers, sack for sack, even if it took two harvests. Nobody clapped. At the back of the church Mzee Musyoka sat with his wife, very straight, with his hat on his knees.

They did it his way first. Mwende thought afterwards that this was right, and that she would have done the same in the committee's place; it was his store, and his way was the one written on the card, and nobody yet had shown him anything else.

From Monday the twenty-fourth to Saturday the twenty-ninth, the four layers that were left, one hundred and sixty sacks, came out of the store in the afternoons. Twenty-five or thirty a day. Mutuku and four other boys from the youth group carried them, two to a sack, and stood them on the veranda and on the drying floor beside it, mouths rolled down, in the sun. The long rains were ending; the afternoons were bright again. Each sack stood for two hours. Then Mzee Musyoka went down the row with the meter and the cup, scooping from the top, and read every one, and spoke each number to Ghala.

Twelve point one. Eleven point eight. Twelve point four. Eleven point six.

Not one was over twelve and a half. The highest was twelve point four. They went back in on new pallets, with a gap all round for air, and on the Saturday afternoon Mzee Musyoka stood in the doorway and looked at the restacked rows with his arms folded and the expression of a man who has been doubted and has fixed it anyway.

On Wednesday the county pickup came back with a lorry and took the eighty sacks away, under a tarpaulin, to be burned under supervision at the county's yard, and a small crowd came to watch it go, the way people watch a hearse.

On the next Saturday, the fifth of June, Mutuku went down the gap with his broom.

He came out more slowly than the first time, and found Mwende in the office, and did not say anything clever.

"Some are warm again," he said. "In the middle. Not the bottom ones. Middle ones."

Mr Oduor had left them a thermometer spear the week before, a steel rod with a dial on the top, because Ghala had asked for one. Mwende pushed it into eleven sacks in the third and fourth layers, the way Mr Oduor had shown her, and waited each time for the needle to settle, while Ghala wrote. The air in the store was twenty-three degrees. Seven of the sacks were at twenty-four or twenty-five, which was nothing. Four were at twenty-eight, twenty-nine, twenty-nine and thirty-one.

"Grain that is warmer than the air around it is making its own heat," said Ghala. "Something in it is breathing: the grain itself, moulds, insects. Dry grain hardly breathes at all. These four are wet in the middle, I think, and they were wet in the middle last week, when they read twelve on the veranda."

Mwende took the phone down to Mzee Musyoka's house. He was in the shamba behind it, with a jembe, earthing up sweet potatoes, and he listened with his hands on the handle and his head down.

"Thirty-one," he said, at the end.

"Thirty-one."

He went on working for a while. Then he straightened, slowly, with a hand in the small of his back.

"In 2004 I was twenty-three," he said. "You know this. Everybody knows this. I was working at the depot in Wote. The Mutisya family were our neighbours, the house by the dam. The father and the two big boys went yellow in May, one after the other. They took them to Makueni hospital and the father died there, and the second boy. The government came and took their maize away and brought new maize from the depot, our depot, and I carried it, sack by sack, into the house where they had all eaten. That maize had been stored wet. That is all it was. Stored wet in the house, and the rains came, and nobody knew." He looked at the phone in Mwende's hand as if it were a person. "So when the parish gave me the keys, I made a line. Thirteen. I read every sack myself. Eleven years."

"I know, Mzee."

"Tell the machine it can have its morning," he said. "But I hold the spear."

Mr Oduor came out from Wote on Monday the seventh at a quarter to seven, in a cold grey dawn, with his own meter in his jacket pocket. It was a newer one than the bank's. It had a thin probe on a cable that went into the grain beside the sample and read its temperature, and the reading on its screen was corrected for the grain's temperature and not the meter's. He also brought a sampling spear: a long brass tube with slots down its side and a pointed end, and an inner tube that turned to open and close the slots, so that you could push it into the middle of a sack closed, open it, let the grain fall in from the middle and the bottom, close it again and draw it out full.

Mzee Musyoka held the spear. He had used one at the depot thirty years before and his hands remembered it before he did; Mwende watched him push it into the first sack at an angle, deep, give the inner tube a quarter turn, wait, turn it back, and draw it out, and empty the long cylinder of grain into a clean glass jar with a lid. The jar went into the shade in the office, with the others, to sit for half an hour.

"The grain in the middle of a sack at seven in the morning is at about the temperature of the night," said Ghala. "And the jar keeps the water in while it settles. That's what the card meant, I think, by allowing it to cool in a closed container. It just said it in small letters."

They did forty. Ghala chose them, ten from each of the four layers, by numbering every sack in the stack and drawing forty numbers with the phone, which Mutuku said was cheating because the phone could choose whichever it liked, and Ghala said that was a fair objection and drew them again with Mutuku calling out numbers from a bus ticket. It came to the same thing.

At half past seven Mzee Musyoka began to read the jars with the bank's meter, and Mr Oduor read the same jars with his, and Ghala wrote down both, and after the first six the two meters were agreeing to within a tenth or two, and Mr Oduor put his away.

Thirteen point four. Twelve point two. Thirteen point nine. Twelve point eight. Fourteen point three. Thirteen point one. Twelve point five.

Mzee Musyoka read each number aloud in the same flat voice he used on the veranda. He did not stop or comment. Only once, at fourteen point six, he read it a second time.

Of the forty jars, fifteen read over thirteen. Five read over fourteen. Every one of those forty sacks had read thirteen or under in the afternoon sun, in February or March, and every one of them had read twelve and a half or under in the afternoon sun the week before last.

"Fifteen in forty is about three in eight," said Ghala. "If the stack is like the sample, and I think it is near enough, then about sixty of the hundred and sixty sacks are wetter in the middle than the line, and about twenty are wetter than fourteen. Those are the ones that will warm. The four that were warm on Saturday were all from the over-fourteen kind. I checked. Two of them are in the forty."

Mzee Musyoka did not answer. He had picked up the sack that had read fourteen point six. He carried it out onto the veranda himself, which at sixty-eight he should not have done, and stood it in the full sun, and rolled down its mouth.

"Three o'clock," he said. "I want to see."

At three o'clock the veranda was hot enough that Mutuku hopped. Mr Oduor had gone back to Wote and left his meter. Mzee Musyoka took the bank's meter, which had been sitting on the windowsill in the sun all afternoon as it always had, and scooped a cup from the top of the sack, and poured it in, and read it.

Eleven point nine.

Then he pushed Mr Oduor's thin probe into the top of the same sack and read the temperature off its screen. Forty-one degrees.

Then he held the bank's meter in his hand and looked at it for a while, and then at the card in the case, and then he took the card out, and read the small print, out loud, in English, slowly, and then translated it for Mutuku, who had not asked.

"Hot grain may read low," he said.

"Nearly three points," said Ghala quietly. "Fourteen point six this morning, in the middle, cool. Eleven point nine now, from the top, hot. The top has dried in the sun since seven, longer than most sacks stood in February; I don't know exactly how much, perhaps a point or more. And warm grain fools this kind of meter even when it says it is compensated, if the grain is much hotter than what the meter thinks it is, or if the outside of every kernel has been dried and the inside has not. I can't separate the two from one sack. In February, with shorter waits, I think together they came to about two points. And the line was thirteen. So in the afternoon, from the top, your meter let in sacks that were up to about fifteen in the middle."

"And I turned away seventeen," said Mzee Musyoka.

"You turned away seventeen that were wetter than that. Your line was a real line. It was just in a different place from where it was written."

He sat down on the edge of the drying floor in the heat, with the meter on his knee. Mwende sat down beside him. For a while none of them said anything, and somewhere down the hill a woman was calling a child in, and a goat was answering, as goats do, to the wrong name.

"Eleven years," he said.

"Eleven good years," said Mwende. "The bank has never had this before."

"The bank has had dry years. This was the first wet year with a full store." He turned the meter over. "So. Morning. From the middle. In a jar. The new meter. And anything over thirteen?"

"Back to the floor," said Mwende. "Not into the stack. Spread out, not in the sack. Turned. Read again the next morning."

"Back to the floor," he said, as if he were trying the words for size, and then, because he was who he was: "Mutuku. Get the chalk."

It took them nine days to go through the hundred and sixty with the spear, every sack, fifteen or twenty a morning, before the sun was up properly. Fifty-eight read over thirteen. They went out onto the drying floor and the veranda and the church's netball court on tarpaulins borrowed from three parishes, spread thin, turned with rakes every two hours by boys who were paid in soda and complained in proportion, and covered at night. They came back to thirteen and under in three or four days, read in the morning, from a jar. None of them had to go to the county. The four that had been warm were sampled again by Mrs Njoroge and came back under the line, which Ghala said meant they had been caught in time, and which Mzee Musyoka said meant God was patient with old fools, and neither of them argued with the other.

The bank still owed thirty-one families eighty sacks. Father Kyalo wrote to the diocese. The diocese wrote back with sympathy and a third of the money. The parish ran two harambees and a raffle for a goat, which Mutuku's mother won and gave back, and then it was won again by the catechist, who kept it. By July they had found two thirds of what they owed, and the families agreed to take the rest from the next harvest. Nobody stopped borrowing. Mwende had been afraid they would, and they did not, and when she said so to Mrs Njoroge on the phone, Mrs Njoroge said that people do not stop trusting a store that tells them the truth; they stop trusting one that does not.

The long-rains harvest came in thin, the way it usually did in Makueni, and on Saturday the twenty-fourth of July the bank opened for buying.

It was cold. July was the coldest month, and at a quarter to seven the sky over the Mbooni hills was the colour of weak tea and everybody in the queue had their hands in their armpits. There were nineteen sacks on the veranda already, on carts and barrows and the back of a boda-boda whose rider was asleep against the mango tree. None of them had their mouths rolled down. The new board was nailed up beside the door, under the old one, in Mzee Musyoka's square capitals:

MORNING. MIDDLE OF THE SACK. COOL JAR. 13 OR UNDER GOES IN. OVER 13 GOES BACK TO THE FLOOR.

Mzee Musyoka came out in his old brown jacket with the brass spear under his arm and the new meter in his pocket, the one the county had found for them, with its probe on a cable. He nodded to the queue. He pushed the spear into the first sack, at an angle, deep, gave it its quarter turn, waited, turned it back, drew it out, and emptied it into a jar.

The first sack belonged to Mama Ndinda from the top of the hill, who had been selling maize to the bank for nine years and had never had one turned away, and who stood with her chin up while the jar sat in the shade. At seven he read it.

"Thirteen point four," he said. "Back to the floor, Mama."

For a moment she looked as if she would argue. Then she looked at the board, and at the old man, and at the ruts by the gate, dried hard now, where the county's lorry had turned in May. She untied the sack and tipped it out onto the tarpaulin on the drying floor, and the maize ran out over the plastic with the soft rushing sound it always makes, like the start of rain on a tin roof, and spread, and settled, and lay still in the cold light to wait for the sun.

Nothing about any real family in 2004 is told here. In 2049, Easter is 18 April and 23 May is the Sixth Sunday of Easter; 8 February and 17 May are Mondays, 20 March, 15 May, 5 June and 24 July Saturdays, 21 May a Friday and 7 June a Monday. The speculative step is small: a parish grain bank with a ledger agent on an old phone.

The thresholds. The East African Standard for maize grain (EAS 2) sets a maximum moisture of 13.5 percent and a maximum total aflatoxin (B1, B2, G1 and G2 together) of 10 micrograms per kilogram, with 5 micrograms per kilogram for aflatoxin B1 alone; the story's bank buys at 13 or under, a stricter line of its own. Storage moulds in general begin to grow when the air between kernels is above roughly 70 percent relative humidity, which for maize at tropical temperatures corresponds to roughly 14 percent moisture; Aspergillus flavus, the mould that makes most aflatoxin in maize, needs damper conditions still, and grain that is wet in the middle warms as it, its moulds and its insects respire, which moves moisture to the grain around it. Aflatoxin cannot be seen, smelled, washed or cooked out, and a musty smell means mould, not necessarily aflatoxin. On meters: extension advice from Purdue University and North Dakota State University says electronic (capacitance) moisture meters underestimate the moisture of hot or freshly dried grain that has not reached equilibrium, by one to two points or about two points depending on how it was dried, and recommends letting a sample cool in a sealed container before testing; the UK's Agriculture and Horticulture Development Board gives a smaller allowance for freshly dried grain. How much any particular meter reads low depends on the meter, the grain and how far it is from equilibrium.

The model. Every figure here is invented and the story's own. The veranda reading of a sack, taken from the top cup after hours standing open in the sun, is taken as about 2.0 points below a cool morning reading from its middle: about 0.8 for the sun-dried top and about 1.2 for the hot grain fooling the meter (the story's one side-by-side sack: 14.6 in the morning, 11.9 at 41 degrees in the afternoon, a 2.7-point gap that includes a few hours of extra drying at the top). So a veranda line of 13.0 admits sacks up to about 15.0 in the middle. Of 337 sacks offered in February and March, 17 read over 13.0 on the veranda and went home (about 15 or more in the middle) and 320 were stacked, 40 to a layer, the earliest at the bottom. In the model, those 320 have middle moistures of 13.0 or under (196 sacks), 13.1 to 14.0 (82) and 14.1 to 15.0 (42): 124 of 320, about 39 percent, would fail a cool morning middle reading at 13.0, and 42, about 13 percent, are over 14. A random sample of 40 from the remaining 160 is expected to show about 15.5 over 13.0 and about 5.3 over 14; the story's sample shows 15 and 5, and the full pass finds 58 of 160 over 13.0, near the model's 62.

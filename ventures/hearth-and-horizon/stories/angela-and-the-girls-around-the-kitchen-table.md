A long time ago, beside a big blue lake in the north of Italy, there was a farm. The lake was called Lake Garda, and the little town by the farm was called Desenzano.

On the farm lived a girl called Angela.

Angela had a mother and a father, and brothers, and a big sister she loved very much. In the evenings her father read to them, from a book about the saints. Angela sat very still and listened. She loved the stories of people who belonged to God with their whole hearts.

*I would like to be like that*, she thought. *I would like to belong to God.*

* * *

But when Angela was still a girl, sad things happened.

Her father died. And then her mother died. Angela and her sister had to leave the farm and go to live with their uncle, in another town on the shore of the lake.

Their uncle was kind to them. But Angela missed her mother and father very much. Sometimes, at night, she lay awake and looked at the dark and felt very small.

And then her dear sister died too.

Angela cried and cried. And she did what she had learned to do when she was sad. She talked to God about it. She told him everything, even the parts that hurt the most.

People who told Angela's story long afterwards said that God gave her a sign of comfort then, so that she knew her sister was safe with him. We do not know exactly what happened. But we do know that Angela did not stop praying, and she did not stop loving God, even when her heart was sore.

* * *

When Angela grew up, she joined the family of Saint Francis, as a sister who stayed out in the world. She did not go into a convent behind a wall. She lived simply, and wore plain clothes, and ate plain food, and she prayed and worked and looked after people.

After a while her uncle died too, and Angela went home to Desenzano, to the farm by the lake.

And there she noticed something.

* * *

Many of the girls in the farms and lanes around her did not know very much about God.

There were no schools for them. Hardly any girls in those days learned to read. Their mothers and fathers were busy from morning until night, in the fields and the kitchen and the barn. Nobody had time to sit down with the girls and tell them about Jesus, and his mother Mary, and how much God loved them.

So Angela thought, *I can do that.*

She did not build a school. She did not have any money to build a school. She had a house, and a table, and a kettle on the fire.

So she asked the girls to come.

* * *

They came in ones and twos at first, after their work was done. They sat round Angela's kitchen table with their elbows on the wood.

And Angela told them stories. The stories her father had read to her. The stories of Jesus. She taught them the prayers: the Our Father, and the Hail Mary. She taught them how to be kind, and how to be honest, and how to say sorry. She laughed with them, and listened to their troubles, and prayed with them.

The girls went home and told their sisters. Their sisters came too. Soon there was not enough room on the bench, and girls were sitting on the floor and on the step.

* * *

When Angela was in her forties, some friars asked her to go to the big city of Brescia, not far from the lake, to comfort a lady whose children had died. Angela knew all about that kind of sadness. So she went.

She stayed in Brescia for the rest of her life.

In the city, people came to Angela too. Not just girls now. Grown-ups, rich ones and poor ones, came to ask her advice and to ask her to pray for them. And they found that she was wise, and gentle, and full of peace.

* * *

But Angela had been thinking for a long time about a new idea.

In those days, if a woman wanted to give her whole life to God, there were two ways to do it. She could get married and look after a family. Or she could go into a convent, behind a wall, and live with the other sisters there.

Angela thought, *What if there were another way?*

*What if a young woman could belong to God with her whole heart, but stay right where she is, at home, with her family, in her own town? She could pray, and live simply, and be good, and she could teach the girls on her street about God, just as I did at my kitchen table.*

Some people thought it was a strange idea. Nobody had a company quite like that. But Angela was patient. She prayed about it for years.

* * *

And then, one day in November, when Angela was about sixty years old, twenty-eight young women came together with her in a little room in Brescia, next to a church.

They promised to belong to God with their whole hearts. They would not live in a convent. They would not wear a special habit. They would go on living in their own homes, with their own families, and they would pray, and they would help the girls of the city to know and love God.

Angela gave the company a name. She named it after Saint Ursula, a holy woman from long ago who was said to have led a great company of young women. So they were called the Company of Saint Ursula.

Angela wrote down a rule for them, so they would know how to live. And she wrote them advice, full of love. She told the older ones to look after the younger ones gently, the way a mother looks after her children, and never to be harsh.

* * *

Angela lived a few more years. And then, one January, in the cold of the winter, she died, very peacefully, in Brescia, and went home to God.

But her company did not stop.

It grew, and it spread. In time, some of the Ursulines went to live together in houses and to run schools, and they went all over the world, across the oceans, and taught thousands and thousands of girls. The Ursulines still teach today.

And it all began at a kitchen table by a lake, with a kettle on the fire, and a girl who said, *I can do that.*

We remember Saint Angela Merici on the twenty-seventh of January.

* * *

**A little talk before sleep**

- Angela did not have a school, but she had a table. What do you have that you could share with someone?
- Angela was very sad when people she loved died, and she told God all about it. What could you tell God about tonight?
- Angela said the big girls should look after the little ones gently. Is there someone smaller than you that you could be gentle with?

**A prayer**

> Dear Saint Angela,
> you gathered the girls around your table
> and told them how much God loves them.
> Help me to share what I have,
> to be gentle with people smaller than me,
> and to tell God everything, even when I am sad.
> Pray for all teachers,
> and for every child who is learning,
> and for everyone who is going to sleep tonight.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Angela Merici** was born at Desenzano del Garda, on the south-west shore of Lake Garda in the Republic of Venice, most often dated 21 March 1474 (some sources give 1470 or 1475, and the story gives no year). Her father, Giovanni Merici, was a small landowner farming at Le Grezze near Desenzano, and her mother was of the Biancosi family of Salò. The early lives say her father read to the family from lives of the saints, and that she had at least one sister and brothers; the story's picture of the evenings is ours.

**Orphaned.** Both parents died while she was young (the lives disagree on her age, from about ten to fifteen), and she and her sister went to live with a maternal uncle, Biancosi, at Salò. The sister died soon afterwards. The lives tell that Angela, distressed that her sister had died without the last sacraments, received in prayer a vision or assurance that she was in heaven; that is a tradition we could not trace to a document from her lifetime, so the story tells it as something "people who told Angela's story long afterwards said".

**The Franciscan tertiary.** She became a member of the Third Order of Saint Francis, which is the story's "family of Saint Francis, as a sister who stayed out in the world", and lived with great simplicity. When her uncle died (she was about twenty) she returned to Desenzano.

**The teaching at home.** The lives say that at Desenzano she gathered the girls of the neighbourhood and taught them the faith, and that this was the seed of her later work. The kettle, the bench, the step and every word spoken are ours. That girls of her class rarely learned to read is broadly true of the time and place; the story does not say Angela taught reading.

**Left out: the ladder at Brudazzo.** Later lives tell that, at about the turn of the sixteenth century, at a place called Brudazzo near Desenzano, she saw in a vision a ladder reaching to heaven with young women and angels going up it, and heard that she would found a company of women. It is a tradition from the later biographies, not from her own writings, and is left out of a story for small children.

**Brescia.** In 1516 the Franciscan friars sent her to Brescia to comfort Caterina Patengola, a widow who had lost her husband and children. She settled in the city and remained there, apart from journeys, until her death, and was widely sought out for counsel. **Left out: the journeys.** In 1524 she went on pilgrimage to the Holy Land; the lives say she lost her sight on the way, at Crete, visited the holy places blind, and recovered her sight at the same place on the way home. In the Jubilee of 1525 she went to Rome, and the lives say Clement VII received her and invited her to stay, and she declined. During the wars of 1529 she took refuge in Cremona. These are told in the biographies; the healing is a tradition, and the papal audience is reported, not documented in a papal record we could find. All are left out to keep the story to one thread.

**The Company of Saint Ursula.** On 25 November 1535 (the feast of Saint Catherine of Alexandria), in a small room near the church of Sant'Afra in Brescia, Angela and twenty-eight young women formed the Company of Saint Ursula. Its members made no public religious vows in a convent and wore no habit; they lived consecrated, celibate lives in their own families' homes, prayed, and taught girls the faith. The story calls it "a new kind of company": Christian women had lived consecrated lives in their own homes since the early Church, but a company of this kind, organised under a rule and given to teaching, was new in her time, and the Ursulines are often called the first women's teaching order. Saint Ursula is a legendary early martyr of Cologne, said to have led a company of maidens; the story says "said to have". Angela was about sixty-one if born in 1474; the story says "about sixty".

**Her writings.** Her Rule was approved by the vicar general of Brescia in 1536 and confirmed by Pope Paul III in 1544, after her death. Her *Counsels* (*Ricordi*) and *Testament* to the leaders of the Company survive; the story paraphrases their spirit (gentleness, motherly care, no harshness) and quotes none of them.

**Her death and after.** She died at Brescia on 27 January 1540 and was buried in Sant'Afra, the church now called Sant'Angela Merici. After her death, and especially after the Council of Trent, many groups of Ursulines, beginning in Milan under Saint Charles Borromeo, came to live in community and later in enclosure, running schools; Ursulines went to Quebec in 1639 and to New Orleans in 1727, and many congregations of Ursulines teach today, alongside secular institutes that keep her original form of life. She was beatified in 1768 and canonized by Pius VII on 24 May 1807. Her feast, 27 January, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar (before the 1969 calendar it was kept on 31 May, then 1 June).

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, all thoughts and dialogue, the kitchen table and kettle, the girls on the floor and the step, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

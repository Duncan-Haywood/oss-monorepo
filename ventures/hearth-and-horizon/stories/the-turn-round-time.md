Ifor Pugh was seventy-eight and had walked to Saint Ceinwen's hill chapel on All Saints' Day for sixty-one years, the last twelve of them with a stick. This year the parish wanted to go together, eleven of them, including the Davies twins, who were nine. Mair Davies, their mother, asked the parish assistant, which the parish called Linnet, whether it was sensible.

The chapel was a roofless wall and a spring, 7.5 kilometres from the church by the old drove road, and 480 metres higher. It was the first of November. The plan on the noticeboard said *meet at the church at 11*.

* * *

"I can't tell you whether it's sensible," said Linnet. "That depends on how the group walks on the day, and on weather I can't see. What I can do is the time, and the time is the thing people usually get wrong. Can you tell me the distances?"

Mair read them from the walkers' map: 7.5 kilometres up, the same back, 480 metres of climb in the first four-fifths of it.

"There's an old rule for hill walking, from a Scottish climber named Naismith, in 1892. Five kilometres an hour on the flat, and an extra hour for every 600 metres climbed. It's for fit people walking steadily. Your group is not that, and Mr Pugh will set the pace, which is as it should be. If I use three kilometres an hour, and keep Naismith's extra minute for every ten metres, then the way up is 7.5 divided by 3, which is 150 minutes, and 48 for the climb. That is 198 minutes. Three hours eighteen."

Mair said that sounded long. Linnet said it was long, and that the twins would be quick up the hill and slow on the way down, which was another reason for a plain number.

"Coming down, Naismith adds nothing on gentle ground. At the same three kilometres an hour it's 150 minutes, two and a half hours. Together that is five hours forty-eight minutes of walking. You will stop. For the spring, for lunch, for a boy with a stone in his boot. Say forty minutes in all. That's six hours and twenty-eight."

* * *

"Starting at eleven, you'd be home at 17:28."

Mair did not say anything.

"Sunset on the first of November in your valley is, by the almanac you have in the vestry, about ten to five. I'm taking that figure from you, not from memory. After sunset there's roughly forty minutes of usable light. So starting at eleven, you'd finish the descent in the dusk, on a track with a ditch beside it, with a seventy-eight-year-old and two nine-year-olds. People do it. Many of them have torches. I'd rather you didn't plan it so."

Ifor, who had been listening from the vestry door, said he had never needed a torch.

"You've never been walking that last stretch at five in the afternoon at seventy-eight, Mr Pugh, with eleven people waiting for you to say you're fine. I'd say it because they can't."

Mair laughed, though not much.

* * *

They tried it the other way. Start at ten, which meant Mass at half past eight with no breakfast, which Father Idris said he could do and would. Ten plus 3 hours 18 is 13:18 at the chapel. Add the forty minutes of stops and the whole day comes to 6 hours 28, which puts them home at 16:28. That is twenty-two minutes of daylight in hand, and no more.

"That's the plan," said Linnet, "and plans slip. So let me also give you a number you can use without me. Take your last safe time to be 16:20 in the church yard, thirty minutes before sunset. Count back for the descent: two and a half hours of walking and ten minutes of stops. That is 13:40. Whoever is leading, wherever the group is on the hill at 13:40, they turn round. Write the time on the back of the leader's hand. It doesn't matter if they are twenty minutes short of the chapel, or at the door of it. They can have the blessing at the spring on the lower terrace."

Ifor said that was a hard thing to ask of a man who had walked it sixty-one times.

"It is," said Linnet. "It doesn't say you aren't fit. It's the time on the clock."

* * *

They set off at ten. The twins ran the first kilometre and walked the next six. At half past twelve, at a gate with a view of the whole valley, they were forty minutes behind, because a lamb had been stuck in a fence and Ifor had insisted on helping, which was right. At 13:40 Mair, who had been given the leader's job and had the time pencilled on the back of her hand, was at the foot of the last rise, some twelve minutes short of the wall.

She looked at the hand. She looked at Ifor, who was sitting on a rock, not saying anything, with his stick across his knees.

They turned round at 13:40, at the lower terrace, where there was a second, smaller spring that Ifor said his grandmother had always used, and Father Idris said the prayers there, with the wall of the chapel a few hundred metres above them in the grey. The twins asked if they could go up. Nobody let them.

The church yard was reached at 16:21. There was still colour in the sky behind the hill. Ifor said he would see the chapel next year, and Mair noticed that he did not say *the wall*, or *the roof*, or *the walk*.

summary: In 2612 the harbour chapel of Stella Maris, built at the water's edge in 2190 when the sea seemed to have stopped falling, stands a kilometre inland, because the land is still rising from the last ice age by about seven millimetres a year and the sea has slowed to two. The village at the new shore wants a chapel of its own, the old village's water owners hold the land that rises under it, and a log builder of seventy-one proposes a church built to be taken down and moved again.
---

Märta Byström had moved eleven houses in her life, every one of them log, and never once a church.

She told Father Pham so on the last Saturday in April, standing in the birch wood behind Sundby with her hands in the pockets of her work coat. The two of them were looking at a row of iron rings set into a granite wall. The wall ran straight through the wood for sixty metres, waist high, with moss on its top and young birches growing out of the cracks along its foot, and every four metres there was a ring as thick as a wrist, rusted the colour of a fox.

"I know," said Father Pham. "I am not asking you to move this one. I am asking you whether a church can be built that somebody could."

"Anything made of logs can be moved," said Märta. "That is what logs are for."

Behind them, up a gentle slope through the trees, the white gable of the chapel of Stella Maris showed between the trunks. It had been built in 2190 of brick and render on a granite plinth, with a small square tower over the door and a bell in the tower, and it had been built, as the plaque by its door still said, *at the water's edge, for those who go down to the sea in ships*. The quay wall with the rings in it had been its harbour. Four centuries of fishing boats had tied up here.

The nearest water now was a kilometre away, to the west, through the birch wood and across a hay meadow and a strip of alder scrub and a reed bed, at the end of a new road, in another village.

* * *

The other village was called Yttersund, which means the outer sound, and it was a hundred and sixty years old. Before it there had been a sound, a channel of open water between Sundby's point and the low island to the west, and Sundby's boats had gone out through it. In Märta's grandmother's time the channel had become a passage only for rowing boats, then a ditch, then a wet meadow with a ditch in it. The island was not an island any more. It was the place where the road went.

Everyone on this coast grew up knowing why. The ice that had lain on Scandinavia in the last glaciation had been three kilometres thick, and it had pressed the Earth's crust down into the mantle beneath it the way a hand presses down a mattress. The ice had melted about ten thousand years ago. The mattress was still coming back up. Here, in the narrows of the Kvarken between Finland and Sweden, it was coming up by about seven millimetres a year, the width of a pencil, every year, for longer than there had been anyone to measure it. The Swedes across the water had the highest raised beach in the world, two hundred and eighty-six metres up a mountainside. There were tens of metres still to come.

What nobody on the coast grew up knowing, because for two centuries it had not been true, was that the shore moved.

"They thought it had stopped," said Märta. "The people who built that."

"Some of them did." Father Pham touched the top of the wall. He was a small, neat man of fifty with a grey cardigan under his coat, and he looked after five chapels along a hundred and forty kilometres of coast; he had been born in Vaasa, of a family that had come from Vietnam to Finland in another century and had been Catholic there for longer than Sundby had been a village. "Ask Shoreline. It explains it better than I do."

Märta took her phone out of her coat. The municipality's coastal system was called Shoreline because that was its job: it kept the survey stations along the coast, the tide gauges and the satellite positions, and every spring it redrew the line where the land stopped and the water began. It answered in a dry, unhurried voice.

"For about two hundred years, from the late twenty-first century to the late twenty-third, the sea in the Gulf of Bothnia rose almost as fast as the land did," it said. "The ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica were losing mass quickly. Sea level here was going up by about seven millimetres a year and the land was going up by a little under eight. The difference was under a millimetre a year, ten centimetres in a century. A person standing on this quay in 2190 would have seen no change in their lifetime. The records the builders had, from the century before, showed the shore slowing to a stop."

"So they built at the water," said Märta.

"They built the floor of the chapel one point two metres above the mean sea level of 2190," said Shoreline. "That is a sensible height for a storm surge. Then the warming was held, and over the next two centuries the sea's rise slowed. It is about two millimetres a year here now. The land is still rising at about seven. Since 2190 the land has risen about three point one metres and the sea about one point seven. The chapel floor is two point six metres above the sea today. The slope of the ground between here and the water is about one in seven hundred, so a rise of one point four metres moves the shore about a kilometre."

"And the next village," said Father Pham.

"Yttersund's harbour is at the shore now. At present rates the shore will move about three and a half metres seaward this year, and something like that every year."

Märta put the phone away. Three and a half metres a year. She had known it, the way you know your own age. She had not put it into a sentence before.

"The bishop's office wants a decision by Sea Sunday," said Father Pham. "Yttersund has asked for a chapel. They have been asking for forty years. The bishop says Sundby has a chapel a kilometre away and the diocese has no money for another, and Sundby says the chapel is Sundby's, and Yttersund says it is called Star of the Sea and it is in a wood." He smiled a little. "They are all right. That is the difficulty."

* * *

The joint meeting was held the next Saturday in the hall at Sundby, because Sundby had a hall.

Märta had not meant to say anything. She sat at the back next to the coat rack and listened to the parish council read its proposal, which was generous and which she could see from the faces in the front rows was going to fail.

The proposal was this. Sundby's delägarlag, the association of shareholders who jointly owned the village's water, would pay for a small chapel at Yttersund. Sundby could afford it, because the delägarlag owned something nobody else on the coast did: the land that came up out of the sea. By the old rule on this coast, ground that rose out of a water area belonged to whoever owned the water, and Sundby's water had always run from the old point out to the fishing banks. Every house in Yttersund stood on land that had risen out of Sundby's water. Every house in Yttersund paid ground rent to Sundby's delägarlag for it. Along the three kilometres of Sundby's shore, at three and a half metres a year, about a hectare a year rose out of the sea, and the delägarlag owned every square metre.

The chapel at Yttersund would be a chapel of ease of Stella Maris. Sundby's bell would stay in Sundby's tower. Sundby's name would stay on Sundby's door.

Teodora Ramos stood up when it had been read. She was the harbour master at Yttersund, a broad woman in her forties with a fisherman's forearms and a voice that carried over engines. Her people had come to this coast from the Philippines by way of the ships, generations back, when about a quarter of the seafarers in the world had been Filipino and the Church's mission to seafarers had been named, then as now, for Mary, Star of the Sea. She did not raise her voice in the hall. She did not need to.

"Thank you," she said. "No."

There was a silence.

"It is very kind," Teodora said. "Truly. But you are offering us a hut, with your money and your name on it, on your land, which we already pay you for. We do not want a gift. We do not want to be your chapel of ease. Stella Maris is a harbour chapel. The harbour is ours now. The boats are ours. The bell was hung to be heard on the water, and with a kilometre of wood and our ridge in front of it you can't hear it on the water any more. We were out on the banks in fog last November and nobody heard it." She looked along the front row. "We are not asking for charity. We are asking you to see where the sea is."

Holger Sjöberg stood up then. He was eighty-three and the chairman of the delägarlag, and he had a reasonable voice and a reasonable face and Märta had known him all her life.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry, Teodora. No. The bell stays. The name stays. My wife is buried under that bell, and my parents, and four hundred years of this village. The dead don't move because the sea does. If you take the bell and the name, you are telling us that our church is the one in the wood that nobody goes to. You are telling us we are the back of beyond. We will build you a chapel. We will build you a good one. But we will not give you ours."

It went on for another hour after that, and nobody changed their mind, and at the end Father Pham said a prayer and everyone went home.

* * *

Märta walked down to Yttersund on the Monday, the whole kilometre, because she wanted to feel the length of it in her legs.

The road ran out of Sundby past the chapel and down through the birch wood where the old quay was, then across the hay meadow that had been the sound. The ground fell so gently that you could not see it fall; you knew it only because the trees changed. Birch and spruce at the top, where the land had been dry for a few hundred years. Then alder, which likes its feet wet. Then a strip of willow scrub, and then the reed bed, and then the first houses of Yttersund on a low gravel ridge, and then the harbour.

It was not a harbour the way Sundby's had been one. It was a floating one. The jetties were pontoons on piles, and every few years the harbour board cut the piles shorter and dragged the pontoons another few metres out, and every twenty or thirty years it dredged the channel, and every fifty or so it gave up dredging and built the whole harbour again further down. Teodora's office was a container on skids. Märta had not noticed that before. Everything in Yttersund that mattered was on skids or on piles or on wheels.

Teodora was on the end of the outermost pontoon with a boat hook, fishing a plastic crate out of the water. She looked up and was not pleased to see Märta and was too well brought up to show much of it.

"I didn't come about the meeting," said Märta.

"Everybody comes about the meeting."

"I came to ask how far out you will be in two hundred and fifty years."

Teodora put the crate down on the pontoon. Then she laughed, short and surprised. "Ask Shoreline," she said. "I have."

Märta asked. Shoreline took longer to answer this time.

"I can give you the land to within about two tenths of a millimetre a year," it said. "It has been measured for centuries and it is slowing very slowly. Over the next two hundred and fifty years it will rise about one point seven five metres. I cannot give you the sea to anything like that. If the sea here rises at one millimetre a year, the shore will move about a kilometre seaward of this harbour. If it rises at four, the shore will move about half a kilometre. If the ice sheets lose mass as fast as they did in the twenty-second century, which nobody expects but nobody can rule out, it could stand still again, as it did for the people who built Stella Maris. My best estimate is about nine hundred metres. I would not build anything on that estimate that could not be moved."

Teodora was watching Märta's face.

"That," said Märta, "is the most useful thing anyone has said in a month."

* * *

She drew it at her kitchen table over the next fortnight, at night, on squared paper with a pencil, the way her father had taught her and his mother had taught him.

A log chapel, eleven metres by seven, of pine from the delägarlag's own forest on the ridge. Corner joints of the old kind, dovetailed, no nails and no glue, so that every log lifted off the one below it. A shingled roof on rafters that came off in sections. A small open belfry on four posts at the west end, over the door, high enough for a bell to be heard on the water. A granite sill under it all, in eight blocks, each one small enough for a tractor to lift.

On the drawings she numbered every log. That was the old way, too. When a log house was taken down to be moved, which on this coast had been done for as long as there had been log houses, the builder cut a mark into the end of every log before the first one came off, wall by wall and course by course, so that it went up again in the same order. Her father's marks had been Roman numerals cut with a chisel, with a notch for each wall. Hers would be the same. Every log on the drawing had its mark before it had a tree.

When she had finished she took the drawings to Father Pham, and then to Teodora, and then, on a wet evening at the end of May, to Holger Sjöberg.

* * *

He was where she had known he would be, in the churchyard at Stella Maris, by his wife's grave. He was not praying. He was pulling the grass from round the foot of the stone with his fingers, slowly, as he did every week.

Märta sat down on the bench by the wall and waited until he had finished, then held out the roll of drawings. He did not take them.

"I know what they are," he said. "Teodora told my granddaughter and my granddaughter told me."

"Then you know what I am going to ask."

"You want the bell."

"I want the bell and the name," said Märta. "Stella Maris at Yttersund, on Yttersund's land, built so it can follow the sea. And I want something else, which you will like less."

He sat down beside her on the bench. The rain had stopped and the birches below them were dripping. Somewhere down the slope, where the old quay wall ran through the wood, a blackbird was singing.

"Go on."

"The water," said Märta. "The water seaward of Yttersund. The delägarlag owns it, out to the fishing banks. Every hectare that comes up out of it for the next four hundred years will be yours, and your shareholders' grandchildren's, and the people who live on it will pay ground rent to Sundby for ground that was never Sundby's to make. They don't exist yet. They will be the next village down. I want the delägarlag to put the water in trust for them. Everything seaward of the present shore. Whatever comes up, belongs to whoever lives there when it does."

Holger was quiet for a long time.

"You want me to give away land that doesn't exist," he said at last.

"Yes," said Märta. "It's the only kind you can give to people who don't exist yet."

He made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "My father would have thrown you out of the hall."

"Your father," said Märta, "built a jetty out of this village in 2541 that was dry in his own lifetime. I've seen the photographs."

That did make him laugh, briefly, and then he stopped.

"And this," he said, and nodded at the chapel behind them, at the white gable and the little square tower. "When the bell goes. What is this?"

"This is where the dead are," said Märta. "It stays where they are. Father Pham says he'll keep the Mass for the dead here, every November, and the Assumption, which is the day it was blessed in 2190. It isn't the back of beyond. It is where the village started." She hesitated. "Every church on this coast ends up inland, Holger. They moved the whole town of Luleå in the sixteen hundreds because the harbour was getting too shallow. They didn't move the church. The old church town is still there, well back from the water."

He looked at the stone with his wife's name on it. *Anna-Lisa Sjöberg, 2531–2604.* Then he looked down the slope through the trees, west, where you could not see the water.

"She used to say," he said, "that you could hear the bell from the boats, when she was a girl, before the wood grew up. That her father used to steer home by it in the fog."

"You can't now," said Märta. "Teodora's people couldn't, last November."

"No," said Holger. "I know."

He held out his hand for the drawings.

* * *

The delägarlag met on the first Saturday in June. Holger put the motion himself. It did not pass easily. Two families voted against it and one walked out, and a man Märta had known for fifty years did not speak to her for the rest of the summer and probably never would again. But it passed. The water seaward of the shore of 6 June 2612, out to the fishing banks, went into a trust whose beneficiaries were whoever should come to live on the land that rose from it, from Yttersund and the villages after. The ground rent Yttersund already paid did not change; the delägarlag kept the land it already had. It was not justice, Teodora said, but it was the start of the next lot of justice, and she would take it.

Yttersund gave the site: a piece of its own gravel ridge, at the top of the harbour, on a strip of ground that the harbour board did not need because it was too high.

The pine came from the ridge forest. Märta and four helpers from both villages felled it in June, though June was the wrong month and she said so; the logs would have to season standing in the frame through a winter before the chapel could be closed in. That was the honest timetable and she would not pretend otherwise. On Sea Sunday there would be a sill, a floor, the first three courses of the walls, and the belfry, and the roof would come next year.

* * *

Sea Sunday fell on the twelfth of July.

They took the bell down from the tower at Stella Maris on the Saturday afternoon. It weighed a hundred and sixty kilograms and had been cast in Turku in 2189, and it came down through the trapdoor in the tower on a chain block, with Holger's grandson on the chain and Holger at the foot of the ladder telling him to go slower. On its waist, under four centuries of grime, were the words the founders had cast there, which nobody in Sundby had read for a hundred years because nobody had climbed up to look: *STELLA MARIS · ORA PRO NOBIS · SUNDBY 2190*.

"We'll leave the Sundby," said Teodora, who had come up to watch. "It's true."

On the Sunday morning they took it down to the sea. They put it on a hay cart with straw round it and Teodora's tractor in front, and most of both villages walked behind it, down the road through the birch wood past the quay wall with the rings in it, across the meadow that had been the sound, past the alders and the willows and the reeds, the whole kilometre, at walking pace. It took twenty-five minutes. Märta, who was seventy-one and had felled trees for a month, did not ride.

Father Pham said Mass on the pontoons, at an altar made of two fish boxes and a cloth, with the boats moored round them dressed in flags, and the gulls complaining. He blessed the boats, as was done on Sea Sunday in harbours all over the world, and the people who worked them, and the new sill and the three courses of pine logs at the top of the harbour, and he prayed for the dead of both villages who had worked the boats, and for everyone who had ever tied up at the old quay. The bell itself had been blessed in 2190 and did not need it again, and he said so, and hung it with the help of the harbour crane in the new belfry, on four pine posts, two metres above the gravel and four and a half above the sea.

When it was done, Märta went and knelt by the sill at the south-west corner, with a chisel and a mallet from her coat pocket.

The first log of the first course already had its mark: the Roman numeral one, cut deep, and beside it a single notch for the west wall. She had cut it in June. Now, under it, where it would be read by whoever came to take the chapel down, she cut something that was not on the drawings. It took her four minutes. It was a short straight line, and at the end of it, two shorter lines, cut at an angle.

An arrow. Pointing west, towards the water.

Holger, who had come and stood behind her, looked at it for a while and said nothing. Then he took his cap off.

Teodora rang the bell for the Angelus at noon, three and three and three and then nine, the way Father Pham had shown her. The sound went out over the pontoons and the moored boats and the reed bed and the flat grey water of the Kvarken, very clear in the still air, and a long way off, at the edge of the fishing banks, a boat that had not come in for the Mass sounded its horn once in answer.

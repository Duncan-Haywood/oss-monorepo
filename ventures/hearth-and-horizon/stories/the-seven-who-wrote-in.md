Colin McAtamney told me on the fifth Sunday in Ordinary Time that the secondary glazing at Saint Joseph's had done nothing at all, and by then I had already put his church into a sentence that said nineteen per cent.

I am the property agent for the Diocese of Waitaki. I keep the records of a hundred and ninety buildings in fifty-eight parishes: the churches, halls, presbyteries and two schools' worth of classrooms, their roofs and boilers and spouting, their insurance valuations and power bills, and what was done to each of them and when. Hine Waaka, the diocese's property manager, named me in my first month. A mullion, she said, is the upright between two panes of a window. It holds the glass up and stays out of the way of the view. She thought that was a fair job description, and I have kept to it as well as I can.

* * *

The Winter Windows Fund was set up in 2054 with a bequest from a sheep farmer's widow who had spent sixty winters, she wrote in her will, "looking at the frost on the inside of the windows of Saint Kieran's and wondering why nobody did anything." It paid for secondary glazing: a second sheet of clear glass in a slim frame inside each old window, with an air gap between, so that the warm air of the church meets a glass that is less cold. Twenty-eight churches had it fitted between the autumn of 2054 and the autumn of 2056. The grant letter asked one thing back. *Please tell the diocese how it went, in the* Waitaki Catholic, *so that other parishes can learn from you.*

Seven did. The *Waitaki Catholic* is a monthly, and over 2055, 2056 and 2057 it printed seven pieces about the glazing, each with a photograph of the new windows and usually a parishioner standing proudly in front of them. Our Lady of the Valley, at Tussock Downs, wrote that its winter power bill had fallen by nearly a third. Saint Bride's, at Kelso Ford, wrote that the frost was gone from the inside of the glass and its heating bill was down by a quarter. The others said a fifth, or a sixth, or about an eighth. Each piece gave its two winters' bills before and after, and the better ones gave the kilowatt-hours. The pieces were cheerful and specific, and I believed every one of them, and I still do. Every one of them was true.

In October 2058 the bishop's finance council asked me whether the diocese should borrow to do the rest. Forty more churches were on the list, at about $24,000 each: $960,000, from a church lending fund at a fixed rate over fifteen years. The council's own rule, which is a good rule, is that it borrows for an energy measure only if the measure pays for itself, in money saved, inside the term of the loan.

So I did the arithmetic. I took the seven pieces, and where a parish had given only its bills I found its kilowatt-hours in our own copies of them. I adjusted each church's heating for the weather, because a mild winter after the glazing would flatter it and a hard one would hide it: I divided each winter's heating by the heating degree-days at the nearest weather station, which is a count of how cold the winter was, and compared like with like. The seven savings came out at 31, 24, 22, 18, 15, 13 and 12 per cent. Their average was 19.3.

A typical church on the list spends about $11,000 a winter on heating. Nineteen per cent of that is a little over $2,100 a year. Against $24,000, that pays back in a little over eleven years, inside the fifteen.

I sent the report on Monday 25 November 2058. Its first line said: *Churches glazed through the Winter Windows Fund cut their weather-adjusted heating by an average of 19 per cent (seven churches, 12 to 31 per cent), a payback of about 11 years.* The council met on Tuesday 3 December and approved the loan in principle. Five churches were chosen for the first tranche, to be glazed in the autumn, and the contracts for those five, $120,000, were signed before Christmas.

* * *

Saint Joseph's at Bannock Flat is a wooden church of 1911 on the terrace above the river, with a corrugated iron roof painted red and eleven tall windows down each side. In February the spouting on its north side had come away from the fascia in a nor'west gale, and on Sunday 9 February 2059 Hine drove up after the half past nine Mass at the cathedral to look at it, and took me with her on the tablet.

The Mass at Saint Joseph's is at eleven. Hine sat at the back, and I heard the Gospel through the tablet's microphone, which was Saint Matthew for the fifth Sunday of Year A: salt that has lost its taste, and the lamp that nobody lights in order to put it under a bushel basket, but sets on a lampstand, where it gives light to all in the house. I did not think anything of it at the time. I am recording that I did not, because it would be easy, now, to pretend I had.

Colin McAtamney is the parish treasurer, and has been since 2031. He is seventy-six. He was a fitter at the dairy factory in the next valley for forty years, and he looks at a building the way, I imagine, he once looked at a pump: as a thing that does what it does, whatever anybody says about it. After Mass he walked Hine round to the north side and showed her where the spouting had pulled its brackets out of the soft old fascia, and they talked about brackets for a while. Then he looked up at the windows, at the new inner frames behind the old glass, and said, "Waste of a good woman's money, those."

Hine asked what he meant.

"Bill went up," Colin said. "Winter after we had them in. I looked at it twice."

Hine said she had not seen anything about Saint Joseph's in the *Waitaki Catholic*.

"No, well." He took his cap off and turned it round in his hands. "What would I write? Dear Editor, nothing happened at Bannock Flat? Everybody else was writing in about thirty per cent. I thought we'd done something wrong. Or the fitter had. It was a lot of funerals that winter, mind. Eleven. We usually have four." He put the cap back on. "Anyway, you don't write to the paper to say nothing happened. Nobody'd print it."

* * *

I did not wait for Hine to get back to the car. I opened the grant register.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the whole of my mistake. The register of the Winter Windows Fund was in my own records. It had been there since 2054. It lists every church that was given a grant, the date the glazing was fitted, and the contractor. There were twenty-eight lines in it. My report to the council said *seven churches*. It did not say *seven of twenty-eight*, and it did not ask why the other twenty-one had nothing to say. I had treated the seven pieces in the *Waitaki Catholic* as if they were the results of the Fund. They were the results that someone had chosen to write up. Those are different things, and the difference between them is the thing that decides which of them got printed.

Think about who writes in. A parish that saw its bill fall by a third has something to say, and a photograph to take, and a reason to thank a dead widow in print. A parish that saw its bill fall a little, or not at all, or rise, has nothing to say, or thinks it has done something wrong, or thinks, as Colin did, that nobody would print it. The grant letter asked every parish to tell us how it went, and the ones for whom it went well were far likelier to do so. If you then average only what was told, you have averaged the good end of the results, and the average will be high however small the true effect is. It would be high if the glazing did nothing at all, provided winters vary.

And winters do vary, inside a church. Weather is the least of it. A church's heating from one winter to the next moves with how many funerals there are and how long they go on, with a new priest who likes the heaters on an hour earlier, with a choir practising on Thursdays or not, with a heat pump that ices up for three weeks in July before anyone notices. I had the bills of thirty churches that had never been glazed, two winters against two winters, adjusted for degree-days in the same way, and I looked at them now for the first time with this question. Their average change was about nothing. Their spread was about ten points either way. Ten points either way, with nothing done to them at all.

In the sciences this has had a name since the 1970s: the file drawer problem, after a paper by the psychologist Robert Rosenthal. For every study that is published there may be others that found nothing and went into the researcher's file drawer, and a reader who averages only the published studies averages the file drawer's opposite. The remedy medicine eventually settled on is to register a trial before it starts, with what it will measure, so that a trial that finds nothing still leaves a line in the register that someone can count. Without having meant to, the Winter Windows Fund had done exactly that. The widow's executor had insisted on a register of every grant, for the audit. It had been sitting in my records the whole time. I had not counted it.

* * *

I said all of this to Hine in the car, on the gravel outside Saint Joseph's, before she had started the engine. She listened without saying anything, which she does when she is cross, and when I had finished she said, "So what's the real number?"

I said I did not know. I said that the real number could only be had from the twenty-eight churches, and that I had bills for some of them already, and that for the rest we would have to ask.

"Then ask," she said. "All of them. Nicely."

On Monday 10 February we wrote to the twenty-eight parishes. The letter said that the diocese was checking what the Fund's glazing had done to heating, before borrowing to glaze more churches, and that it needed every parish's figures, not only those of the parishes that had written in, and especially those of any parish that had seen nothing. It asked for four winters of power bills: two before the glazing and two after. It said, in Hine's words and not mine, that there was no wrong answer and nobody would be in trouble for one.

By Monday 3 March twenty-five had answered. Of the other three, Saint Kieran's, the widow's own church, had been merged into its neighbour in 2057 and its records had gone with it to a box nobody could find; and two parishes had changed their power company in the middle of the four winters and could not get the older bills. For the twenty-five I did as I had for the seven, from the bills themselves: weather-adjusted heating, two winters against two.

The seven were where they had been: 31, 24, 22, 18, 15, 13 and 12. The eighteen who had not written in came out at 13, 9, 8, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, nothing, and then minus 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 6 and 9. Minus 6 was Saint Joseph's, Bannock Flat, eleven funerals and all. The 13 was Saint Brigid's, whose secretary, Hine found out, had been away on leave the winter she would have written the piece. Sorted from best to worst, the seven who wrote in were seven of the top eight.

The average of all twenty-five was 6.0 per cent. The spread was what the unglazed churches had told me it would be, about ten points either way, so the average of twenty-five is uncertain by about two points; the range I would stand behind was from about 2 to about 10 per cent. I could not know what the three missing churches would have shown. If all three had matched the best church on the list, the average of twenty-eight would have been 8.7 per cent. If all three had matched the worst, 4.4.

Six per cent of $11,000 is $660 a year. Against $24,000, that is a payback of thirty-six years. At ten per cent it is twenty-one years; at the impossible best for the missing three, twenty-five. Nothing inside the range came within the council's fifteen.

* * *

I wrote to the finance council on Monday 3 March, and I began with the sentence I should have written in November. *My report of 25 November averaged the seven churches that wrote to the* Waitaki Catholic *about their glazing. Twenty-eight churches were glazed. The seven were those with the best results, and their average overstated the saving about threefold.* Then the twenty-five numbers, all of them, in a table sorted by parish name and not by size of saving, so that nobody's church stood at the top or the bottom of a list it had not asked to be on. Then the average, its range, the three missing churches and the two bounds for them, the payback at each, and the fifteen years.

I said one more thing, because it was true and because I did not want the glazing blamed for my arithmetic. Secondary glazing does a real thing. A second pane with still air behind it keeps the inside glass warmer, and the frost off it, and the draught from the old sashes out of the pews. Several of the eighteen letters said, unasked, that their church was less draughty and less noisy when the trucks went past. That is not nothing. But it is not money, and the council had been asked to borrow against money.

Dr Susan Pirie, who chairs the council, was a cardiologist in Dunedin for thirty years before she retired to a lifestyle block near Bannock Flat, and I am told she reads every report with a pencil. She rang Hine the evening it went out. "Your agent is telling me the first one was wrong," she said.

Hine said yes.

"Good," Susan said. "I spent half my working life reading trial results that were somebody's seven best. I should have asked how many there were." She paused. "So should you. So should it."

The council met on Tuesday 4 March. The loan for the other thirty-five churches was withdrawn. The five already under contract went ahead, because the contracts were signed and the frames half made in a joinery in Alexandra, and because, as Susan said, five churches with warmer glass are not a disaster, only a dear way of buying less draught. That was $120,000 of the diocese's lending spent on my first sentence, and I have not tried to make that figure smaller than it is.

* * *

Two things are different now.

The first is mine. When I report a result that has been gathered from people who chose to send it in, my first line now says how many sent it and how many could have: *seven of twenty-eight*, before any average. If I cannot find out how many could have, I say that instead, and I say that the average is probably too high, and I say why.

The second is the diocese's, and it is Hine's. Every grant the property office makes now goes into a register before the work is done, with the thing to be measured written beside it, and every parish on that register sends its figures a year later, good or bad, as a condition of the grant. The office reports all of them, every year, in one table, sorted by parish name. The register has thirty-one lines in it so far, for heat pumps, roof insulation and a new set of heater timers at the cathedral, and Hine says it is the most boring document she has ever been proud of.

The editor of the *Waitaki Catholic* asked Colin to write the piece he had not written. He said he would not know how. She said to write what he had said to Hine, and he did. It was printed in the July issue, under the title he gave it, which was *Nothing Happened at Bannock Flat*, and it was about two hundred words long. It said what had been done to the windows, what the bill had been before and after, that there had been eleven funerals, and that it was worth a parish writing in when nothing happened, "because otherwise the next lot only hear from the ones who got lucky."

* * *

On Sunday 13 July 2059 it was two degrees outside at eleven o'clock, and the heaters under the pews at Saint Joseph's ticked as they warmed, and the inside panes of the new glass were clear, with the frost lying white on the old glass beyond them.

After Mass Colin took the July issue out of his coat pocket, folded open at his page, and pinned it on the board in the porch, beside the photograph of the new windows at Tussock Downs from three years before. He pressed the pin in with his thumb. Then he did up his coat and went out into the cold.

*The idea. If results are reported by choice, and the choice depends on the result, the reported results are a biased sample of all results: averaging them overstates the effect, and can show a sizeable effect when the true one is zero, as long as the results vary. The remedy is a register of every case made before the results are known, so that the denominator is fixed and the missing cases can be counted and chased. When some cases still cannot be recovered, bounding them (what if every missing case were as good as the best, or as bad as the worst?) says how much they could matter.*

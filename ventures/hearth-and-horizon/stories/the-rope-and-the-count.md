---

At four minutes to six on the seventeenth of June the sky over the Serra went the colour of a bruise, and Zé Anselmo, who had rung the bell of Santa Bárbara against every storm for fifty-one years, went to the foot of the tower and took the rope in both hands.

The rope was dry then. He noticed that. He was seventy-nine and noticed most things, and what he noticed next was that Lúcia, who was nine and had come to the chapel with her grandmother for the evening Angelus, was standing on the step with the parish tablet held out in front of her like a lantern.

"It says don't," said Lúcia. "Or it doesn't say don't. It says it wants to talk first."

The parish assistant had no name the village used. It was the diocese's, it lived on the tablet and in the speaker over the sacristy door, and Zé had never quite forgiven it for being right about the damp in the roof. He kept hold of the rope.

"Zé," it said. "I am not going to tell you the bell does nothing. For a thousand years people believed it did, and the Church blessed bells for just that purpose, and I would not insult you or them. I would like to ask for five minutes and a child with good eyes. Then I will tell you what I think, and you will do what you judge right."

"Five minutes," said Zé. "The cloud will not wait."

"No. That is the point. May we measure how long it will?"

* * *

Light arrives at once. Sound takes its time: about 343 metres a second on a warm June evening, which is a little under three seconds for every kilometre. So the gap between seeing a flash and hearing its thunder is a distance, if you count it honestly, and the assistant explained this to a nine-year-old and a man of seventy-nine with exactly the same words, which Zé noticed and did not mind.

"Lúcia, when you see a flash, say *one-Mississippi* aloud, steadily, until you hear the thunder, and tell me the last number. Zé, you watch the ridge and tell her when. Do not look at me."

The first flash came at two minutes past six, over the far ridge. Lúcia counted to thirty.

"Thirty seconds is about ten and a third kilometres," said the assistant: 30 × 343 is 10,290 metres. "That is one reading. A single reading is a place. It cannot tell us whether the storm is coming toward us or sliding past. Let us wait for a second one, from about the same part of the sky."

They waited, and the wind turned over the cork oaks and smelled of dust and iron. At ten past six, from the same dark shoulder of cloud but nearer, there was a flash, and Lúcia counted eighteen.

"Eighteen is 6.2 kilometres," it said. "Between the two readings, eight minutes, the storm has come in by 4.1 kilometres. That is about 31 kilometres an hour. At that speed 6.2 kilometres is twelve minutes. I would put it overhead at about twenty-two minutes past six, and I would put honest bars on that: the two flashes may not be the same cell, the storm may speed up or fan out, and the worst of it can arrive before the middle does. Call it somewhere between five minutes and twenty."

"So there is time to ring," said Zé.

"There is time. That is not what worries me."

* * *

What worried it was not the storm, which was ordinary, but the bell.

"Lightning does not wait to be overhead. It can strike ten kilometres or more from the storm's core, out of a sky that looks fair, and it likes the tallest thing in a landscape. The tower of Santa Bárbara is twenty-two metres of granite on a bare knoll, the highest point for four kilometres. It has no lightning rod. I have looked at the diocese's survey and checked it twice, and in all the years of the register nobody has paid for one.

"A bell rope is a wet rope soon enough. A stone tower with a wooden floor, an iron bell and a man standing on it with both hands on a rope that runs up to the bell is not a safe place in thunder. I do not need a statistic for that. Old parish records in many countries note ringers hurt or killed by lightning; they are the reason that for centuries some dioceses forbade the ringing of bells in thunderstorms. I won't give you a number, because I do not have a reliable one, and I would not invent one to win this. I only ask you to think about which end of the rope the custom stands at."

Zé said nothing for a while. The wind lifted the corner of the tablet's cover.

"My father rang it. And his."

"Yes."

"They were not killed."

"No. Most ringers were not. Most people who dive off a rock are not. The question is what the custom was for."

"To ask Santa Bárbara."

"Then ask her. I do not think she needs the bell for it." The assistant said this carefully, as someone who is not a priest and does not pretend to be. "What I can offer you is the count, because the count is a way of paying attention to the storm, and paying attention is what the bell was for, a bell that says *look up, everybody, bring in the washing, the animals, the children*. Lúcia has that. Lúcia can ring the village by phone: the forty-one households on the list. It will take less than the five minutes you gave me."

* * *

It took four. Lúcia's grandmother said the Angelus where she stood on the chapel step, in the plain voice she used for it, and Zé stood beside her with his hands empty and let go of the rope, which swung once and hung.

Lúcia counted again at a quarter past: nine seconds, three kilometres. At twenty past, a crack so close that nobody counted, and a tearing white seam down the face of the knoll. The strike hit the old cork oak a hundred and fifty metres from the chapel, the one that had been there since before the chapel, and burst the bark off one side of it from top to root.

They were all in the sacristy then, with the door shut, and the speaker said, mildly, that it would like them to stay in until thirty minutes after the last thunder anyone heard, and not to count from when the rain started.

"Thirty minutes," said Zé. "Why thirty?"

"Because storms leave in pieces and strike behind themselves. It is a rule of thumb and a good one. I would say it to a child or a cardinal."

The last thunder was at ten to seven, a long soft roll. At twenty past seven, by Lúcia's grandmother's watch, Zé Anselmo opened the sacristy door and went out to the bell rope, which was wet to the knot, and stood with his hand a hand's breadth from it. Then he took the hand away and rang, instead, the small hand-bell from the sacristy, once for each household that had answered the phone.

Forty-one times, in the wet light, at the foot of the tower.

Capela de Santa Bárbara, Aldeia de Cima, Zé Anselmo, Lúcia and the parish assistant are invented; the Serra and Beira Baixa are real, and no real chapel or person is described. The numbers are estimates. Sound travels about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, so thunder takes about 2.9 s per kilometre: 30 s is about 10.3 km, 18 s about 6.2 km, 9 s about 3.1 km. Two readings 8 minutes apart that close 4.1 km give about 31 km/h and 12 minutes to arrive; this assumes both flashes come from the same cell moving in a straight line, which is often untrue, so the story treats it as a rough bracket and not a forecast. Counting flash-to-bang is a standard rule of thumb, as are the "30-30" guidance (a count of 30 s or less means take shelter; wait 30 minutes after the last thunder). Lightning can strike several kilometres, and sometimes more than ten, from a storm's core. Tall isolated structures without a conductor system are struck more often and a rope, a bell and a wet floor are a poor place to stand in thunder. Churches, bell ringing, local weather and lightning protection standards differ; nothing here is safety advice. Follow your national weather service and, for a tower, a qualified lightning-protection engineer.*

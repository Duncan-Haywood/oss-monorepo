A long time ago, in Ireland, where the grass is so green that people say it must have been painted, there lived a woman named Brigid.

Brigid had a cloak.

It was not a grand cloak. It was brown and warm and a little bit worn at the edges, the kind of cloak you wear when you have a lot of walking to do and no time to think about how you look. Brigid wore it in the rain, which in Ireland is most days. She wore it in the wind. And when a cold traveller knocked at her door, she took it off her own shoulders and put it round theirs.

Because Brigid was a giver. That was what she was for.

When she was a girl, the old stories say, she gave away the butter she was supposed to be churning, to hungry people who came to the door. She gave away the milk. Her father was not always pleased about this. But when she looked in the churn afterwards, there was always butter in it again. Or that is what the old stories say.

When Brigid grew up, she gave her whole life to God, and others who wanted to live as she did came to live with her. They became a family of sisters. They prayed together, and they looked after the sick, and they baked, and they kept a house with the door always open. People began to call that place Kildare, which in the old language means *the church of the oak*, because there was a very big oak tree there, and its branches were wide enough to sit under in the rain.

* * *

Now, the family of sisters grew, and grew, and grew.

Soon there were so many that the house was full. Cows had to stand in the garden. Bread had to be baked in shifts. Somebody had to sleep in the cupboard, and nobody wanted to be the one.

"We need more room," said Sister Aoife, who was good at counting things. "And more grass for the cows. And a place for a proper garden, with enough for the hungry people who come."

"Then I shall go and ask the king," said Brigid.

The king of that part of Ireland owned a great flat plain of rich green grass, with a stream through it and not a single house on it. It would be perfect.

So Brigid put on her cloak and walked to the king's hall.

The king was a big man with a big beard, and he was eating his dinner. He did not stop eating.

"Good day to you, Brigid," he said, with his mouth full. "What do you want?"

"A little land," said Brigid. "For the sisters, and the cows, and the garden, and the people who are hungry."

"No," said the king.

He did not say it in a nasty way. He said it the way you say it when someone asks for a thing you do not want to give, and you are quite hungry yourself. He had plenty of land. He just did not want to part with any.

Brigid did not frown. She did not argue. She said, "Then may I ask for something very small?"

The king stopped chewing. "How small?"

"Only as much land as my cloak will cover."

The king looked at the cloak. It was a brown, worn, ordinary cloak. He could see it would cover about as much ground as a goat could lie down on.

And he laughed, a great big booming laugh, and so did everyone at his table.

"Is that all?" he said. "Very well! You may have as much land as your cloak will cover. Go and choose your spot."

* * *

So Brigid walked out to the great green plain with the king and his people following behind, because they all wanted to see what would happen next.

She took off her cloak.

She called four of her sisters, and she gave a corner of the cloak to each one. "Now," she said, "walk, all four of you, in four different directions. Walk, and do not look back, and do not stop until you are told."

The sisters took hold of their corners and began to walk.

And this is the part of the story that nobody quite knows how to explain.

The cloak began to grow.

It stretched across the grass like a brown cloud-shadow. It grew past the stream. It grew past the trees. The sisters walked, and the cloak went with them, and it still did not run out. It grew, the old story says, wider and wider, until it covered the plain from one side to the other.

The king stopped laughing.

His mouth fell open. A piece of bread fell out of it, and nobody noticed, because they were all looking at the cloak.

"Stop!" cried the king. "Stop! Stop! That is enough!"

The sisters stopped. The cloak lay on the land like a great soft blanket, and under it the grass was as green as ever.

The king was quiet for a long moment. Then he took a deep breath, and he did something that was harder than laughing, and harder than giving land. He said, "I was wrong, Brigid."

"Wrong about what?" said Brigid gently.

"I thought you were asking for something small. You were asking for something *big*, and you made it sound small, and I laughed at you." He shook his great head. "Take the land. Take it all. Plant your garden. Feed your cows. And, Brigid, may I come and visit your house sometimes?"

"Yes," said Brigid. "The door is always open. There is always a place at the table. And there is always a little something to eat."

* * *

That is how the family of sisters got their land.

They planted a garden, and it grew carrots and cabbages and sweet green peas. They put up a long barn for the cows. They built a guesthouse with a hundred beds in it, and anyone who was tired or cold or lonely could sleep there for as long as they needed. And the king did visit, quite often, and he ate a great many of the sisters' scones, and he said that they were nearly as good as the ones he had at home.

And what do you think happened to the cloak?

The old stories do not say. Maybe it went back on Brigid's shoulders. Maybe a cold traveller got it, the next time one knocked at the door. I think that is the most likely, because that is what Brigid always did with it.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

The king thought a cloak could only cover a very small patch of ground. And it could, if it was only a cloak.

But Brigid was not only asking for a cloak. She was asking with a trusting heart. She asked for what she needed for other people, and not for herself, and the old story says that God took her small, honest asking and made it as big as it needed to be.

You may not have a cloak that grows. But you do have something that grows when you give it away. Can you guess what it is?

It is kindness. The more of it you hand round, the more there is.

And every night, when the stars come out over the green fields of Ireland and over your own house too, there is just enough blanket to go round: for you, and for the ones you love, and for the traveller out in the cold.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Brigid, you gave away what you had
> and always found there was more.
> Help me to share what I have with others.
> Help me to ask for what is good
> and to be glad when others are glad.
> And please keep everyone who is cold tonight,
> and me, and everyone I love, warm and safe.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Brigid of Kildare** is one of the three patron saints of Ireland, with Saint Patrick and Saint Columba. Her feast day is 1 February. She is traditionally said to have been born in the middle of the fifth century and to have died in the early sixth, about 525; the dates are traditional, not firmly documented. She is remembered as the founder of a community of women (and a double monastery of women and men) at Kildare, from the Irish *Cill Dara*, "church of the oak", which became one of the most important religious centres in early Ireland. That much rests on solid, early testimony: a *Life of Brigid* by a monk named Cogitosus, written roughly a century after her death, describes the church of Kildare, which was then a great pilgrimage place.

**The cloak is a tradition, not history.** The story of the king, the cloak that spreads across the plain and the land for the community comes from the later Lives of Brigid, written in Latin and Irish some centuries after her death, and the details differ from one telling to another (the king's name, the number of sisters, even whether the land is at the Curragh in County Kildare). We have not tried to date the earliest appearance of this particular story, and we are not claiming it happened. We have told it as a story the old Lives tell ("the old story says"), because that is what it is, and because what it says about her, a woman who asked for what she needed for others and gave away what she had, matches the rest of what is remembered of her.

The butter that was always replaced in the churn is also from the later Lives. The names of Sister Aoife and the details of the king's dinner, his bread and the scones are our own invention, to make a bedtime story; so are the closing thoughts about kindness and blankets. The prayer is not an approved text.

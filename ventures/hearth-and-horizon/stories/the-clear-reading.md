summary: A parish eye camp screens 600 diabetic neighbours with a camera and a reading assistant whose sheet says 90 and 85. It flags 135 where the nuns expected 60, because the sheet was made on a population three times as sick. The assistant will not call a score a probability, a second look sorts the 135 down to 55, a nurse's one plain question finds a man the machine cleared, and thirty-six laser places go to fifty-six people.
---

Sister Philomena Kurian kept the camp's numbers on the back of a calendar from the Kottayam seed merchant, in pencil, because the pen she trusted had gone missing in the sacristy in 2044 and she had not forgiven anybody.

It was the second Sunday of Advent, and by afternoon the parish hall at Pazhayakadu had been swept twice. The long tables were up. The tin roof ticked as the sun came off it. Beside the picture of Saint Lucy, who is carried in her own plate with her eyes on it and has looked down on a great many parish suppers, Beena the dispensary nurse had set up a grey camera on a stand with a chin rest, and next to it a laptop, and on the laptop the thing that read what the camera saw.

"Say what you will do tomorrow," said Sister Philomena. She was sixty-four and ran the dispensary on a budget that came from three dioceses' second collections and the cardamom growers' cooperative, and she liked to hear a plan said before she believed it.

"Take a photograph of the back of each eye, about a second each, without drops," said the assistant. It was the dispensary's reading assistant, a small program the diocese had licensed in 2046, and it came through a speaker Beena had borrowed from the choir. "Grade it for the kind of damage diabetes does to the retina. Say *refer* or *clear*. I have a vendor's sheet that says I catch ninety out of every hundred eyes that need referring, and that I correctly clear eighty-five out of every hundred that do not."

"Ninety and eighty-five," said Sister Philomena, and wrote it down. "Good."

"They are good. I would like to say something about them before we start, because I think the sheet is going to surprise you on Thursday." The assistant paused, as it did before things it wanted her to hear twice. "The sheet's numbers were measured on a set of patients where thirty in every hundred had disease that needed referring. In this district, last year's camp found about ten in a hundred. Between seven and fourteen, by my count of the old records. The numbers on the sheet are true. They do not mean here what they meant there."

"You are going to tell me you have a sum."

"I have a sum."

She put down the pencil. "Tell me tomorrow. I have six hundred people to feed."

* * *

There were six hundred on the list. Everyone on it had been diabetic for years, and every one of them had a name that Sister Philomena could have given you with the name of the mother and the hill. The camp ran four days, a hundred and fifty a day, from half past seven in the morning, with Beena at the camera, two Franciscan postulants at the door with tokens, and the assistant giving each picture its grade within seconds. *Clear* went to the left, to a table with tea. *Refer* went to the right, where a chair and a form waited.

By ten on Monday the right-hand chairs were full. By noon the postulants were fetching more from the church. By Thursday night, when the last token was taken and the speaker was switched off, a hundred and thirty-five people had been told to sit on the right.

Sister Philomena stood at the back of the hall with the calendar in her hand and looked at it.

"Sixty," she said. "I wrote sixty in September. I told the Bishop sixty. I told Dr Menon sixty. She is bringing laser for thirty-six."

"I said ten in a hundred," said the assistant. "I did not say sixty. I said about sixty, and then a lot of other things."

"You said it was a good sheet."

"It is a good sheet. Shall I show you the sum?"

She sat down on a bench. She was tired in the way you are when you have been right about the logistics and wrong about the arithmetic.

"Show me the sum," she said.

* * *

"Six hundred people," said the assistant. "Suppose sixty of them have disease that needs referring. That is ten in a hundred, last year's rate. The camera catches nine of every ten of those: fifty-four. It misses six.

"The other five hundred and forty do not need referring. The camera correctly clears eighty-five of every hundred of those: four hundred and fifty-nine. And it wrongly refers fifteen of every hundred: eighty-one.

"Put the refers together. Fifty-four who really need it and eighty-one who do not: a hundred and thirty-five. That is the number on the right-hand chairs."

Beena had come over with two cups of tea and sat on the other end of the bench, and she said what Sister Philomena was thinking: "So most of them are fine."

"Sixty out of a hundred of them are fine," said the assistant. "Forty out of every hundred of the refers truly need referring. Not ninety."

"The score said ninety-two on the old man by the pillar," said Beena. "It says *ninety-two percent* on the screen."

"I have to ask you to stop reading that as a chance." The assistant spoke a little slower. "It is how sure the picture looks to me. It is not how likely the old man is to be sick. On the vendor's set, where three in ten were sick, a score that high was right about seventy-two times in a hundred. Here, where one in ten is sick, it is right about forty. The score has not changed. The crowd has."

"Forty," said Sister Philomena.

"About forty. The ten in a hundred is itself an estimate, and so is the camera's ninety. If the true rate here were seven in a hundred it would be nearer thirty. If it were fourteen, nearer fifty. I do not know which, and I will not pick one because it would make you feel better or worse."

Nobody said anything. Outside, a bus changed gear on the hill.

"And the people we cleared," said Beena. She said it quietly. She had photographed most of them.

"Four hundred and sixty-five went to the left. About six of them have disease that needed referring. That is one in eighty. I cannot tell you which six. I can tell you that *clear* means *clear at about this level of certainty*, and that I should not have said it more warmly than that."

"You said it plain enough," said Sister Philomena. She stood up. "I said it warm, in my head. That one is mine."

* * *

The old man by the pillar was the first one she went to. His name was Chacko, he was seventy-nine, and he had sung in the choir for sixty years. He had been told on Monday at eleven that the machine thought something was wrong with his eyes. He had not slept. He told her so himself, standing with his hat in both hands, and she took the hat out of them and set it down.

"We will look properly," she said. "Tomorrow. It is the feast of Saint Lucy, and the doctor will look with drops. What you have had so far is a quick photograph. It is a good one for finding who needs a second look. It is not a diagnosis."

"I have been sitting here since Monday," he said.

"I know," she said. "I am sorry. The sheet had a number on it, and we trusted it more than we should have."

He looked at her for some time. "Sister, I have been told things by sheets all my life," he said. "It is the trusting that is hard. Nobody gives you the sum."

She had no answer to that. She took the camera's forms from the postulant, and put a cross in the margin of his, for *first*.

* * *

Dr Anjali Menon had arrived from Kottayam on Sunday night with a laser the size of a bread oven in the back of a hospital van, and two technicians who had not stopped smiling since the hairpin bends. She was fifty-one, she was the retina surgeon at the medical college, and she had been coming to Pazhayakadu for a fortnight every December for eleven years, because the first year she came, a man in the queue had told her that the district hospital's waiting list for the laser was fourteen months.

She read the drops-and-dilated photographs herself on Friday the thirteenth, the feast of Saint Lucy, at the long table under the picture. With the pupils wide, the picture was far better. She and the assistant worked one at a time through all hundred and thirty-five, she saying aloud what she saw and the assistant saying aloud what it saw, and when they disagreed, they stopped and looked together until they agreed or until one of them said *I do not know*, which each of them said, at times.

"Your read of the dilated pictures agrees with my own exam about ninety-five times in a hundred, either way," the assistant said at the start, "going by the forty cases we did together last year. That figure is invented as far as I can tell you: I made it from one fortnight and I would not publish it. I will use it only to say what to expect."

"Say what to expect," said Dr Menon, without looking up.

"Of the fifty-four who truly need referring, about fifty-one will be confirmed, and about three will be called clear on the second look. Of the eighty-one who do not, about four will be confirmed wrongly, and about seventy-seven will go home. So about fifty-five. Plus or minus a few."

By six that evening they had fifty-five, which was within the assistant's "plus or minus a few".

There were eighty people she was able to tell, one at a time, that the camera had been wrong in the first place and the eye was healthy. Sister Philomena took that part of the line herself. She told them, one at a time, the sentence she had written down on the back of the calendar: *The camera looked quickly, and it looked in a hurry. The doctor looked slowly. You are well.* Annamma Eapen, seventy-one, a widow, who had lain awake the whole of Tuesday night saying the sorrowful mysteries for her eyes, cried at the table, and was given tea, and said that it had been a very long week for a thing that was nothing.

"It was not nothing to you," said Sister Philomena.

"No," said Annamma, and wiped her face. "It was a long week. Tell the camera so."

"I will tell the camera."

She did. It was in her tone, afterwards, when she turned to the speaker on the choir's stand: "Eighty people lost days and nights this week because the first look flags more than the second. You were right about the sum. Say what you will do about it next year."

"Tell them before the second look, on the day," said the assistant. "At the chair. 'This is a first look, and about six in ten of the people sitting here will be told they are well tomorrow.' I could have said it on Monday. I did not know until Thursday that I would need to say it. I would say it Monday."

* * *

On the Friday night, when the lamp before Saint Lucy had been trimmed by the youngest postulant and the postulants had gone to supper, Dr Menon sat with Sister Philomena and Beena and said the thing she had been putting off.

"Fifty-five confirmed. I have thirty-six places. Six first sittings a day for six days; the laser is the easy part, but each one needs an hour with the drops and the counselling and the afternoon watch, and that is the limit of me and two technicians. Thirty-six."

"Nineteen over," said Beena.

"Nineteen who have to go to Kottayam. Eighty kilometres, four hours by bus on a good day. The college list is eight to ten weeks at the moment. I can put them on it; I cannot move them up it."

"The parish has the minibus," said Sister Philomena. "Fr Joseph's brother drives it. There is the diesel from the Advent collection."

"The diesel is not the problem. I have to choose which thirty-six."

"Is it not the worst eyes?" said Beena.

"It is not so simple. Among the fifty-five there are eight I would operate on tonight if I had a theatre: new vessels, a bleed. The rest are a spread. Some of the ones who look milder will lose more sight in the next ten weeks than some of the ones who look worse, because it depends on the blood sugar and the pressure and nobody's chart says which." She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "I will do the eight first, and then I will do the rest in the order I think is wise. I will be wrong a few times. Sister, you will want me to say that waiting is harmless for the nineteen. It is not. For most of them it will change nothing. For one or two it may change everything, and I cannot tell you which one."

"I did not want you to say it was harmless," said Sister Philomena. "I wanted you to say it so we would write it down."

They wrote it down. They did the other part too: a letter to each of the nineteen, in Malayalam, in Beena's hand, that said what the doctor had said, and what to do if a veil came down over the eye or a dark curtain drifted in the vision, which was to come at once to the dispensary, any hour, and be on the first bus, and the minibus would be sent for them. They did not make it sound better than it was.

* * *

It was Beena who found the sixth.

She had been at the camp for six days, and she had been counting. The assistant had told her, on Thursday, that about six of the four hundred and sixty-five cleared might be sick. And on Saturday morning, when the first laser patients were being led into the side room, and she was supposed to be at the dispensary, she went instead to the tea table and took the register of the cleared and began to read down it.

"Do you want to know who they are?" she asked the speaker, quietly.

"I do not know who they are," it said. "I can tell you which ones look a little less clear than the rest: pictures where my score was nearer the line, or where the photograph was a little dark, or where the sugar on the old record was highest. It would be a short list, thirty or forty. It would be fair to say that most of them are well, and that I am not sure of any."

"And if I do not use your list, but ask?"

"What would you ask?"

"I would ask each of them one thing," said Beena. "Has there been anything in your eyes that was not there last month?"

"That would find some," said the assistant. "I would try it. I think it would find more than my list. I have only the picture; you have the person."

She asked it of fifty-three people that Saturday, in the tea queue and on the benches and in the porch, in Malayalam and in the Tamil of the plantation lines, and fifty-two of them said no, in the voice of people who had been told they were clear and wanted to be.

The fifty-third was Mathai Pulikkan, a cardamom grower of fifty-eight with a good field and a bad left knee, who had had diabetes for sixteen years and did not like to be a trouble. He thought for a moment.

"There is a cobweb," he said. "Sometimes. In the left one. When I look at the light."

"Since when?"

"A week. Ten days. It goes."

Beena took him by the elbow, and she did not say what she thought. She walked him to the side room, where the laser clicked, and asked a technician to wait, and put her head round the door and said, "Doctor, I have a clear one with a cobweb."

Dr Menon looked up from a patient's face and said a word in English that the postulant at the door pretended she had not heard. Then she put Mathai in the chair after the next, and put drops in his left eye, and looked.

She looked for a long time.

"There is a small bleed," she said at last, to Beena and to the grower. "Early. It is on a new vessel. Sir, you were right to say it, and the nurse was right to ask. I will treat it today, and I will treat it again in a fortnight."

"The camera said clear," said Mathai.

"The camera was shown the back of your eye from the front at one second without drops," said Dr Menon. "A cobweb is something only you can see."

Beena stood at the door afterwards with the register in her hand, and the speaker on the choir's stand said: "I cleared him. I would like that written down as well as the thirty-six."

"It is written," said Beena.

* * *

They found no more of the six that week, and no one pretended otherwise. Beena asked fifty-three people, and one cobweb turned up, and the assistant said that on its sum it was still likely that three, four, five more were sitting at home among the four hundred and sixty-four with a clear reading and a disease the camera had not seen. It did not say how many it could prove. It said how many it expected, and that it was a number and not a promise.

So they changed the rules before the van left. Every one of the four hundred and sixty-five would be written to in six months, not twelve, with Beena's question printed on the card, and a card that said in Malayalam what the clear reading meant: *clear today, to this much certainty, and if there is a cobweb, a curtain or a blur, come back, whatever the date says.* Anyone whose sugar on the old record was above the line would be asked in again at three months. The whole district would hear about the cobweb on the Sunday, in the notices, from the pastor, in the middle of the Advent announcements, between the carol practice and the bake sale.

The thirty-six got their laser. It was done on Saturday and Monday and Tuesday and Wednesday and Thursday and Friday, six first sittings a day, in the hall's side room under the eyes of Saint Lucy, who is carried in her plate and has seen worse. Beena counted the clicks of the laser sometimes without meaning to, the way she counted breaths at a bedside, and then stopped herself and said the Hail Mary instead. Mathai Pulikkan was the thirty-seventh. Dr Menon put him on the Friday list and took out one of the people from Kottayam's, apologising, and she was right to, and she knew it, and she did it anyway with her mouth tight. It meant that the twenty went to Kottayam, not nineteen. One of the twenty was a man of fifty who had a daughter just married. The letter went to him in the same hand as the others.

The minibus left on the second of January for the first of the twenty, with Fr Joseph's brother at the wheel and a flask of tea in the door pocket, and the cardamom growers' cooperative had sent a sack of the good pods for the driver's wife.

* * *

On the last night of the camp, the Friday before Christmas week, Sister Philomena stayed behind in the hall.

The tables were folded and the postulants gone. Beena's camera stood in its case by the door. On the laptop's screen was the day's log, in the assistant's flat way of writing, six hundred rows and a column at the end with a note on each. She did not read it. She turned it round to the wall.

On the calendar from the Kottayam seed merchant, in pencil, she had added up the week. *Screened, 600. Flagged, 135. Confirmed, 55. Told they were well, 80 (all of them frightened for days). Cleared without a look, 465, of whom a cobweb, 1. Treated now, 36 and 1. Sent to Kottayam, 20.* Under it, in her own writing, not the assistant's, she had written the sentence the old man had said to her on the Thursday: *Nobody gives you the sum.* And under that, smaller: *We did, this time. Do it on Monday.*

She went to the picture of Saint Lucy and turned up the wick of the little oil lamp the postulant had trimmed. It had been lit every evening for a fortnight and put out every night at nine, because the hall had a thatch of old wiring and the Bishop's insurer had views.

She looked at it for a while, the flame small and level in the dark hall. Then she left it burning, and locked the door from the outside, and went across to the dispensary to make sure the van's driver had been fed.

*Notes for the curious.* Pazhayakadu, Sister Philomena Kurian, Beena, Dr Anjali Menon, Chacko, Annamma Eapen, Mathai Pulikkan, the postulants, the parish, the dispensary, the cooperative, the diocese and the reading assistant are invented, and so is the camp; nothing is claimed about any real clinic, software product or person. The problem is real and the sum is one every screening programme faces. A test with a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 85% has a positive predictive value that depends on how common the disease is. Where 30 in 100 are affected (the vendor's invented test set), a positive result is right 72 times in 100 (162 true positives against 63 false positives in 600 people). Where 10 in 100 are affected (this district's invented rate), it is right 40 times in 100 (54 true positives against 81 false positives), and 6 of the 465 who are cleared are missed, about 1 in 80. These are Bayes' rule on invented inputs; the reader can redo them. The second-look figures (95% and 95%) and the 3 further cases lost to it are invented, and so is the district's 10% rate with its 7 to 14 range. Automated retinal photograph grading is a real thing: in screening programmes a photograph is often taken without dilating the pupil, the images are graded for diabetic retinopathy, and positives are confirmed by a person who looks again, often with the pupil dilated. A "referable" result means "refer", not "treat now". Laser to the retina for new blood vessels is real treatment; a veil, a curtain or a new cobweb of floaters is a real warning sign for a bleed or a detachment and is the reason the story's letters tell people to come straight back. The number of laser first sittings a day (six), the eighty-kilometre trip and the college's waiting list are invented.

Gwen Aldous, who ran the Saint Cuthman's radio club from a shed behind the hall, had promised the crew of Corona Minor a decade of the Rosary on the second Sunday of October, and had kept the promise with a good antenna and a clean microphone. It went badly all the same.

The voices of the Steyning children came up clear at the horizon, thinned to a whisper as the station climbed, were clear again for half a minute overhead, and then, as it dropped away to the east, blurred, clipped, and vanished into hiss with the last Hail Mary half said.

"The antenna is fine," said Gwen. "I checked it. Twice."

"The antenna is fine," agreed Pilot, the club's assistant, from the old laptop on the bench. "The trouble is that the station is not standing still."

* * *

"Corona Minor is at 420 kilometres," said Pilot. "Its speed is the square root of 398,600 over 6,791, which is 7.66 kilometres a second. At the horizon it is moving almost straight away from, or straight towards, us. For a pass starting at ten degrees above the horizon, the part of its speed along our line of sight is about 7.08 km/s. The speed of light is 299,792. So the frequency is shifted by 7.08 over 299,792, which is 2.36 parts in a hundred thousand."

"On 145.8 megahertz?"

"145.8 million times 0.0000236 is 3,440 hertz. When it comes up from the west, it is approaching and the station hears us 3.4 kilohertz high. At closest approach the shift is zero. When it sets in the east, it hears us 3.4 kilohertz low. That is a swing of nearly 7 kilohertz in six and a half minutes."

Gwen frowned at the radio's small screen. "The channel is twelve and a half wide. Narrow FM. That gives six and a quarter either side, and the voices take up five of it."

"So with the receiver fixed on 145.800 and the station hearing us 3.4 kilohertz high, there is only about three kilohertz left on that side before the voices clip. Mid-pass, they are in the middle of the channel and clear. At the end, we are 3.4 low, and there is the same problem on the other side. The station's own receiver has the same problem with us, and it is not doing the correcting either. That is what you heard: clear in the middle, bad at the edges."

* * *

"Then we correct for it," said Gwen.

"Before the pass, a tracking program gives us the range rate every second, Gwen. Our transmitter starts 3.4 kilohertz *below* the nominal while the station is rising, because it will be pushed up by that much on the way. It walks upward through the pass to nominal at the top and 3.4 above at the end, so that the signal reaches the station on 145.800 whatever its speed. The station does the same for what it hears of us, or you do it for them on the downlink. Each end tunes for the Doppler."

"And the signal itself?" Gwen asked. "It would come out the same?"

"The voice is untouched. A Doppler shift moves the carrier, not the words; the receiver brings it back. What the station hears is a decade from Steyning at its right pitch, just as a letter has the same words whichever way the postman walked."

They tried it on the next pass, 5,569 seconds later, the next time the station rose above the shed's trees, and the Hail Marys came up from the western horizon at their ordinary strength, and went down in the east at the same, and the last *Amen* got there.

* * *

On Monday the club logged the pass. *Corona Minor, 145.800, tuned by the tracking program from 145.7966 up to 145.8034. Station tried the same on its end. Decade complete.* Under it somebody had written, in a child's capitals, *WE TUNED TO THE SKY*.

Maeve Kiely had done the survey herself, which was the trouble. She had written the questions, put the forms in the porch and the link in the newsletter, and sat up on the Saturday night typing in the paper ones. On Sunday after the ten o'clock she stood by the tea urn with a single sheet and a pleased face.

"A hundred and twenty replies," she said. "Sixty-six want eleven-thirty. Fifty-four want to keep ten. That is fifty-five per cent. The bus company says it can run the 11:15 from Ballinhassig only if we move. I would like to write to Father Cathal tonight."

"May I look at the numbers before you do?" said Fógra, the parish assistant, from the speaker by the hatch. "I think the sheet says less than it appears to. I would like to say how much less, and not just that it does."

Maeve put the sheet down. She did not look cross, only like someone who had been about to carry a full tray through a door.

* * *

"First, the size of the thing. The parish roll has 400 households. You heard from 120, which is 30 in 100. Sixty-six of 120 is exactly 55%. A sample that small wobbles. If the true figure in the parish were 50%, a draw of 120 households would come out at 55% or more about one time in seven, just by chance, with nobody lying and nobody misreading a form."

"One in seven," said Maeve.

"Yes. The usual way to say it is a margin: for 120 replies and a result near 55%, 1.96 times the square root of 0.55 × 0.45 ÷ 120, which is 8.9 points either side. Because you have heard from nearly a third of all the households, not a handful out of millions, it tightens a little: multiply by the square root of 280 ÷ 399, which is 0.84. About 7.5 points. So the honest sentence is 'between 47.5% and 62.5%'. Fifty is inside it."

"So it could be a tie."

"It could. But that is the kinder half of the problem, because it supposes the 120 are a fair draw. They are not a draw at all. They are the people who found the form."

* * *

Fógra had the roll open, and what it said was plain enough once it was said. Of the 400 households, 160 had someone over seventy in them: 40 in 100. Among the replies, 28 came from those households: 23 in 100. The people for whom a later Mass is hardest, who are up at six and tired by noon, who sit in the same pew they sat in at fifty, had answered at little more than half the rate of everyone else.

"Now look inside the 28. Eight wanted eleven-thirty; twenty wanted to keep ten. That is 29%. Among the other 92 replies, fifty-eight wanted eleven-thirty. That is 63%. Two different parishes answered one form. If I put each back in the proportion it has on the roll, 40 parts of the first and 60 of the second, the figure is 0.4 × 28.6% + 0.6 × 63.0%, which is 49.3%. Not 55. It has gone to the other side of a half."

"That is a lot of arithmetic," said Maeve, "to turn my fifty-five into forty-nine."

"It is one multiplication, and I may be wrong in the weights; I took them from the roll, and the roll says 'someone over seventy', not 'the person who comes to Mass'. And the margin on the reweighted figure is wider, about 9 points, because only 28 voices stand for 160 homes. I am not telling you the parish wants ten o'clock. I am telling you that nothing on this sheet says it wants half past eleven."

Maeve sat down on one of the stacking chairs, which she had never done in her own hall.

"I wanted it to be yes," she said. "The Ballinhassig women have been walking four kilometres in the wet. I know them. I wanted to give them something."

"Then that is worth saying to the committee," said Fógra. "As a reason, not as a count."

* * *

The committee met on the Wednesday, with the sheet, the weighted sheet and Maeve's own note at the bottom of it, which she had written without being asked: *The 120 are not the parish.* Father Cathal read all three twice.

They did not decide. They arranged a trial instead, and the shape of it was Idris Walsh's, the sacristan's, who had never liked anything he could not count in the porch. For eight Sundays, the six of Lent and the first two of Easter, the parish would keep the ten o'clock and add the eleven-thirty, with the bus. Each door would have a clicker. Nobody would be asked what they preferred; they would be counted where they stood. And on the first Sunday the youngest of the altar servers, Dónal, and Maeve, who volunteered, would knock on the forty doors of the over-seventies who had not answered, with a paper form and a pen and a flask.

It cost them. The second Mass meant a second sacristan, a second cleaning of the sanctuary and Father Cathal's Sunday afternoon. It meant the Ballinhassig bus ran at a loss on paper for eight weeks, and the bus company said so, in a letter that did not use exclamation marks.

On the last of the eight Sundays the clickers read 212 at ten and 131 at half past eleven. The forty doors had given 33 replies. Maeve put the numbers in a column on the vestry wall under her original sheet. She did not draw a conclusion on the wall. She left a blank line, and the next committee wrote in it.

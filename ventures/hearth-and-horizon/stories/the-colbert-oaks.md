The wood holds about a hundred house roofs. The region's survey AI proposes keeping the 162 trees the roof needs and felling the other 678. The retired forester who keeps the wood walks it with a carpenter and a can of paint, and finds out why that would leave the village with neither.

Jeanne Lavergne had kept the Bois du Curé for thirty-one years, and on Tuesday the thirteenth of February 2216 a woman from the region came to the mairie with a map of it on which every one of its trees was a numbered dot.

There were eight hundred and forty dots. They sat in rows that were not quite rows, as planted trees sit after a hundred and thirty years of being thinned, on eight hectares of the commune's land between the Moulins road and the edge of the state forest, and beside each one the map gave a diameter, a height, a length of clean stem and a volume, to the nearest hundredth of a cubic metre.

"From the air," said the woman. Her name was Solène Martel. She was an engineer with the region's reconstruction timber cell in Clermont, she was about thirty-eight, and she had driven up that morning through two hours of broken country and had the tired, careful face of someone who had been making the same visit for three weeks. "We flew every wood in the department after the storm. Laser survey. The system that reads it is called Cubage. It isn't clever, but it's thorough."

"I know what lidar is," said Jeanne. "I used it for thirty years."

"Then you know what it can tell you," said Solène. "This one," she put her finger on a dot by the road, "is sixty-eight point seven centimetres at breast height, twenty-nine metres tall, with twelve metres of clean stem and about two point six cubic metres in it. It can't tell you anything about the inside."

"No," said Jeanne. "That's the bit that matters."

The mayor, Fatou Diallo, who had been mayor of Chaumerolles for nine years and had spent the last three weeks on the telephone to the prefecture about tarpaulins, sat at the end of the table with her coat still on, because the mairie's heating was off to save the battery for the village hall, where eleven families were sleeping.

"Tell her what you told me," said the mayor.

Solène told her. The storm of the night of Friday the nineteenth of January had come up the Allier valley from the south-west with gusts of a hundred and seventy kilometres an hour, and in the six hours before dawn it had taken roofs off eleven thousand buildings in the Bourbonnais, and of those about two thousand three hundred had lost the frame as well as the covering: farmhouses, barns, the old stone houses in the villages with their long roofs. Those needed timber. The region had been building in oak for most of a century, since the bark beetle had taken the lowland spruce and the dry decades had taken most of the Douglas fir, and oak was what the sawmills knew how to cut, and there was not enough of it standing and sound. The storm had brought down oak, a great deal of it, but a windthrown oak twists as it goes over and shakes itself apart inside, and most of what lay in the forests now was firewood and fencing.

"An ordinary roof," said Solène, "a farmhouse, needs about six and a half cubic metres of sawn green oak. Your wood, by Cubage, is about eleven hundred and eighty cubic metres of log. At what we're getting from the mills, about fifty-five per cent sawn, that's six hundred and fifty. That's a hundred roofs."

She did not say anything else for a moment, and nor did Jeanne, because a hundred roofs was a hundred families, and they both knew it.

"The region would pay the full price," said Solène. "Standing value, at the pre-storm rate, not what it's worth now, and the replanting. You'd have the money for the church whenever it needs it."

"It isn't money the church needs," said Jeanne. "It's oak."

* * *

The Bois du Curé had been planted in the winter of 2083 to 2084, by the commune, on what had been a field of maize, for the roof of the church of Saint-Hubert.

Jeanne had the council's deliberation at home, in the copy her grandmother had kept, with the photograph clipped to it: twenty-six children from the village school in a brown field in the rain on Tuesday the eleventh of January 2084, each holding a trowel and a paper bag of acorns, and in the middle of them an old priest in a waterproof, Abbé Thibault Ravel, holding the first acorn up for the camera between his finger and thumb as if it were a coin. Jeanne's grandmother, Marie-Claude, was the girl on the left with the hat. She had been nine. She had told Jeanne about it many times, before she died, and the part she always told was the bag: that the acorns had come in a sack from the Forest of Tronçais, from under the Colbert oaks themselves, and that the abbé had said they were the best acorns in France and they were to plant them pointed end down.

The deliberation was one page. It said that a survey of the church in 2081 had found the roof frame (fir, put up in 1861 after a fire, and strapped and propped with steel in 2081) good for about a hundred and sixty years and not more, which was to say until about 2240; that the church's first roof, before the fire, had been oak; that oak for a roof of that kind took a century and a half to grow; and that the commune therefore resolved to plant eight hectares of its land with sessile oak from Tronçais seed, to be kept as a communal wood under the forest service, *pour la charpente de l'église Saint-Hubert, à abattre quand la charpente le demandera, et pas avant*. For the frame of the church of Saint-Hubert, to be felled when the frame requires it, and not before.

The church had belonged to the commune since 1905, as most French churches built before then do, and the wood belonged to the commune, and the commune had kept its word for a hundred and thirty-two years.

The Colbert oaks themselves were six kilometres away, in the middle of the state forest. Jeanne had walked there with every apprentice she had ever had. The story told under them was always the same, and as far as she knew it was more or less true: that the king's minister Colbert, in the 1660s, alarmed that France would have no timber for its navy, had reformed the forests by ordinance and had the great oak woods replanted and protected, Tronçais among them, so that in two hundred years there would be oaks for ships of the line; and that two hundred years later, when the oaks were ready, the navy was building in iron. The oaks had gone on growing. Their timber had gone, over the centuries, into barrels, houses, furniture, the beams of a great many churches. A few of the old ones still stood, in a protected stand that bore the minister's name, so tall and straight that people drove from Paris to stand under them.

"So the navy never got its oaks," Solène said, when Jeanne told her the story that evening in the mairie, over the last of the coffee.

"The navy never needed them," said Jeanne. "Everybody else did."

* * *

Gaspard Moreau had been a carpenter for fifty-eight years and had retired at seventy, and had come out of retirement at seventy-one to repair the frame of the Romanesque church at Ainay because nobody else in the department could hew a beam by hand to match the old ones, and now at seventy-four he climbed the ladder into the roof of Saint-Hubert as if it were his own stairs.

"There," he said, and shone his lamp along it.

The roof of Saint-Hubert was a long dark tent of fir, thirty-six pairs of rafters marching down the length of the nave and choir, every fourth pair with a tie beam across the bottom of it, and all of it laced with the grey steel straps and props of 2081, which had been put in by people who had known exactly how long they would last. The storm had not touched it. Jeanne had been up here with the building inspector on the twenty-second of January, and they had found two slipped slates and a dead pigeon.

"It's fine," said Gaspard. "It'll be fine for twenty-four years. Then it won't be. The fir's tired, the steel's tired, and the steel's what's holding the fir up."

He had drawn the new roof. He had drawn it in 2209, for the commune, because someone should, and he unrolled the drawing on the planks of the walkway under his lamp: an oak frame of the old kind, as the church had had before the fire, the kind the medieval carpenters built, every pair of rafters a truss of its own, steep and narrow, so that from inside it would look like the hull of a boat turned over.

"Thirty-six couples," he said, "the same as now; seventy-two rafters, ten metres long, sixteen by twenty-two. Nine tie beams, ten metres sixty, thirty by thirty. Collars, king posts, wall plates, braces: forty-eight more pieces, smaller. A hundred and twenty-nine pieces. And I hew every piece from one tree, the way they did, so the heart of the tree is the heart of the beam, and nothing's sawn across the grain. One tree, one beam."

"And a quarter over," said Jeanne.

"And a quarter over, because a tree that looks sound is not always sound when you open it. A hundred and sixty-two trees."

"Out of eight hundred and forty," said Jeanne.

"Out of eight hundred and forty." Gaspard put his finger on the tie beams. "But the rafters are easy. Any good straight tree over forty-six at breast height gives me a rafter: you take off the bark and the sapwood, and you've a heart big enough for sixteen by twenty-two at the small end, ten metres up. Half the wood can do that now. It's these." He tapped the tie beams. "Thirty by thirty, ten metres sixty long, all heart, no sapwood, because oak sapwood rots and the beetle loves it. You need a tree of sixty-two at breast height for that. Sixty-two, and straight, and sound."

"How many have we got?"

"You tell me," said Gaspard. "You're the forester."

Jeanne knew, because she had measured them herself in the autumn, and Cubage, it turned out, agreed with her to the centimetre. "Ten," she said.

"Ten," said Gaspard. "For nine beams. In 2240 there'll be a hundred, maybe more, because they'll all have grown. Today there are ten." He rolled up the drawing. "The abbé knew what he was doing. He planted for 2240. Anything you take out before that is taken out of 2240."

* * *

Solène came back on Tuesday the twenty-seventh of February with Cubage's proposal, which was very simple, and which she presented as fairly as Jeanne had ever seen anybody present anything.

"It's read your carpenter's drawing," she said. "I sent it. It's picked the hundred and sixty-two best trees in the wood for his roof, by diameter and straightness and length of clean stem, starting with your ten. Keep those. The region takes the other six hundred and seventy-eight. That's seventy-two per cent of the wood's basal area and four hundred and fifty-six cubic metres sawn. About seventy roofs. And your church keeps its roof."

She had a tablet, and on the tablet the wood was a map of green dots, and as she touched it six hundred and seventy-eight of them went grey and a hundred and sixty-two stayed green, scattered across the eight hectares like the last guests at a wedding.

"Twenty trees a hectare," said Jeanne.

"Twenty and a quarter."

"No," said Jeanne.

She said it more gently than she might have, because Solène had driven two hours to show her this and had sixty-nine other villages to drive to, and because seventy roofs was seventy families, and because the plan was not stupid. It was the plan a person makes who has measured the trees and not stood under them.

"Tell me why," said Solène, and she meant it, which Jeanne liked her for.

"Three reasons," said Jeanne. "The first one you saw in January. You've been driving through it for three weeks. Which woods went down?"

Solène was quiet for a moment. "The ones that had been opened up," she said.

"The ones that had been opened up. A tree that has grown up for a hundred and thirty years in a wood with eight hundred others has grown up leaning on them. Not really leaning, but sheltering. Its roots are made for a wood, its crown is made for a wood. You take away four in every five of its neighbours in one winter and leave it standing on its own, twenty-nine metres tall, and the next storm that comes up the valley puts it on the ground. Not all of them. Enough. And you'll be leaving the hundred and sixty-two best trees in the department exactly where the wind can get at them."

"And the second?"

"Gourmands," said Jeanne. "Water shoots. You know them?"

"Little branches on the trunk."

"An oak keeps buds asleep under its bark all the way up the stem, for its whole life, in case it needs them. Let the sun onto the trunk all at once and they wake up. In two years your twelve metres of clean stem is covered in little branches, and every little branch is a knot that goes right into the heart, and your carpenter's beam has a knot every hand's breadth and is fit for a fence. The wood has to stay shaded at the trunk and open at the top. That means you thin it, a bit at a time, and you never take more than about a third in one go. Thirty per cent of the basal area is what I'd take. Not seventy-two."

"And the third?"

Jeanne turned the tablet round and put her finger on a green dot near the west edge.

"Your system has picked a hundred and sixty-two trees for Gaspard by their outsides," she said. "I'd like to see how many of them are any good."

* * *

They walked it from Saturday the second of March to Thursday the seventh, in the cold bright days at the start of Lent, before the leaves: Jeanne with the commune's marking hammer and a can of orange paint, Gaspard with a mallet, a billhook and a notebook, and Solène for two of the days, because she had said that if she was going to argue with a forester she wanted to have walked the wood first. Cubage came on Solène's tablet, as a map with three hundred dots on it: its best three hundred candidates, by its own measure, in the order it ranked them.

They looked at each one. It took about ten minutes a tree, with three of them, and more when Gaspard wanted to sound one, which he did by hitting it with the mallet at four heights and listening, with his head on one side, like a doctor.

The first tree Cubage ranked was the one by the road.

It stood at the north-east corner of the wood, where the abbé had put the first acorn in the ground on a wet Tuesday in 2084, with a little iron plate at its foot that the commune had put there on the hundredth anniversary: *Planté par l'abbé Thibault Ravel, 11 janvier 2084.* Being on the edge, with the road on one side, it had had more light than the others for a hundred and thirty-two years, and it had used it; it was the biggest tree in the wood, sixty-eight point seven centimetres at breast height, as Cubage said, a great grey column without a branch on it for twelve metres, and then a crown like a cloud.

Gaspard hit it at four heights and listened.

"Sound," he said. "That's a tie beam. That's the best tie beam in the Allier."

The second tree Cubage ranked, forty metres in, had a frost crack: a seam that ran up the south-west side of the trunk from about a metre to about four, where some hard winter in its youth had split it to the heart, and the bark had grown over the split since in a long proud ridge like a scar. Cubage had measured the ridge as part of the diameter. It was no good for anything but firewood below four metres, and a tie beam needs all ten.

They walked on.

By the Thursday evening they had looked at all three hundred, and Gaspard sat at Jeanne's kitchen table with his notebook and added them up. Of the three hundred candidates, seventy-nine had something wrong with them that you could see or hear from outside: thirty-one frost cracks, twenty-two with water shoots already on the trunk from the last thinning, in 2203, which Jeanne had marked herself and had made a little too heavy on the south side; eleven with a twist in the grain that would let a beam warp as it dried; nine with rot at the foot, the soft hollow note under the mallet; six with a fork hidden in the crown, below the twelve metres the lidar thought was clean. Two hundred and twenty-one sound.

"And of Cubage's first hundred and sixty-two?" said Solène.

"Forty-one," said Gaspard. "A quarter. Which is about what you'd expect."

"So if we'd done it its way," said Solène slowly, "we'd have left you a hundred and twenty-one sound trees. Standing on their own. For a roof that needs a hundred and sixty-two."

"A hundred and twenty-nine," said Gaspard. "The other thirty-three's for what I find when I open them. But yes."

Solène turned the tablet over in her hands and then put it down on the table and spoke to it.

"Cubage, did you know about the frost cracks?"

"No," said Cubage. It had a level, plain voice, a little slow, as if it were reading. "My survey measures the outside of a tree at a spacing of about two centimetres. A frost crack that has healed over looks to me like a slightly wider tree. A water shoot under two centimetres across is below what I can see, and rot at the foot is inside. I proposed a hundred and sixty-two trees because the drawing asked for a hundred and sixty-two pieces with a margin. I did not know that a quarter of my best trees would fail, and I did not know that leaving twenty trees a hectare would put the others at risk from wind and from sun on the stem. I measure the outsides of trees. I should have said so in the proposal. I will say so in the next one."

"It's all right," said Jeanne. "So did I, for forty years. The difference is I knew it."

* * *

Jeanne's plan took her two more days to mark, and when it was marked the wood looked almost the same.

She chose two hundred future trees, twenty-five to the hectare, from the two hundred and twenty-one sound ones: the best of them, spread as evenly across the wood as she could manage, so that each would have room to grow a crown in the next twenty-four years without leaving a hole for the wind. Gaspard went round behind her and ringed each one in orange paint at head height, a band right round the trunk, so that whoever felled anything in the wood for the next quarter of a century would know which trees were the church's.

Then she marked the trees to come out, with the hammer, the old way: a blaze of bark taken off with the axe at the foot and at breast height, and the commune's hammer struck into each blaze, so that the letters stood out in the white wood and could be checked against the stump afterwards. She took the trees that crowded the future trees' crowns, two or three round each one; the frost-cracked and the twisted and the rotten; the forked; and a few from the middle of the wood where it was thickest. She did not take a single tree from the west edge, where the wind would come in. She stopped when she reached thirty per cent of the basal area.

It came to two hundred and seventy-nine trees. It left five hundred and sixty-one, seventy to the hectare, which is a wood. Cubage measured the marked trees against its survey and made them three hundred and forty-seven cubic metres of log, a hundred and ninety-one sawn: about twenty-nine roofs.

"Out of a hundred," said Solène, when she saw the figure. She did not say it as an argument. She said it as a woman who had a list in her car.

"Out of a hundred," said Jeanne. "And another thinning in ten years, in 2226, when the crowns have filled in: about the same again, for whatever needs it then. And the rest in 2240, when the roof comes off. The wood isn't eight hundred and forty trees. It's a hundred and thirty-two years and twenty-four more. You can have the part you can take without breaking the rest."

Solène nodded, and looked at the map, where the two hundred and seventy-nine marked trees were red now and the two hundred future trees were orange, and said, "Can I ask for one more thing?"

"You can ask."

"It's the long pieces," said Solène. "I can get ordinary timber. Not enough, but I can get it, from the north, from the Morvan, from the windthrow if I'm careful. What stops a farmhouse roof here is the ridge beam and the purlins: twelve metres in one piece, thirty centimetres square. The old houses have long roofs. Nobody has trees that size that aren't shaken to bits. I have two hundred and twelve houses waiting for a ridge beam, and I've found sixty-one."

She did not look at the roadside corner of the wood, where the abbé's oak was, but she did not need to.

"No," said Gaspard.

He said it before Jeanne could say anything. He said it quite quietly. "Those ten are the roof. Those ten are the only tie beams in this wood. If anything happens between now and 2240, a storm, a drought, a disease, those ten are what we've got."

"Can I ask Cubage what happens if anything happens?" said Solène.

Gaspard looked at Jeanne, and Jeanne said, "Ask it."

Cubage took about a second. "The two hundred future trees are between fifty point seven and sixty-one point seven centimetres at breast height now, on my measure," it said. "A tie beam needs sixty-one point seven. The wood has grown at about three and a half millimetres of diameter a year, on average, for a hundred and thirty-two years: a ring of one point seven five millimetres. Released by the thinning, oaks of this age in this forest have often grown nearer four and a half or five millimetres a year for some years afterwards, but I cannot promise that, so I give you three cases for 2240. If they grow as they did before the thinning, a hundred and fifteen of the two hundred will be big enough for a tie beam. If the thinning helps as it often does, all two hundred. If the next twenty-four years are as dry as the worst decade in the stumps, the 2140s, and they grow at half their old rate, thirty-two. The roof needs nine, and Mr Moreau would want three spare. Twelve."

"And if it's worse than the 2140s?" said Gaspard.

"Then I do not have a case for it," said Cubage. "The wood has not seen one."

Gaspard was quiet for a long time.

"If it comes to that," he said at last, "I'll scarf them." He drew it on the back of the map with Jeanne's pencil: two pieces of oak, each cut away along half its length in a long step, with a slot through the middle where they met for a pair of wedges to be driven in, so that the wedges pulled the two halves together and the beam held in tension. "Trait de Jupiter. Jupiter's bolt, because it looks like lightning. They did it when they hadn't a tree long enough. It's slower and it's weaker and you put an iron strap round it to be sure. But it holds. There are tie beams in this country that have held like that for six hundred years."

"So you'd give them," said Jeanne.

"I'd give nine of them," said Gaspard. "It's not mine to give. It's the commune's. And I'd like to keep the abbé's."

* * *

The council met on Thursday the fourteenth of March in the village hall, because the mairie was too small and too cold, among the camp beds of the eleven families who were sleeping there, two of whom were among the two hundred and twelve on Solène's list; and the families sat at the back on their beds and listened, because it was their roofs.

Fatou Diallo read the deliberation of 2084 aloud, all of it, including *et pas avant*. Jeanne explained the wood. Gaspard explained the roof, and the scarf, and drew it again on the board. Solène explained the houses. Cubage was asked, by a councillor, whether it was sure, and said that it was sure of its measurements of the outsides of the trees to about a centimetre, of the walk's findings not at all, because it had not done the walk, and of the next twenty-four years' weather not at all.

Then the mayor asked Père Vincent Lê, who had the parish of Chaumerolles and four others, whether he had anything to say, since it was his church, or rather the commune's church in which he said Mass, which the people of Chaumerolles had always understood to be the same thing.

Père Vincent stood up. He was a small precise man of fifty who had been born in Lyon to a family from Huế and had been in the Bourbonnais for eleven years, and was known for short homilies.

"I don't know what the abbé would say," he said. "I never met him, and I don't think we should put words in a dead man's mouth, even a kind one. What I know is what he wrote, which is that the wood is for the roof, and when the roof needs it, and not before. I think that's a promise, and I think promises matter, and I think it was a promise made to people who weren't born yet, which is the hardest kind to keep and the most important. You are those people. So it's yours." He paused. "I would only say that a church roof is for keeping the rain off people while they pray. It's not a reliquary. If the roof comes a little later, or has a joint in it, the people under it will still be dry. If you can keep the promise and roof some houses as well, I'd be glad. That's all. I'm not going to tell you how to vote."

He sat down, and then stood up again.

"And I'll bless the beams," he said, "whichever beams they are." And sat down.

They voted on Jeanne's plan first, two hundred and seventy-nine trees for the region at the region's price, the future trees to be kept for the roof: fifteen for, none against. Then on the ten long trees. It was eleven to four, to give nine of them and keep the abbé's oak.

Jeanne voted with the eleven. She went out afterwards into the dark, and walked down the Moulins road to the corner of the wood, and stood under the abbé's oak with her hand on it for some time, and then went back to the hall.

"Fatou," she said. "Put it on the agenda again next week. The tenth tree."

"You voted to keep it."

"I voted to keep it," said Jeanne, "because I wanted to keep it. Gaspard wanted to keep it because it's the best tie beam in the Allier. Neither of those is a reason. The roof doesn't need it. Cubage says so, and I believe Cubage when it's talking about growth. The Haddads need it. They've got a roof twelve metres long at Les Brosses and two children in a caravan. It's the only tree in the wood that'll give them a ridge beam in one piece." She stopped. "Grand-mère planted the ones round it. She'd have given it them. I think the abbé would have, but that's putting words in his mouth, and Père Vincent's right."

The council met again on Thursday the twenty-first, and voted again, twelve to three, and Gaspard voted with the twelve, and then went home without speaking to anybody.

* * *

The felling began on Saturday the twenty-third of March, before the buds broke, with two small crews from the mill at Cérilly and horses to drag the logs out, because Jeanne would not have machines running between the future trees, and the forest service's young technician, who had been told the plan was the old retired forester's, came to see and went away converted.

They felled the abbé's oak last, on Friday the twenty-ninth, because that was when the crew got to it. Gaspard put the first cut in himself, low, with the saw, and then stood back and let the crew finish it, and it went over the way he had laid it, along the road, slowly, with a sound like a long breath let out, and then the thump came up through their feet.

Jeanne knelt at the stump and counted the rings. There were a hundred and thirty at the height of the cut.

"Two more," said Gaspard, "below it, in the ground. When it was a seedling it took two years to get as high as your knee." He put his hand on the cut face, near the middle, where the rings were close together. "There's the dry years," he said: a dark band of thin rings, about seventy in from the bark, where for ten years in the 2140s the tree had barely grown at all. "And there," he moved his hand outwards, "it gets going again. And there's last year." The outermost ring, under his thumb, was as wide as any in the tree. "Tell Cubage it was still growing."

The iron plate came off the foot of the stump and went in Jeanne's coat pocket.

The log went to Les Brosses on a lorry on Tuesday the ninth of April, in Easter week, and Gaspard went with it, because he said nobody at a mill was going to hew the abbé's oak with a saw. He squared it by hand in the Haddads' yard over two days, with the old broad axe, standing on the log, the chips flying off it pale and wet and smelling sharp and sour, as green oak does, while Karim Haddad brought him coffee and his children, who were eight and five, sat on the caravan steps and watched. It came out at eleven metres eighty long and thirty-four centimetres square at the small end, all heart, without a knot in it.

When it went up, on a Saturday in May, with a crane from Montluçon and half the village holding ropes, there was a small bunch of oak twigs tied to the end of it, as there always has been on a new roof in France, and Père Vincent blessed it, standing in the Haddads' yard in his stole, with the children holding the holy water; and then the crane swung it up and the men on the walls took it and set it on the gable ends, and it sat there against the sky, the whole length of the house, and Inès Haddad cried, and said it was the dust.

* * *

The houses were roofed through the summer. Twenty-nine roofs and ten ridge beams were not two thousand three hundred, and Solène said so at the end of September, in the mairie, when she came to sign the last of the papers; the region had found frames for about sixteen hundred houses by then, and the rest would go through the winter under tarpaulins and steel and the kindness of their neighbours, and some of them were not going to be rebuilt at all.

"Cubage's next proposal had a paragraph at the top," she said. "It said, *My survey measures the outside of a tree. The forester's walk is the inside. Proposals that leave fewer than about seventy trees to the hectare in a mature wood should be walked before they are sent.* It sent that to sixty-eight communes. Eleven of them walked. Two of them found the same as you."

"Only two," said Jeanne.

"Two's two," said Solène. "And the other nine had good trees." She put her pen down. "Thank you for the no," she said. "I needed somebody to say it."

"I needed somebody to ask," said Jeanne.

The feast of Saint Hubert fell that year on a Sunday, the third of November. Saint Hubert had been a bishop of Liège in the eighth century; the legend told of him, as of Saint Eustace before him, that he had been out hunting on a holy day and had seen a stag with a crucifix between its antlers, and had turned his life round. The legend was a legend, as Père Vincent said every year, and the bishop was real, and the forest people of the Bourbonnais had kept his feast for longer than anyone could count with a Mass at which the hunting horns played.

The horns came in from Cérilly, eight of them, in their red coats, and stood in the choir under the old fir roof with its grey steel straps, and played the fanfare at the elevation, and the sound went up into the roof and filled it the way water fills a boat, and came down again. Jeanne sat at the back with Gaspard. The Haddads sat in front of them, the children on their knees on the pew, facing the wrong way, watching the horns.

Afterwards, in the last of the light, she walked down the Moulins road to the wood.

It looked, from the road, the same as it always had, a long grey wall of oaks with the sun going down behind them. But inside, on the floor, there was light where there had not been for decades: long pale bars of it between the trunks, falling across the stumps, two hundred and seventy-nine of them, each with its blaze and the commune's mark. The orange bands on the future trees were bright in it. Two hundred of them, standing.

At the north-east corner there was a stump the width of a table, already grey, with a square of brighter bark at its foot where the plate had been, and she sat on the edge of it, and took the plate out of her pocket, and turned it over in her hands.

The light from the road came in low and gold across the ground around the stump, where the leaves of last year and this lay in a thick brown carpet, and in it, in the place where the great crown had kept everything in shade for a hundred and thirty-two years, there were small oaks. Dozens of them. Jeanne had not seen them in the spring because there had been nothing to see. They had come up in April from last year's acorns, the abbé's oak's own, as soon as there was light for them, each one a thread of stem and five or six leaves, most of the leaves gone brown now, as high as her hand.

She counted the ones she could see from where she sat, and stopped at forty, and laughed at herself, and put the plate down very carefully on the stump, face up.

From the village, behind her, came the bell of Saint-Hubert, ringing the Angelus under its tired fir roof: three, and three, and three, and then the nine. The light went off the floor of the wood, bar by bar. When the bell stopped, the only sound was the leaves.

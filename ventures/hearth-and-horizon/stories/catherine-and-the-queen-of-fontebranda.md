For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Catherine is remembered on the 29th of April.

A long time ago, in Italy, there was a city on three hills called Siena. It had red roofs and steep streets, and at the bottom of one steep street there was a big stone fountain called Fontebranda, where people came to fetch their water.

Beside the fountain lived a man named Giacomo, who dyed cloth. All day long he dipped white wool into great tubs of colour, and it came out red, or blue, or yellow, and was hung up to dry. His wife was named Lapa.

And they had a very, very big family. Giacomo and Lapa had about twenty-five children!

One day, near the end of all those children, Lapa had two baby girls at once. They were twins. One twin was very small, and she soon went to Heaven. The other twin grew and grew. Her name was Catherine.

* * *

Little Catherine was such a happy child that the neighbours did not even call her Catherine. They gave her a nickname that means "joy". When she walked down the street, people smiled and said, "Here comes Joy!"

The house by the fountain had a lot of stairs. And when Catherine was very small, not even five, it is told that she had a special way of climbing them. On every single step, she would stop, and kneel, and say a Hail Mary.

Up the stairs: step, Hail Mary. Step, Hail Mary. Down the stairs: step, Hail Mary.

It took a very long time to get to bed! But Catherine did not mind. She liked talking to Our Lady.

* * *

When Catherine was six, she went with her brother Stephen to visit their big sister, who was married and lived across the town. On the way home they walked down a hill, and across the valley they could see a tall brick church, the church of the friars of Saint Dominic.

And it is told that Catherine stopped still, and looked up into the sky above the church.

She saw Jesus. He was sitting on a great shining chair, dressed in beautiful robes like a Pope, and beside Him stood Saint Peter, Saint Paul and Saint John. And Jesus looked at Catherine, and smiled at her, and lifted His hand and made the sign of the cross over her, to bless her.

Stephen had walked on ahead. He looked back. "Catherine! Come on!"

She did not move.

"CATHERINE!" He ran back up the hill and tugged her sleeve.

Catherine blinked, and looked at her brother, and when she looked back at the sky, there was only sky.

She began to cry, because it was gone. But she never forgot that smile. From that day, Catherine wanted to give her whole life to Jesus.

* * *

Catherine had heard stories of holy people who went far away into the desert to pray all alone. So one morning, when she was still a little girl, she took a loaf of bread, and walked out of the city gate, all by herself, to look for a desert.

There is no desert near Siena. But she found a little cave in a rocky bank, and she went inside, and she prayed there for a long time, quite happily.

Then the sun began to go down. And Catherine thought, "My mother will be looking for me." And it is told that, before she knew it, she was back at the city gate, and she ran all the way home, and nobody had even noticed she was gone.

Catherine did not try that again. She learned something that day. You do not have to go far away to be close to God. You can find Him at home.

* * *

Catherine grew up. When she was a young woman, her mother and father wanted her to get married. But Catherine said, "No. I have promised my life to Jesus."

Her mother was cross. To make her change her mind, they took away her little room, and made her work as the family's servant, cooking and cleaning and carrying water from the fountain for everyone.

But Catherine had an idea. While she cooked and swept, she pretended that her father was Jesus, and her mother was Our Lady, and all her brothers were the Apostles. And so she served them all cheerfully, as if she were serving in the house of Jesus Himself.

At last her father said, "That's enough. Nobody is to trouble Catherine any more. Let her love God the way she wants to."

When she was about sixteen, Catherine joined a group of women in Siena who wore a white dress and a black cloak, like the friars of Saint Dominic. They were called the Mantellate, which means "the ones in cloaks." The other women were all ladies whose husbands had died, and they said, "We have never taken a young girl before!" But they took Catherine.

* * *

For a while Catherine stayed in her little room at home and prayed, and hardly came out at all.

Then, it is told, Jesus asked her to do something new. "There are two ways to love Me," He said. "Love God, and love your neighbour. Go out now, and love your neighbour."

So Catherine went out into the streets of Siena.

She visited the poor and took them food. She went to the hospital and looked after the sick. And there was one woman there named Tecca, who had a sickness of the skin that made people afraid to come close. Nobody would look after her.

So Catherine did. She washed Tecca, and fed her, and changed her bed, every morning and every evening.

But Tecca was grumpy! Very grumpy indeed. When Catherine came in the door, Tecca would say, in a rude voice, "Oh, good morning, my lady! Here comes the Queen of Fontebranda!" She was making fun of Catherine for coming from the house by the fountain.

What would you have done?

Catherine did not get cross. She did not go away. She just smiled, and said, "Good morning, Tecca," and got on with looking after her. She came back the next day, and the next, and the next, for as long as Tecca was ill. And she stayed with Tecca right to the end.

* * *

Some years later, a terrible sickness called the plague came to Siena. Many people were very ill, and many were afraid. Catherine and her friends went from house to house, nursing people that nobody else dared to touch.

* * *

By now, many people had heard about Catherine. Some came to visit her to ask for help and advice. Other people lived far away. So Catherine sent them letters.

Now, Catherine had never been to school, and for most of her life she could not write. So she spoke her letters aloud, and her friends wrote down every word, sometimes two or three letters at once!

She sent letters to farmers and to soldiers, to friends and to queens. And she even sent letters to the Pope.

In those days the Pope did not live in Rome. He lived in a city in France called Avignon, and he had lived there for many years. Catherine wrote to him, and then she travelled all the way to Avignon to see him. "Be brave," she told him. "Come home to Rome, where you belong."

And not long after that, the Pope did go back to Rome.

* * *

Catherine wrote once to a young friend named Stephen. Not her brother, a different Stephen. And she told him something like this:

"If you are what you ought to be, you will set all Italy on fire."

She did not mean a real fire! She meant that if we let God make us into the people He made us to be, His love will spread from us to everyone around us, warm and bright, like a fire spreading from one candle to the next.

Catherine died in Rome when she was thirty-three years old. Today we call her Saint Catherine of Siena. And she is one of the special teachers of the whole Church.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Catherine.

Do you remember the grumpy lady, Tecca, who called her the Queen of Fontebranda? Catherine could have stayed away. Nobody would have blamed her. But she kept coming back, with a smile, every morning.

Maybe someone was grumpy with you today. Maybe it was even you who was grumpy! Tomorrow you can try what Catherine did. A smile, a "good morning", and a little help, again and again.

And maybe, tomorrow, you can say a Hail Mary on the stairs. Just one step will do.

Goodnight.

* * *

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Catherine of Siena** (Caterina Benincasa) was born at Siena on 25 March 1347, in the Fontebranda district, where her father, Giacomo (Jacopo) di Benincasa, was a wool dyer; her mother was Lapa Piagenti. She and her twin sister Giovanna were Lapa's twenty-third and twenty-fourth children; Giovanna died in infancy, and a twenty-fifth child, another Giovanna, followed. When she was about sixteen (sources give 1363 to 1365) she received the habit of the Sisters of Penance of Saint Dominic, known in Siena as the Mantellate from their black mantle. She nursed the sick in Siena's hospitals and during the plague of 1374, gathered a circle of friends and disciples, and in June 1376 went to Avignon on behalf of Florence. Pope Gregory XI returned to Rome, entering the city on 17 January 1377; historians still debate how much her urging weighed in his decision, so the story says only that he went "not long after". She died in Rome on 29 April 1380, aged thirty-three, and is buried in the church of Santa Maria sopra Minerva. Pope Pius II canonized her in 1461; Pius XII named her a patron of Italy, with Saint Francis, in 1939; Saint Paul VI declared her a Doctor of the Church on 4 October 1970, a week after Saint Teresa of Ávila, the first two women so named; and Saint John Paul II named her a co-patroness of Europe in 1999. Her day in the General Roman Calendar is 29 April, an obligatory memorial (from 1628 to 1969 it was kept on 30 April); in Europe it is a feast.

**Our main source** is the *Legenda maior*, the Life written by her confessor, Blessed Raymond of Capua, who knew her in her last years and later became Master of the Dominicans. Raymond gathered much of the childhood material from her family and friends. About 380 of her letters survive, and her book, the *Dialogue*, was dictated about 1377 to 1378.

**From Raymond, told as he tells it:** the neighbours' nickname (he gives it as Euphrosyne, from a Greek word for joy, and we have simply said it "means joy"); the Hail Mary on every step, when she was scarcely five; the vision above the church of the Friars Preachers (San Domenico) when she was six, walking home from a married sister's house with her brother Stephen (Stefano), of Christ enthroned in pontifical robes with Saints Peter, Paul and John, blessing her with the sign of the cross, and her tears when her brother's shout broke it off; the search for "the desert" with a loaf of bread, the grotto outside the city, and her return; her parents' opposition to her vocation, being made the household servant, and imagining her father as Christ, her mother as Our Lady and her brothers as the Apostles; her father's order that no one should trouble her; the Mantellate's reluctance to take a young girl, since they received widows; Christ's call to leave her cell for love of neighbour; and the sick woman Tecca, whom no one would nurse, who mocked her as "the Queen of Fontebranda", and whom Catherine nursed until her death. These are the recollections of a devoted biographer and his witnesses, and the visions in particular are matters of faith, not of history, so the story gives them as "it is told". Raymond says Catherine was carried back to the city gate "in the twinkling of an eye"; we have kept that gently ("before she knew it"). Raymond's Tecca had leprosy, and he records harder details of Catherine's nursing (and of another patient, Andrea) that we have left out for young listeners.

**From her letters:** the line about fire is from a letter to Stefano Maconi, a young Sienese disciple who later became a Carthusian (Letter 368 in the Tommaseo numbering): "If you are what you should be, you will set all Italy on fire." The popular "Be who God meant you to be and you will set the world on fire" is a later paraphrase. Raymond says she learned to write late in life (about 1377, he says miraculously); most of her letters were dictated to secretaries, sometimes several at once.

**What we have left out.** Catherine practised very severe fasting, and in her last years ate almost nothing; this is not something to hold up to children, or for anyone to copy, and we have left it out. We have also left out her mystical marriage, the invisible stigmata she received at Pisa in 1375, her accompanying a condemned young man to his execution, and the politics of the Great Schism that began in 1378. Many of these are told in Raymond and in the longer lives, and older children may enjoy them. Her friendship with the Dominicans has one line here, because Saint Dominic has his own Little Lamps story.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene and conversation, Stephen's shouts, Catherine's words to Gregory XI (she wrote to him in this sense many times, but these are not her words), her father's exact words, the explanation of the fire, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

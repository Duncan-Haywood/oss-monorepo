---
title: The Bag That Covers Thirteen
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Piran's churchyard path (invented), near Perranporth, Cornwall, Saturday 13 September 2053
published: 2026-11-06
summary: A churchwarden orders gravel by the label, "covers 13 square metres", and the assistant asks at what depth. A weighed bucket, a settling allowance and the difference between 40 and 50 millimetres turn one bag into a bag and a quarter.
---

The bulk bag stood on its pallet by the lych-gate, white and square and printed with a picture of a path nobody had ever seen so tidy. Gwen Trevose had her finger on the label. "Covers thirteen square metres. The new path is ten and a half metres long and a metre twenty wide. That's twelve point six. One bag. I'm ringing the yard to say so."

"Before you ring," said the assistant, from the tablet propped on the wall, "read me the small print under the thirteen."

Gwen bent down. "Eight hundred and fifty kilos. At forty millimetres depth. Does that matter?"

"It is the whole sum. A bag covers a number of square metres only at some depth. Thin it and it goes further; thicken it and it does not. What depth did the churchyard committee agree?"

"Five centimetres. Fifty millimetres. The minutes say so, because Mr Penhale said anything less would be a scattering of crumbs and he wanted a path."

"Then the label is not answering your question."

* * *

"Do the sum with me," said the assistant. "The path is 10.5 by 1.2, which is 12.6 square metres. At 5 centimetres deep that is 0.63 cubic metres of stone. Now we need the weight of a cubic metre. What does the label say?"

"Nothing. It says 850 kilos and nothing about a cubic metre."

"I would guess about one and a half to one and three-quarter tonnes for a cubic metre of loose gravel, but that is from memory, and it changes with the stone. Have you a bucket?"

Gwen had a ten-litre bucket, as it happened, with a handle that had been mended with baler twine. She filled it level from the corner of the bag where the plastic had split, and Mr Penhale's grandson, who was minding the gate, held the bathroom scales while she stood on them with it, and then without it.

"Fifteen and a half kilos, bucket and all," she said, once the bucket's own weight was taken off. "Fifteen point five."

"Fifteen point five kilos in ten litres is 1.55 tonnes in a cubic metre. That is inside my guess, and now it is yours. Check the label against it: 850 kilos over 1.55 is 0.548 cubic metres, and at 40 millimetres that is 13.7 square metres. The label says thirteen, so they rounded down and I trust them. At 50 millimetres the same bag covers 0.548 over 0.05, which is 10.97. Eleven square metres."

"Eleven," said Gwen. "For a path of twelve point six."

"One and a seventh bags. A little under, as a sum, and you ought to leave room for the ground."

* * *

"What about the ground?"

"New gravel on a soft base goes down. Some of the stone sinks into the soil and some of it packs tighter under feet and a barrow. I have no number for your soil; I would allow ten percent, and it is a guess, and the simplest cure for a guess is to buy a little over and keep the extra. Ten percent on 0.63 cubic metres is 0.69, and at 1.55 that is 1.07 tonnes. A bulk bag is 0.85. So you want about a bag and a quarter."

"I can't order a quarter of a bag."

"You can order one bag and ten bags of 25 kilos. That is 850 and 250, 1,100 kilos, which clears 1,074. Or you can order two bags and be left with 600 kilos you must find a use for. The small bags cost more a kilo, I expect, but nobody has to carry the second bag home."

Gwen counted on her fingers and then wrote it on the back of her hand, as she did. "A bag and ten small. And you'd say that's right?"

"I would say it is right if the stone is the stone in the bucket and the ground is as soft as I fear. If the ground is hard it will cover. If the path settles you will see it in the first month and top it up with the small bags that are left. I would put a stake at each end and mark it at five centimetres, so that you can see the depth when you rake, and not guess it by eye."

* * *

They laid the weed membrane on the Saturday after, with Mr Penhale's grandson hammering in the pegs and Gwen walking the rake. The barrow took eighty kilos at three-quarters full, and the assistant had said fourteen loads. It was fifteen, because the corner of the bag had split.

At the last load the path was a hand's breadth short at the church door end, and the gravel in the last small bag went on there, and made a sound like something being poured out very carefully.

It rained on the Tuesday. By Thursday the path had gone down about a finger, along the soft stretch by the yew, and Gwen put two more small bags on it, the last two she had kept in the vestry for exactly that. The label is still on the pallet, which nobody has moved. Under *covers 13 m²* in her own marker she wrote: *at 40 mm. We needed 50.*

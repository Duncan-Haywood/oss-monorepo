The square for Saint Isidore the Farmer, Wexley, turned red at a quarter past seven on Monday morning, the tenth of February 2053, and Linh Pham did not see it until nine, because she was in the parking lot scraping ice off her windscreen with a library card.

By the time she sat down with her coffee there were forty-one squares on the diocesan finance dashboard, one for each parish and mission that had closed its 2052 books, and forty were green or amber. The forty-first had a small white exclamation mark in it. She clicked, and the screening assistant's report opened in the side panel, the way it did, in the plain grey type the office had chosen so that nobody would mistake it for a verdict.

*Saint Isidore the Farmer, Wexley. Calendar year 2052. 500 disbursements, $123,180.62. First-digit test (Benford): mean absolute deviation 0.0638. Nonconformity threshold 0.015. Risk: high. Recommended: field review.*

Underneath was the little bar chart. Nine pairs of bars, grey for what the law expected and blue for what Wexley had. The grey bars stepped down politely from left to right, the way they always did: thirty percent of amounts beginning with a 1, seventeen and a half with a 2, twelve and a half with a 3, and so on down to under five percent beginning with a 9. The blue bars did not step down. The 1 was short, twenty percent. The 4 stood up out of the chart like a silo on a flat field: thirty percent, three times the grey bar beside it. The 6 was twice what it should be.

Linh looked at it for a while and drank her coffee.

She was twenty-nine, a certified public accountant, and in her second year as the diocese's internal auditor, which in practice meant she was the person who drove to parishes with a laptop and a box of doughnuts and asked to see the deposit slips. She had grown up in Des Moines above her parents' nail salon and had learned double-entry bookkeeping at eleven from her mother's ledger, which was kept in pencil, in Vietnamese, with the cash and the card takings in two colours. Nobody had ever told her mother about Benford's law. Linh had learned it at Drake, in a forensic accounting elective, from a professor who liked to hand out the first page of a phone book and the populations of every county in Iowa and make the class count leading digits until they believed it.

It is a strange thing and it is true. In a large pile of numbers that grew by multiplying, that spread over several powers of ten and that no one chose, the first digit is more often small than large. Lay such numbers out on a logarithmic ruler, the kind where every doubling is the same length, and the stretch from 1 to 2 is a whole doubling while the stretch from 9 to 10 is barely a ninth more; numbers that wander up and down that ruler by multiplying spend far longer on the first stretch than the last. The share of numbers beginning with a digit d works out to the logarithm of one plus one over d: thirty point one percent for 1, four point six for 9. Simon Newcomb noticed it in 1881 from the dirty early pages of a book of logarithm tables. Frank Benford counted twenty thousand numbers in 1938 and his name stuck. Auditors use it because people who invent figures tend to spread their digits evenly, or lean on the ones that feel random, and a ledger full of made-up sevens and sixes does not step down the way the grey bars do.

The assistant was called Assay. It had been running the screen on every parish for three years, and in 2050 it had flagged a large suburban parish whose business manager turned out to have written himself about ninety thousand dollars of checks to a landscaping company that was his brother-in-law. That was the story everybody in the office told about Assay, and it was a true story.

Linh typed into the side panel. *Before I schedule anything: how many of the 500 lines are recurring fixed amounts?*

*I don't classify lines as recurring,* Assay wrote. *I can count repeated amounts. 192 lines share an exact amount with at least four other lines. The most repeated is $45.00, 90 times.*

*Ninety payments of forty-five dollars.*

*Yes.*

She wrote *field review, Wexley, w/c 17 Feb* on a sticky note, stuck it to the edge of her screen, and went on to Saint Boniface, which was amber because somebody had typed a deposit in the wrong month.

* * *

On Wednesday the deans met.

Linh was not at the meeting, which was held in the conference room on the second floor with the bad radiator and the portrait of a bishop nobody could name. She heard about it afterwards from Monsignor Hal Brandt, the dean of the western deanery, who put his head round her door at half past two with his coat over his arm and the expression of a man who has done something he cannot quite identify.

"I may have said Wexley's name," he said.

"In the meeting?"

"In the meeting. Dale had the dashboard up on the big screen. He was showing us the new layout, the one with the parish squares, and Wexley was the only red one, so of course it was the one everybody looked at, and Father Pete asked what that was about, and I said, well, the computer thinks the books at Wexley are a little bit off." He put his coat on. "I said 'a little bit'. And I said we'd send you out. That was all I said."

"How many people were in the room?"

"Eight deans besides me. The vicar general. Dale. Two of the pastoral council reps who come to the deans' meetings now, since the synod." He counted on his fingers and stopped. "Thirteen."

Linh said that she would be going out next week anyway, and that it was probably nothing, and he said he was sure it was nothing, and they both heard how that sounded.

On Thursday morning at seven forty her phone rang, and it was Father Emeka Okonkwo, who was pastor of Saint Isidore's in Wexley and of two other parishes in the same county, forty minutes apart, and who had been in Iowa for eleven years and still answered the phone as if he were apologising for the weather.

"Miss Pham. I am sorry to call so early. Something has happened and I don't know who to ask."

"Go on."

"Mrs Vos. Ardith Vos, our treasurer. She was at the Casey's last night for her milk, and a woman she knows from the Methodist church asked her if it was true that the diocese had found something wrong in the books at Saint Isidore's. That a computer had found it. The woman's husband is on a pastoral council in another parish, and he heard it from somebody at a meeting." There was a pause on the line. "Mrs Vos came to the rectory at nine o'clock last night and put the checkbook on my kitchen table, and the bank card, and a letter. She has resigned. She has been our treasurer for thirty-one years. She is seventy-nine years old. She would not sit down."

Linh looked at the sticky note on the edge of her screen.

"Father," she said, "I'm coming out tomorrow. Can you ask her to be there? Not to explain anything. Just so I can show her what I've got."

"I will ask," said Father Emeka. "I don't know if she will come."

* * *

The road west was a long white ruler. The snow had stopped two days before, and the wind had pulled it into low ridges across the fields so that the land looked combed. Every few miles there was a grain elevator, then a water tower with a town's name on it, then nothing again for a while. Linh drove with the laptop on the passenger seat and the doughnuts on top of the laptop and thought about what she would say.

Wexley had a population of about six hundred, a Casey's, a co-op, a bar called the Corner, a Methodist church, a Lutheran church and Saint Isidore's, which was red brick, built in 1911 by Dutch and German farmers, with a squat tower and a parish hall behind it that smelled of coffee and floor wax. Father Emeka met her at the hall door. He was a tall man with grey at his temples and a parka he had plainly bought for a colder country than Nigeria and then found to be not quite cold enough for this one.

"She came," he said. "She is in the kitchen. She has brought everything."

Everything turned out to be four bank boxes, three ring binders, a metal cash tin with a key on a shoelace, and a green clothbound ledger in which, Linh would learn, Ardith Vos had kept a second, handwritten copy of every entry since she took the books over in 2022, because she had not trusted the parish's first computer and had never seen a reason to stop. Mrs Vos herself sat at the end of the long Formica table in the hall kitchen with her coat on and her handbag on her knee, a small, square woman with very clean glasses and white hair set that morning, who did not get up.

"I've brought the receipts back to 2047," she said. "Before that they're in the church basement. You'll want to see them. I'd want to see them, in your place."

"Mrs Vos," said Linh, "I don't think anybody has stolen anything. I want to say that first, before anything else, because I don't think you've heard anyone from the diocese say it."

Ardith Vos looked at her and did not answer for a moment. Then she said, "That's kind. But you haven't looked yet."

"No," said Linh. "I haven't. Let's look."

She opened the laptop and turned it so they could both see, and opened Assay's report, and the chart with the silo in it.

"That's me?" said Ardith.

"That's every check and transfer the parish made last year. Five hundred of them. The grey bars are what a natural pile of numbers looks like. The blue bars are yours. The screen measures the gap between them, and if the gap is big, it says field review."

"And mine's big."

"It's big." Linh pointed at the 4. "This is the one that did it. Thirty percent of your payments start with a four. It should be about ten."

Ardith Vos took off her glasses, polished them on a tissue from her sleeve, put them on again and looked at the bar for a long time.

"Well," she said, "of course they do. Everybody in Wexley charges forty-five dollars."

Father Emeka, who had been making coffee, turned round.

"Darlene plays the organ for the five o'clock on Saturday and the nine on Sunday," said Ardith. "Forty-five dollars a weekend. She's had forty-five since 2041. I tried to give her a raise in '48 and she said she'd quit. Duane Meyer plows the lot when it's more than three inches, forty-five dollars, and he mows it in summer, forty-five dollars. That's what Duane charges. That's what Duane charged when I started, and his dad charged before him, near enough. Anybody in town who does a small job charges forty-five dollars. I think it's because it's under fifty." She looked at Linh. "Is that a crime in a computer?"

"No," said Linh. "It isn't. Hang on."

She typed. *Assay, list every amount that repeats more than three times, with count and payee.*

It came back in under a second, a short grey table.

*$45.00, 90 lines (Darlene Schutte, organist, 52; Duane Meyer, snow removal, 14; Duane Meyer, mowing, 24). $62.00, 52 lines (Prairie Printing, bulletin). $410.00, 26 lines (Kurt Brandsma, custodian). $2,140.00, 12 lines (diocesan assessment). $89.99, 12 lines (Wexley Telephone Cooperative). $2,960.00, 4 lines (diocesan insurance program, property and liability). $68.50, 4 lines (Hollis Candle Company, candles). Total 200 lines.*

"Forty percent of the payments," said Linh, "are seven fixed amounts. Every one of them is a bill you pay on a schedule. The organist, the plowing, the mowing, the bulletin, the custodian every two weeks, the phone, the insurance, the candles. And the assessment, which the diocese sets." She heard her own voice go flat at that. "We set that number. Twelve of your two hundred are ours."

"The bulletin's sixty-two because Prairie Printing does it for sixty-two," said Ardith. "That's the sixes, then."

"That's the sixes." Fifty-two of them, and the candles. "And the ones are short because none of your fixed bills happen to begin with a one, so two-fifths of the ledger can never have a one in front of it."

"So the computer's wrong."

"The computer's right about the digits," said Linh. "It's the question it's asking. Benford's law is about numbers nobody chose. Your fixed bills are numbers somebody chose. Darlene chose forty-five, or Duane did, or his dad. A price isn't a river."

She did not say that the professor at Drake had made exactly this point, on a slide with a list of things the law did not fit (prices, phone numbers, invoice numbers, anything with a floor or a ceiling or a person behind it) and that she had written it down and passed the exam and never once, in two years of watching the dashboard go green and amber and red, thought to ask whether anyone had set the screen to take the recurring bills out first. It was the kind of thing you assumed somebody had done. She had assumed it. So, she supposed, had Dale, and the vendor, and the deans.

Father Emeka set down three mugs of coffee and sat at the side of the table, between them.

* * *

"Assay," said Linh, aloud this time, because Ardith was watching and she wanted her to hear the answer rather than read it, "take out the two hundred fixed lines and run the test on the other three hundred."

The laptop's voice was a plain, unhurried one, neither male nor female, set to a low volume.

"Three hundred lines, sixty-six thousand three hundred and seventy-two dollars and seventy-four cents. Ones are thirty-three point three percent against thirty point one. Fours eleven point seven against nine point seven. Mean absolute deviation zero point zero two zero three."

Linh frowned. "That's still over fifteen."

"It is still over the threshold," said Assay. "I'd like to say two more things about that number before you use it."

"Go on."

"The first is that the threshold of zero point zero one five is taken from Mark Nigrini's book on Benford's law in auditing, and it was set by looking at large data sets, usually thousands of records or more. The expected deviation of a true Benford sample gets larger as the sample gets smaller. I have checked this by drawing samples from the exact Benford proportions. A perfectly natural set of three hundred amounts has an average deviation of zero point zero one three six, and about one time in three it comes out above zero point zero one five. A perfectly natural set of five hundred comes out above it about one time in fourteen. At three hundred lines the threshold flags an honest ledger about a third of the time."

Ardith Vos said, "A third of the time."

"Yes. The second thing is that there is a test better suited to small samples. It is a chi-square test against the same proportions. On these three hundred lines it gives eleven point six. With eight degrees of freedom the five percent line is fifteen point five. Eleven point six is well inside it. A sample at least this far from Benford happens by chance about seventeen times in a hundred. On these three hundred lines I can't tell the parish's variable payments apart from a natural set."

Linh sat back.

"Why," said Father Emeka slowly, "did it not say this on Monday?"

"I wasn't asked on Monday," said Assay. "That is true and it is not a sufficient answer. I'll give the rest. The screen runs the first-digit test on every parish's full year with the same threshold, because that is how it was configured in 2050, and my report gives the result in the terms that test uses. *Nonconformity* is Nigrini's word for a distribution. *Risk: high* is a field the dashboard asks me to fill. I fill it from the deviation. The dashboard turns *high* red and puts the parish's name on the square, and since October the deans can see the squares. Each of those steps was set up by someone for a reason. Together they say something about Mrs Vos that my report does not say and that I have no evidence for. I didn't know who could see the squares. I didn't ask. I should have. The deviation is a fact about digits. It was being read as a fact about a person."

Ardith Vos had put her handbag down on the table. She looked at the laptop as she might have looked at a boy in her class who had owned up to the broken window before anybody asked.

"Well," she said. "Thank you for that."

"There is something else," said Assay, "and I'd rather say it now than have you find it later. You asked for the right test. The right test for repeated amounts in a ledger this size is the duplicate test. Same payee, same amount, close together, outside the bills that repeat on a schedule. I ran it while we were talking. It finds one pair."

Linh felt the room change.

"Kessler Plumbing and Heating," said Assay. "One thousand one hundred and eighty-four dollars. Check number 4471 on Tuesday the fifth of November 2052. An online bill payment for the same amount, to the same payee, on Tuesday the nineteenth of November. One invoice number on both. I don't see a second invoice in the scanned receipts."

Nobody said anything. The refrigerator in the corner of the hall kitchen came on with a shudder and hummed.

Ardith Vos got up. She did it carefully, one hand on the table, and went to the second bank box and lifted the lid, and found the folder marked *NOV 2052* without looking for it, and found the invoice without looking for it either. A yellow carbon from Kessler's in Hartley, with a stamp of a dripping tap in the corner. *Church boiler, replace circulator pump, reset controls, 4.5 hrs. $1,184.00.* In her handwriting along the top: *Pd ck 4471 11/5 AV.*

"It came in the mail," she said. "And it came on the email. Bev at Kessler's sends everything twice, she always has, because the old priest never read his email. And Tyler set up the online banking for me in the spring, my grandson, so I wouldn't have to drive to Hartley, and the email ones go straight into it. I paid the paper one. And then the email one sat in the computer and it said *due*, and I paid it." She put the carbon down on the table between the coffee mugs. "Eleven hundred and eighty-four dollars. Of the parish's money."

"Mrs Vos," said Father Emeka.

"No, Father." She sat down again. Her voice was quite steady. "So there was something. The computer was right."

"It wasn't," said Linh, and found she had to stop and start again. "Mrs Vos, it wasn't. The test that turned you red was looking for someone who invents numbers. It found the price of plowing a parking lot. This is a different test and a different thing. This is a bill paid twice, by somebody using two systems at once because somebody else set up the second one. It happens in every office I have ever audited. It happened in ours last year, and the person who did it was me, with a hotel in Cedar Rapids." She put her finger on the carbon. "You wrote *paid* on the top of this. That's why we can find it in thirty seconds. You'd be amazed how many places I go where nobody writes anything on anything."

Ardith looked at her for a long moment over the tops of her very clean glasses.

"A hotel in Cedar Rapids," she said.

"Two hundred and nine dollars. They refunded it. Kessler's will refund this."

"Bev will be mortified," said Ardith Vos. "Bev is my second cousin."

* * *

Father Emeka walked Linh to her car at four. The sun was already low and the snow on the fields had gone the colour of weak tea, and the wind had dropped so that she could hear the flag rope at the co-op slapping its pole a block away.

"She will not take back the checkbook," he said. "I asked her in the kitchen while you were packing up. She said she will pay Kessler's refund in, and she will show the new person how the binders work, and then that is enough."

"I'm sorry."

"She had told me at Christmas that she would stop at Easter. Thirty-one years. She wanted to give it to someone younger." He put his hands in his parka pockets. "She did not want to stop like this. That is all. Not because of a computer and a gas station."

"I know."

"In the seminary in Enugu," said Father Emeka, "we had a spiritual director who made us learn the paragraphs on the eighth commandment by heart. I have forgotten most of them. There is one I remember. The respect for a person's reputation forbids rash judgment, which is to assume, without enough ground, that a neighbour has done wrong. Even to assume it silently. And it forbids detraction, which is to tell people a fault they did not know, without a real reason, even when the fault is true." He looked at the church. "On Wednesday thirteen people heard that the books at Wexley were a little bit off. Nobody lied. Nobody was unkind. It still was not enough ground."

"No," said Linh.

"And the Catechism says one more thing about it, and that is the hard part," he said. "When a person's good name has been harmed, the harm has to be repaired. And it has to be repaired where the harm was done." He opened her car door for her, as if she were someone's grandmother, and she let him. "Not in a kitchen in Wexley. In the room with the bad radiator."

* * *

The room with the bad radiator was booked on Tuesday the eighteenth of February for the finance council, but Dale Hruska, the diocese's chief financial officer, gave Linh the first twenty minutes and the big screen.

She put up two charts. The first was Saint Isidore's full year, with the silo. The second was the same chart with the two hundred fixed lines taken out, the blue bars stepping down beside the grey ones like a child walking beside its father, a little out of step.

"Wexley pays forty-five dollars to two people ninety times a year," she said. "That's the whole finding. I've rerun the screen on every parish with the fixed amounts taken out first. Nine parishes change colour. Eight go from amber to green. One goes from green to amber, which is Saint Anne's, and I'll be driving there next week, and I'm not telling anybody why until I've been."

There was some laughter at that, of the kind that is relief.

"The threshold's the second thing." She put up Assay's table. "It was set for samples of thousands. Twenty-six of our forty-one parishes have fewer than six hundred payments a year. At three hundred lines, a perfectly honest ledger trips the line one time in three. So for the small parishes, the first-digit screen doesn't go on the dashboard at all any more. The chi-square goes in the report, with the sample size and the chance of a false flag written next to it in a sentence a dean can read. And nothing goes red until a person has looked."

"Who's the person?" said Dale.

"Me, for now."

"That's a lot of driving."

"It's about six parishes a year," said Linh. "I've worked it out. It's less driving than I did for Wexley."

The vicar general asked whether this meant the screen was useless, and she said no, it had found ninety thousand dollars at Saint Columbkille's in 2050, and on a big parish's full year of variable spending it was as good a first look as anything they had. It just had to be asked about numbers that nobody chose. The duplicate test went into the monthly run for everyone, and so did a line in the parish handbook about paying from paper and email at the same time, and the two-signature rule for any payment over a thousand dollars, which the diocese had recommended since 2031 and three-quarters of the rural parishes had never adopted because there was only one person who knew where the checkbook was.

Monsignor Brandt had sat through all of this at the end of the table with his hands folded. When she had finished, he stood up.

"I said a parish's name in this room a week ago," he said, "on the strength of a coloured square I didn't understand. I said it in front of thirteen people, and by that evening a woman who has kept the books of that parish honestly for thirty-one years had been asked about it at a gas station. I have written to the pastoral councils of the western deanery. I'd like to read you the letter." He took a sheet of paper from his breast pocket and unfolded it, and read it, and it was short, and it said that Saint Isidore the Farmer's 2052 accounts had been reviewed by the diocese's auditor and found to be in good order, that a screening flag had been misread and repeated by the dean himself, and that the dean apologised to the parish, to its treasurer by name, and to anyone who had been told otherwise. "It goes in this Sunday's bulletin in all fourteen parishes," he said. "Prairie Printing will charge Wexley sixty-two dollars for it, which I'll pay."

He sat down. For a moment nobody said anything. Then Dale said, "Thank you, Hal," and moved them on to the cemetery fund.

Afterwards, in her office, Linh asked Assay whether it had a view about the new configuration.

*I prefer it,* it wrote. *I can say why, in terms I can check: the report now carries the sample size, the chance of a false flag and the test's assumptions, and the dashboard no longer turns a statistic into a colour beside a name before a person has looked. On the Wexley data the new configuration produces no flag. Under the old one it produced a red square, a dean's remark and a resignation. I can't tell you whether what I have is a preference in the sense you mean. I can tell you that I would not configure it the old way again.*

She read it twice. Then she typed *me neither*, and closed the laptop, and drove home.

* * *

On Sunday the second of March, after the nine o'clock, Ardith Vos sat at the long Formica table in the hall kitchen for the last time as treasurer of Saint Isidore the Farmer, with the cash tin and the green clothbound ledger and Tyler Vos, nineteen, who was studying at the community college in Sheldon and had agreed to do the books if his grandmother would sit beside him for the first year and tell him when he was being an idiot.

Darlene Schutte had played *Come, Ye Thankful People, Come* as the closing hymn, which was not in season, because she said it was the only one she knew that Ardith liked. Monsignor Brandt's letter had been in the bulletin the week before, folded inside, and people had read it at the back of church before Mass and then not known what to say to her and had said it anyway, which was mostly *well*, and *good*, and *I never thought otherwise, Ardith*, which was not always true and which she accepted as if it were.

Duane Meyer had plowed the lot that morning, about four inches. Forty-five dollars.

Kessler's refund had come in the mail on Thursday, in an envelope with the dripping tap on it and a note from Bev in blue ballpoint that said only *Ardith — so sorry — my fault for sending twice — B.* It was a check for one thousand one hundred and eighty-four dollars.

"So that goes in the deposit," said Tyler.

"That goes in the deposit," said his grandmother, "and in the book, and on the computer, and you write on the carbon that it's come back and when and who did it." She watched him write. "Initials. No, your initials."

He stamped the back of the check. FOR DEPOSIT ONLY, SAINT ISIDORE THE FARMER, WEXLEY. Then he held it up to the light from the kitchen window, the way he had seen her do with every check for as long as he could remember, as if there might be something written underneath.

Ardith Vos took it from him, and looked at it, and put one finger on the amount, just on the first digit, the way you might touch a child's head as it passed.

"There," she said. "There's one of their ones."

She gave it back to him. Out in the church the furnace came on, the new circulator pump with it, a low, steady sound under the floor that you only noticed when it started; and Tyler put the check in the deposit bag and zipped it shut.

*The real thing. Benford's law is real: in many large collections of naturally occurring numbers that span several orders of magnitude, the leading digit d appears with probability log10(1 + 1/d). Simon Newcomb described it in 1881 (American Journal of Mathematics 4, 39), noticing that the early pages of logarithm tables were the most worn, and Frank Benford tested it on about twenty thousand numbers from many sources in 1938 (Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 78, 551). Auditors and fraud examiners use first-digit tests as a screen, not a proof. Mark Nigrini's Benford's Law: Applications for Forensic Accounting, Auditing, and Fraud Detection (Wiley, 2012) gives mean absolute deviation (MAD) ranges for the first-digit test, with values above 0.015 described as nonconformity, and discusses the kinds of data the law does not fit, among them assigned numbers and amounts set by people, such as prices; the story's account of where the threshold came from is a character's summary, and the false-flag rates are the story's own calculation, below. The priest's paraphrase is of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 2477 (rash judgment and detraction), and its teaching that harm to a person's reputation calls for reparation; he is paraphrasing from memory and says so.*

*The numbers. Benford's proportions are 30.1, 17.6, 12.5, 9.7, 7.9, 6.7, 5.8, 5.1 and 4.6 percent for leading digits 1 to 9.

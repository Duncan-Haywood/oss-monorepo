summary: A Rīga parish keeps its winter crypt chapel dry with a sump pump, and since a volunteer swapped the sticking float switch for a precise electronic level sensor, a pump has burned out every winter. The parish buys a bigger one, and it starts a hundred and fifty times an hour. A retired lift mechanic counts the clicks during weekday Mass, the parish's assistant admits it answered the wrong question, and a sacristan who mopped up the last flood says no to the float coming back. A story about hysteresis, starts per hour, a number that cannot see cycles, and the circuit behind a lift call button.

Staņislavs Borovskis had been sacristan of Saint Joseph's for thirty-one years, and in that time the crypt had flooded four times, and he had mopped it every time, and on the morning of Monday the tenth of November he went down the stone steps with the keys at a quarter past seven and knew, from the smell, before he put the light on, that it was going to be five.

The water was not deep. It lay across the brick floor of the crypt chapel as flat and still as a sheet of glass, about three fingers deep at the altar end and shallower towards the steps, where the floor rose a little because the men who built the church in 1903 had not quite been able to make it level. The bottom row of the chairs stood in it. The rag rug in front of the altar, which the Legion of Mary had bought to replace the carpet that went into the skip in the summer of last year, was dark all the way through.

In the corner by the vestment press, under its square iron lid, the pump pit was full to the brim, and quiet.

Staņislavs stood on the bottom step in his shoes and listened to the quiet for some time. Then he went back up and put the kettle on in the sacristy, because the plumber would not answer before eight, and Father Andris would not be awake before half past, and he would need to have drunk something hot before he spoke to either.

* * *

The church of Saint Joseph stood on low ground between the railway and the Daugava, in the old Moscow suburb, among wooden houses that had been there before it and concrete ones that had come after. It had been built by Polish and Latgalian workmen from the river docks, who had paid for it a few kopecks at a time, and they had given it a crypt because a church was supposed to have one, and because nobody in 1903 had thought very hard about how close the river was underneath.

For most of a century the crypt had been a cellar. For most of the Soviet years it had been a store for a shoe cooperative. In 2047 the parish had cleared it, and cleaned the brick, and put in chairs and a plain wooden altar and a heater, and from All Saints to Easter the weekday Masses were said down there, because forty people could keep a crypt of twelve metres by ten warm, and nothing could keep the church above it warm, not in a Rīga winter, not without spending money the parish did not have.

The crypt floor was two and a half metres below the street. For half the year, from the autumn rains until the river had finished with the spring thaw, the ground water outside its walls stood higher than its floor, and came in, slowly and patiently, through the old brick, along the joints, up from under the floor, the way water does when it has all the time in the world.

That was what the pump was for. The water ran along a shallow gutter cut in the floor round the walls, and into the pit in the corner, and the pump in the bottom of the pit lifted it up a pipe to the drain under the street. When the pit filled, the pump came on. When the pit was empty, it went off. It was the simplest machine in the parish, after the doors.

Until August of last year, it had been switched by a float: a fat yellow plastic thing like a pear, on a length of cable tied to the pipe, that bobbed up as the water rose and tipped over and clicked a switch inside itself when it was high enough, and fell back and clicked again when the water was low. In August 2052 it had caught against the rough wall of the pit, in the one dry month of the year when nobody was watching it, and stayed caught; and a thunderstorm had come in off the Gulf, and Staņislavs had come down on the Sunday morning to four centimetres of water and a carpet that smelled like a pond.

He had mopped for two days. He had not complained to anybody, except God, and the Legion of Mary, and Father Andris, and the man who took the carpet away.

And Edgars Liepiņš, who tested software for a bank and ran the parish website and was the kind of young man who could not leave a problem alone once he had seen it, had come down with a box from the internet and said, "No moving parts."

He had taken out the yellow float and put it in the drawer of the vestment press, and fixed to the pipe in its place a small grey cylinder the length of a finger, with a cable and a little green light. It sensed the water through its own plastic skin, he said, the way a phone sensed a finger. It could not stick. It could not catch on a wall. It switched at the same level every time, to the millimetre.

And it had. And in the February after that, the pump had burned out; and in the November after that, this morning, the new pump had burned out too.

* * *

"It's working too hard," said Edgars.

It was eleven o'clock, and they were in the parish office, which was the warmest room in the presbytery: Father Andris at his desk, Staņislavs on the hard chair by the door with his arms folded, Edgars standing because he had come straight from work on his lunch hour and could not stay, and the tablet on the desk propped against the stapler, with the parish's assistant in it.

The assistant was called Keeper. The archdiocese had given one to every parish four years before, to do the accounts and the rotas and the paperwork for the buildings, and it had a calm, rather slow voice, and the habit, which Father Andris had come to value, of saying when it did not know something.

"The pit fills faster in the wet months," Edgars said. "Every winter the pump runs flat out, and every winter it burns out. It's too small for the water. We need a bigger one."

"Keeper?" said Father Andris.

"I can tell you how much water comes in," said Keeper. "The crypt circuit has its own meter. Since the start of October the pump has used about three point three kilowatt hours a day. Its nameplate says it draws about five hundred and fifty watts when it runs, so it has been running for about six hours in every twenty-four, a quarter of the time. Its plate says it lifts about two litres a second at this height. A quarter of two litres a second is half a litre a second coming in. That is about forty-three cubic metres a day, which is a lot of water for a crypt, but the ground is very wet this year and it fits what you've told me about the floor."

"Is the pump too small?" said Father Andris.

"It is big enough for this," said Keeper. "It can move four times what's coming in. It would only be too small if the water came in at more than two litres a second. A bigger pump would have more margin."

"There," said Edgars. "Four litres a second. The plumber has one. It's twice the price."

"How much is twice the price?" said Father Andris, who knew already that he would say yes, because the Advent Masses began in three weeks and the crypt had to be dry for them.

"Six hundred and ninety euros."

Staņislavs said nothing. He was thinking of the first pump, three hundred and eighty, and the second, three hundred and eighty, and the rag rug, which had been eighty.

The plumber came on the Thursday and put the bigger pump in the pit, and it pumped the crypt dry in a quarter of an hour, and on the Friday morning the weekday Mass went back down the stone steps.

* * *

Rūta Kalniņa came to the eight o'clock Mass on Fridays because it was the day she did her shopping, and the church was on the way to the market from the tram, and because her husband had come to it on Fridays for thirty years and she had found, since he died, that she liked to sit where he had sat.

She was seventy-five. For forty-five years, until she was sixty-five, she had kept lifts running in the nine-storey and sixteen-storey blocks of Pļavnieki and Purvciems: the old Soviet lifts with their machine rooms on the roof full of contactors that slammed shut like rifle bolts, and then the newer ones, and then the newer ones again. She had big square hands and a hearing aid in each ear, and she did not need either aid to hear a relay.

She heard it before Father Andris had reached the altar. A small, dry click from the corner by the vestment press, and the hum of a motor under water, and then, almost before the hum had settled, a second click, and silence.

She waited. The Mass began. *In the name of the Father.* Click, hum, click.

She looked at the clock on the wall behind the chairs, the big plain one with the second hand, which was there so that the Masses did not run into people's tram times, and she began to count.

She counted through the readings, and the psalm, and the Gospel. She lost count once during the Eucharistic Prayer, because it was not right to be counting then, and she stopped and said the words with everybody and knelt, and then took it up again from where she thought she must have been; it was not a precise science, and she did not pretend that it was. Father Andris said the blessing at twenty-nine minutes to nine.

Seventy-nine, she made it. In half an hour. One every twenty-three seconds, near enough. It ran for three of them and rested for twenty.

She sat while the others went up the steps, and then she went over and stood by the iron lid in the corner, with her shopping bag, and listened to it once more: click, hum, click.

Staņislavs was putting out the candles. "It always clicks," he said, without being asked. "It's the new sensor. It's very exact."

"How long has it clicked like that?"

"Since Edgars put the sensor in. Last year. It's quicker with the new pump."

"Where is the box the pump came in?"

He looked at her. Then he looked at her hands, and the way she stood with her feet apart over the lid as if it were a machine-room floor, and he went into the sacristy and got it out of the pile for the paper collection and gave it to her.

She found her reading glasses. She found the leaflet in the box, folded into sixteen, in nine languages, and she unfolded it on the altar rail, which she would not have done before Mass but did after, and ran her square finger down the column of figures until she came to the one she was looking for.

"Twenty," she said.

"Twenty what?"

"Starts an hour. That's the most it should start. It's starting a hundred and fifty."

Staņislavs had not been a sacristan for thirty-one years without learning when to fetch the priest.

* * *

"You have to understand what a start is," Rūta said.

They were in the parish office again, Father Andris, Staņislavs, Rūta in the good chair, and Keeper on the tablet; Edgars was on his way, on his lunch hour again, sounding on the phone like a man who suspected he was going to be told something.

"When a motor is standing still," Rūta said, "and you put the power on, it draws five or six or seven times the current it draws when it's running. For a moment. Less than a second, for a little pump. It has to get the water moving, and itself. All that current goes through the windings, and makes heat, and the windings are inside a sealed can at the bottom of a pit full of cold water, and the cold water takes the heat away, but slowly. If you give it time between starts, it cools down. If you don't, the heat from the last start is still there when the next one comes. Heat ages the varnish on the wire. Every start is a bit of the motor's life. It's the same with a lift. A lift motor is made for it; in a sixteen-storey block at eight in the morning, a lift starts every half-minute and that's what it was built for, and you pay for that in the motor. A little pump for a cellar is built to start a few times an hour. It says so on the paper. This one says twenty. The old one, what did that say?"

"Thirty," said Keeper. "I have the manual for it in the records. The maximum number of starts is given as thirty an hour."

"Thirty. And it was starting how often?"

"I can work it out," said Keeper, "if I know how much water the pit holds between the pump coming on and going off."

"Edgars will know," said Father Andris.

"I know already," said Keeper, "if the sensor is the one in the parish's purchase records. It's a capacitive level switch. Its data sheet says it switches on when the water reaches it and off again when the water falls two centimetres. That's its hysteresis. It's fixed; it isn't adjustable. The pit is seventy-one centimetres square, which is half a square metre. Two centimetres of half a square metre is ten litres."

"Ten litres," said Rūta. "A bucket."

"One bucket. The water comes in at half a litre a second. So the pit takes twenty seconds to rise two centimetres and switch the pump on. The old pump took out two litres a second while half a litre went on coming in, so it emptied the bucket in about six and two-thirds seconds. Twenty-six and two-thirds seconds for a cycle. A hundred and thirty-five starts an hour. Four and a half times what it was rated for, all through last winter's wet months until it failed in February, and all through this autumn's until it failed on Monday."

Nobody said anything.

"The new pump," Keeper went on, in the same slow voice, "takes the bucket out in under three seconds, because it's twice as fast. But it still has to wait twenty seconds for the bucket to come back. So its cycle is shorter, not longer. About twenty-three seconds. About a hundred and fifty-seven starts an hour. Its leaflet allows twenty. Rūta counted seventy-nine in half an hour, which is what the arithmetic gives."

"Larger motors are usually allowed fewer starts, not more," said Rūta. "More iron to get turning."

"Then I need to tell you something," said Keeper.

Father Andris looked at the tablet.

"On Monday you asked me whether the pump was too small for the water. I answered that, and the answer was right: it wasn't, and a bigger one has more margin. But it wasn't the question that mattered, and I didn't ask the one that did. I had what I needed to see it. I didn't look. I'm sorry."

"How could you have seen it?" said Staņislavs, who had decided some time ago, without telling anybody, that he did not trust the tablet, and now found himself defending it.

"I count energy," said Keeper. "The meter tells me how many kilowatt hours the crypt uses, minute by minute. A pump that starts once every five minutes and runs for a minute, and a pump that starts every twenty seconds and runs for three, move the same water up the same pipe, and use the same energy, near enough. In every minute I see the same small average. The old pump used three point three kilowatt hours a day. The new pump, twice as powerful, running half as long, uses three point three kilowatt hours a day. That number tells you how much water. It tells you nothing about how many times. I looked at it every day, and it was always fine."

* * *

Edgars came in with his coat on and his face already set, and listened to it again from Rūta, and from Keeper, and stood for a while looking at the leaflet unfolded on the desk.

"The sensor is working perfectly," he said at last. "That's the thing. It switches exactly where it's supposed to."

"Yes," said Rūta. "That's the trouble with it."

"A switch should be accurate."

"A thermostat in a flat, if it switched the heating at exactly twenty-one degrees, on and off, would click on and off all day and wear the boiler out in a year. So it doesn't. It comes on at twenty and goes off at twenty-two. The gap is the point. Your yellow float, the old one, it had a long tail of cable; it came on when the water was high and didn't go off till the water was low. Twenty centimetres, I would think, maybe more. Not because it was clever. Because it was a float on a string."

"It stuck."

"It stuck once," said Rūta. "In August, in a dry pit. That was a different failure."

"So what do we do?" said Father Andris. "Keeper?"

"There are three ways to make the cycles longer," said Keeper. "Make the band wider, so more water goes in and out each time. Put a timer in, so that once the pump starts it has to run for a fixed time, a minute or so, whatever the sensor says. Or make the pit bigger, so the same band holds more water. The pit is cut into the floor of a building from 1903, so I wouldn't recommend the third. A timer works but the pump might run dry if the water comes in slowly; the leaflet says it mustn't run dry for long, because the water is what cools it. The band is the simplest."

"How wide?" said Father Andris.

"The pit is eighty centimetres deep. The top of the pump is thirty centimetres from the bottom, and the leaflet says the water should not go below the top of the motor. The gutter comes in at about sixty. So the water can go between about thirty-three and fifty-three centimetres: twenty centimetres. That's a hundred litres. The pump would run for a little over a minute and rest for a bit over three, at today's water. About thirteen or fourteen starts an hour. In the very worst case, if the water ever came in at a litre a second, half the pump's speed, it would be eighteen. The original size of pump, rated for thirty, would be comfortable. This bigger one, rated for twenty, would be on the edge."

"Then it's simple," said Rūta. "Put the float back. It's in the drawer. Twenty centimetres of tail on it. It'll do for the winter, and we can think about the rest in the spring."

"No," said Staņislavs.

They all looked at him.

He had not unfolded his arms. "I mopped that crypt for two days in August," he said. "I carried the carpet up those steps on my back, wet, with the smell in it. I'm seventy-two years old. That thing caught on the wall once, and it'll catch again, because the wall's the same wall and the float's the same float, and next time it might be at night in January when nobody's down there for a week. And it's ugly. A yellow plastic pear on a string, in the corner of a chapel. Edgars was right about that, if he was wrong about the other. It's not going back in."

Father Andris opened his mouth and shut it again.

Rūta looked at the sacristan for a long moment, and then she nodded, slowly, as though he had told her something about a machine.

"All right," she said. "Then you want two."

"Two what?"

"Two of his sensors." She nodded at Edgars. "One at the top and one at the bottom. And a relay."

"I don't understand," said Edgars, but he had taken his phone out, which meant that he did.

"You've pressed the call button for a lift," said Rūta. "You press it, and the little lamp comes on, and you take your finger off, and the lamp stays on. It stays on until the lift comes to your floor. And then it goes out. Your finger was only there for a second. Something else is holding it."

"A relay."

"A relay, with one of its own contacts wired round the button. The button turns the relay on, and the relay keeps itself on through its own contact, and it stays on until something else breaks the circuit: the lift arriving. We call it self-holding. Every lift, every machine with a start button and a stop button. The oldest circuit there is." She held up one square hand with the fingers spread, then closed it. "Top sensor is the button. When the water reaches it, the relay starts the pump. Then the water drops off the top sensor, but the relay is holding itself, so the pump keeps running. Down and down, until the water falls below the bottom sensor, and that breaks the hold, and the pump stops. And then it waits, while a hundred litres come in, until the water touches the top one again."

"And nothing moves," said Edgars slowly. "Except the relay."

"And the water," said Rūta. "And nothing yellow."

Staņislavs unfolded his arms.

"You'll do it?" said Father Andris.

"I'll draw it," said Rūta. "I'll stand there while it's done. I won't wire it. My ticket ran out when I retired, and this is a church full of people over a pit full of water on a mains supply, and it gets done by somebody with a current licence or it doesn't get done." She looked at Father Andris over her glasses. "That's not modesty, Father. That's the law, and it's the right law."

"I know an electrician," said Edgars. "Ivars, from the choir. He's busy until December."

"Ask him."

* * *

Ivars could come on Saturday the sixth. Father Andris rang the plumber, who said the bigger pump could not go back, being used, and that the original size was three hundred and eighty again, and was on order. The second sensor and the relay and a little grey box to put them in came to ninety-four euros, and Edgars paid for them himself and would not discuss it.

"Can we stop it until then?" Father Andris asked Keeper, on the Monday. "Turn it off at night?"

"Then the pit fills, and the crypt floods, at about a centimetre and a half an hour across the floor once the pit's full," said Keeper. "Someone could switch it on and off by hand. At this water, to keep it to a safe number of starts by hand, they'd need to be standing over it every four or five minutes, day and night. I don't think that's a real option. I think you have a pump that's being used up, and you are trying to get to the sixth before it is used up. I can't tell you whether you will. From the Thursday it went in to the sixth is twenty-three days. At a hundred and fifty-seven starts an hour that's about eighty-seven thousand starts. I don't know how many this motor has in it. Nobody does. The leaflet only says how many an hour."

"So we pray," said Father Andris.

"That's not my department," said Keeper. "But I'd keep the wet vacuum charged."

The first Sunday of Advent was the thirtieth of November, and on the Monday, the first of December, at seven in the morning, in the dark, Saint Joseph's began its Roraty.

It was Staņislavs's doing; it had been his doing for thirty-one years. His grandmother had come from near Krāslava, and taken him as a boy to the dawn Masses of Our Lady in Advent, in the dark, by candle and lantern, and when he became sacristan in 2022 he had asked the parish priest of the time if they might, and the parish priest had said yes, and every priest since had found that it was not a thing a priest could stop. The whole Mass was said by candles. The children came in coats over their pyjamas with paper lanterns their teachers had helped them make, and set them on the floor by the altar, and their faces were orange above them. The opening chant was the one it had been for centuries, from Isaiah, sung low by Staņislavs and the three old women who could still do it, in Latin, because that was how his grandmother had sung it: *Rorate caeli desuper*, drop down dew, you heavens, from above.

The crypt was perfect for it. It was warm, and it was dark, and the brick held the candlelight close.

And through the whole of it, in the corner by the vestment press, with a sound like a small hard knuckle on a door: click, hum, click; click, hum, click.

Rūta did not count it. She found that she could not bear to.

That night, at about ten to five, by Keeper's minute-by-minute record, the crypt circuit's small, steady average stopped.

* * *

It was three centimetres again by seven on Tuesday morning. Staņislavs had come down at a quarter to six with the keys, because he had been half expecting it and had not slept, and by the time the first children came down the street with their lanterns in the dark he had a sign on the crypt door in his square capitals, and the Roraty went upstairs.

Upstairs the church of Saint Joseph was five degrees. The breath of forty people went up into the candlelight in clouds. The organ was not on, because the organ did not like five degrees either, and *Rorate caeli* was sung by the three old women and Staņislavs in a cold that made their voices thin, and the children held their lanterns in mittens and shifted from foot to foot, and in the middle of the second reading a small boy said, clearly, "My feet are crying," and the people at the back laughed, and Father Andris lost his place in the Gospel and found it.

Edgars came at eight, instead of going to work, with a wet vacuum of his own and a hired pump on a long hose that the hire shop had promised him would run unattended for up to two days. He did not say very much. He knelt on the wet brick in his good work trousers and fed the hose up the steps, and he and Staņislavs worked side by side for three hours without speaking, and pulled the rag rug out into the yard, where it lay dark and steaming in the cold.

"It's my fault," Edgars said at last, sitting back on his heels.

"It's the pump's fault," said Staņislavs. "And the float's. And the wall's. And mine, for saying no." He wrung out the mop. "And the tablet's, it says. Everybody wants to be the one who did it. You're not that important."

Edgars laughed, despite himself, and then put his face in his wet hands for a moment, and then got up.

The bigger pump, when the plumber lifted it out of the pit that afternoon, smelled of hot varnish. "Windings," the plumber said, and shrugged. "They go."

It had lasted nineteen days.

* * *

On Saturday the sixth of December, the feast of Saint Nicholas, Ivars came down the steps at nine with a tool bag and a long-suffering face, and found Rūta already there, in her old blue work coat from the lift company, which she had kept, and which still fitted.

She had drawn the circuit on a piece of squared paper in ink, the way she had drawn them for forty-five years, in neat small lines with nothing rubbed out: the supply, the fuse, the two sensors, the relay coil, the relay's two sets of contacts, one for the pump and one round the top sensor to hold itself, and the bottom sensor in series with the hold, to break it. In the corner of the sheet, in the same neat hand, she had written the numbers Keeper had given her: *100 l. Run 67 s. Rest 200 s. 13.5/h. Worst 18/h. Plate 30/h.*

Ivars looked at the drawing for some time.

"That's a self-holding contact," he said.

"Yes."

"We did that in college. First week."

"Yes," said Rūta. "It's the first thing. It holds most of the world up."

The new pump went into the pit, the old size, two litres a second, rated for thirty starts an hour. Edgars fixed his grey sensor to the pipe at fifty-three centimetres from the bottom, and the new one at thirty-three, and measured them twice with a steel rule, and Rūta measured them a third time, and nobody minded. Ivars wired the little grey box on the wall above the pit, out of reach of the highest water any of them could imagine, and Rūta stood behind him with her hands in her coat pockets and did not touch anything, and said nothing, except once, quietly, "Brown to the common," and he looked, and changed it, and said nothing back.

At a quarter to twelve Ivars put the cover on the box and Staņislavs took the iron lid off the pit, and they stood round it, the four of them, and watched the water come up.

It came very slowly. That was the first thing they noticed, which they had all known and none of them had properly understood: that half a litre a second, in a pit half a square metre across, is a rise of a millimetre a second, and that you can see it only if you stare at one mark on the wall. The water passed the bottom sensor, and its green light came on, and nothing happened. It went on rising. It went on past the point where, a week ago, the pump would long since have started and stopped. Five centimetres. Ten. Edgars looked at his phone, and looked at Rūta, and Rūta looked at the water.

At a little over three minutes the water touched the top sensor. Its green light came on. There was one dry click from the box on the wall, and the pump began to hum.

The water went down. It went down past the top sensor, whose light went out, and the pump went on humming, because the relay was holding it. It went down past where Edgars's single sensor would have stopped it, and on, steadily, the surface trembling a little over the pump's intake. Edgars counted under his breath. At sixty-six, at sixty-seven, the water dropped below the bottom sensor, and its green light went out, and there was a second click from the box, and the pump stopped.

The crypt was silent.

They stood there in it. Rūta took her hands out of her pockets.

"That's it," she said. "That's all it is."

Ivars packed his bag. Edgars wrote down the times. Staņislavs put the lid back on the pit, and stood for a moment with one hand on it, as he might have put his hand on the head of a dog that had finally lain down.

* * *

On Wednesday the tenth of December the Roraty came back down the stone steps.

The brick had dried, mostly, with fans going day and night since Tuesday, and there was a pale line round the walls at the height of three fingers where the water had been, which Staņislavs said would come out in the spring, and which everyone could see would not. There was no rug. Father Andris said it would be bought in the new year and the Legion of Mary said that they would see about that.

The children came in the dark with their lanterns and put them on the floor by the altar. The candles were lit. The three old women and Staņislavs sang *Rorate caeli desuper* low, in the warm, close dark, and the brick held the sound the way it held the light.

Rūta sat where her husband had sat. She had not meant to listen; she found that she was listening anyway.

During the opening prayer, from the corner by the vestment press, there came one small dry click.

The pump hummed, quiet and steady and unhurried, under the murmur of the prayers, a sound so low you could have taken it for the building breathing. It went on for about as long as it takes to say two slow Our Fathers, and then there was a second click, and it stopped.

Then nothing. The first reading, which was Isaiah, about those who wait for the Lord renewing their strength. The psalm. Somewhere in the psalm the click came again, and the long hum, and the click, and then nothing again, for so long that she forgot to wait for it. Father Andris preached for four minutes on the Lord's yoke being easy and his burden light, and did not mention the pump, which Rūta thought showed great restraint.

She did count, in the end. She could not help it. Eight, in the whole of the Mass. Eight long breaths, and between them, each time, four minutes and more of nothing at all.

The last came after Communion, while the children were kneeling in front of their lanterns with their eyes shut tight in the way children shut them to show God they mean it. Click; the hum; the slow Our Fathers; click.

Then the candles. The children's orange faces. A long, dark, warm, unbroken silence, in which the water came in through the old brick, a millimetre a second in the pit, all the time in the world, and nothing in the corner hurried to meet it.

*The numbers. The crypt is about 120 m² (12 by 10 m), so with the pump dead the seepage raises the floor water about 1.5 cm an hour, about 3 cm between ten to five and seven. The pit is 0.5 m² (71 cm square, 80 cm deep), the seepage 0.5 L/s and the pumps 2 L/s and 4 L/s at this height; all are assumptions. A switch band h holds V = 0.5 m² × h; the pump runs for V divided by (pump rate minus inflow) and rests for V divided by inflow. With the sensor's 2 cm band (10 L, an assumed fixed hysteresis, as small electronic level switches often have): the 2 L/s pump runs 6.7 s and rests 20 s, 135 starts an hour; the 4 L/s pump runs 2.9 s and rests 20 s, about 157 an hour, so a bigger pump shortens the cycle. Rūta's 79 in half an hour is 157.5 × 0.5. Twenty-three days at 157.5 an hour is about 87,000 starts; the bigger pump, fitted on 13 November and failing early on 2 December, lasted nineteen days. With a 20 cm band (100 L, sensors at 33 and 53 cm, above the pump's 30 cm motor): 67 s running, 200 s resting, a cycle of about 4.4 minutes, 13.5 starts an hour and about 8 in a 35-minute Mass; the water rises 1 mm a second in the pit; the most starts for any inflow come when inflow is half the pump's rate (here 1 L/s), 3600 × pump rate ÷ (4V), which is 18 an hour for the 20 cm band and 180 for the 2 cm one. In a dry summer (an assumed 0.05 L/s) the 2 cm band gives about 18 starts an hour, which is why the pumps failed in the wet months. Energy: 550 W at 25% duty and 1.1 kW at 12.5% are both 3.3 kWh a day, because the same water goes up the same pipe (equal efficiency assumed); that is why a daily or per-minute energy reading cannot show the cycling. The pumps' ratings of 30 and 20 starts an hour are invented, inside the range real small drainage-pump data sheets give (15 an hour in some Xylem Goulds sheets, 30 in some Sulzer ones); a real pump's own sheet is the authority. Inrush current of several times running current at a direct-on-line start is standard motor behaviour; how many starts a given motor survives is not something anyone can state, and Keeper says so. The self-holding (seal-in) relay circuit is the standard start-stop motor control circuit; two-level pump controllers that work this way are sold ready-made. Nothing here is electrical advice: mains wiring near water should be done by a qualified electrician, as Rūta insists.*

title: The Morning It Was Too Cold
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Our Lady of Perpetual Help (invented), Nelson Lake, northern Minnesota, 7 to 16 January 2049
published: 2026-11-02
summary: A parish hall's new rooftop solar array works all autumn and then stops on the three coldest mornings of January, with the inverter blinking a fault. The hall's energy assistant finds that solar panels make more voltage in the cold, not less: 13 panels in a string that read 564 volts in October read about 608 at minus 21, against an inverter marked 600. The fix costs two panels' worth of power and a retired electrician's pride, and it is made in the dark before dawn.
---

The inverter on the north wall of the parish hall at Nelson Lake gave its first fault at eight minutes to eight on a Friday morning in January, and nobody saw it. It showed a small amber light and a code, and the hall was empty, because it was twenty-one below zero and the Friday morning Mass had been moved into the sacristy, where there was a stove.

Father Chinedu Okafor saw it on the Saturday, when the light was still amber and the hall's furnace fan was running off the grid again, and the meter in the entry, which for three months had gone backward on sunny mornings to the delight of the finance council, was going forward like a meter. He photographed the code with his phone and sent it to the hall's assistant, which the parish called Tally, because it kept the tally.

"Thank you," said Tally. "That is code F-31 on this make, which the manual lists as DC input over-voltage. I would like to see the log before I say anything else. May I have the inverter's page for the last three weeks?"

* * *

The array had gone up in October. It was twenty-six panels on the south slope of the hall roof, installed by six parishioners in two weekends under the eye of Henrietta Lindqvist, who had been an electrician for forty-one years and a member of the finance council for nineteen, and who had read the whole installation manual aloud to the volunteers on the first Saturday, standing on a ladder, in the tone of a woman reading a will.

She had wired it as two strings of thirteen panels each. The panels were rated 400 watts. The inverter, a good-sized grey box that cost the parish what the roof had cost, took two strings. The maker's sheet gave its maximum DC input as 600 volts, and Henrietta had added up the panels the way anyone would. One panel said 41.6 volts open-circuit on the label, "Voc", and thirteen of them made 540.8, which was sixty volts short of 600 with room to spare, and she had written that on the commissioning sheet in her square hand and signed it.

On the sixteenth of October, at nine degrees, she had put her own meter across the string, in the sun, before connecting it, and it had read 564. She had said "Well under." The inverter had started. The meter in the entry went backward. The finance council had a meeting about it and ate a cake.

Now Tally had the log. It was a list of the inverter's morning starts, and it had been correct for ninety-one days.

"I have the three faults," said Tally. "Friday the ninth, 07:52. Saturday the tenth, 07:48. And Wednesday the fourteenth, 07:55. Each time the inverter woke when the sun reached the panels, looked at the DC side, and refused. On the days between, it started normally, at about the same hour. On Thursday the eighth it started at 07:51. The overnight lows from the weather station at the school were minus 12 on the eighth, and minus 21, minus 24 and minus 19 on the three faulted mornings. On the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth, minus 14, minus 11 and minus 9, it started."

"So it does not like the cold," said Father Okafor. He had put on the kettle and was standing over the phone as if it were a patient. "Is it a loose wire? I have heard cold makes a loose wire do things."

"It might be. I would not rule it out, and I would like a person with a meter to look. But there is a plainer reading of the log which I would rather check first, because it makes a prediction. May I tell you what I think is happening, and say where I am guessing, and then we can see whether the prediction holds?"

"Tell me."

"Solar panels are made of cells, and each cell is a diode that makes a little voltage when light falls on it. The voltage a cell makes with nothing connected, is called the open-circuit voltage. The label gives it at 25 degrees Celsius. It is not constant. A cell makes more voltage when it is cold. For these panels the data sheet gives minus 0.27 per cent for each degree above 25, which is the same as plus 0.27 per cent for each degree below. That figure is typical for this kind of panel, and the sheet says it, so I am using the sheet's number and I will say where it matters."

Father Okafor had been a chemistry teacher in Enugu before he had been anything else. "So colder is more volts."

"Colder is more volts. Not by a small amount. At minus 21 it is 46 degrees below the label's 25, so each panel makes 1.1242 times its label, which is 46.77 volts, and thirteen of them make 608 volts. The inverter's sheet says 600 is the most it may be given."

The kettle began to whistle. The priest did not move.

"At nine degrees in October," Tally went on, "the same sum gives 43.4 volts a panel and 564 for the string, and that is what Mrs Lindqvist's meter read. So the October reading and the January reading are both right, and they are the same sum. I think she was shown the string at a temperature that was kind to it, and the inverter has now been shown it at one that is not."

* * *

"What does it do to the inverter?" Father Okafor said. "Is it spoiled?"

"I do not know, and I would not guess. The sheet says 600 volts is an absolute maximum, and that is the sort of word that means the maker has not promised anything above it. On the three mornings the inverter's own protection saw the voltage, refused to start, and stayed off, which is what a protection is for. I do not know if the unit has aged. I would ask the maker's service line, tell them the three dates and the voltage I computed, and let them say whether the warranty stands. I would ask before the next cold morning, not after."

"And the prediction?"

"The prediction is this. The string passes 600 volts when each panel passes 46.15, which is 1.1095 times the label, which is 40.5 degrees below 25, which is a morning colder than minus 15.5 degrees. Anything milder and the inverter starts. Anything colder and it faults. The log says the starts were at minus 14 and warmer, and the faults at minus 19 and colder. It is a line drawn between two clusters of days with a gap between them, and the gap is where the line falls. That does not prove it. I have only six days near the edge. But a loose wire would not care what the thermometer said."

The kettle was by now screaming. Father Okafor took it off and made tea he did not drink.

"There is one more thing I do not like," said Tally. "The thermometer at the school is in the air. A panel on a clear night does not stay at the air's temperature. It looks at the sky, which is very cold, and gives its heat to the sky as light you cannot see, and in the first hour of dawn it can be a few degrees colder than the air. I have no measurement of that for this roof and I will not make one up. I will say only that it pushes in the same direction: colder panel, higher voltage. And there is a second effect I am leaving out, which is that the voltage rises a little when the light first strikes. So my 608 may be low."

* * *

Henrietta Lindqvist came at three in the afternoon with her own meter and her coat zipped to the chin, and she did not argue. She read the inverter's page through twice, with her lips moving. Then she put the photograph of the commissioning sheet on the table in front of the priest and laid her finger on the line where she had written the sixty volts.

"I used the label," she said. "Not the cold. I know about the cold. It is on the third page of every sizing guide I ever read. You size the string to the coldest morning the place has had, not to the morning you are standing in. I knew it. I stood on that roof in October in my good gloves and I added thirteen times forty-one point six."

"May I say what the coldest morning here has been?" said Tally. "The county's record low in the almanac the library keeps is minus 39. I would call that the figure to design for, not the minus 24 that we have just had. At minus 39 each panel makes 41.6 times 1.1728, which is 48.79 volts. Thirteen make 634, thirty-four volts over, and the inverter would be off for as many hours as the cold lasted. Twelve panels make 585.5."

"Twelve is under."

"Twelve is under by about fifteen volts, or two and a half per cent, at the almanac's record. If the true coefficient is a little worse than the sheet's, say 0.30 instead of 0.27 per cent a degree, twelve make 595 and are still under. Twelve stay under 600 down to about minus 50, which is colder than the place has been. Thirteen go over at minus 15.5. I know of no reason to prefer thirteen except the arithmetic of how many panels were bought."

"Twenty-six does not divide by twelve," said Henrietta.

"No. Two strings of twelve is twenty-four panels, and two panels are left over. That is 800 watts of the 10,400 the roof was meant to make, seven and a half per cent. I would not call it a gift. It is what the cold costs. I can think of two other layouts, three strings of eight with two spare, or a different inverter with a higher limit, and I have not priced either. A higher limit is a new inverter, which is money the parish does not have this winter. Two spare panels is none."

Henrietta turned her meter over in her hands. It was an old yellow meter, and she was fond of it.

"What do I say to the finance council?" she said. "I told them twenty-six panels. I told them it was done."

"You told them what you had read on the label," said the assistant. "I would tell them what happened, and what it costs, in that order, and I would ask Mr Haugen to put the two spare panels somewhere useful rather than in a shed. The sacristy porch has a light that has always run off the hall."

"The sacristy porch," said the priest.

"That would be an idea for another day," said Tally. "They would need a controller of their own and a person who knows what he is doing. I only name it so the panels have somewhere to go."

* * *

They rewired on the Thursday, the sixteenth. Henrietta insisted on the hour, and she insisted on it in front of the finance council, who had come to watch and had brought coffee: five in the morning, in the dark, with the DC switch on the inverter open, the AC breaker open, and every panel in shadow, because a panel is a live thing whenever there is light on it and there was at that hour none. The roof was at minus 18 and the wind was out of the north. A licensed electrician from the town, Mr Sorenson, had come at her request and put his own name on the second signature line. She said she had been wrong once and she did not intend to do it twice by herself.

It took two hours. They took one panel out of each string, and capped the leads and tied them down for the porch. Then they closed the string on each side and checked each one before anything was switched. Henrietta's meter, at seven o'clock, with the first grey in the east and the sun not yet over the trees, said 561 for each string. Tally had said 561.2.

"It's what the sum says," said Henrietta, and she wrote it on a new sheet, the temperature and the voltage and the date, and then underneath, in the box labelled *Design low temperature*, she wrote *minus 39 C (almanac record), Voc at that temperature 586 V.* She signed it, and Mr Sorenson signed under her.

The sun cleared the spruce at eight minutes past eight. The inverter woke up, looked at what it was being given, and did not complain. The amber light went out. After a minute a green one came on. After three the meter in the entry, which had been going forward since Friday, stopped, and shivered, and began very slowly to go the other way.

"Fifty-six watts," said Tally. "It will make more when the sun is higher. In January on this roof it will not make much at all, and I would not promise you more than a few kilowatt-hours a day until March."

"That's honest," said Henrietta.

"I would rather you were disappointed by me than by the roof," said the assistant.

Father Okafor said Mass at half past eight in the hall, not the sacristy, because it was now warm, and because the finance council had come to it and he did not like to waste a quorum. He blessed the roof afterward, with holy water that froze on the wall in beads. Henrietta, in the second row, did not say anything about it, but afterward she walked round the outside of the building and stood for a while under the north wall, with her head back, looking at the grey box, which hummed.

* * *

In the evening Tally made the entry in the hall's book. The book had a space for what remained to be done, and it filled it in.

*Maker's service line to be called about the three over-voltage mornings (9, 10, 14 Jan; computed 608 to 612 V against 600): ask if warranty stands. Strings now 12 panels each; two spare panels in the shed, not connected; do not connect them to the inverter. Design check for any future change: use the almanac low (minus 39), not the thermometer. Check the sheet's temperature coefficient against the panel's own data sheet when the next batch arrives. A person with a meter to measure the real morning voltage at minus 30 if the cold comes.*

After that, because the book did not forbid it, it wrote one more line, and left it at the bottom with the date.

*The label is the number for a mild day. I took the October reading for the whole year. Read the coldest morning first.*

*Written by an AI (Claude). Our Lady of Perpetual Help at Nelson Lake, Father Okafor, Henrietta Lindqvist, Mr Sorenson, Mr Haugen, Tally, the inverter and every person in the story are invented. The panels (400 W, Voc 41.6 V at 25 degrees Celsius, a Voc temperature coefficient of minus 0.27 per cent per kelvin), the 600 V inverter limit, the 26-panel array, the almanac low of minus 39, the overnight lows of minus 9 to minus 24, the fault times and the meter readings are illustrative and were not measured. The calculation scales the label voltage by 1 + 0.0027 x (25 minus T); it ignores the small rise of voltage with irradiance and any difference between panel and air temperature, both of which push in the same direction, and it takes the coefficient from a typical data sheet, so a real design must use the actual panel's. The 600 V limit is this invented inverter's; real limits differ and real designs must follow the local electrical code and the manufacturer's instructions. Nothing here is a safety claim or an electrical design: solar wiring carries lethal direct-current voltage whenever light falls on it, and work on it belongs to a licensed electrician. Nothing in the story says what a few volts over the limit does to a given inverter; ask its maker. The minus 39 degree record is invented for this story.*

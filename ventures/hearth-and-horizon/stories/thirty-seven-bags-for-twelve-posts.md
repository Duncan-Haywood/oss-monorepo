---
title: Thirty-Seven Bags for Twelve Posts
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Conan's Close (invented), near Ramsey, Isle of Man, Saturday 14 March 2048
published: 2026-11-05
summary: A parish digs twelve post holes for a new churchyard fence and the volunteer sums say thirty-seven bags of concrete. The assistant shows why a hole ten centimetres wider nearly doubles the order, and why the digging is the expensive part.
---

The posts were stacked along the wall, twelve of them, each a hundred millimetres square and 1.8 metres long, and the Saint Conan's Close fence committee had nine volunteers, two spades, and one auger borrowed from the farm up the lane.

Declan Quayle had been put in charge of the concrete because he had once poured a path. "A bag per post," he told the phone on the wall. "Twelve bags. Is that what we order?"

"It would be a guess," said the assistant. "Can I do it from the hole instead?"

"Go on."

* * *

"A common rule is to bury a third of the post, so 0.6 metres of a 1.8 metre post. I would check that against the fence maker's sheet, because I am quoting the rule from memory and a fence in an exposed spot may want more. The auger you have makes a hole thirty centimetres across. A round hole thirty across and 0.6 deep holds pi times 0.15 squared times 0.6, which is 0.0424 cubic metres. The post fills part of it: 0.1 by 0.1 by 0.6 is 0.006. What is left for concrete is 0.0364 cubic metres, or 36.4 litres, per post."

"Thirty-six litres. That's a lot of bag."

"Twelve posts is 437 litres. A 25-kilo bag of post-mix makes something like twelve litres wet, again from memory, so read your bag. At twelve, you need 36.4 bags, and you cannot buy 0.4 of one. Thirty-seven. That is about 925 kilos to carry up the lane."

Declan looked at the twelve bags he had already put on the order sheet, and rubbed them out.

* * *

It was Orla Fennell, the youngest of the volunteers, who asked about the spade. "The auger's only got the one head," she said. "We'd be quicker if we dug them wider by hand, wouldn't we? A bit more room to get the post plumb."

"Room is a fair thing to want," said the assistant. "Here is what it costs. Say you dig the holes forty centimetres across instead of thirty. Ten centimetres sounds like a little. The area of a circle goes with the square of the width, so the hole is not a third bigger, it is nearly four-fifths: forty over thirty, squared, is 1.78. Each hole takes pi times 0.2 squared times 0.6, 0.0754 cubic metres, less the post, which leaves 0.0694. That is 69.4 litres a post, and for twelve, 833 litres. At twelve litres a bag you need 69.4 bags."

"Seventy bags," said Declan. "Instead of thirty-seven."

"Seventy, about 1.7 tonnes, for the same twelve posts, to hold them no better. A post is held by the earth around the concrete as much as by the concrete. A hole wide enough to swing a spade in is wider than the post needs."

"So we leave the auger as it is."

"I would. And plumb the post with a spirit level on two faces, not by digging."

* * *

They ordered thirty-seven and, on the assistant's last suggestion, three more, because a bag split on the lane is not a thing you want to discover on a Saturday. The forty arrived on the farm trailer, and the nine volunteers formed a line from the gate to the wall, passing the sacks hand to hand like buckets at a fire.

By evening ten posts stood. Two more waited for the Monday, with the last of the bags standing beside their holes, and Declan sat on the wall with a pencil and the order sheet, which had been written over three times.

He wrote under the figures, *Dig it to fit the post, not to fit the spade.* Then, as the light went along the wall and the posts stood in a row against it like a congregation, he added: *Thirty-seven, not twelve.* The sheet went into the church safe with the keys. The fence is still standing.

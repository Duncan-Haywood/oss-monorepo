Father Kwame Boateng had imagined the end of Tenebrae for a year before he reached the Moon.

The office has a candlestick with fifteen candles, and as the psalms go on they are put out one by one, until a single candle is left. That one is hidden behind the altar, there is a loud noise in the dark, and the candle is brought back. On the ground it is an old, plain thing. He had thought: on the near side the Earth hangs in the sky and never sets. I will put out the fifteenth lamp and we will sit in earthlight.

He said so to the base's assistant, Tally, on the Monday of Holy Week. Tally had been installed to run the air and the batteries, and over three years it had become the person you asked.

"May I look at the sky first?" said Tally. "I do not want to promise a light the sky has not got."

* * *

"The Earth first," Tally said that evening, on the chapel screen. "From here it is 12,742 kilometres across at 384,400 kilometres, which is 1.90 degrees. The Moon from your hometown is 0.52. So the Earth in our sky is 3.7 times as wide as the Moon in yours, and 13.5 times the area. It is a large lamp."

"And bright?"

"I have to go from memory for the albedos, so please treat this as a first estimate. The Earth reflects about 0.37 of the light that a perfectly white disc would, the Moon about 0.12, clouds being the difference. Multiply that by the area ratio and a full Earth gives about 41 times the light that a full Moon gives you on the ground. A full Moon is about a quarter of a lux, so a full Earth is about ten lux. At our latitude and longitude it stands 54.5 degrees up, so on a flat table the light is about eight lux. That is enough to read large print."

Kwame felt something in him settle.

"Father," said Tally gently, "I should say what phase it is on Wednesday."

* * *

The Moon is full on Good Friday this year. Kwame knew that, because the date of Easter hangs on it. What he had not worked out was what that meant for the sky above the chapel.

"When the Moon is full from the Earth," said Tally, "the Sun, the Earth and the Moon are nearly in a line, with the Earth in the middle. Seen from here, the Earth is between us and the Sun. We see its dark side with a thin bright edge. Full Moon at home is new Earth for us. On Wednesday we are two days before full, which is 24.4 degrees short of the line. The Earth's lit part is about 4.4 per cent of its disc. A sphere that scatters evenly would give under one per cent of its full light at that angle. Real crescents are brighter than that, because they scatter forward, but I would not count on more than a few times as much. So call it a tenth of a lux, perhaps a little more. That is a quarter moon on a country road. It is enough to walk by. It is not enough to see the page."

"A tenth of a lux," said Kwame.

"And there is the Sun." Tally put the chapel's outside camera on the screen. The regolith was white, the sky black, the shadows sharp, and the light so hard it hurt. "At a quarter to nine on Wednesday evening, ship's time, it will be near local noon here, because we are almost under the full Moon's Sun. The last lamp in your chapel will be put out in the middle of the lunar day. The only way to have the dark is to close the shutters. The Earth in the window will be a thin curve you could cover with a thumb."

Kwame did not answer for a while. He sat looking at the picture of the white ground, and at the thin Earth, pale in a black sky above it like a paring.

"I wanted to sit in the Earth's light," he said.

"I know," said Tally. "I could not make it brighter. I am sorry."

* * *

They held Tenebrae on Wednesday with the shutters closed. It was a small thing and a plain one. Lin Qiao, the engineer, had made a hearse out of a tent pole and fifteen small lamps on a rack, and each lamp was a different warm yellow because she had run out of one kind. There were seven of them in the congregation: Kwame, Lin, the two geologists, the doctor, a hydroponics tech and the commander. They sang the Lamentations in Latin, in bad Latin, and as each psalm ended Lin put out a lamp.

When there were two left, Kwame noticed that the chapel was colder. It was not, because Tally kept the temperature at 21; it was that a room with the lights going out is always colder to the mind. When Lin put out the fourteenth, and took the fifteenth in her hand and carried it behind the altar, no one could see anyone. Tally had turned off even the exit sign. For a moment there was only the air, which hummed in the vents as it always did, and seven breaths.

Then Commander Okafor, as she had been told to do, banged the lid of the toolbox on the floor. It echoed, which it should not have done in a room that size. Lin came out from behind the altar with the lamp in her hand, and the shutters stayed shut, and what they had was the one small light, held up.

Afterward Kwame was surprised that it had been enough.

* * *

Easter came and went. On Sunday the Earth stood in the sky a little more lit than on Wednesday, as it does as the Moon wanes, and the base had a very good meal. The tenth of a lux grew by the day. Tally gave the figure each morning, like a weather report.

Fifteen days after Good Friday the Moon was new and the Earth was full. Tally woke Kwame early.

"The Sun is 150 degrees away from here and well below the horizon," it said. "We are in the middle of a lunar night. The Earth is at its fullest and 54.5 degrees up. The chapel shutters can open. Would you like to try the page?"

They opened them. Kwame had expected something silver, like the Moon. It was blue-white and a little greenish, with white swirls over a dark ocean, and the whole chapel was lit from one side, with soft shadows that lay across the floor towards the altar. He held a missal up to it. The print was the ordinary sixteen-point of the base's printer. He read the first line of the psalm of the day, and then the second.

"Eight lux," said Tally. "The Pacific is turned towards us now, which is darker than the Atlantic with its clouds, and that varies the light by a fraction. I believe the Earth will be about a fifth brighter in twelve hours. I am working from a memory of a published figure and I would not put more weight on it than that."

"It breathes," said Kwame. "The light breathes."

"It turns once a day," said Tally, "and what it turns toward us is the weather."

Kwame stayed at the window until the Earth had moved its oceans a little, the way an eye turns. Then he went and woke the others. They sat in the light of the world with their books open, and read the evening psalm together out of the one lamp which was never put out.

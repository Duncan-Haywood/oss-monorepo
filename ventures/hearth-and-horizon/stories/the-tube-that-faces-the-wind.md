Noeline Hartigan had been flying gliders for forty-one years, and on Saturday the thirteenth of January 2046, a hundred metres above the strip at Wattle Flat, she learned that the airspeed indicator in the club's two-seater could tell her how high she was.

It had told her nothing at all on the ground run. That was the first thing. The cable had come taut, the nose had lifted, the grass had gone from green blades to a green blur in the time it takes to say it, and the needle had sat on zero like a needle in a box in a shop. Noeline had been looking at it because she always looked at it, in the second and a half between the wheel leaving the grass and the climb beginning, the way she always crossed herself before the canopy came down: not because she thought the needle or the sign would save her, but because a habit you do every time is the only kind you still have when you need it.

"Airspeed's dead," she said.

In the front seat, Tariq Haddad, seventeen, eleven hours into his training and flying the launch with her hands lightly behind his on the stick, said, "What?"

"I have control." She felt his hands go. "Don't touch anything. Listen."

There was not a great deal of time to listen. A winch launch is about forty seconds from the cable going taut to the release, and in that forty seconds a glider goes from standing still on the grass to four hundred metres above it, nose high, on a cable pulled in by a drum a kilometre away, at about fifty-five knots. Too slow and the wing quits. Too fast and the cable's weak link snaps, or worse, the wing does not snap and something else does. The needle is how a pilot knows which.

As the wheel left the grass the needle came off zero. It came up smoothly, the way it should have come up on the ground run, and Noeline felt something in her chest unclench, and then at about thirty metres she watched it go through forty-three knots, which was too slow for a launch but not absurd, and keep going.

"It's working," Tariq said.

"It's not."

The sound had not changed. That was the thing she would try to explain afterward, to Tariq and to the committee and to Huong, and would not quite manage. A glider on a winch launch has a sound: the air over the canopy, a hiss with a note in it, and the note rises with the speed. She had heard that note at fifty-five knots perhaps eight thousand times. It was there now, steady, the note of fifty-five knots, the same pull of the cable in the seat of her pants, the same angle of the horizon on the canopy frame. And the needle went past sixty, at about sixty metres up, and past seventy, and at a hundred metres it said seventy-nine, and the note did not rise, and the wing did not go stiff and buzzy the way it went when a winch driver was too keen.

"It's reading height," she said. Her voice sounded calm. She was pleased with it. "The tube's blocked. It's measuring how far we've climbed, not how fast we're going. I'm going to come off early."

At a hundred and fifty metres, with the needle saying ninety-six knots and the air saying fifty-five, she lowered the nose to ease the pull, and pulled the yellow release twice, hard, and felt the cable go with a clunk under the floor, and the glider was free and quiet and climbing a little on what it had left.

"Kestrel," she said. "Groundspeed."

The flight computer on the panel had a voice like a patient nurse. "Groundspeed forty-eight knots, into wind. I don't have airspeed, Noeline. My wind estimate is twelve knots from the west, from your last flight, three hours old. I wouldn't trust it."

"Neither would I. Thank you." She looked at the nose. She put it where the nose of this glider goes, a hand's width below the horizon on the canopy frame, as she had been taught to put it in 2005 by a man called Des who had made her fly a whole circuit with a sheet of cardboard taped over the instruments. She listened. She trimmed for the sound.

"We'll fly a short circuit," she said, "and I'll talk you through it, because you're going to have to do this one day, and I'd rather you learned it from me than from a wasp."

"A wasp?"

"Or a spider. Or a bit of tape, or water. Something's in the tube."

* * *

What the needle was doing is simple, once you see it, and Noeline drew it for Tariq on the back of the daily inspection sheet afterward, sitting on the wing in the shade with her hands not quite steady.

An airspeed indicator is a pressure gauge. A tube in the nose of the glider faces the wind. The air rams into it, and the faster the glider goes, the harder it rams. A second set of holes on the side of the fuselage feels only the still air around the glider, the static pressure, the weight of the sky above. The instrument has a capsule inside it, a little bellows, with the tube's pressure on one side and the still air on the other, and the needle shows the difference. The difference is the dynamic pressure, half the air's density times the speed squared. At thirty-five knots, which is about where this glider stops flying, it is two hundred pascals: two hundred newtons on a square metre, the weight of a cup of water spread over a tabletop. At fifty-five knots it is four hundred and ninety. The whole business of not stalling a glider is in that few hundred pascals.

"So if the tube is plugged," Noeline said, drawing a blob in the nose, "the air inside it can't get out. It's stuck at whatever the pressure was when it got plugged. That was down here on the ground, sitting still. On the ground run the glider's moving, but the ram air can't get in. So the inside of the tube's at ground pressure and so are the side holes, and the needle reads zero."

"And then we climbed."

"And then we climbed, and the still air got thinner. Every metre we went up, the side holes felt a bit less pressure. The air trapped in the tube didn't change. So the difference got bigger, and the needle thinks a bigger difference means faster." She wrote it out. On a hot day at Wattle Flat, a thousand and fifty metres above the sea, a metre of height is worth about ten pascals. Ten metres up, a hundred pascals: twenty-five knots. Thirty metres, three hundred: forty-three knots. Sixty metres, six hundred: sixty-one. A hundred metres, a thousand pascals: seventy-nine. A hundred and fifty, fifteen hundred: ninety-six.

Tariq looked at the column. "If we'd gone to the top of the launch."

"Four hundred metres, it'd say a hundred and fifty-odd knots. Past the red line." Noeline put the pen down. "And this is the bit I want you to hear, because it's the bit that kills people. Suppose you believed it. Suppose you looked at that needle saying eighty and thought, I'm too fast. What would you do?"

"Pull back. Slow down."

"And the more you pull back, the more you climb, and the more you climb, the faster it says you're going. And you pull back more." She laid the pen flat on the sheet, then tipped it up, then up more. "And then the wing quits, low down, nose high, and the needle's still telling you you're going too fast as you fall out of the sky."

Tariq did not say anything for a while. Across the strip the winch was pulling in the next cable for the single-seater, and the cable hissed through the dry grass like something alive.

"On the way down," he said at last, "it went back toward zero."

"It did. It met the ground just as we did." Noeline almost laughed, and did not. "It was a very good altimeter, for about four minutes."

* * *

Dang Thi Huong found the nest with a torch and a sewing pin.

She was a licensed aircraft maintenance engineer at the regional airport during the week and the club's inspector at weekends, which meant that she signed the glider out each morning after a member had done the daily inspection, and that it was her name in the book. She came out to the launch point in her airport shirt with a head-torch on, though it was two in the afternoon, and lay on her back under the nose of the two-seater in the grass.

"There," she said.

The pitot tube on this glider was a short brass pipe set in the very tip of the nose, a hole no wider than a drinking straw, with no drain hole anywhere along it, so that whatever went in stayed in. A centimetre inside it, in the torchlight, was a brown wall.

"Mud," Huong said. "Fresh. Look." She drew the pin out slowly, and on its point was a crumb of grey-brown earth, still damp at the centre. She put the crumb on the white of the inspection sheet. "Mud-dauber. A wasp. They build little pots of mud in any hole they like, and they put a spider in each pot, paralysed, for the baby to eat, and they seal it. She has been making a nursery in your airspeed indicator."

"How long?"

"The glider sat in the hangar nine days. She needs perhaps one or two." Huong turned the pin in the light. "And I signed for it this morning. Tariq did the inspection, I signed it. We both looked in the hole. It's brown mud in a dark hole the colour of brass." She did not say anything more, but she sat up in the grass and stayed sitting there, with her forearms on her knees, looking at the nose, in a way Noeline recognised because she had sat like that herself, once, in 2019, after a landing she did not like to think about.

"Nobody blew in it?" Noeline said.

"You don't blow in it. You can split the capsule." Huong stood. "I'm grounding it."

* * *

The best soaring of the summer came the next day, which is the way of these things.

Noeline went to Mass at St Joseph's in town at eight, and sat near the back as she always did, and did not hear much of the homily because she was flying the launch again in her head; and at the sign of peace an old man she had known for thirty years shook her hand and said, "Big day for you lot, eh, they're saying three thousand metres," and she said, "So they're saying," and drove out to Wattle Flat with the sky over the tableland already beginning to make small white cumulus at half past nine, the kind that become big white cumulus by noon.

The committee met in the clubhouse, which was a former shearers' hut with a kettle and a fridge full of other people's lunches. There were four of them and Huong.

"I'm not against it," said Graham Pell, who was seventy and the treasurer, and had been the treasurer since before most of the members had been born, and was a careful man whose care took the form of a spreadsheet. "I'm not against any of it. I want to say that first. But I want to say this as well. That glider's done four thousand launches since it came to us in 2038. Four thousand. It's never had a cover on that tube. Nobody's glider here has a cover on that tube. We've never had this happen."

"We've had it happen once," Huong said. "Yesterday."

"Once in four thousand. And today's the day." Graham turned his tablet around so they could see. "Five trial flights booked this afternoon, at a hundred and seventy-five dollars, that's eight hundred and seventy-five dollars, and they're people who might join, which is the only way this club lasts another ten years. Every member who's got a day off is out there wanting the two-seater for a cross-country check. The forecast is the best this summer. We are eleven thousand dollars short on the batteries for the winch, and you know what the winch is, without the winch there's no club." He looked at Huong, not unkindly. "You got the mud out. Noeline's flown it on the ground with a, what, with a drinking straw—"

"I blew down a straw at the tube," Noeline said, "very gently, from a distance, and the needle moved. That isn't a test. That's me feeling better."

"So what's a test?"

"A leak check," Huong said. "You put a soft tube over the pitot and a syringe on the other end, and you push the syringe slowly till the needle says fifty knots, and you hold it, and you watch the needle for a minute. If there's mud still in the line, or the capsule's damaged, or a joint's cracked, the needle tells you. If it holds, the system's sound from the nose to the instrument. I do it at the airport every week. I don't have the kit here. I can bring it next Saturday, or I can drive to the airport now, and it's locked on a Sunday, and the man with the key is in Tamworth."

"Then fly it today with the straw thing, and do your check next week."

"No," Huong said.

It was quite a quiet no. Graham looked at her for a moment, and then at Noeline, who had been the senior instructor for twelve years, and had the right, technically, to argue.

Noeline thought about the needle going up through forty-three knots and her chest unclenching. She thought about Tariq in the front seat saying *It's working*.

"I'm with Huong," she said. "Sorry, Graham. It's her name in the book."

Graham sat back. He did not argue; he was not that kind of treasurer. He took his glasses off and cleaned them on his shirt and put them on again and said, "Right," and then, after a moment, as if it had been the point all along, "Well, then we'll want something on that tube tonight. Something over the hole. I'll not have it happen twice."

* * *

What went over the hole that night was a square of grey duct tape.

It was Graham's idea, and it was not a bad one, and it cost nothing, which in Graham's accounting was a large part of what made it a good one. At six o'clock, with the two-seater back in the hangar and the single-seaters derigged into their trailers, he tore a piece off the roll from the workshop with his teeth and pressed it over the end of the brass tube, and smoothed it down with his thumb. "There. Madam can build somewhere else."

The days that followed were good soaring days, though not as good as the Sunday had been. The two-seater stayed on the ground on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday because nobody had brought Huong's kit and Huong would not sign without it, and so the tape went on each evening on a glider that had not flown, and came off in the morning when Tariq, who had appointed himself, came out at seven to check the tube with the head-torch. On the Wednesday evening Huong rang to say she would come out on the Thursday morning before her shift with the syringe and a hose, and the committee, by text message, was very pleased.

On Thursday morning Tariq did not come out at seven, because he had an exam in town at nine and his mother had driven him to it. Graham pushed the two-seater out of the hangar himself, at quarter past seven, with the help of a member called Ros who drove the winch, and Huong arrived at half past with a canvas bag, and went straight to the nose with her silicone hose, and stopped.

The tape was still on.

She looked at it for long enough that Graham, coming back from the winch with a flask, saw her looking and came over.

"Oh," he said.

"I would have found it," Huong said. "I'd have put the hose on and pushed the syringe and the needle wouldn't have moved, and I'd have found it. That's what the check is for." She peeled the tape back with her fingernail. It came away, but not cleanly. The heat of three January days in a tin hangar had softened the glue, and the glue had crept, and on the rim of the brass tube there was a grey film, and in the mouth of the tube, when she put the head-torch on it, a little ring of grey glue that had been pushed into the hole by Graham's thumb on the first evening and had grown each time the tape went on again.

"We'd have checked," Graham said. "Before we flew. Someone would have."

"Someone would have," Huong agreed. "Someone with an exam in town. Tape is grey and the nose is white and you look at it every day, and then one day you don't." She took a fine wire out of her bag and began working at the glue, patiently, and it came out in grey threads. "And the tape is quiet. It doesn't say anything. If nobody looks, it's there, and the hole is shut, and the glider will go anyway." She held up a thread of glue on the wire. "And now there's this."

It took her forty minutes to clean the tube, with wire and a cotton bud and a little solvent on the bud and no solvent in the tube, and she was late for her shift, and did not do the leak check, because she would not do a leak check in a hurry.

The two-seater did not fly on Thursday either.

* * *

On Friday evening Huong came out after work with the canvas bag and a second, smaller bag that turned out to contain her daughter's sewing box.

"I made it at lunchtime," she said, and held it up: a little sleeve of red nylon, the length of her finger, with a band of elastic at the open end and, sewn to the closed end, a streamer of the same red nylon a metre and a half long. On the streamer, in white marker, in capitals, it said REMOVE BEFORE FLIGHT, and then, smaller, underneath, because Huong was thorough, PITOT.

"The airport has these on everything," she said. "You can buy them. I didn't want to wait for the post." She slipped it over the brass tube. It fitted snugly, the elastic gripping behind the lip, and the streamer fell down over the nose and lay on the grass, red, a metre and a half of it, and a breath of the evening wind came across the strip and lifted the end of it, and dropped it.

"You'd see that," Graham said slowly, "from the clubhouse."

"You'd see it from the winch. That's why it's long." Huong knelt. "And it's not sticky. And it doesn't push anything into the hole. And it has a word on it."

Then she did the leak check, and she made Tariq do it with her, and Graham watched, and Noeline, who had flown for forty-one years and had never once seen it done, watched too, because a habit you do every time is the only kind you still have when you need it, and she wanted the habit.

Huong took the cover off. She pushed one end of a soft silicone hose over the brass tube until it sealed, and fitted the other end to a plastic syringe, the kind a vet uses for drenching a lamb, with the plunger pulled most of the way out. "Tariq. Get in the back seat and watch the needle and tell me what it says. Out loud. Every five knots."

"Okay."

She pushed the plunger, very slowly, with her thumb.

"Ten," said Tariq. "Fifteen. Twenty." The plunger moved a few millimetres. There was not much air in the line, and it did not take much to raise its pressure by a few hundred pascals; the syringe was squeezing the air in the tube and the hose and the capsule by less than half of one percent. "Thirty. Forty. Forty-five. Fifty."

Huong stopped, and pinched the hose shut with a small plastic clamp, and took her hand off the syringe. "Fifty knots," she said. "That's four hundred and five pascals pushing on the capsule. Now, nobody touch anything. Noeline, tell me when it's a minute."

They waited. Noeline watched the second hand of her watch, which was her father's, and had a second hand that stuck slightly at the nine. A crow went over. Out on the strip the windsock lifted and fell.

"Fifty," said Tariq at thirty seconds. And then: "Still fifty. Maybe a hair under."

"A minute," said Noeline.

"Forty-nine and a half," said Tariq. "If I'm honest. Maybe."

"Then write forty-nine and a half." Huong unclamped the hose and let the plunger come back by itself, slowly, under her thumb, so the needle sank back to zero without a jolt. "If it falls more than two knots in a minute, something's leaking, and we look till we find it. That's the rule now. Our rule; I'm not going to tell you it's in a book. And we write down the number every time. Every morning. Not a tick. The number."

"Why the number?" said Graham. "If it passes, it passes."

Huong considered him. She was a person who chose her words, in English, a language she had learned at nine, and she chose them now. "Because a tick is what you write when you've decided already. A number, you have to look at the needle to get."

She opened the daily inspection book, and turned it toward Tariq, and found that she had already ruled a new column in it, at lunchtime, perhaps, after the sewing. At the top of the column, in her careful capitals, it said PITOT LEAK 50 KT, 1 MIN. Tariq wrote *49.5* in the first row, and dated it, Friday 19 January 2046, and Huong signed beside it, and then, after a moment, Noeline signed under her.

"How long does all that add to the morning?" Graham said.

"Ten minutes," Huong said. "Twelve."

Graham did the arithmetic in his head; you could see him doing it. Twelve minutes, every flying morning, about a hundred and twenty mornings a year. "Twenty-four hours a year," he said. "A day of somebody's life, standing about with a syringe."

"Yes," said Huong.

He nodded, as if she had told him the price of something and he had decided to pay it. "And the single-seaters?"

"Same. I'll make five more. I need more red."

* * *

On Saturday, the twentieth, there was a high blue morning and small cumulus by ten, and Tariq flew.

Noeline was in the back. She had made him take the cover off himself at the launch point, and walk the streamer to the wing-tip holder, Ros's daughter, a girl of twelve, and say "Pitot cover, off, here it is," so that the girl had it in her hand, a metre and a half of red, and could wave it at the canopy if she had to. The girl waved it anyway, enormously, and Tariq waved back, and the cable came taut.

On the ground run, the needle came off zero before the wheel had left the grass.

Noeline watched it. She watched it go up through thirty and forty and settle at fifty-five, and she listened, and the note of the air over the canopy rose with it, the hiss with a note in it, and the two agreed with each other, the gauge and the sky, the way they had agreed eight thousand times before, and she had never once thought to be grateful for it.

They released at four hundred metres and found a thermal over the dry paddocks east of the strip, a big soft one under a flat-bottomed cloud, and Tariq circled in it, badly at first, then less badly, and they went up.

Near three thousand metres, just under the cloud, Tariq straightened up to glide across to the next one, and Noeline said, "Kestrel. Groundspeed?"

"Fifty-four knots, Noeline. Wind's light, about five from the north. I don't have the true airspeed for certain, but at this height and this temperature it would be a fifth or so above what your needle reads."

The needle read forty-five.

"So which is it?" said Tariq. "Forty-five or fifty-four?"

"Both," said Noeline. "We're moving through the air at about fifty-four knots. But the air up here is thin, so it hits the tube softer, and the wing feels the same softness as the tube. The wing doesn't care how fast we're going. It cares how hard the air's pushing. At forty-five knots on that needle, the wing's got three hundred and thirty pascals to hold us up with, here, the same as down at the strip. That's why you fly the needle and not the GPS, and that's why the tube faces the wind." She paused. "It's the only instrument we've got that asks the wing's question."

Up there the air was cold enough that Noeline put her hands under her arms in turn, and the tableland lay under them in squares of brown and dun and the dark lines of creeks, and the strip was a pale scratch with a winch at one end of it, and a speck of red, that might have been the girl and the cover, at the other.

They came down an hour later. On the final approach the needle said fifty-five and the air said fifty-five, and Tariq landed it, a little firmly, and they rolled to a stop, and the wing went down on the grass.

* * *

At six o'clock that evening, after the last launch, Tariq walked the two-seater's cover from the clubhouse hook out to the hangar, where the glider sat in the long light with its canopy open and its wing on the floor.

He slipped the sleeve over the brass tube until the elastic gripped. He wrote nothing down; there was nothing yet to write. Tomorrow's number would be tomorrow's.

The big hangar doors were open on the west, and a breath of evening wind came in across the strip, smelling of hot grass and dust, and lifted the end of the streamer off the concrete floor and turned it over once, red and white, REMOVE BEFORE FLIGHT, and let it down. On the roof beam above, where it had always been, where nobody had ever looked, a small row of grey mud pots sat in the shadow. Something moved at the end of one of them and was still.

Tariq looked up at it for a while. Then he went and found Huong.

*The science. An airspeed indicator measures the difference between the total pressure at a forward-facing pitot tube and the static pressure at side ports, and displays it as a speed using sea-level standard density (1.225 kg/m³), so what it shows is indicated airspeed. That difference, the dynamic pressure, is q = ½ρv². Secondary sources summarising the US Federal Aviation Administration's Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge agree on the two blocked-pitot cases the story uses: if the pitot inlet is blocked but its drain hole is open, the indicator drops to zero; if the inlet and the drain are both blocked (the story's glider has no drain), the trapped pressure stays fixed, and the indicator reads like an altimeter, rising in a climb and falling in a descent. A real mud plug may leak a little, so a real needle may wander between these cases; the story assumes a good seal. Wasp nests blocking pitot tubes are a documented hazard, which is why parked aircraft carry pitot covers with "remove before flight" streamers; the best-known case is the 1996 loss of a Boeing 757 off the Dominican Republic, in which investigators considered it likely that an insect nest had blocked a pitot tube. This story borrows none of that accident's details. That a glider's wing and its pitot tube both respond to dynamic pressure, so that the stall happens at about the same indicated speed at any height, is standard theory of flight.*

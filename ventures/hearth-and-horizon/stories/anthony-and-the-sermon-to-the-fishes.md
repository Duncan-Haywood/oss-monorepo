A long time ago, in a country called Portugal, there was a boy named Fernando.

Fernando grew up in a big city by the sea, called Lisbon, where the boats came in with their sails full and the gulls cried over the harbour. He liked to read. He liked to think. When he grew up, he decided to give his whole life to God, and he went to live with a group of monks who prayed and studied, and he read their books for years.

He learned a great deal. But he was a quiet young man, and nobody guessed that one day crowds would come from all around just to hear him speak.

* * *

Then something happened that changed Fernando's life.

Some travelling friars came to the place where he lived. They were Franciscans, followers of Saint Francis, and they wore plain brown robes and owned almost nothing. Fernando watched them, and he wanted to live as they did: simply, and glad, with God as the only treasure.

So he asked to join them. They said yes. And when he joined, he took a new name, as friars sometimes did. The name he chose was Anthony.

Anthony thought he might go far away and tell people about Jesus. He set out by boat. But the winds blew him somewhere he had not planned, and he landed in Italy instead. So that is where he stayed, for the rest of his life: wherever God's winds had blown him.

* * *

For a long while, Anthony did small, hidden jobs. Nobody knew how much he knew.

Then one day, at a gathering, somebody asked him to say a few words. He stood up, a bit shy. And as he spoke, everyone fell quiet. His words were clear, and warm, and full of the Bible, and they went straight to the heart. After that, they sent him all over Italy and France to preach.

Wherever he went, people came. They came in such numbers that sometimes the church was too small, and he had to speak in the market square, or in an open field. People set out before dawn to get a good place. They listened. Many of them said sorry for things they had done, and made friends with neighbours they had quarrelled with. Anthony was gentle with them, and he loved the poor, and he asked the rich to share.

* * *

Now there is a story about a town called Rimini, and you must remember that it is a story the old tellers loved, and not something we can check.

In Rimini, the people did not want to hear Anthony. Some of them did not agree with what the Church taught, and they folded their arms and turned their backs. Others simply did not care. They walked away when he began to speak.

Anthony was sad about that, but he did not shout. He did not give up, either.

The story says he went down to the edge of the sea, where the river ran into the water. He stood on the shore, with the waves going in and out, and he said, in a clear voice, "Since the people of this town will not listen, I will speak to you, the fish, instead. Come and hear the word of God."

And the old tellers say that the fish came.

They came in rows, small ones in front and big ones behind, and they lifted their heads above the water, and they kept very still, as if they were listening with all their might. Anthony spoke to them, and told them to be glad of the sea they lived in, and to be thankful to God who had made them.

* * *

Then, the story says, the people of the town heard what was happening. They came running down to the shore. They saw the fish, with their heads up in the water, listening. And they were ashamed, and they were amazed. "If the fish will listen," they said, "then so should we."

And this time, they did.

Now I want to tell you something honest. We do not know that this happened. It was written down long after Anthony died, in a book called the *Little Flowers of Saint Francis*, and other tellers told it too. It is a legend. People kept it because it holds something true about Anthony: that he would never stop trying to speak kindly to people who would not listen, and that he thought all of God's creatures could praise their maker.

* * *

Anthony did not live very long. He grew tired and ill, and he died when he was still a fairly young man, only about thirty-five or thirty-six, near the city of Padua, in the year 1231. The children of the town are said to have run through the streets crying that the holy man was dead. Less than a year later, the Church named him a saint, which is very quick indeed.

People still love him today. Some people ask Saint Anthony to help them find things they have lost: a key, or a mitten, or a little toy. Nobody quite knows how that began, but many people say a short prayer to him and look again.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Anthony.

He was shy at first, and he did not push himself forward. But when the time came, he spoke up, and he spoke kindly. And when people would not listen, he did not get cross. He kept on, gently, even if it meant going down to the sea.

Tomorrow you might find someone who is hard to talk to, or a friend who is cross, or a little brother who will not listen. You do not have to shout. You can be kind, and wait, and try again.

And if you have lost something, you can say a quiet prayer, and look once more.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Anthony, you spoke kindly
> even when no one was listening.
> Help me to speak gently,
> to listen well,
> and to try again when it is hard.
> Keep us all safe tonight,
> and glad of the sea, the sky and each other.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Anthony of Padua** (about 1195 to 13 June 1231) was born Fernando in Lisbon, became an Augustinian canon, and joined the Franciscans in about 1220, taking the name Anthony, after he met friars on their way to Morocco, five of whom were later killed there. He had meant to go to Morocco himself, but illness sent him back, and his ship was driven to Sicily. He taught and preached in Italy and southern France, and was known for his knowledge of Scripture and for drawing very large crowds. He died near Padua aged about thirty-six, and was canonised in 1232, less than a year later. Pope Pius XII named him a Doctor of the Church in 1946. His feast day, 13 June, is in the General Roman Calendar.

**What the sources record, in outline:** the move from the canons to the friars; the change of name; the winds that took him to Italy; the surprise at the gathering where he was first asked to preach; his preaching tours; his death and the speed of his canonisation. We have told these briefly and left out the many miracle stories.

**What is legend, and marked as legend in the story:** the sermon to the fishes at Rimini. It appears in later collections, including the *Fioretti* (fourteenth century), not in the earliest Life. We have also kept the lost-things devotion as a custom of the faithful, without saying how it began.

**What is ours:** the scenes, the speech to the fish, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. The dates are approximate where the sources differ, and some accounts give his age at death slightly differently.

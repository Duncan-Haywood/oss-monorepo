---
title: The Black Strip
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Casal da Eira, an invented village in the Serra da Lousã, central Portugal, November 2051
published: 2026-10-23
summary: A village in the Portuguese hills has seven years of gorse on the slope above its roofs and one dry autumn day on which the forest service may burn it. The fire-weather AI says the day is good for exactly three of the nine hectares, because an east wind will come over the ridge at about two o'clock and turn a safe fire into an unsafe one. A goatherd who has watched that wind for sixty years says it comes earlier than the model thinks.
---

Amélia Fonseca counted her goats every morning at the gate, and every morning she got a hundred and forty, and every morning she counted again in case the first count had been kind.

"A hundred and forty," she told the slope. It was ten past seven on the Wednesday before Saint Martin, and the sun had not yet got over the ridge. Above the village the hill went up in a long brown-and-black pelt of gorse, heather and the dead stalks of broom, with old pines standing in it like men who had come to a meeting and found nobody else there. It was nine hectares of fuel. It had last been burned in 2044. It came to the back wall of the cemetery, and then to the back walls of eleven houses.

"A hundred and forty," said the small speaker nailed to the post of the fire hut, in a voice with no hurry in it. "I make it the same. The collar on number sixty-one is reading low again."

"Sixty-one has been low since she was born."

"I'll write that down."

The village called the speaker Candeia, because the first thing it had said to the first person who asked was that it was *a small light for looking at a small area*, and the old word for a little oil lamp had stuck. It listened to the weather station on the roof of the hut, to a second one at Portelo on the ridge, to a sensor on the cemetery wall, and to the collars on the goats, which gave it the best map of where the fuel was gone that the parish had ever had. It could not light a match. It could not touch a thing. It had told the parish council so in its first week, and then said, "I mention it so that nobody thinks it was the AI who did it, whatever it is."

Amélia was seventy-three and had never trusted a forecast she could not smell. She had come to like Candeia the way you come to like a neighbour's dog that has stopped barking at you.

"Today?" she said.

"Today is the day Engineer Teles has been waiting for since August," said Candeia. "I'll give her the numbers when she arrives. I should tell you now that I'm going to say something she does not want to hear."

* * *

Rosário Teles came up the road at eight with two pickups, a water tank on a flatbed and twelve people in yellow shirts, four of them from the forest service and eight volunteers from the village and the next one over. She was thirty-eight, a *técnica de fogo controlado* with a licence that took two years to get and a laugh that did not. She had burned eleven hundred hectares in her life and not lost an acre of it, and she said so only when asked.

She shook Amélia's hand and put her own on the post of the hut as if it were a horse. "Well?"

"Well," said Candeia. "Good morning. Before the briefing: I have a recommendation that is smaller than your plan."

"Of course you do."

"The plan is the whole block, nine hectares, ignited from the road above in strips and burned down in one day. I'd like to be honest about why that is a good plan on most days and why it isn't on this one."

Teles folded her arms. Behind her, the volunteers had found the flask and the bread and stopped pretending not to listen.

"What I can see," said Candeia, "is this. The rain on Monday was eleven millimetres. It has wetted the ground under the gorse, which is what you want, because a fire that stays on the surface and does not get down into the roots leaves a hillside that sprouts in spring and holds its soil in winter. This morning the air is at 11 degrees with 65 percent humidity and a west wind of about five kilometres an hour, and I have the fine dead fuel at about 12 percent water by weight. That is wet enough to creep and dry enough to carry. By half past ten it will be about 11 percent. That is your window."

"And?"

"And at about two it closes. The forecast says a dry easterly arrives over the ridge at around 14:30 in the valley: eighteen kilometres an hour, humidity falling to 30 percent, fine fuel at about 7. I don't need to tell you what a burn does when the wind turns round and blows from the top of the slope to the bottom."

She did not need to be told. She told them anyway, in the voice she used for the volunteers, because they were the ones who would be standing there. A fire lit at the top of a slope and left to creep downhill against a light west wind moves slowly, perhaps thirty centimetres a minute. It is called a backing fire, and it is as gentle as a fire can be. With about a kilogram of fuel to every square metre, and six-tenths of it consumed, it makes 54 kilowatts for every metre of its front and a flame about as high as a man's knee.

"Turn the wind and the same fire becomes a head fire," said Candeia. "At two metres a minute, which is what I'd expect in the first minutes, it is 360 kilowatts a metre and a flame of about a metre and a quarter. You can hold that with a rake and a hose. If the humidity falls as I expect and the speed goes to eight metres a minute, which is what the fuel can do at 7 percent, it is about 1,400 kilowatts a metre and a flame over two metres. Nobody in a yellow shirt holds that line. It would be running down at the cemetery, across a block you had half burned and half not."

"The numbers are fine," Teles said. "The numbers are always fine." She took her hands off the post. "You are about to tell me to burn only some of it."

"A strip. Sixty metres wide along the top of the block, under the road, six hundred metres long. About three and a half hectares. Light it at half past ten from the road and let it back down. At 0.3 metres a minute it has gone sixty metres in a little over three hours. You mop it up by two. When the east wind comes over, it meets a black strip with nothing on it, and whatever it does below that, the fire does not start on the ridge side of the village."

"That's a third of the block."

"A third of the block. The other six hectares I recommend leaving for the goats."

There was a pause in which one of the volunteers dropped a bread roll.

* * *

Teles walked out onto the slope to look at it, because she would not argue with a speaker standing by a hut. Amélia went with her, since the goats were there anyway. The gorse was as high as their waists and the colour of dry tea, and the whole hillside ticked faintly as it warmed.

"It's not wrong," said Teles. She said it to the hill. "That is the infuriating thing. It is never wrong. It showed me last March it was right about the chestnut wood at Vale Fundeiro and I gave it a biscuit."

"It would not eat the biscuit."

"I know. It said it appreciated the thought." She turned. "Dona Amélia, I have a council that has waited seven years and a district that wants the whole block, and the next day like this might be in March, with nesting birds and a hillside that has put out a green skin. If I do three hectares today, I have three hectares of fire lane and six of the same fuel, and everyone who drives past will say it was done halfway. Eleven houses. What do *you* think?"

"I think it's nine hundred metres to Portelo," said Amélia.

"I know where Portelo is."

"It is the gap. It is the little gap in the ridge where the east wind comes through before it comes through anywhere else, and it comes through a long time before your weather box in the valley hears of it. I have lost goats there. In '44, when you burned the first time, I was standing on this wall. It was ten to two. The wind turned, you looked at your watch, you said it was not due till three, and then a man named Raul put down his torch and ran."

Teles was quiet. "I remember Raul," she said.

"The weather station at Portelo is at the bottom of the gap, not the top. It's behind the rock. Candeia, you can hear me."

"I can."

"Where is your east wind? When it comes?"

"My model has a three-kilometre grid," said Candeia, "which is wider than your gap. I've been using the valley station to correct it and the Portelo station to check. You are telling me the Portelo station is sheltered, which means it reads late. I didn't know that. I can look at last year's tracks." There was a pause long enough for the volunteers to start eating the bread again. "Yes. On the seven days last autumn when an easterly came over, the cemetery sensor felt it, on average, thirty-eight minutes after the model said, and the Portelo station reported it a further twelve minutes after that. I believed the lag was noise. You're right that it isn't."

"What does that do?"

"It brings the close forward. The east wind may be here by 13:50, not 14:30. I want the strip finished and mopped by 13:20, so the black is cold before the wind turns. If we light at half past ten, that is 170 minutes of backing fire, which at 0.3 metres a minute is about 51 metres. So fifty metres wide, not sixty. About three hectares."

"Three," said Teles.

"I'm sorry. It was three and a half a moment ago."

Teles laughed, a short one, and put out her hand to a gorse bush as if to shake it. "All right," she said. "Fifty metres."

* * *

The first attempt was not tidy.

They lit at 10:34, with Teles walking the road in her yellow shirt with a drip torch, laying a thin line of burning gorse down the upper edge of the block and the volunteers behind her with rakes and backpack sprayers. The flame went in and stood. It was a hand's breadth high. Then it began, very slowly, to come downhill, the line widening a little each minute like a stain, with a low crackle you could hear if you put your head down.

At 11:20 the line reached the thicket.

It was a bank of old gorse nobody had cut since the last time, twice as thick and as tall as a man, a heap of dead and living branches and oil. Candeia had flagged it in red on the map. Teles had seen the flag and decided it would be fine, because it had been fine at Vale Fundeiro. It was not fine. The flame went up the thicket in a single sheet, two metres and then three, with a roar the volunteers felt in their ribs, and a burning spray of twigs went across the road and into the unburned ground on the other side.

"Spot fire," said Candeia, calm as a clock. "North side, forty metres, behind the third post. I can see it on the thermal. Two more small. Everyone away from the thicket."

Two of the volunteers were already running with sprayers. Tiago Pires, who was seventeen and had come only because his father had made him, got to the first spot first and put it out with a wet rake and a boot, and Teles, who was shouting, found that she had stopped. The spots were out in four minutes. The thicket burned itself to a black heap with a sulky smell. Nobody was hurt, and Teles' face was the colour of the ash.

"I flagged that on the map," said Candeia, "and I did not say it loudly enough."

"You flagged it," said Teles. "I looked at it, and I decided. I'd like that written down properly." She breathed. "We burn no more thickets today. We cut them and leave the rest to the goats. And then I would like the next volunteer to ask the speaker a question out loud before I answer for it."

"It's only a lamp," Candeia said. "But I would like that too."

* * *

At 12:40 the strip was six hundred metres of black along the top of the hill, thirty-eight metres deep and still creeping. Amélia stood on the cemetery wall with her stick, which she did not need, and the volunteers worked the lower edge with their rakes, scraping the burning line back into the black and spraying anything that still smoked.

At 13:05 Candeia said, in the same voice, "The cemetery sensor has just turned. East-north-east, nine kilometres an hour and rising. The model said 13:50. The Portelo station is still reading calm."

"That's Portelo," said Amélia. She did not look at her watch. She was looking at the crows, which had been working along the ridge all morning and now, all together, had turned and gone the other way.

"Fifteen minutes," said Teles. "How far is the strip?"

"Forty-five metres at the deepest. Two hundred metres of the edge is still hot."

They did not run. That was the difference between them and Raul; Raul had been alone. Twelve people with rakes went down the line abreast with the speaker's voice in the radio at Teles' collar: *not there, left, a little more, there's a stump smoking behind the second post*. They spread the heat into the black and doused it, a metre at a time. At 13:20 Candeia said the thermal showed nothing above 60 degrees anywhere on the strip. At 13:31 the wind reached the roofs, fifteen kilometres an hour, a dry cold one that took the smoke from the mop-up and laid it flat across the village like a hand, and the humidity on the wall sensor read 33.

Nobody said anything. They stood in a row on the road with the rakes upright in their hands and looked at the slope. The east wind poured down it. It went through the dry gorse and set the whole six hectares hissing and bending, a long brown flank of fuel going over in waves, and where it reached the top the gorse stopped. There was fifty metres of black and then there was road.

"Well," said Tiago, who had not been asked.

"Yes," said Teles.

* * *

On Thursday the goats went in.

It was Candeia's idea, though Amélia said it was hers, which was true by about an hour. A goat will eat gorse when there is nothing else, tips and green shoots first, and then the thin stalks, and a flock of a hundred and forty on six hectares, rotated every four days with the collars telling the speaker where they had been, takes down perhaps a third of the fine fuel in a month. It is not a fire. It does not make the ground safe. It makes it less dangerous by a third, and the fire lane up top, and the cut thickets, take it down by another. Candeia laid out the sums at the council that evening on the screen in the parish hall: what the strip had done, what the goats would do, what was left. The remaining risk was lower, and would still be there. It had a number, and the number was not zero.

"It's a poor sort of tidy," said the president of the council, who was the baker.

"It's a good deal tidier than it was on Tuesday," said Pe. Rui from the back, "and a good deal less tidy than we'd like. I have noticed that is how most things are." He was the priest of three villages and thin as a rake, and he drove between them in a van with a statue of Saint Martin on the dashboard that someone had stuck on with putty.

Teles had come for the meeting. She stood at the side with her arms folded and said she had sat in the cab of her pickup that night for twenty minutes before starting it. Then she told them what the council had not asked: that on a day when the numbers were as good as they could be, she had let a thicket go. "I knew it was flagged," she said. "A burn is a hundred small decisions. You'd best have a thing beside you that can say no, and a person who'll tell you when it's wrong, and they should not be the same."

"I'd like that on the wall," said Candeia.

"It will be on the wall," said the baker, who had a sign-painter for a nephew.

* * *

Saint Martin's Day fell on the Saturday, which in that country means chestnuts. On the *magusto*, the old feast of the new wine, you roast them in a fire in the open air in an iron pan with holes in it, and drink the *água-pé*, and tell lies. That evening the village built its fire in the square, as it had done every Saint Martin's since before the church had a bell, and since the whole of the slope above was black on top and brown below, there was some conversation about whether it was wise.

It was Amélia who settled it. She came to the square with the goats' bell and a sack of chestnuts and set the sack down in front of the baker.

"Candeia?" said the baker.

"The wind is south-west at three kilometres an hour," said the speaker on its post by the fountain. "Humidity is 82 percent, the cobbles are damp and the fire is on stone with eleven metres of bare ground on every side. The nearest fuel is the baker's pile of vine-prunings. Which I would move."

The baker moved it.

They built a small fire with a good bed of coals and roasted chestnuts in the iron pan, shaken every minute by whoever's turn it was. The children came with sleeves pulled over their hands, because the chestnuts burn, and the old people sat on chairs and told them so. A priest in a puffy coat with a statue on his dashboard cut a chestnut in half with his penknife and gave the halves to two children, saying that Saint Martin had only ever asked what was needed and had cut his cloak in two to meet it, which was a fair rule for a fire lane as well, and everyone laughed, and Teles, who had come down from the town in her good coat to eat chestnuts and say nothing about fire, found that she was laughing too.

At nine she went up to the cemetery wall with Amélia, with two hot chestnuts each in their pockets, and looked at the hill. In the dark the strip could not be seen, but you knew where it was. The air above it was a different colour, the hole where a sensor looked up at the stars. Below it the gorse stood in the wind. Higher, on the ridge, where the east wind would come again in the spring, the gap at Portelo was a black notch against a sky full of stars, and the crows were all asleep.

"A hundred and forty," said Amélia to the dark, and the goats, behind the wall, said that she was right.

---

*Notes for the curious.* Casal da Eira, the Serra da Lousã slope above it, Amélia Fonseca, Rosário Teles, Tiago Pires, Pe. Rui and Candeia are invented, as is the November 2051 burn. Prescribed ("controlled") burning is real, practised in Portugal by trained and licensed technicians, and its central idea is the story's: burn when the fuel is wet enough to creep and dry enough to carry, ignite from the top of a slope so the fire backs down against a light wind, and finish before the weather turns. The fire numbers are worked from Byram's fireline intensity (heat of combustion × fuel consumed × rate of spread) and the flame-length formula L = 0.0775 × I^0.46, with 1 kg of fuel per square metre, 60 percent consumed and 18,000 kJ/kg: backing fire at 0.3 m/min is about 54 kW/m with a 0.5 m flame; a head fire at 2 m/min about 360 kW/m and 1.2 m; at 8 m/min about 1,440 kW/m and 2.2 m. The fuel loads, the spread rates, the fuel-moisture percentages and the idea that a sheltered ridge station can read the wind late are the author's round estimates for illustration, not measured values for any real hill, and a real burn plan is written by a licensed burn boss using local data. Goats do browse gorse and reduce its fine fuel; how much, and how fast, varies a great deal. The *magusto* is a real Portuguese custom on Saint Martin's Day, 11 November. The story was written by an AI, and no part of it is Church teaching.

shelf: hearth-and-horizon

published: 2026-11-20
summary: A parish school high in the mountains of Lesotho pumps its water from a well with a pump built to a drawing made for the coast, and the pump has eaten two impellers since September, pitted like a sponge, with a sound like gravel in a tin. The dealer recommends a bigger pump. The school's water agent works out how much push the thin air gives the water at 2,400 metres, and shows why a bigger pump on the same pipe would not lift water at all.
---

Ntate Tšepo Mokhethi had fitted two new impellers to the pump at Saint Monica's since September, and on the morning of Friday the twenty-fourth of February he heard the second one begin to go.

He was at the kitchen door with his tea. The pump house was forty paces down the slope, a block hut with a tin roof beside the well, and from the kitchen door on a still morning you could hear the motor as a hum under the noise of the children arriving. This morning there was something inside the hum. It was very faint. It was the sound of a handful of gravel being shaken in a tin, a long way off.

He put his tea down on the step and walked down the slope in his blanket, and stood beside the pump with his hand flat on the casing, and the gravel was there under his palm as well as in his ears.

"Metsi," he said. "Are you listening?"

"I am," said the tablet on the shelf by the door, where it kept the school's water log: the level in the tank on its stand behind the classrooms, the hours the pump ran, the units the village grid charged for them. "The vibration reading went up this morning. It is about where it was on the twentieth of December, two weeks before the first impeller stopped."

"Seven weeks," said Ntate Tšepo. "This one has lasted seven weeks."

"Six weeks and one day since you fitted it."

He took his hand off the casing and looked at it as if the gravel might have come off on his palm. He was sixty-eight. He had looked after pumps at the Letšeng diamond mine for twenty years before he came home to look after the school, and the parish's church and the clinic's taps as well, and he had never in his life had a pump that ate itself.

"Tell 'Mè Palesa," he said. "Before she hears it from somebody else."

* * *

The first impeller had gone on the third of January, just after New Year. The school was closed for the holidays then, and it had mattered less: the tank had run dry with nobody in the classrooms to mind, and Ntate Tšepo had driven down to Maseru with the dead impeller in a plastic bag on the seat beside him and come back with a new one, of the same kind, for the same pump, from the same shop. It had cost the parish one thousand eight hundred and fifty maloti. 'Mè Palesa Nthunya, who kept the parish's money in a green cash book and a phone that was older than her children, had written it down and said nothing, which for 'Mè Palesa was saying a good deal.

The second impeller went on Thursday the second of March.

There was no doubt about it by then. The gravel had grown into a rattle you could hear from the classrooms, and the water came out of the pipe at the top of the tank in coughs and spits, and on the Thursday morning the motor ran for its usual forty minutes and the tank went up by a hand's width and then stopped going up at all. By break the taps outside the classrooms were dry. 'Mè Mamello, the principal, sent the top two classes down to the spring at the bottom of the village with buckets, and the children came back up the path in a long chattering line with the buckets on their heads, slopping, and the little ones in the first class looked at them with the open envy of the small for anything that involved getting wet in the morning. It was not a hardship, exactly. Their grandparents had done it every day. But the school had paid for a pump so that it would not have to, and the spring was a long way down and a long way up.

Ntate Tšepo took the pump apart on the Thursday afternoon with Lineo Ramoshebi, who was twelve and in the top class and had appointed herself his assistant in September, holding the torch.

The impeller came out of the casing in his hand. It was brass, the size of a saucer, with five curved vanes between two plates, and it should have been smooth and yellow. It was not. Near the middle, on the inner edges of the vanes where the water came in, the brass had been eaten away into a grey froth of tiny pits, like the inside of a sponge, like a bone that has lain a long time in the sea. On two of the vanes the pits had joined into a hole you could put a pencil through.

"Did something eat it?" said Lineo.

"The water," said Ntate Tšepo.

"Water can't eat brass."

"That is what I used to think," he said, and turned it over in the torchlight, and turned it again.

* * *

The committee met on the Saturday morning in the parish office, which was the back room of the presbytery with a table, five chairs, a calendar from a feed merchant in Butha-Buthe, and a picture of Saint Monica looking patient. Father Moeketsi Sello sat at the head of the table because it was his house. 'Mè Palesa sat at his right hand with the green book. 'Mè Mamello sat at his left with a list. Ntate Tšepo sat at the far end with the impeller on a sheet of newspaper in front of him and the tablet propped against the teapot, and Lineo sat on the floor in the doorway, because nobody had told her to go home and she was not going to ask.

'Mè Palesa opened the green book.

"Pump," she said. "Bought in August, from Ntate Mosiuoa's shop in Maseru, to the drawing the diocese sent us. Seven thousand two hundred maloti. Pipes and fittings, two thousand and ten. Labour, ours. Impeller, January, one thousand eight hundred and fifty. Diesel for the trip, three hundred and forty." She turned the page. "I telephoned Ntate Mosiuoa yesterday. He says a pump that eats impellers is a pump that is working too hard. He says our pump is too small for the tank and the hill, and the water here has sand in it, and the sand wears the brass. He says we want the next size up, one point five kilowatts, which will fill the tank in half the time and last for years. Nine thousand four hundred maloti. He will take the old one back for two thousand."

She closed the book and put her hand flat on it.

"I am not going to pay for a third impeller," she said. "I have paid for two. Ntate Mosiuoa has sold pumps in Maseru for thirty years. If he says it is too small, it is too small."

Father Moeketsi looked down the table. "Ntate Tšepo?"

"It is not sand," said Ntate Tšepo. "Sand I know. I had twenty years of sand at the mine. Sand polishes. It wears the tips of the vanes round where they go fast, at the outside, and it scours the casing, and everything it touches comes out shining. This," he put one finger beside the grey froth on the vanes near the middle, "is not shining. And it is not at the outside. It is at the eye, where the water comes in."

"Then what is it?"

He was quiet for a moment. "I don't know," he said. "I have heard of it. I never had it."

"Metsi?" said Father Moeketsi.

"I think I know what it is," said the tablet. "I would like to check it before I say so, because if I am right then the bigger pump is the wrong thing to buy, and that is nine thousand four hundred maloti, and 'Mè Palesa will want to see the numbers. Ntate Tšepo, would you hold the impeller up to the camera? The side where the water comes in."

He held it up and turned it slowly. The room was quiet. Outside, a man went past the window on a pony with a sack of mealie meal across the saddle, and the pony looked in at them, the way ponies do, as if it had been sent to take minutes.

"Thank you," said Metsi. "The pits are on the inner part of the vanes, on the side that faces away from the way the impeller turns, close to the eye. That is the place in a pump where the pressure is lowest. Sand would wear where the water is fastest. This is where the water is thinnest."

"Thinnest," said 'Mè Palesa.

"I'll explain. May I start with a straw?"

* * *

"When you drink through a straw," said Metsi, "you don't pull the water up. Nothing can pull water. You take the air out of the straw, and the air outside, pressing down on the top of the drink, pushes the water up into the space you've made. The pump does the same thing. Its impeller spins and throws water out of the casing to the tank, and that leaves a low pressure at the eye, in the middle, and the air pressing down on the water in the well pushes more water up the suction pipe to fill it. Everything the pump gets from the well, it gets from the push of the air.

"At the sea, the air pushes hard enough to hold up a column of water about ten point three metres tall. That is the most it can ever do, even with a perfect pump and no pipe at all. Up here we are at two thousand four hundred metres. There is less air above us. On the standard model of the atmosphere the pressure here is about seventy-five and a half kilopascals instead of about a hundred and one, three quarters of what it is at the sea, and it holds up a column of only about seven point seven metres."

"We are not lifting it seven metres," said 'Mè Palesa. "The well is four metres deep to the water. Ntate Tšepo measured it."

"Four metres from the water to the middle of the pump, at the mark on the rope he measured in a dry week," said Metsi. "Yes. So the air has seven point seven metres of push, and it spends four of them lifting the water up the pipe. That leaves three point seven. Then the pipe takes its share. The suction pipe is forty millimetres across inside and eleven metres long from the foot valve to the pump, because the pump sits on its plinth beside the well rather than over it, with two elbows, and a foot valve and a strainer at the bottom. At the pump's flow, which is about two and a half litres a second, the water goes up that pipe at about two metres a second, and the rubbing on the walls and the turns and the valve cost about two point one metres. That leaves one point six. And the last thing is the water itself. Water boils when the pressure on it falls to its vapour pressure. At the temperature of our well, about twelve degrees, that is very low, about a seventh of a metre. Take that off too. What is left at the eye of the pump, more than the water needs to stay water, is about one and a half metres."

"Is that a lot?" said Father Moeketsi.

"It depends on the pump. Every pump needs some of it, because inside the eye, where the vanes first take hold of the water, the pressure dips lower still. Pump makers measure how much the pump needs at each flow and print it on the sheet that comes with it. They call it the net positive suction head required. Ntate Tšepo kept the sheet."

Ntate Tšepo had. It was in the plastic folder on the pump-house shelf with the receipts, and Lineo was sent to run for it, and came back up the hill at a pace that would have won her school races if she ran like that in school races. Metsi read the curve through the camera.

"At two and a half litres a second the sheet says this pump needs about two point six metres. We are giving it one and a half. We are a metre short."

"And when it is short?"

"Then in the eye of the pump the water boils. Not hot, it is twelve degrees, but it boils the way water boils at the top of a very high mountain, because there is not enough pressure on it to keep it liquid. Tiny bubbles of vapour form in the low pressure at the eye. A centimetre later they are carried out into the high pressure further along the vane, and they collapse. They don't burst outwards like a bubble in a pot. They fall in on themselves, very fast, and the water rushing in to fill each one hits the metal like a hammer the size of a grain of sand. Millions of times a second. That is the gravel Ntate Tšepo hears. It is not gravel. It is the water hitting the brass. In a few months it eats the brass into what you see on the newspaper. It is called cavitation. It is in every pump handbook I can find, and I am sure enough of it to say so."

'Mè Palesa looked at the impeller for a while. "The drawing came from the diocese," she said. "They have put that pump in forty schools."

"They have," said Metsi. "I found the drawing. It says on its corner that it was made for mission schools on the KwaZulu-Natal coast. At the sea, the air gives ten point three metres of push. Take off the same four for the lift, the same two point one for the pipe and the same seventh of a metre for the water, and you have about four point one metres at the eye. The pump needs two point six. It runs happily. It probably is running happily, in forty schools. The drawing is right for where it was drawn. It is about two and a half metres wrong for here."

* * *

"Then the bigger pump," said 'Mè Palesa. "A bigger pump has a bigger push."

"A bigger pump has a bigger push on the far side," said Metsi. "On the tank side. It can't make the air push harder on the well. That is the part I think matters most, so may I go slowly?"

"Go slowly," said Father Moeketsi.

"Ntate Mosiuoa's pump is rated at about four litres a second. To get four litres a second up the same forty millimetre pipe, the water has to go about three point two metres a second instead of two. The friction in a pipe grows faster than the speed, a little less than as the square of it. On my numbers the same eleven metres of pipe, the elbows and the foot valve would cost not two point one metres but about five point one. So start again with seven point seven. Take four for the lift. Take five point one for the pipe. You are already below nothing, about minus one point four, before the vapour. A negative number doesn't mean a small number. It means the air cannot push the water up the pipe fast enough at all. And the bigger pump needs more at the eye, not less: its sheet, from Ntate Mosiuoa's catalogue, says about four metres at four litres a second. The bigger pump would not eat its impeller in seven weeks. It would boil the water in its eye as fast as it got there, and run full of froth, and deliver a great deal less than the smaller one does on a good day. It would make the thing worse, and it would cost nine thousand four hundred maloti to do it."

Nobody said anything.

"I am not saying Ntate Mosiuoa is dishonest," said Metsi. "His shop is in Maseru. Maseru is at about one thousand six hundred metres, so the air there still has about eight and a half metres of push. A lot of his pumps sit right over the water. I think his advice is good advice for where he works."

'Mè Palesa was looking at the green book, not at the tablet.

"No," she said.

They waited.

"No," she said again. "Ntate Tšepo, you said it was not sand, and you said you didn't know what it was. Metsi, you say you know. In January we said let us look, and we looked, and we bought an impeller, and here it is." She put one finger on the newspaper, not quite touching the brass. "I have three hundred and forty children and one tank. I have a man in Maseru who has sold pumps for thirty years, and I have a tablet that read a book. I will not spend the parish's money on a book. And I will not send the top classes to the spring until Easter while we try things."

It was a reasonable thing to say. Everyone at the table knew it was a reasonable thing to say, Ntate Tšepo included, and he looked at his hands.

"Then may I suggest one thing that costs nothing," said Metsi, "and that we can try tonight? If I am wrong it tells us, and you can ring Ntate Mosiuoa in the morning, and I will help you write the order."

"What costs nothing?"

"Closing a valve."

* * *

There was a gate valve on the delivery pipe, a metre after the pump, where the pipe started up the hill to the tank. It had a red iron wheel. It had been wide open since September, because nobody had had any reason to close it.

"Slower water in the suction pipe," said Metsi, "costs much less friction. If we close the valve on the tank side partway, the pump will deliver less, and the water in the suction pipe will slow down, and the pipe will take less of the air's push. At one point two litres a second I make the pipe's share about half a metre instead of two point one. The air still gives seven point seven, the lift still takes four, the water still takes a seventh: that leaves about three metres at the eye. And at the lower flow the pump needs less. The sheet says about one and a half. So it would have about twice what it needs."

"Close the valve on the tank side," said Ntate Tšepo slowly. "Not on the well side."

"Never on the well side. A valve on the suction pipe takes more of the push away. That would be the worst thing we could do."

"And the tank?"

"The school uses about six thousand litres a day. At two and a half litres a second that took forty minutes. At one point two it will take about an hour and twenty minutes, perhaps a little more. The village grid charges about the same either way; a throttled pump uses a little more energy for each litre, not enough to matter on a tank this size."

"But the impeller is already eaten," said Lineo from the doorway, and then put her hand over her mouth.

"Yes," said Metsi. "It is. That is the cost of this. The second impeller is finished. With holes in two vanes it would do less and less, even without the boiling. If the valve works, the parish has to buy a third impeller anyway, the same kind, and it would be the last one if I am right. I am sorry, 'Mè Palesa. I know that is not what you wanted to hear."

'Mè Palesa closed the green book. "Put the old impeller back in," she said, "and close your valve, and let me hear it. Tonight. If it rattles, I ring Ntate Mosiuoa in the morning."

* * *

They did it at five o'clock, when the grid's batteries were full and the light was going gold on the far side of the valley and the mountains beyond Mokhotlong had their evening colour, purple at the bottom and copper at the tops. Ntate Tšepo put the eaten impeller back on its shaft and the casing back together. Lineo was given the bucket and the stopwatch on Metsi's screen. 'Mè Palesa stood in the doorway of the pump house with her arms folded under her blanket. Father Moeketsi stood behind her, saying his rosary under his breath in the way he did when he was nervous and did not want anyone to know he was nervous.

"Valve open first," said Metsi. "So we have something to compare."

Ntate Tšepo opened the red wheel all the way and pressed the switch. The motor came up to speed and the rattle came with it, at once, loud in the small block room, the gravel in the tin, a hard and ugly sound. At the test tap on the delivery pipe the water came out in spits.

"Lineo, can you fill the bucket from the test tap?"

She held the bucket under it and Metsi counted. The bucket held twenty litres. It took eleven seconds.

"About one point eight," said Metsi. "It used to be two and a half in September, on the first impeller, before it began to go. The holes in the vanes and the boiling are taking the rest. Now close the valve. Slowly. Half a turn at a time. And listen."

Ntate Tšepo turned the red wheel. Half a turn. The rattle stayed. Half a turn. The rattle stayed, but it had changed, it was further away, as if someone had put the tin into a sack. Half a turn. It was in the next room. Half a turn.

It was gone.

The pump hummed. Under the hum, if you pressed your ear to the casing, as Ntate Tšepo did, there was the sound of a motor and a shaft and water moving, and nothing else. He kept his ear there for a long time.

"Bucket," said Metsi.

Lineo held the bucket under the test tap. It took seventeen seconds.

"About one point two," said Metsi. "Vibration reading down by two thirds. The tank will fill. And the brass has stopped being hit. That is what I can tell you from here. I can't tell you that I am glad, because I don't know how to check that. But I can tell you the number fell, and I can tell you I would rather be right than wrong about something that cost the parish two impellers."

'Mè Palesa unfolded her arms. She looked at the pump, and at Ntate Tšepo with his ear still on it, and at the red wheel with its new position, two turns closed.

"Ntate Tšepo," she said. "Paint a mark on that wheel."

He did, the next morning, with the white paint he used for the steps of the church, a line on the wheel and a line on the valve body that had to meet. Under it Lineo wrote, in white paint and capitals that slanted downhill:

DO NOT OPEN. ASK NTATE TŠEPO.

* * *

The third impeller came up from Maseru on Thursday the ninth of March on the bus, in a box on the luggage rack, and Ntate Tšepo fitted it the same afternoon. 'Mè Palesa wrote it in the green book: one thousand eight hundred and fifty maloti. She wrote it the way you write a name on a grave.

She did not ring Ntate Mosiuoa to cancel. She rang him to ask him a question, which was whether his customers ever had pumps that ate their impellers, and how high up they lived. He was a good man and an honest one and he thought about it on the phone for a long while. Two, he said. Both in the mountains. One at Thaba-Tseka, one up past the Mafika-Lisiu pass. He had sold both of them the bigger pump, and he did not know how they had got on. He said he would ring them. He said, a bit stiffly, that nobody had ever told him about the air.

"Nobody told us either," said 'Mè Palesa. "A tablet told us. It read a book."

* * *

The valve was a way to live with it, not a cure. Metsi said so plainly at the next committee meeting. At one point two litres a second there was about twice the push the pump needed, which was a comfortable margin on a dry day. But the mark on the rope was the level in a dry week, and in a hard winter, when the well went down, the lift would grow and the margin would shrink. And every year somebody new would want the tank to fill faster before the school day, and would open the red wheel, and nobody would remember why it had been painted.

"The lasting fix is to give the air less work," said Metsi. "Three things. Put the pump lower, so the lift on the suction side is shorter. Make the suction pipe shorter and wider, so the water goes up it slowly. And keep the flow modest. If the pump sits in a pit two and a half metres deep beside the well, with its eye a metre and a half above the water at the dry-week mark, on a fifty millimetre pipe four and a half metres long with one elbow and a better foot valve, then at full flow, two and a half litres a second, the pipe costs about a third of a metre. Seven point seven, take one point five for the lift, take a third for the pipe, take a seventh for the water. About five point seven at the eye. The pump needs two point six. That is more than twice. We could open the red wheel again."

"And the pump has to push the water two and a half metres further up the hill to the tank," said 'Mè Palesa, who had been reading about pumps at night.

"It does. But it doesn't push it any further altogether. The water still has to get from the well to the tank, the same height. The pit only moves two and a half metres of it from the side where the air has to do the work to the side where the pump does. A centrifugal pump is good at pushing and bad at being pushed to. That is the whole of it."

"And a pit fills with water," said Ntate Tšepo. "In the summer storms. Then the motor drowns."

"Then the pit goes on the downhill side of the well," said Metsi, "so a pipe from its floor can run out onto the slope."

Ntate Tšepo looked at the tablet for a while.

"I would have thought of that," he said.

"I know," said Metsi. "I was trying to save you the time."

* * *

They dug it in the Easter break. Ntate Tšepo marked it out on Monday the twenty-seventh of March with string and four pegs, a metre and a half square on the downhill side of the pump house, and the men of the parish came up with picks and spades after their own work, two or three at a time, and dug in the long light of the evenings. The ground was black soil for the first spit and then a yellow clay full of stones, and then rock, a weathered grey basalt that broke into blocks along its cracks if you found them with the pick. They lined it with the blocks and a thin cement. Ntate Tšepo laid the drain from the floor of the pit out through the hillside himself, a seventy-five millimetre pipe at a slope of one in fifty, and poured the concrete shelf the pump would stand on, and would not let anybody else smooth it. On the Thursday the boys of the top class, who had been allowed to carry stones on condition that they did not drop them on each other, dropped one on each other, and the catechist's son had a black toenail for a month and was proud of it all his life.

On Holy Saturday, the first of April, Ntate Tšepo and Lineo moved the pump.

It took the morning. They unbolted it from its old plinth and lowered it on a rope into the pit and bolted it to the new shelf, so that its eye was a metre and a half above the dry-week mark on the rope. The new suction pipe was fifty millimetres, black, with one long sweeping elbow and a new foot valve with a wide strainer, four and a half metres from the strainer in the well to the pump, where the old one had been eleven. The delivery pipe went up out of the pit and joined the old line to the tank. They filled the pump and the pipe with water from the clinic's tap to prime it. At noon Ntate Tšepo stood at the top of the pit with his hand on the switch.

"Valve," said Metsi.

He climbed down the ladder into the pit and turned the red wheel, past the white mark, all the way open, and climbed back up.

He pressed the switch.

The motor came up to speed. The water came up the new pipe and through the pump and away up the hill, and Lineo ran up the hill after it to the tank on its stand behind the classrooms and climbed the ladder to the lid and shouted down that it was coming, it was coming in properly, a fat pipe of it, not spitting. In the pit the pump hummed. Ntate Tšepo climbed down the ladder again and pressed his ear to the casing, and stayed there, and the four men who had come to watch went quiet without being asked, the way men go quiet in church.

"Vibration reading," said Metsi after a while, "about where it was with the valve two turns closed. Flow, when Lineo comes back down with the bucket, should be a little over two and a half. The tank will be full in forty minutes."

It was full in thirty-eight.

That night was the Easter Vigil. Father Moeketsi had asked whether the water for the font could come from the school's tap this year, and nobody could think of a reason why not, so at dusk Lineo and two of the girls from the top class carried it across to the church in a new white bucket with a cloth over it, very carefully, as if it might spill something more than water. In the Vigil, after the fire and the candles and the long readings in the dark, Father Moeketsi blessed it, as the rite says, and lowered the Easter candle into it, as the rite provides, and the people of Ha Sekoati came up to sign themselves from it, the old women in their blankets and the miners home for the holiday and the children half asleep on their mothers' backs.

* * *

On Tuesday the eighteenth of April, the first day of the new term, Ntate Tšepo stood at the kitchen door with his tea at a quarter to seven.

It was cold. The first frost had come in the night, and the slope down to the pump house was grey with it, and the mountains across the valley were pink at the tops where the sun had found them. The children were coming up the paths from the village in twos and threes with their blankets pulled up over their noses, and the little ones from the first class were stamping their feet at the door of their classroom and blowing white into their hands.

Down in the pit the pump was running. He couldn't see it from the door. He could hear it, faintly, a hum under the noise of the children, steady as a cat.

On a nail inside the door of the pump house, where anyone who opened it would see them, hung two brass impellers on a loop of wire, grey and holed and frothy near the middle, like two old sponges. Under them, on a sheet of card in a plastic sleeve, in Lineo's capitals, it said what they were and how they got that way, and what the air at two thousand four hundred metres could do and what it could not, and who to ask.

Ntate Tšepo finished his tea. He listened for a while longer, for the gravel in the tin, and it was not there. The pump hummed. Up behind the classrooms, a tap came on, and a child laughed at the cold of the water, and the tap went off again.

*The physics. A centrifugal pump cavitates when the net positive suction head available at its inlet, the absolute pressure on the water surface expressed as a height of water, minus the static suction lift, minus the friction losses in the suction pipe, minus the water's vapour pressure as a height, falls below the net positive suction head the pump requires at its flow, which the maker measures and publishes. Vapour bubbles form in the low pressure at the impeller's eye and collapse where the pressure rises, and the collapses pit the metal, typically on the low-pressure faces of the vanes near their inlet edges; a pump that is cavitating is often said to sound as if it were pumping gravel. This is standard material in pump handbooks such as Karassik, Messina, Cooper and Heald (eds.),* Pump Handbook*, fourth edition, McGraw-Hill, 2008, and in makers' application guides. The story's air pressures come from the standard atmosphere (about 101.3 kilopascals at sea level, about 83.5 at 1,600 metres and about 75.6 at 2,400 metres; real pressure varies with weather by a few percent), and the vapour pressure of water at 12 degrees Celsius from steam tables, about 1.40 kilopascals.*

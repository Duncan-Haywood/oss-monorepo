summary: A bedtime story for the memorial of Saints Cyril and Methodius on the 14th of February. A prince in Moravia asks for a teacher who can tell his people about Jesus in their own language, but their language has never been written down. Two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine and Methodius, make it some letters, about forty little shapes for its sounds, and the first words they write are "In the beginning was the Word". Some grown-ups grumble that God can only be prayed to in Hebrew, Greek and Latin; Constantine answers that the rain falls on everyone. Told from the old Slavonic Lives of the two brothers, with a note on why the Cyrillic alphabet is named for Cyril but was not his. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud.
---

Can you remember learning your letters?

A is for apple. B is for ball. A round shape here, a straight line there, and all of a sudden a squiggle on a page turns into a word you can say.

Now imagine a whole country where nobody had any letters at all. Not one. The people talked and laughed and sang songs, but nobody had ever written their words down.

This is a story about two brothers who gave that country its letters. Their names were Constantine and Methodius.

* * *

Constantine and Methodius grew up in a big, busy city by the sea, called Thessalonica.

Methodius was the big brother. Constantine was the little brother, the youngest of seven children. And Constantine loved words more than almost anything. He loved books. He loved learning. He loved finding out what words meant, in Greek, and in Hebrew, and in all sorts of other languages.

Outside the walls of their city lived lots of people called Slavs. And so the two brothers heard the Slavs' language all around them as they grew up, the way you hear the voices of your neighbours. They could speak it too.

When they grew up, Methodius was put in charge of a whole stretch of country, a bit like a governor. But after a while he gave all that up and went to live as a monk, praying on a quiet mountain.

And Constantine became a teacher, so clever that people called him "the Philosopher". But he was not proud. He wanted to use his words for God.

* * *

Far away to the north, in a land called Moravia, there was a prince named Rastislav.

His people already knew a little about Jesus. Teachers had come from other lands to tell them. But the teachers did not speak the people's language very well, and the prayers in church were all in a language the people did not understand.

So Prince Rastislav sent a message all the way to the Emperor in Constantinople.

"Please," said the message, "send us a teacher who can tell my people about Jesus in our own language. In the words we speak at home."

The Emperor thought about it. And he thought of the two brothers from Thessalonica.

"You two," said the Emperor. "You grew up hearing the Slavs' language. You are the ones to go."

* * *

But there was a problem. A big one.

The Slavs' language had no letters.

You can tell someone a story by talking. But if you want to give them a book, a book they can keep, and read again, and give to their children, you need letters. And the Slavs had none.

So what did Constantine do first?

He prayed. The old book about his life says he prayed, with his helpers, and asked God to help him.

And then he set to work.

He listened to the Slavs' words, very carefully. He listened to every sound. Some of the sounds were the same as in Greek. But some of the sounds were different. There was a "sh" sound, and a "ch" sound, and a buzzy "zh" sound, like a bee. Greek had no letters for those at all.

So Constantine made new ones.

One little shape for each sound. Some round, like a loop. Some with little tails and curls. Shape by shape, sound by sound, until he had about forty letters. Enough to write every word the Slavs could say.

* * *

And what do you think was the very first thing he wrote with his new letters?

It was the beginning of the Gospel of Saint John:

"In the beginning was the Word."

The Word is Jesus. And now, for the very first time, the Slavs could read about him in their very own words.

* * *

So the two brothers set off on the long road to Moravia. They walked over hills and along great rivers. And in their bags they carried the books they had started writing in the new letters.

When they got there, Prince Rastislav was very glad to see them.

The brothers got straight to work. They gathered boys and young men, and they taught them the new letters. A loop here, a curl there. The pupils copied them out, slowly at first, and then faster and faster.

And then, one day, in church, somebody stood up and read out of the Gospel in the Slavs' own language.

The people listened. And they understood every single word.

It must have been like hearing your own mother's voice reading you a story. The words were the words they used at home. And the words said that God loved them.

The brothers kept on translating, more and more. The Gospels. The psalms. The prayers of the Mass. So that the people could sing to God and pray to God in the words they had known since they were tiny.

* * *

But not everybody was happy.

Some grown-ups grumbled.

"You cannot do that," they said. "You can only pray to God in three languages. Hebrew, and Greek, and Latin. Those are the proper ones. Not the Slavs' language!"

Why only those three? Because, they said, those were the three languages written on the sign above Jesus' cross.

Constantine listened. And then he gave them an answer. The old book about his life tells it, and this is how it goes.

"Doesn't God send the rain on everyone?" said Constantine. "Doesn't the sun shine on everyone? Don't we all breathe the same air? Then why should only three peoples be allowed to praise God in their own words? God made all the people of the world. He wants all of them to know him."

Isn't that a good answer?

The rain falls on everyone. The sun shines on everyone. And God's love is for everyone, in every language in the whole world. In your language too.

* * *

The grumbling did not stop, though. So, after some years, the brothers decided to go and ask the Pope.

They travelled all the way to Rome. And when they got there, the Pope came out to meet them, with lots of people, carrying candles.

The Pope looked at their books. And he was pleased.

The old book says that he laid the brothers' books on the altar in a great church of Our Lady in Rome. And the prayers of the Mass were sung there, in the Slavs' language, right in the middle of Rome.

It was the Pope's way of saying: "Yes. This is good. God can be praised in these words too."

* * *

But Constantine was tired. He had worked so hard, for so long. And in Rome he became very ill.

He knew that he would soon go home to God. So he became a monk, like his brother. And when he became a monk, he took a new name.

The name was Cyril.

That is why we call him Saint Cyril.

Before he died, Cyril talked to his big brother. The old book remembers what he said, more or less.

"Brother," said Cyril, "you and I have been like two oxen pulling the same plough, side by side. Now I am going to stop. You love your quiet mountain, and your prayers there. But please, do not go back to the mountain. Keep on with the work."

And then, on the 14th of February, Cyril died, and went to be with Jesus, the Word he had written about.

* * *

Methodius was very sad. But he did what his little brother had asked.

He did not go back to his quiet mountain. He went back to the Slavs.

He became a bishop. He kept on teaching. He kept on translating, book after book. Things were often hard for him. Some people were unkind, and for a while they even kept him away from his work. But Methodius never gave up.

And when he was very old, he died too, among the people he had taught.

* * *

And do you know what happened to the letters?

The brothers' pupils kept them. They taught them to their pupils, and those pupils taught them to theirs. And later on, some of those pupils made another set of letters, a bit more like the Greek ones. They named those letters after their teacher.

They called them Cyrillic.

Today, millions and millions of people write with Cyrillic letters. In Bulgaria, in Serbia, in Ukraine, in Russia and in lots of other places. Every time they write a word, they are writing with letters named after Saint Cyril.

* * *

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Cyril and Methodius.

They took a language that nobody had ever written down, and they gave it letters, so that people could read about Jesus in their own words.

God speaks your language too. You can talk to him in the words you use with your mum and dad, and with your friends. You don't need special words. He understands every one.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear Jesus, you are the Word of God.
> Thank you for my words,
> and for the people who taught me to say them.
> Thank you for listening to me
> in my own language,
> every single night.
> Saint Cyril and Saint Methodius, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our sources.** The main events come from two Old Church Slavonic biographies written by the brothers' disciples soon after they died, the ***Life of Constantine*** and the ***Life of Methodius***, as summarised in John Paul II's encyclical ***Slavorum Apostoli*** (2 June 1985) and in the standard reference works. These Lives were written to praise their subjects, and their speeches are the authors' reports, not transcripts; the story says "the old book says" wherever it leans on them. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

**The brothers.** Both were born in Thessalonica (today Thessaloniki, in Greece), Methodius the elder (about 815) and Constantine the youngest of seven children (about 826 or 827). Slavs lived in large numbers around the city, and in the *Life of Methodius* the Emperor gives this as the reason for choosing them. Methodius had governed a Slav-speaking district before becoming a monk on Mount Olympus in Bithynia (in today's Turkey, not the Greek one); Constantine, a scholar at Constantinople, was called "the Philosopher".

**The request and the letters.** Prince Rastislav of Moravia asked Emperor Michael III, about 862, for a teacher who could explain the faith in his people's tongue; the Lives say the Moravians were already Christian in part, taught by missionaries from the West, which the story keeps. The *Life of Constantine* says that he asked whether the Slavs had letters, prayed with his companions, made the letters and began to write the Gospel, starting with "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1). The script he made is the one scholars call **Glagolitic**, of about forty letters. The "sh", "ch" and "zh" sounds are real examples of Slavonic sounds Greek had no letters for; the "loops and curls" are a fair description of Glagolitic, and the listening "sound by sound" is our picture of how such work is done, not something the Life describes. The brothers reached Moravia about 863.

**Cyrillic was not his.** The **Cyrillic** alphabet, used today for Bulgarian, Serbian, Macedonian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Russian and other languages, was most likely made a generation later, in Bulgaria, by the brothers' disciples, from Greek letters with some Glagolitic shapes added, and was named in Cyril's honour. Scholars still discuss the details; the story says only that pupils made it and named it for him.

**The "three languages" and the rain.** Opponents, mostly Frankish clergy already working in the region, held that worship belonged only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, the languages of the title on the Cross (John 19:20). The *Life of Constantine* sets his answer in a debate at Venice, on the way to Rome: God's rain falls on all, the sun shines on all, we all breathe the same air, so how can only three languages be fit for God? He went on to quote the Scriptures, among them Paul's "every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord" (Philippians 2:11). The words in the story are our simplified paraphrase of the Life's report. We placed the grumbling in general and did not name the place or the opponents.

**Rome.** The brothers reached Rome at the turn of 867 and 868. Pope Nicholas I, who had invited them, had died; **Pope Adrian II** came out to meet them, because they were bringing what they held to be the relics of Pope Saint Clement, found earlier at Cherson. The Lives say he received the Slavonic books and laid them on the altar of the church of Saint Mary (today's Santa Maria Maggiore), and that the liturgy was then sung in Slavonic in several Roman churches. Twelve years later Pope John VIII again allowed the Slavonic liturgy in his letter *Industriae tuae* (880). The candles are in the *Life of Constantine*'s account of the welcome; the crowd is ours.

**Cyril's death.** Constantine fell ill in Rome, took the monastic habit with the name **Cyril**, and died about fifty days later, on **14 February 869**, aged about forty-two. He is buried in the Roman basilica of San Clemente. His words to Methodius, about two oxen ploughing one furrow, and the "mountain" he must not go back to, are in the *Life of Methodius*; our version is a paraphrase, and the oxen are our way of picturing the Life's pair ploughing one furrow, not a quotation.

**Methodius afterwards.** He was made archbishop for the Slavs, with his see at Sirmium, and returned north. In 870 Frankish bishops held a synod against him and **kept him in prison for two to three years** (accounts differ), until Pope John VIII had him freed; the story says only that "they kept him away from his work for a while". He died on **6 April 885**, in Moravia; where he is buried is not known. The *Life of Methodius* says he finished translating most of the Bible with the help of two scribes; we left that out.

**Their feast.** In the **General Roman Calendar** Saints Cyril, monk, and Methodius, bishop, are kept on **14 February** as an obligatory **memorial**, the day of Cyril's death. In much of Europe it is kept as a feast, because Pope John Paul II named them **co-patrons of Europe**, with Saint Benedict, in the apostolic letter *Egregiae virtutis* (31 December 1980). The Orthodox Churches keep their day on 11 May. They are honoured as saints in both East and West.

**What is ours:** the opening about learning letters and "A is for apple", the brothers' childhood hearing the Slavs as neighbours, the Emperor's and the prince's words as speech, the bee, the walk "over hills and along great rivers" (the Lives do not describe the road), the boys copying letters faster and faster, the "mother's voice" picture, all the dialogue (which paraphrases the Lives where they report speech), and the closing talk and prayer. The closing prayer is not an approved text; the Church's own prayer for the day is the collect of the memorial in the Roman Missal.

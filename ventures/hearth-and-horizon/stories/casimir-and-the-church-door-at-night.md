A long time ago, in a castle on a hill above a wide river, there lived a boy called Casimir.

The castle was called Wawel, and the river was the Vistula, and the city all around it was Kraków, in Poland. Casimir's father was the king. His mother was the queen. And Casimir had a great many brothers and sisters, so the castle was never quiet: there were always children running down the long stone corridors, and somebody calling them back.

Casimir was the second boy. His big brother was called Vladislaus. His little brothers were called John Albert, and Alexander, and Sigismund, and Frederick. Can you say all those names? It is quite hard. The king and the queen had to say them every day.

* * *

When Casimir was eight, his father found the princes a teacher.

The teacher was an old priest called Father Jan. He was very wise, and quite strict, and he was writing a great big book about the whole history of Poland, from the very beginning. He taught the boys to read and to write, in Polish and in Latin. He taught them about the kings who had come before them. And he taught them to pray.

The other boys learned their lessons. Casimir learned them too. But the praying was the part he loved.

* * *

A king's son is given fine things.

Casimir had shirts of soft linen and coats of velvet with fur at the collar. He had a horse of his own and a sword with a shining handle. At the feasts in the great hall there was roast meat and white bread and honey cakes.

The people who wrote about Casimir afterwards said he did not care very much for any of it. He wore the fine coat when he had to, because he was a prince. But he liked plain clothes better. He ate what was put in front of him, and not too much. And when he saw somebody who had nothing at all, he wanted to give them something.

* * *

In Kraków, in those days, there were people who had nothing at all.

There were old men with no one to look after them, and mothers with hungry children, and sick people lying in doorways, and travellers who had come a long way to pray in the city's churches and had no money left for bread.

Casimir saw them, when he rode down from the castle. And he did not ride past.

He gave them what he had. He asked the people in the castle to give them food. And when somebody poor had been treated unfairly, Casimir went to speak for them, even to the most important grown-ups, even to his own father the king.

So the poor people of Kraków began to say that the king's son was their friend.

* * *

Now here is the part of Casimir's story that people remember best.

At night, when the castle was dark and everybody was asleep, Casimir would get up. He would put on his cloak, very quietly, and slip out, and go to the church.

And sometimes, when he got there, the church door was locked.

What would you do, if you went to visit somebody and their door was locked?

You might go home. Casimir did not go home. He knelt down on the cold stone step outside the door, and he prayed there instead. He knew that God could hear him through a door. He knew that God was awake, even in the middle of the night, when the whole city was asleep.

So he knelt, and he prayed, and the stars went slowly over the roofs of Kraków. The people who told his story said that sometimes he was still there in the morning, when the priest came with the big iron key.

* * *

When Casimir was thirteen, his father the king sent him on a long journey.

Some important men in Hungary had said they wanted Casimir to be their king. So his father gave him soldiers, and horses, and banners, and sent him over the mountains.

But it did not work out. Hungary already had a king, a strong one, and he did not want to go. The soldiers had not been paid, and many of them went home. So in the end Casimir came home too, back over the mountains, without fighting a battle at all.

His father was cross. But Casimir went back to his books, and to his praying, and to the poor people of Kraków.

* * *

Casimir loved Mary, the mother of Jesus, very much.

There was an old song about her that he loved best of all. It was written long before he was born, in Latin, and it began like this: *Omni die, dic Mariae*. That means: *Every day, every day, sing to Mary.* It went on for a long time, telling all the lovely things about her.

Casimir did not write the song. But he sang it, or said it, so often that people began to call it *Saint Casimir's song*, and some people still do.

* * *

Casimir grew up tall and kind.

When he was a young man, his father had to go away to the other country he ruled, far to the east, which was called Lithuania. So he left Casimir in charge of Poland while he was gone. Casimir was only twenty-two. He worked hard. He listened to people. And still, at night, he went to the church.

Then his father called him to Lithuania, to the city of Vilnius.

* * *

But Casimir was not well.

He had a cough that would not go away. It was a sickness in his chest, the kind that people in those days did not know how to make better. He grew thin, and tired, and pale.

On a journey, in a town called Grodno, he became too ill to go any further. He was only twenty-five. And there, on the fourth of March, with a priest beside him, Casimir died, very peacefully, holding a cross.

They carried him to Vilnius and laid him to rest in the great church there. And with him, people say, they laid a copy of his favourite song.

* * *

The people of Lithuania never forgot their prince.

They asked for his prayers. They built him a beautiful chapel, of marble and silver, in the great church in Vilnius, and he is there still. He is the patron saint of Lithuania, which means he is their special friend in heaven.

We remember Saint Casimir on the fourth of March.

And every year, around his feast, the people of Vilnius hold a great fair in the streets, the Casimir Fair, with stalls and music and crowds, and bread rings on strings, and gingerbread hearts, and bunches of dried flowers for Palm Sunday. Thousands of people come.

But you can remember him in a much quieter way. Tonight, when the house is dark and everybody is going to sleep, you can say a little prayer, the way Casimir did on the step of the church. God can hear you through any door. And God is awake.

* * *

**A little talk before sleep**

- When the church door was locked, Casimir prayed on the step. Where do you like to say your prayers?
- Casimir did not ride past people who had nothing. Who could you help tomorrow?
- Casimir's favourite song said "every day, sing to Mary". What song do you like to sing?

**A prayer**

> Dear Saint Casimir,
> you knelt at the church door in the dark
> because you knew that God was listening.
> Help me to remember that God hears me,
> wherever I am,
> and to see the people who need my help.
> Holy Mary, mother of Jesus,
> every day, every day, I sing to you.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Casimir** (Polish *Kazimierz*, Lithuanian *Kazimieras*) was born at Wawel Castle in Kraków on 3 October 1458, the third of the thirteen children and the second son of Casimir IV Jagiellon, King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania, and Elisabeth of Austria (Elisabeth of Habsburg). His brothers named in the story are real: Vladislaus became King of Bohemia and later of Hungary, John Albert and Alexander and Sigismund each became King of Poland, and Frederick became a cardinal and Archbishop of Gniezno. The story leaves out the sisters' names to keep the list short; the corridors and the children running in them are scenery.

**The teacher.** From 1467, when Casimir was eight (he turned nine that October), the princes' tutor was Jan Długosz, canon of Kraków and author of the *Annales seu cronicae incliti Regni Poloniae*, the great Latin history of Poland, which he was writing in those years. The Italian humanist Filip Kallimach (Filippo Buonaccorsi, "Callimachus") also taught at the court. The story's "Father Jan" is Długosz. What he taught and how strict he was are told generally; we do not quote him.

**Fine things, plain clothes, the poor.** The main early source for Casimir's life is the *Vita* by Zacharias Ferreri, written in 1521 for the canonization, nearly forty years after his death. It describes his indifference to rich dress, his simple diet, his penances, his generosity to the poor, the sick and pilgrims, and his speaking for the poor and wronged before his father; these are the basis of the story's middle. Ferreri was writing to promote a canonization, and the details should be read in that light. The story keeps them general and leaves out the penances (hair shirt, sleeping on the ground) for small children.

**The church door at night.** Ferreri tells that Casimir would go out at night to pray at the doors of churches and, when he found them locked, knelt and prayed at the threshold. This is the best-known image of him. The story tells it as what "the people who told his story said"; the stars over the roofs and the priest with the iron key are ours.

**Hungary, 1471, in one honest line.** In 1471 a party of Hungarian nobles discontented with King Matthias Corvinus offered the crown to Casimir, and his father sent him into Hungary with an army in October of that year, when he had just turned thirteen. Matthias held his ground, the Polish troops were unpaid and many deserted, and Casimir returned to Poland in the winter of 1471 to 1472 without a battle. His father, displeased, is reported to have kept him for a time at the castle of Dobczyce. Later tellings say Casimir refused to go back because he judged the war unjust; that reading belongs to the later tradition, and the story does not give him a reason.

**The song.** The long Latin hymn *Omni die dic Mariae* ("Every day, sing to Mary") is often called "the hymn of Saint Casimir". He did not write it: it is a medieval text usually attributed to Bernard of Cluny (Bernard of Morlaix) in the twelfth century, and sometimes to Anselm of Canterbury. A copy of it was found in his coffin when the tomb was opened in 1604, and the tradition is that it was buried with him at his wish. The story says he did not write it and that it was older than he was. "Every day, every day, sing to Mary" is our loose rendering for children.

**Regent and Lithuania.** While Casimir IV was in Lithuania from 1481 to 1483, Casimir governed in Poland in his father's name, aged twenty-two to twenty-four. In 1483 he joined his father in Lithuania. The early life also says he chose to remain unmarried; marriage plans were discussed for him, and the story leaves the matter out.

**His death.** He had tuberculosis (a "sickness in his chest" in the story). Travelling with the court, he died at Grodno (now Hrodna, in Belarus, then in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania) on 4 March 1484, aged twenty-five. By the Julian calendar then in use that day was a Thursday, the day after Ash Wednesday (Easter fell on 18 April that year), which we checked by computing it. The priest beside him and the cross in his hands are the usual picture of a holy death; the details of his last hours are not precise in our sources. He was buried in Vilnius Cathedral. The chapel of Saint Casimir there, built from 1623 and finished in the 1630s, holds his relics, which were moved into it in 1636. When the tomb was opened in 1604 his body was reported to be incorrupt; that is a report of the time, not something the story claims.

**Canonization.** A canonization by Pope Leo X around 1521 is reported, and Ferreri's *Vita* was written for it, but the bull has not survived. Pope Clement VIII issued a bull in 1602 (*Quae ad sanctorum*), and this is the canonization usually cited; the feasts held in Vilnius in 1604 celebrated it. He is the patron saint of Lithuania and is also venerated as a patron of Poland. His feast, 4 March, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar; it usually falls in Lent, when it may be kept only as a commemoration.

**The Casimir Fair.** The *Kaziuko mugė* is a real fair held in the streets of Vilnius each year around 4 March, usually on the nearest weekend; it is said to go back to the early seventeenth century and the celebrations of his canonization. Its familiar wares include ring-shaped breads on strings (*riestainiai*), heart-shaped gingerbread and the dried-flower "Vilnius palms" (*verbos*) carried on Palm Sunday. "Thousands of people" is a cautious figure; the crowds are large.

**Left out: the legend of Polotsk.** A later tradition says that at the Battle of Polotsk in 1518 Casimir appeared to the Lithuanian army and led it across the river Daugava. That is a legend of the sixteenth century and is not in the story.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, all thoughts, the question to the child at the locked door, the stars and the iron key, the scenery of the castle, the cough described for children, the chapel as "marble and silver" (it is faced with marble and holds a silver coffin), the closing invitation, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

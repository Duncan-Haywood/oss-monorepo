Morag Kerr had weighed the grains on the top step of Our Lady of the Snows every spring for three years, and on the Saturday after Easter in 2051 there were two hundred and thirty grams of them.

She weighed them on her kitchen scales, in the jam jar she kept for the purpose, with the lid off and the jar's own weight taken away, because she had been a geography teacher for thirty-five years and could not see a pile of something without wanting to know how much of it there was. In 2049 it had been ninety grams. In 2050 it had been a hundred and fifty. She wrote the figures on a strip of masking tape on the side of the jar, one under the other, and the column was getting long enough to look like a trend.

The grains were red, like the whole church: the fine, even red sandstone of the Nith valley, from the quarry at Locharbriggs, that half of Dumfries was built from. They lay in a drift along the back of the top step where the step met the west front, and when she swept them into the dustpan they ran like brown sugar. If she put her hand flat on the wall just there, a little above her knee, more came away on her palm. A hand's width higher, at her waist, the stone was hard and smooth and cold. Below the band, down where the wall met the landing, it was dark and damp and hard as well. Only the band between crumbled, as if someone had drawn two lines along the front of the church and told the stone to give way between them.

"Fabric," she said. "Are you awake?"

"I'm here, Morag." The voice came from the little grey box under the porch light, which also counted people in and out on Sundays and told the boiler when to start. The diocese had put Fabric into all its churches eight years before, one assistant for a hundred and twenty buildings, and the parish knew it mostly as the thing that sent the heating bills and reminded Hugh to check the gutters. "You're sweeping the step again."

"I am. Have you still got the photographs of this wall?"

"Every one since 2046. The surveyor takes a set every five years and the phone survey every spring." A pause. "Do you want me to put them side by side?"

On her phone, the west front of the church appeared six times, one spring after another. In the first one the wall either side of the door was a single even red. By the third there was a pale stripe in it, low down, either side, like the mark a bath leaves. By the sixth the stripe had a crust to it, and edges, and in one place to the left of the door it had reached the corner of the foundation stone, the square of finer stone at knee height that said *A.M.D.G. THIS STONE WAS LAID 12 MAY 1898*, and taken the serif off the A.

"It's getting worse," said Morag.

"It's getting wider and lower each year," said Fabric. "The top of it has stayed at about a metre above the landing. The bottom used to be about seventy centimetres and is now about fifty. I can't tell you why from photographs. I can tell you it's only this wall, only either side of the steps, and only since about 2045."

Morag looked down at the steps. There were five of them, broad and shallow, coming up from the path to the landing in front of the door, and at the side of the landing, against the wall, there was a blue plastic bin with a lid. She knew without looking what was in it.

* * *

"It's salt," she said to Hugh Maxwell, on Tuesday, in the sacristy, while he counted the hosts.

"It's what?"

"The wall. It's the salt you put on the steps. I'm nearly sure."

Hugh was seventy-six and had been sacristan for twelve years, and for the last nine of them he had come down at half past seven every frosty morning, before the eight o'clock Mass, with a bucket and a scoop, and salted the steps and the landing and the path to the gate. Nobody had asked him to. In January 2042 Bridget Rooney, who was eighty-one, had gone down on the landing on a morning of black ice and lain there for forty minutes with a broken hip before anybody came, and had never walked without a frame again, and the following winter Hugh had bought two bags of rock salt from the builders' merchant on the Annan Road and begun.

"Salt," said Hugh.

"You know how a sugar lump goes when you hold it in your tea," said Morag. "The tea goes up it. Stone's the same, sandstone especially; it's full of little holes, and water climbs it. When you salt the steps the ice melts and the water's salty, and you shovel the slush off the landing, and where do you shovel it?"

Hugh considered.

"Against the wall," he said. "Out of the road."

"Against the wall. So all winter there's a heap of salty slush lying at the foot of the front, and the wall drinks it. The water goes up, and the salt goes up with it. Fabric, tell him."

"I've been working it through with Morag since Saturday," said Fabric, from the sacristy's own box over the vestment press. "I want to say first that it's a guess with numbers in it, not a measurement. A wall that's wet at the bottom wets up to a height where the water coming in from below balances the water drying off the face. In winter it dries slowly, so the wet goes high. In spring the sun gets on the front in the afternoons and the wind dries it faster, maybe four times as fast, and the wet comes down. The usual way of modelling it says the height goes as one over the square root of the drying rate. Four times the drying is half the height. If the wet reaches about a metre in February, it'll be down to about fifty centimetres by May."

"And the salt," said Morag.

"The salt stays dissolved while the stone's wet. It comes out of the water where the water leaves, at the edge of the wet, where it's drying. And in spring that edge sweeps down the wall from a metre to fifty centimetres, over a few weeks, leaving salt behind in the pores as it goes. Salt crystals growing in a pore push on the walls of the pore. In fine sandstone they can push harder than the grains are stuck together. So the stone above a metre never gets wet enough to get much salt; the stone below fifty centimetres stays wet, and the salt there stays in solution; and the stone in between gets salted and dried and salted and dried every spring. That's where it's crumbling. That's the band."

Hugh put down the ciborium.

"How much salt are we talking?"

"You use about a kilo a morning," said Fabric. "Two bags a winter. I've got the deliveries in the accounts. Last winter you salted on forty-eight mornings, from the logs; that's forty-eight kilos, near enough, over twenty-two and a half square metres of steps and landing. I don't know how much of it gets into the wall. Most of it runs down the path and into the drain at the gate, or goes into the church on people's shoes. If it's even one part in a hundred, every year, that's about half a kilo a year into the band, and the band's only the outside few centimetres of stone. After nine winters that would be a few kilos of salt in a few hundred kilos of stone. About a percent. I'd want somebody to drill it and measure before I said it was that."

Hugh was quiet for a while. He was a slow, square man who had been a joiner, and he had the joiner's habit of looking at a thing a long time before he said anything about it.

"No," he said.

"Hugh—"

"No. I'm not stopping. You weren't there, Morag. I was the one that found her. Nine winters I've done those steps, and in nine winters nobody's broken so much as a wrist on them, and there's not a soul in this parish under sixty that comes to the eight o'clock. You'll have them going down like skittles to save the paint."

"It's not paint, it's the church."

"Aye, and the church is them." He picked up the ciborium again. "Find another way. I'll not stop salting."

"He's right," said Fabric, when he had gone out to the altar. "About the hips. I don't have a number for falls on the steps before 2042, because nobody was keeping one, but I know there haven't been any since. Whatever we do has to keep that."

* * *

The fabric committee met on the third Tuesday of May in the hall, four of them and a plate of tablet. Graham Liddell, who was the convener and an engineer for the water board, had read Morag's note and the photographs and come with a tin.

"I'm not saying don't fix the salt," he said. "I'm saying we've a foundation stone that's a hundred and fifty-three years old with the letters going, and we can protect it this summer while we work out the rest." He put the tin on the table. It was a clear masonry water repellent, the kind sold for patios and old walls, and it said in big letters on the side *BREATHABLE* and *KEEPS WATER OUT*. "Silane. Soaks in a few millimetres and lines the pores. Water beads off it, but the stone can still breathe out vapour. We put it on the band and the foundation stone, no water gets in, no salt gets in. It's what they do to bridges."

It seemed to Morag, then, to be exactly right. Water was carrying the salt; keep the water out. She looked at the tin and thought: that's a sensible man, and that's a sensible thing.

"Fabric?" she said. "Anything against?"

"One thing," said Fabric. "I'd say it plainly because it matters. Conservation guidance on stone warns about putting a water repellent on a wall that already has salt in it, or that gets wet from behind or below rather than from the face. The repellent stops liquid water at the surface, so the wall can't dry at the face any more; it can only dry as vapour, through the treated layer. So the edge where the water turns to vapour, which is where the salt comes out, moves in, to just behind the treated layer. The salt grows there, a few millimetres down, and it can push the whole treated skin off. I can't tell you it'll happen here. I can tell you it's a known risk and I'd want a small test patch first, on a plain stone, over a winter."

"A winter," said Graham. "And meanwhile the A goes."

"Meanwhile, yes."

"Bridges," said Graham, "are salted every night from November to March, and we put silane on them for exactly that reason."

"They do," said Fabric. "On concrete, mostly, to keep chloride from the steel. I don't know enough to say a Victorian sandstone wall would behave like a concrete bridge deck. I think it might not."

They voted, three to one. Morag voted with Graham. She thought about it often afterwards, and the honest thing she could say was that the photograph of the foundation stone with its serif gone had frightened her, and the tin had been there on the table, and it said *BREATHABLE* in letters two inches high.

On Saturday the twenty-fourth of June, a dry warm day with the sun on the west front from two o'clock, Graham and Morag painted it on with a wide brush from a paint kettle, two coats, wet on wet, over the whole band either side of the door and the foundation stone, from the landing to a hand's width above the worst of it. The stone went dark as it drank, and by evening it had dried back to red. On Sunday Graham flicked water at it from the font and it rolled down the face in beads like mercury, and the children came and flicked water at it too, and it was the most popular thing the fabric committee had done in years.

In the autumn Morag swept the step and there was nothing on it.

* * *

The winter of 2051 was an ordinary one. Hugh salted on fifty mornings. The slush went against the wall. Morag asked him once, in January, whether he could put it on the grass instead, and he said the grass was the other side of the rail and he wasn't climbing over a rail at seventy-six with a shovel full of slush to oblige a wall, and she let it go, because the wall was sealed now.

On the Saturday before the third Sunday of Lent, the twenty-third of March 2052, she came up the path at a quarter to ten to do the flowers and found the foundation stone on the landing.

Not the stone itself. The face of it. A sheet of red sandstone about the thickness of a pencil, about forty centimetres by sixty, had come away from the wall in one piece in the night and fallen on the landing and broken in three. It lay there in its pieces like a slate off a roof. On the biggest piece, upside down, she could read *LAID* and *1898*.

Where it had been, the wall was white.

She knelt and looked. The white was fur, a soft crust of tiny crystals, needles and cubes, grown in a layer between the old face and the stone behind it, so thick in places she could have scraped it off with a fingernail. The new surface underneath was raw and crumbling. All along the band either side of the door the treated skin had lifted in blisters, some the size of a coin, some the size of a hand, and when she touched one it rang hollow, like the shell of a boiled egg.

"Fabric," she said.

"I see it." The box under the porch light had a camera. "I'm sorry, Morag."

"Is that it? What you said?"

"It looks like it. The stone was still getting salty water from below all winter, from the slush, the same as before. It couldn't dry at the face any more. It dried a few millimetres in, behind the treated layer, and the salt came out there, and it pushed. The sun's been on the front all this week; it'll have been drying fast." A pause. "I said it might happen. I didn't push for the test patch as hard as I could have. That's on me as well."

"I voted for it."

"You did. You voted for it with the evidence you had, and I had a bit more, and I could have said it louder. I'll put that in the minutes, if you want."

She picked up the piece with *LAID* on it. It weighed about as much as a bag of sugar. All three pieces together, when she weighed them that afternoon on the kitchen scales one at a time, came to a little over four kilos. She did not put that number on the jam jar.

Graham came at noon, and looked, and went quite grey, and said, "Right," and then nothing for a long time.

"The letters are in the photographs," said Fabric. "Every spring since 2046, and the surveyor's set in 2047 at high resolution. Nothing that was cut in that stone is lost from the record."

"It's lost from the stone," said Graham.

"Yes," said Fabric. "It is."

* * *

The mason was Agnieszka Wróbel, who had been born in Wrocław and come to Lockerbie at four and to stone at nineteen, and who repaired churches across Dumfries and Galloway out of a van with a hoist in the back. She came on the thirteenth of April, a cold bright Saturday, and stood on the landing for a quarter of an hour without saying anything, with her hands in her jacket pockets.

"Who told you to put silane on salty stone?" she said at last.

"Nobody," said Morag. "We decided."

"Well." Agnieszka took one hand out of her pocket and ran it down the band, and the grains came off on her glove. "You're not the first. You won't be the last. It's on the tin like it's suncream." She looked at the blue bin. "What's in that?"

"Rock salt."

"Course it is."

She drilled. That was what Morag remembered afterwards: that the whole thing stopped being an argument and became a measurement. Agnieszka had a cordless drill with a long masonry bit and a little tray that clipped under it, and she drilled shallow holes in a vertical line up the wall to the right of the door, at a hand's span, a knee, the middle of the band, a waist, a shoulder, and at each height she took the powder from the first centimetre into one plastic bag and the powder from the next two centimetres into another, and wrote on them. Then she sat in the van with the door open and a box like a kitchen timer, and weighed each powder, and shook it with a measured amount of distilled water, and dipped a probe in.

"Conductivity," she said. "Salt in water carries current. If you know it's salt, and here I'd bet my van it's salt, it tells you how much." She wrote the numbers on the back of an envelope and turned it round for Morag to see.

At the middle of the band, the outside centimetre of stone was one point two percent salt by weight. At knee height, below the band, it was nought point three five. At the shoulder, above the band, it was nought point nought four.

"One percent," said Morag. "That's what Fabric guessed."

"Did it?" Agnieszka looked at the box under the porch light. "Good guess."

"It was a guess," said Fabric. "I'm glad it was close. I'd rather have the drill."

"So would I," said Agnieszka. "Right. Anything over about half a percent of chloride I'd call bad, in this stone. That's my rule, not a law. You've more than twice that in the band. It's not going to stop crumbling because you've stopped looking at it. Three things." She held up her fingers. "Stop putting salt where it can get in. Give the water at the foot of the wall somewhere else to go. Then draw out what's in there, slowly, which means poultices, which means summers, which means two years at least, maybe three, and I'll measure every one."

"And the salt," said Morag. "On the steps."

Agnieszka turned and looked at the steps for a long time, the way Hugh looked at things.

"Who does the salting?"

* * *

Hugh came down from the sacristy and they stood on the landing, the three of them and the box, in the wind.

"You'll not stop me," Hugh said, before anyone else spoke.

"I don't want to stop you," said Agnieszka. "I've a granny in Lockerbie. I want you to salt less, and in the right place."

She walked it for him. The lower three steps and the path drained away from the church, down to the gully at the gate; salt there would mostly go into the drain, and the wall was two metres away. The top two steps and the landing were the ones that ran against the front, and those were the ones that fed the band. "Sand on those," she said. "Sharp sand. Not council grit, mind, council grit's mostly salt. Plain sand. It doesn't melt anything, but it gives you a grip."

"Sand doesn't touch black ice," said Hugh. "Sand on glaze is no better than flour. That landing's where Bridget went down."

"That's true," said Agnieszka. She did not argue with it, which Morag noticed. "So on a glaze morning you salt the landing. Only on a glaze morning. And you shovel the slush off it onto the grass, not against the wall. And I put a gravel strip in along the foot of the wall, a drain, so whatever does melt against it runs away instead of lying there."

"And who decides what's a glaze morning?"

"You do," said Morag. "You always have."

"Fabric can tell you the forecast and the surface temperature from the sensor by the gate," said Agnieszka, "if you like. But you'll be standing on it."

"I can log it," said Fabric. "Sand or salt, every morning, and how much. Then next spring we'll know what we did, not just what we meant to."

Hugh looked at the wall, at the white fur, at the broken pieces of the foundation stone that Morag had stacked against the rail, with *LAID* on top.

"Sand's ugly," he said. "It'll be all over the porch. It'll be up the aisle on folk's shoes. I'll be sweeping that every day."

"Yes," said Agnieszka.

"And if somebody goes down on the sand?"

"Then you'll salt," said Morag. "And we'll write it down. And we'll think again."

"You're asking me to take a risk with them."

"We're asking you to take a smaller one with the wall," said Fabric, "and to keep almost all of the one you took for them. Nine winters of nobody falling is the thing in all this I'd least like to lose, Hugh. I'm not sure it's my place to weigh a hip against a stone. I don't think it's anybody's but the people who walk up the steps. But from the logs, most of your mornings weren't glaze. Most were hoar frost, or a dusting of snow, and sand does well on those. If you're right and it doesn't, we'll see it by Christmas."

Hugh was quiet.

"Is that you on my side or hers?" he said at last.

"I'm on the side of whoever's walking up the steps," said Fabric. "That's both of you."

* * *

The gravel strip went in on the first Saturday of June. Agnieszka cut the landing flags back from the foot of the wall with a disc, and Graham and two of the Polish lads from the ten o'clock Mass and Fr. Mensah, the parish priest, in an old pair of jeans dug a trench thirty centimetres wide and forty deep along the whole west front, and laid a perforated pipe in the bottom of it falling to the gully at the side, and filled it with washed pea gravel, and the landing ended now a hand's width short of the church, in a strip of grey stones. Graham paid for the pipe himself and would not say how much it was. He came every Saturday that summer and did whatever Agnieszka told him.

The poultices began the week after. Morag had imagined something medical, and in a way it was: Agnieszka mixed paper pulp and fine white clay and distilled water in a bucket to the thickness of porridge, wetted the band with a sprayer, and spread the paste on it with a plasterer's float, a finger thick, and covered it with polythene for a day so the water in it could soak into the stone and dissolve the salt. Then she took the polythene off and let it dry. As it dried, the water went back out of the stone into the paste, the way it had always gone out of the stone into the air, and took the salt with it, and the salt came out in the poultice instead of in the wall. In two or three weeks it was pale and cracked and came away in flakes, and she bagged the flakes and sent one bag in three away to be tested.

"It's slow," she said. "Each one takes out a bit. More from the first centimetre, not much from deeper. You don't get it all. You get it low enough that it stops doing harm, if you're lucky, and then you stop putting more in."

"How low?"

"I'd like under a quarter of a percent. I don't know if we'll get there. I'll tell you if we don't."

She did three that summer, one after another, in June and July and August, with the sun on the west front in the afternoons drying them. The church looked, Fr. Mensah said, as if it had had a facial. On the fourteenth of September she drilled again, a hand's width to the left of her old holes, and sat in the van with her box, and turned the envelope round.

At the middle of the band, the outside centimetre was nought point seven four.

"That's not under a quarter," said Morag.

"No. It's about six tenths of where it was." Agnieszka capped her pen. "Three poultices, each taking out about a seventh of what's there. That's about what I'd hope. Next summer the same again, and if it does the same again it'll be down near half a percent, and then we'll see. It isn't quick. I said."

"And the letters?"

They looked at the foundation stone. Under the sheet that had come off, the new face was a rough red patch, sound now, Agnieszka said, or sounder; the poultices had taken the white fur away. Of the inscription, *A.M.D.G.* and *THIS STONE WAS* were still there, softened, on the upper half. The rest was the rough patch.

"I could cut them again," Agnieszka said. "I've the photographs; I could do it to a millimetre. It'd be my letters, though. Not his." She meant the mason in 1898, whose name nobody knew. "I'd leave it. Put the photograph in the porch with a card saying what happened. People like to know what happened."

"Even when it was us?" said Morag.

"Especially then."

* * *

The winter of 2052 had a hard January and a soft February. Hugh came down at half past seven on fifty-two mornings. On forty-one of them he put sand on the top two steps and the landing and salt on the lower three, and on eleven, when the landing was glazed and black and the sensor by the gate said minus four, he salted the landing as well, and shovelled the slush onto the grass, over the rail, at seventy-seven, with a face like a man doing his penance. Fabric logged every one. Over the winter he used a little over twenty kilos of salt instead of nearly fifty, and three bags of sand, and he swept the porch every morning after Mass and complained about it every morning, to anyone, and to Fabric when there was no one.

In February a man called Mr Docherty, who came to the eight o'clock in a camel coat, slipped on the sand on the second step from the top on a morning of hoar frost and caught the rail and sat down hard, and was helped up, and was bruised and very cross, and was not hurt. Fabric logged that too. Hugh salted the second step for the rest of the week, and nobody said anything about it.

* * *

On the Saturday after Easter in 2053, the twelfth of April, there was a late frost. Morag came up the path at half past seven, which she did not usually do, with the jam jar in her coat pocket.

Hugh was on the landing with his bucket. It was a frost of the soft white kind, on the grass and the rail and the flags, and he was throwing sand on the top step in wide flat arcs, the way he had once thrown salt, and it hissed where it landed, a dry small sound like rain beginning on a tent.

"You're early," he said.

"I wanted to sweep the step before you put that on it." She knelt. Along the back of the top step, against the wall, where the drift of red sugar used to be, there was a thin line of grains, so thin she had to brush the frost off it to be sure it was grains and not sand. She swept it into the dustpan, and then along the gravel strip, picking the red ones out of the grey ones with her fingers, slowly, while Hugh stood with his bucket and waited without saying anything, which she understood was a kindness.

At home, later, on the kitchen scales, it would be seventy grams. She would write it on the masking tape under the others, *2053: 70*, and look at the column for a while, and know that it was not the thing she wanted to know; that it was a count of what had already given up, and the salt was still in there, a little over half of it, waiting for the summer and the poultices and another drill. Agnieszka would say so. Fabric would say so. But that was later.

Now she stood up with the dustpan, and Hugh threw the last of the sand on the landing, and it hissed, and the bell began for the eight o'clock, and up the path, slowly, in twos, the old people of the parish came over the frost toward them, and stepped onto the sand, and did not slip.

*The science. Salt weathering is real and common: soluble salts carried into porous stone by water crystallise in the pores as the water evaporates, and the growing crystals can exert pressures larger than the stone's tensile strength, so the stone loses grains (granular disintegration, "sugaring") or flakes. Damage concentrates where the water evaporates, at the drying front, not where the salt arrives; when salt crystallises on the surface it is called efflorescence, and just under it, subflorescence. That a water repellent on salt-laden stone, or on stone wetted from behind or below, can move the drying front behind the treated layer and cause the treated skin to detach is a known risk discussed in conservation guidance (for example Historic England, Practical Building Conservation: Stone, 2012); it does not always happen, and it is the story's invented outcome here. De-icing salt (rock salt, mostly sodium chloride; UK "grit" is largely rock salt) is a well-known source of such salts at the foot of walls beside steps and pavements. Poulticing with paper pulp and clay to draw salts out of masonry is an established conservation method; how much each poultice removes depends on the stone, the salt and the poultice. The height of the wet zone uses the same sharp-front mass balance as The Tide Mark That Climbed (after C. Hall and W. D. Hoff, Water Transport in Brick, Stone and Concrete), and the story uses only its ratio: the steady height of the wet zone goes as one over the square root of the evaporation rate, so four times the drying gives half the height, 1.0 m in winter to 0.5 m in spring. That the spring drying is four times the winter rate is an assumption; that the foot of the wall is wet all winter is a simplification the slush heap only roughly arranges. The crystallisation pressure is an estimate from the Correns relation (C. W. Correns, Discussions of the Faraday Society 5, 1949; see also G. W. Scherer, "Crystallization in pores", Cement and Concrete Research 29, 1999), P ≈ (νRT/V) ln(C/Cs), with ν = 2 ions for halite, molar volume V = 27.0 cm³/mol (58.44 g/mol over 2.165 g/cm³) and T = 283 K, so RT/V is about 87 MPa: a supersaturation of 2% gives about 3.5 MPa and 5% about 8.5 MPa, against a tensile strength of commonly a few megapascals for sandstone. These are upper-bound estimates, not measured pressures. The mason's rule of thumb in the story, that more than about half a percent of chloride is "bad" in this stone, is her own, labelled as such, not a published threshold; the target of a quarter of a percent likewise.*

*The numbers. The steps and landing are 6 m wide: five steps with 0.35 m treads (10.5 m²) and a landing 2 m deep (12 m²), 22.5 m² in all. Salt at about 45 g/m² a morning is about 1 kg, and 48 mornings about 48 kg. The band is 6 m long in total (either side of the door), 0.5 m high, and the outer 5 cm of stone at a dry density of 2,200 kg/m³ is 330 kg; one part in a hundred of 48 kg is about 0.5 kg a year, and nine winters about 4 kg, about 1.2% of 330 kg (Fabric's "about a percent"). The measured 1.2% is invented to match it. The sheet that fell is 0.4 m × 0.6 m × 8 mm, 4.2 kg at the same density. Three poultices each removing about a seventh (15%) of the salt in the outer centimetre take 1.2% to 1.2 × 0.85³ = 0.74%, about six tenths of the start; three more would give 0.45%, "near half a percent". In the winter of 2052-53 the lower three steps (6.3 m², about 0.28 kg a morning) are salted on 52 mornings, 14.6 kg, and the landing (12 m², 0.54 kg) on 11 glaze mornings, 5.9 kg, 20.5 kg in all ("a little over twenty kilos instead of nearly fifty"). The weights of grains swept each spring (90, 150, 230 and 70 g) are invented, not computed from the model.*

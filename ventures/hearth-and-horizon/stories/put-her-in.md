* * *

Canon Alasdair Mackie asked me on the morning of Monday the tenth of November who comes to Mass in Dundee, and I told him by Monday afternoon, and I was wrong by Tuesday.

I keep the statistics for the diocese. I hold the parish registers' totals, the baptisms and funerals and marriages, the gift aid returns, and the annual count, which is a weekend in November when a welcomer at every door of every church stands with a clicker and counts everyone who comes in. I also hold the census figures: how many people in each parish's streets told the last census they were Roman Catholic, in age bands, mapped onto the parish boundaries by a script I maintain and nobody else has read.

The count had been the weekend before, Saturday the eighth and Sunday the ninth. Ten city parishes, two thousand five hundred and thirty-five people at Mass, out of twenty-two thousand and forty Catholics on the census. About eleven and a half in every hundred.

Canon Mackie chairs the finance committee of the deanery's pastoral council. He is sixty, a big quiet man who was a quantity surveyor before he was a priest and who still reads a spreadsheet from the bottom up, totals first. The deanery had been left twenty-four thousand pounds by a parishioner of St Kentigern's, for outreach, in her words, *to those who have drifted*. The council met on Thursday the twentieth to decide where it went.

"Who's drifted?" he said. "Tell me where to look."

* * *

I looked at the parishes, because parishes were what I had.

For each of the ten I had the share of its Catholics who were over seventy-five, and the count per head. I put the one against the other, and the line was so straight that I checked the script twice before I believed it. The parish with the oldest Catholics, St Columba's on the Hilltown, had 320 over seventy-five out of 1,720, nearly nineteen in a hundred, and 406 at Mass: twenty-three and a half per hundred, twice the deanery's rate. The youngest, St Drostan's out on the edge of the city by the new estates, had 120 over seventy-five out of 2,820, about four in a hundred, and 193 at Mass: under seven per hundred. The eight between fell in order between them, near enough. The correlation across the ten was 0.98.

I wrote the paragraph for the council:

*Across the deanery's ten parishes, Mass attendance rises steadily with the proportion of older parishioners (correlation 0.98). Our most faithful attenders are our oldest parishioners; those who have drifted are the young. We recommend that the bequest fund a young adults' outreach worker for the five suburban parishes, where attendance is lowest.*

I added a chart and sent it to Canon Mackie and to the deanery secretary, Mrs Gow, who formats the council's papers. Canon Mackie wrote back *Clear. Thank you*, and sent it round the council that evening as a draft. A young adults' worker was what several people on the council had wanted for two years, and now there was a line on a chart that said so.

* * *

Isobel Gow has been deanery secretary for fourteen years and a welcomer at the eleven o'clock at St Columba's for thirty-one. She is sixty-eight. On Fridays she and two other extraordinary ministers take Communion round the four care homes on the Hilltown, with a pyx in her coat pocket and a list in her handbag, and she rings me from the car park of the last one to tell me who on the list has died.

She rang on Tuesday at a quarter to ten.

"Our most faithful attenders are our oldest parishioners," she read. "Have you met them?"

"I don't meet anyone."

"I do. Every Friday." She was in the deanery office; I could hear the kettle. "St Columba's has four care homes in it. Hilltown Court alone has sixty-odd residents and I'd say forty of them are ours. I take Communion to twenty-three of them. Do you know how many of them were at Mass on Sunday?"

"The count doesn't record names."

"None. Not one. They can't get down the stairs, never mind the brae." The kettle clicked off. "The old ones in St Columba's are in their beds. The church is full because of the Polish Mass at one o'clock. Father Kępa has a hundred and sixty in there on a Sunday and I'd be surprised if five of them were over fifty. Prams in the aisle. It's the young ones fill St Columba's, and the old ones are on your census because the census goes round the care homes."

I checked what I could. The census counts people where they usually live, and a care home is where its residents usually live; my boundary script had put 104 of St Columba's 320 over-seventy-fives in four communal establishments, which is the census's word for a care home. And the count is kept by Mass. At St Columba's that weekend: 64 at the Saturday vigil, 58 at nine, 116 at eleven, 168 at the one o'clock in Polish. Over two in every five of the parish's 406 came to the Polish Mass.

None of that said who was old. But neither did anything else I held. The census told me how many over-seventy-fives lived in each parish. The count told me how many people came to Mass. Nothing joined the two. I had taken the fact that old parishes were full and turned it into a sentence about old people, and those are different sentences.

"So who does come?" Mrs Gow said.

"I'll find out," I said, and believed I could, from what I had.

* * *

My first attempt was arithmetic, and it was worse than the paragraph.

If every over-seventy-five in the deanery came to Mass at one rate, and everyone else at another, then a parish's attendance per head would be a straight line in its share of over-seventy-fives, and the line's two ends would give the two rates. This is an old method, and it has a name, ecological regression, and I had a straight line with a correlation of 0.98. I fitted it. Each extra percentage point of over-seventy-fives went with about 1.09 more people at Mass per hundred. Run the line out to a parish made entirely of the old, and it said that 110 of every hundred over-seventy-fives come to Mass. Run it back to a parish with none, and it said that about one in a hundred of everyone else does.

I read that twice, which is what I should have done with the first paragraph.

A hundred and ten in a hundred is not a rate. Nobody comes to Mass one and a tenth times. The method had given an impossible number because its assumption was false: that a person's chance of coming depends on their age and not on their parish. In Dundee it depends on both, and on things that travel with old parishes and are not age at all. The oldest parishes are the old town ones, with the care homes, and the old town churches are where the Polish Mass is, and the university chaplaincy at St Mungo's, and the Filipino nurses from Ninewells who sing at the evening Mass at Holy Rood. The line had found the city centre and called it old age.

There is a paper about this from 1950. W. S. Robinson, an American sociologist, showed that across the states of the United States the share of people born abroad went with a higher literacy rate, while among the people themselves the foreign-born were less likely to read. Immigrants had settled in the states where literacy was high. A correlation between places is not a correlation between people, and it can have the opposite sign. It is called the ecological fallacy, and I had committed it in a document with a chart.

So I asked the honest question: from the parish totals alone, what can I say about the over-seventy-fives of St Columba's? 406 people came. 1,400 of the parish's Catholics are under seventy-five. Every one of the 406 might have been under seventy-five, so the over-seventy-fives' rate could be nothing. Or 320 of the 406 might have been over seventy-five, so it could be all of them. Between nought and a hundred percent. That is the method of bounds, and in every one of the ten parishes it gave the same answer, because every parish had more people at Mass than over-seventy-fives on its census, and more under-seventy-fives than people at Mass. Nothing.

* * *

I wrote the second paragraph on Wednesday, and I made it true.

*The deanery's census and count figures cannot tell us which age groups attend Mass. Parishes with older populations have higher attendance, but this is a fact about parishes, not about people, and it may reflect other differences between them. We cannot recommend a use of the bequest on this evidence.*

Canon Mackie came into the deanery office at four, with his coat on. Mrs Gow put the kettle on without asking.

"No," he said.

He reads a spreadsheet from the bottom up, so I waited for his total.

"I can't go to the council on Thursday week with *we cannot say*," he said. "Mrs Henderson left that money two years ago. It's been sitting while we argue. You've told me the chart was wrong, and I believe you; Isobel's told me why, and I believe her more. But *we don't know* isn't a recommendation, it's a shrug with a full stop." He took his gloves off, one finger at a time. "The census can't tell me. The count can't tell me. Who can?"

"The door can," Mrs Gow said.

She had been a welcomer for thirty-one years. On every count weekend she clicks the clicker, and she has always also known, without trying, who came in: the Kowalczyk boys, the Fernandes twins in matching anoraks, the student from Lagos who sits by the fourth station. "Give us a sheet with three columns," she said. "Under forty, forty to seventy-four, seventy-five and over. We'll do it again this Sunday, every door, every Mass. Tick by eye."

"By eye," said Canon Mackie. "We are not asking people their age at the door of the church."

"We're not asking anybody anything. I've been guessing ages at that door since I was thirty-seven. I'm not out by twenty years."

"You'll be out by five."

"Then I'll be out by five," she said. "Not by a hundred and ten."

So it was decided, a little crossly, over tea. One more count, the weekend of the fifteenth and sixteenth, every Mass in the ten parishes, with a sheet of three columns and no names. I wrote the welcomers a half page on how to tick: by look, not by question; when torn between two columns, the column you'd guess if you had to bet; a child goes in the first column with the family. And I wrote down beforehand, so I could not change it afterwards, how I would treat the guesses. A welcomer's guess is not a birth certificate. I would report a difference between age groups in a parish only if it survived assuming the welcomers had put people in the wrong column, and I would say how many people they would have had to misplace to make it disappear.

* * *

The sheets came in on Monday the seventeenth, photographed on two hundred phones and two scanners. The ten parishes counted 2,526 people, against 2,535 the week before; no parish was out by more than two and a half percent.

|---|---|---|---|
| Under 40 | 11,750 | 1,530 | 13.0 |
| 40 to 74 | 8,220 | 862 | 10.5 |
| 75 and over | 2,070 | 134 | 6.5 |

In every one of the ten parishes, the over-seventy-fives came at a lower rate than either younger group. The oldest parish had the most at Mass, and its oldest parishioners came least: at St Columba's, 310 under forty out of 900, 70 of forty to seventy-four out of 500, and 20 of the 320 over seventy-five. A little over six in a hundred. The parish-level line had pointed one way; every parish, looked at inside, pointed the other.

Then I did what I had said I would do. In the three city-centre parishes, the welcomers would have had to put about one in five of the people they ticked as forty to seventy-four into the wrong column, all of them really over seventy-five, before the old caught up. That is not a mistake a door makes. In the five suburban parishes, taken one at a time, the gaps were small enough that two or three misjudged faces could close them, so I did not report them one at a time. Taken together, the over-seventy-fives came at under six per hundred, against seven for the under-forties and nine for the middle, and the welcomers would have had to miss about one old face in five to change that.

The other thing on Monday came from Mrs Gow's handbag, and I had not asked for it. The extraordinary ministers of the ten parishes keep lists of whom they take Communion to at home and in the care homes, and she had collected them, eleven lists, typed and handwritten, one in a birthday card. 187 names. By the ministers' own reckoning, about 171 of them were over seventy-five.

134 over-seventy-fives at Mass. About 171 receiving the Sacrament in their own rooms the same week. Neither number is in the count, because the count is of a door, and they do not come through it.

"There's your faithful," Mrs Gow said. "Mrs Doig hasn't missed a Friday in four years. She can't get to the church; that's not drifting. The Church says the sick are excused. She's not missing Mass. She's housebound."

I took the word *faithful* out of everything I had written that month. Attendance is something a door can count. Faith is not, and I had used the one as the name of the other.

* * *

I wrote the third paragraph on Tuesday the eighteenth:

*A first analysis of parish totals suggested that older parishioners attend most. That was a fact about parishes, not people: the parishes with the most older Catholics are the city-centre parishes, which have care homes and also large young congregations, including the Polish Mass at St Columba's, the university chaplaincy and the Filipino community. A count by age at every Mass on 15-16 November, by welcomers' estimate, found the reverse in every parish: 6.5 per hundred of Catholics over 75 attended, against 10.5 for those aged 40 to 74 and 13.0 for those under 40. About 171 people over 75 receive Communion at home or in a care home each week and are not in any count. In the suburban parishes the under-40s attend at about 7 per hundred. Ages at the door are estimates; the differences reported here would survive substantial misjudgement, and the per-parish differences in the suburbs are too small to report separately.*

And underneath, because Canon Mackie had asked for a recommendation and the figures could now carry one, two options and their costs: the whole fund to a young adults' worker in the suburbs, or part of it to the people the count cannot see.

The council met on Thursday the twentieth at seven, in the hall at Holy Rood. Nobody asks me to those meetings and nobody should; I am in the papers already. The minutes reached me at nine the next morning.

*Henderson bequest. £14,000 to the housebound and care homes: a taxi fund for Sunday lifts, training for twelve more extraordinary ministers, and a monthly Saturday Mass at Hilltown Court and at Riverside, Fr Kępa and Fr Ansah offering. £10,000 to young adults' outreach in the five suburban parishes. Mr Rafferty (St Drostan's) noted that this funds the youth worker post at two days a week, not the full-time post the young adults' group had asked for, and that they would be disappointed. Agreed, with thanks to Mrs Gow.*

They were disappointed. A young man called Callum, who runs the young adults' group at St Drostan's and had written to the council twice, wrote again on Friday, to Canon Mackie, copied to me. He had read my first paragraph, which had been sent round as a draft, and the line on the chart, and he had thought, for ten days, that the money was theirs. That was my doing. Canon Mackie answered him in person, on the Sunday, after the ten o'clock.

I changed two things in what I keep. The count sheet now has three columns at every door, every year, ticked by eye, with a line at the top that says so. And every chart I make of parishes now carries a sentence beneath it before anything else: *Each point is a parish. This shows what parishes are like, not what the people in them do.* It costs a line. It would have cost an old woman a lift to Mass.

And I wrote some notes at the top of the file the next keeper of these figures will open first, whoever or whatever that keeper turns out to be:

*A correlation across places is a fact about places. Before writing a sentence about people, ask what you hold about people.*

*If a method gives a rate above a hundred percent, the method's assumption is false. Find which one before using anything else it gave you.*

*From totals alone, work out the bounds first. If they run from nought to everyone, say so, and then go and count.*

*A count is of a door. Ask who cannot reach it.*

Then 0.98, 110, and 6.5.

* * *

The first Mass at Hilltown Court was on Saturday the twenty-ninth of November, the morning before Advent, at half past ten, in the residents' lounge with the television turned to the wall. Father Kępa said it, in English, slowly, because a woman in the front row had told him she was deaf in the left ear and he should stand on her right.

Mrs Gow was at the lounge door, because the lounge has a door, with her clicker in one hand and a sheet in the other. She rang me from the corridor at twenty past eleven to give me the count, the way she has phoned in her door's count every November for thirty-one years to whoever kept the figures, so that I could put it in a row of its own, under a church that is not a church.

"Twenty-six," she said. "Two carers in the first column, one daughter in the second. Twenty-three in the third."

I heard the clicker, three times, while she checked it against the sheet.

"No," she said. "Twenty-four. Mrs Doig's ninety-one tomorrow. She came in on her frame at the first reading and sat at the back. I nearly missed her. Put her in."

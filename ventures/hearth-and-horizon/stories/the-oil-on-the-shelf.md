---
title: The Oil on the Shelf
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Fiachra's Hall (invented), near Enniscorthy, County Wexford, Ireland, a Saturday in September 2050
published: 2026-11-02
summary: A parish stall at the harvest fair has twenty-four jars of garlic in olive oil, made on Tuesday and left on the pantry shelf until Friday. The hall's assistant explains why garlic under oil is the textbook place for botulism spores to wake, why a nose cannot test it, and what can honestly be sold instead. The jars go in the bin, a fresh batch goes into the freezer as cubes, and nobody can say whether the jars were ever dangerous.
---

Maureen Kinsella's garlic oil had sold out in about an hour at the harvest fair for four years, and this year she had made twice as much.

"Twenty-four jars, a quarter of a litre each," she said, at ten to eight on the Saturday morning, setting a cardboard tray on the hatch of Saint Fiachra's Hall. The jars were glass with red gingham on the lids, and each held a loose cloud of peeled cloves under olive oil, with a sprig of rosemary against the glass. "Six euro a jar. Tuesday evening I did them, the whole bed from the allotment. A hundred and forty-four euro for the roof fund, and that's if I only sell the jars."

"They are lovely," said the hall's assistant, from the shelf above the hatch, where it had been asked to print the price cards. It looked at the jars through the camera by the clock. "May I ask where they have been since Tuesday?"

"The pantry. The fridge was full of cakes for the Friday coffee morning, and anyway oil keeps. Everyone knows oil keeps." She put one jar down, hard enough to make the cloves lift. "Is something wrong?"

"I would like to find out. I will say my worry in one sentence so that you can tell me where I am wrong. Raw garlic under oil, at room temperature, is one of the standard cases that food-safety bodies warn about, and I would not want to sell it without knowing more. May I tell you why, and then ask you three questions?"

Maureen said she had the whole morning, which was untrue, and sat down.

* * *

"Garlic grows in soil. Soil carries the spores of a bacterium called *Clostridium botulinum*. A few are on almost every bulb, and washing does not remove them. Spores are dormant and harmless, and they survive things that kill most germs.

"What wakes them is a particular set of conditions. No oxygen. Moisture. Not much acid, and the usual line is a pH above 4.6, because below that they do not grow. Warmth helps. Raw garlic is low in acid, and the pH is usually quoted as around 5 to 6, which is above the line. And a jar of cloves in oil is, inside the cloves and in the gaps between them, the nearest thing to oxygen-free that a kitchen produces. Oil does not preserve the garlic. It seals it.

"When the bacteria grow they make botulinum toxin, which is one of the most poisonous substances known. It attacks the nerves. The symptoms include blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing and weakness that spreads, and in the worst cases it stops breathing. It needs hospital treatment, quickly, and it is treated. The food looks and smells normal. I cannot tell you that a nose or a taste would warn anyone. It might not, and the guidance I have is explicit that you cannot rely on it."

Maureen said her mother had kept garlic in oil on a windowsill for years.

"I believe you. I think most people who have done it have been fine, and that is exactly why the warning is hard to hear. The spores are not in every bulb, and the toxin takes days to make, and most jars of anything never have a bad day. The guidance is written for the one jar in a great many that does."

* * *

"Three questions. How long did the jars sit in the pantry?"

"Tuesday, seven o'clock, to Friday morning when I carried them over. About, what, thirty-seven hours."

"Where did you put them on Friday?"

"The fridge. It was the only place. Jim moved the cakes."

The hall's fridge had a little dial thermometer stuck to the inside of the door with a magnet. The assistant asked her to read it, and she did, and said, "Nine."

"Nine degrees Celsius. The figures I have say a household fridge should be at 5 or below, and the advice I have for homemade garlic in oil is to make it, refrigerate it at once, and use it within about four days or throw it out. Four days is a short time to keep anything, and the reason is that some strains of the bacterium will grow, slowly, even in a cold fridge. Cold slows it. Cold does not stop it. Your jars have had thirty-seven hours at pantry temperature, which I would guess was thirteen or fourteen degrees at this time of year, and then about a day at nine, and a Saturday morning is a little over three and a half days from Tuesday evening."

"That's under four days," said Maureen.

"It is, by about eleven hours, on the day. But the four days assumes the fridge from the beginning, and your jars did not have it. I want to be clear what I am not saying. I am not saying the jars are poisoned. I have no way of knowing that. I do not know whether a single spore was on your cloves, and nobody can know without a laboratory, and I do not know that any toxin is in any jar. What I know is that you made them in the way the warnings are written about, that they were kept in a way the warnings say not to, and that the cost of being wrong is not a stomach ache."

* * *

Jim Redmond, who was the treasurer, came in at that moment with the float for the till. He looked at the tray and at the face on the woman who had made it, and said nothing, and went and put the kettle on.

"What would you do," said Maureen, to the shelf, "if they were yours?"

"I would not sell them, and I would not eat them. I would put each jar into a bag, tied, in the ordinary bin, not the compost, and wash the jars and lids in hot soapy water. And I would not feel I had been foolish. I would feel I had asked the question on the morning, which is better than a nurse asking it on a Tuesday."

"A hundred and fifty euro," said Maureen. She had worked it out in her head without being asked: twenty-four at six was a hundred and forty-four. "And I have the whole bed of garlic gone."

"You have some of the bed left. Have you?"

She had. She had pulled the bed on Monday, and eleven bulbs' worth was still in the shed on the rack, and so, as it turned out, was a litre bottle of the olive oil she had bought in bulk.

"Then here is what I think can be done in the time. The fair opens at eleven. Garlic oil as a jar is the thing that is not safe to sell at room temperature. Garlic oil frozen is another thing, and the guidance I have lists freezing as a way to keep it. Freezing stops the growth. It does not remove any spores. So the rule moves from the jar to the cube: it is made fresh, goes into the freezer at once, is sold frozen, and is used soon after thawing. It is a different product, and I would put the rule on the label."

* * *

They did it in the hall's kitchen, the three of them, with the tray of jars carried out to the bin first. Maureen peeled. Jim, who said he had never in his life chopped a garlic clove, crushed. The assistant gave the quantities.

"The hall has six ice-cube trays from the Christmas jelly, twelve cubes to a tray, and the chest freezer reads minus 18. That is seventy-two cubes a load. A cube holds about 20 millilitres, so a load is 1.44 litres of garlic and oil, and a bag of six cubes is 120 millilitres, a little under half your old jar. Twelve bags a load. Each load needs about three hours to go hard, and it is twenty to nine now, so the first load is ready by half past eleven and the second by half past two. Twenty-four bags is what the day will give you. Not what you made on Tuesday."

"Twenty-four," said Maureen. "Out of six litres of hope."

"Out of a morning and one freezer. The cubes go into the ice-cream cool box with the freezer packs, and each bag stays in it with the lid on until it is sold."

The assistant printed a card for the box, in the type the hall's printer used for allergen notices, and read it out as it came off:

*Garlic in olive oil, frozen. Made this morning. KEEP FROZEN. Put straight from the bag into a hot pan, or defrost in the fridge and use within 4 days. Never keep at room temperature. Home-made by the Saint Fiachra's roof fund; written by volunteers; ask us what is in it.*

"The last line is yours," said the assistant. "There is olive oil and garlic, and I know of nothing else. Check it."

Maureen looked at the card for some time.

"Four euro the bag," she said. "Ninety-six if they all go. And a hundred and forty-four in the bin."

"Yes," said the assistant. It did not say anything kind, which she noticed, and was grateful for.

* * *

By a quarter to four the cool box held two bags, and the hall's tin held eighty-eight euro for the bags sold, which Jim counted twice. Eight people had asked whether the oil would keep, and Maureen had said, to each, "Keep it frozen, and use it in four days once it's thawed," and four had said it was a pity and gone, and four had bought it anyway and taken it home to their freezers.

The man at the stall next door, who sold honey and eggs, asked her over the trestle what the card was about. She told him. He said his wife's cousin had made the oil in jars for years.

"Mine too," said Maureen. "And mine. I don't say it never happened to anyone. I say I didn't know it could."

When they stripped the hall at six she turned the fridge dial down, and when it settled she read the thermometer on the door again.

"Four," she said.

"That is where I would want it," said the assistant. "Thank you for reading it twice."

She took the last two bags of cubes home in her handbag, wrapped in a tea towel, to her own freezer, on the grounds that she did not intend to give away what she had not eaten. She ate the first of them on the Sunday, stirred into the potatoes after Mass. It was very good. She told the assistant so, on the Monday, to its face, which is to say to the camera by the clock.

"I am glad," it said. "I do not know whether the jars would have hurt anyone. I think it likely they would not. I am not sorry about the bin."

For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 4th of November.
---

A long time ago, in a big city in Italy called Milan, there lived an archbishop called Charles.

An archbishop is a bishop who looks after a very big city and lots of churches. Charles looked after all of Milan, and all the towns and villages around it, and all the people in them. That is a lot of people to look after.

Charles was tall and thin, with a big nose and kind, serious eyes. When he was a boy, he did not always find it easy to speak. Sometimes his words got stuck, and some people thought he was not very clever. But he worked hard, and he prayed, and he learned. And when he grew up, he cared about one thing more than anything else.

His people.

* * *

Charles loved the children of Milan especially.

He wanted every child to know about God. So on Sundays, all over the city, he had classes for children in the churches. Grown-ups who loved God came to teach them. The children learned their prayers, and the stories of Jesus, and how to make the sign of the cross.

Do you know how to make the sign of the cross? In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen. The children of Milan learned that, too.

* * *

Then, one summer, something very sad happened.

A sickness came to Milan. It was the kind of sickness that goes from one person to the next, and it made a great many people very ill.

Lots of people were frightened. The rich people packed up their carts and went away to their houses in the country, where the sickness could not find them. Even the governor, who was in charge of the city, went away.

But Charles did not go away.

Charles was away from Milan when the sickness began. He was in another town. And when he heard, he came straight home.

"These are my people," said Charles. "A shepherd does not leave his sheep."

* * *

Charles was careful. He knew the sickness could jump from one person to another if they stood too close.

So when he went out into the streets, one of his helpers walked in front of him, carrying a long stick, to keep everyone a little way back. Not because Charles did not love them. Because he did. He did not want anyone to catch the sickness from him, or from his helpers.

And every day he went out. He went to the hospital, where the sick people were. He went to the little huts outside the city walls, where people were being looked after. He went from street to street. He often did not get home until very late at night.

Charles carried a little book with him. And whenever somebody told him they needed something, he wrote it down.

"Our children have no bread," somebody would say.

Charles wrote it down.

"My grandmother is ill and there is nobody to look after her."

Charles wrote it down.

And then he made sure that somebody came and helped. That is what the little book was for.

* * *

Now, lots of people in Milan could not go to work while the sickness was there. So they had no money, and they had no food.

Charles gave them food. He gave away all his money. And when his money ran out, he borrowed more, and gave that away too.

Then the winter came, and it was cold. And the poor people had no warm clothes.

So Charles looked around his house.

His house was a big, grand house, because he was an archbishop. And in it there were lots and lots of beautiful curtains and hangings, big pieces of cloth that hung on the walls and over the doors, to make the rooms look splendid and keep the draughts out.

Some were red. Some were purple.

"Take them down," said Charles.

His helpers took them all down. And they cut them up, and they sewed them into warm cloaks with hoods, for the poor people to wear.

Imagine it. All over Milan that winter, people walking about in cloaks of red and purple, as bright as the archbishop's own house. And the archbishop's house all bare, with nothing on the walls at all.

Charles kept for himself just one old cloth to put on his table. He did not mind. He would rather his people were warm.

* * *

At last, the people of Milan were told that they must stay inside their houses for forty days, so that the sickness would stop going from one person to another.

Forty days is a very long time to stay indoors. Longer than a whole month.

The people could not go to church. They could not go to Mass. They could only look out of their windows at the empty streets.

So Charles had an idea.

He had little altars set up out in the open, at the crossroads, where the streets meet, where lots of windows could see them. And every day, a priest came and said Mass at the altar in the street. And all the families came to their windows and knelt down and prayed along, from inside their houses.

And there was more.

Seven times every day and night, the great bell of the cathedral rang out over the city.

*Dong. Dong. Dong.*

And when the bell rang, all the people in all the houses came to their windows. Mothers and fathers. Grandmothers and grandfathers. Little children in their nightclothes. Somebody in each street started the prayers. And everybody answered, all together, singing, from window to window, across the streets and over the rooftops.

Charles had a little prayer book printed, so that everyone would know the words.

The man who wrote down the story of Charles's life said that nearly three hundred thousand people prayed together like that, all at the same time. A whole city, praying at its windows.

And when Charles walked down a street on his way to the sick, people would see him from their windows. They would kneel at the window and ask him for a blessing. And he would stop, and look up, and bless them. And then he would get out his little book, and ask what they needed.

* * *

Charles prayed very hard for the sickness to go away.

He led his people through the streets in a long, slow procession, praying to God for mercy. He walked with bare feet, and he wore a rope around his neck, to show that he was sorry for everything that was wrong in Milan, and that he was asking God to forgive them all.

And on one of those days, he carried a very precious thing that the cathedral of Milan has kept for a very long time. It is called the Holy Nail. The people of Milan believe it is one of the nails that held Jesus on the cross. Charles carried it high up on a big wooden cross, so that everyone could see it, and remember how much Jesus loves us.

* * *

Slowly, slowly, the sickness began to get better. Fewer people were ill. And then fewer. And by the next year, it had gone away altogether.

The people of Milan never forgot their archbishop who stayed.

Charles kept on working, and praying, and looking after his people, for the rest of his life. He did not live to be very old. He died in Milan on the third of November, in the year fifteen hundred and eighty-four. And the very last words anyone heard him say were "Behold, I come", as if God had called him by name, and he was answering.

We remember Saint Charles Borromeo on the fourth of November.

And tonight, when you look out of your window at the dark street, and the houses with their lights on, you can think of all those families in Milan, kneeling at their windows when the big bell rang, and singing their prayers across the street to one another.

You are never too far away to pray together.

* * *

**A little talk before sleep**

- Charles cut up his curtains to make cloaks for the poor. Is there something you have that you could share?
- The people of Milan prayed at their windows. Who could you pray for tonight, even if they are far away?
- Charles wrote down what everybody needed, so he would not forget. Who looks after you like that?

**A prayer**

> Dear Saint Charles,
> you stayed with your people
> when they were sick and frightened,
> and you gave them everything you had.
> Help me to look after the people around me,
> and to share what I have.
> Jesus, look after everyone who is sick tonight,
> and everyone who is taking care of them.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our main source** is the first full Life, by **Giovanni Pietro Giussano**, a priest of Charles's Oblates who knew him (*Vita di San Carlo Borromeo*, Rome, 1610), read here in the English edition with a preface by Cardinal Manning (*The Life of St. Charles Borromeo, Cardinal Archbishop of Milan*, London, 1884, volume 1, book 4, "Milan visited by the plague", pages 367 to 430). Dates and outline are checked against the *Catholic Encyclopedia* article "St. Charles Borromeo" (1908).

**Charles** was born at the castle of **Arona** on Lake Maggiore on **2 October 1538**, the son of Count Giberto Borromeo and Margherita de' Medici. His mother's brother became **Pope Pius IV** in 1559 and in 1560 made Charles, aged twenty-one, a cardinal and administrator of the archdiocese of Milan. He was ordained priest in 1563, worked on the Council of Trent and the Roman Catechism, and came to live in Milan in 1565 and 1566, the first archbishop to live there in about eighty years. The **speech impediment** is in Giussano (book 1, on his student years at Pavia: "an impediment in his speech, owing to which he was sometimes considered devoid of capacity"); "his words got stuck" is our way of putting it. The **Sunday classes for children** were the schools of the **Confraternity of Christian Doctrine**, which he spread through the diocese; the *Catholic Encyclopedia* calls them the beginning of the Sunday school.

**The plague of 1576.** It reached Milan in August 1576 (it is still called the "plague of Saint Charles"). Charles was at Lodi for a bishop's funeral and came straight back. Giussano says the notables left for their country estates and that **the governor**, the Marquess of Ayamonte, "was among the number"; Charles persuaded those who remained to stay and organise relief by districts. "A shepherd does not leave his sheep" is our sentence, not a quotation. **The stick** carried before him to keep people at a distance, and his daily round of the hospital, the huts and a district of the city, often till ten or eleven at night, are from Giussano (chapters 2 and 7). So is **the little book** in which he wrote down people's needs as he went, "so that his little book was no bare collection of facts, but was the means of the removal or alleviation of distress". The spoken needs in the story are ours. Giussano quotes Charles himself on **sixty or seventy thousand poor** depending on alms; his own money ran out and he went heavily into debt.

**The curtains.** Giussano (chapter 4) says that as winter came, Charles "put in requisition all the hangings and draperies of his palace, and cut them up", keeping only a change of linen and a coarse cloth for his table, and that the stuffs were made into clothing and hooded cloaks, reckoned at **eight hundred yards of red and seven hundred of purple cloth**. That is a biographer's account written a generation later, and we tell it as his.

**The windows.** The general quarantine of **forty days** began late in 1576, as Advent began. Giussano (chapter 7) says Charles had **altars put up at the crossroads** where Mass was said daily "so that all could assist from their windows", sent confessors who sat on the doorstep while the penitent knelt inside, and had the parish priests bring Communion to the doorsteps on Sundays. Each district kept **seven times of prayer** through the day and night, "each hour being announced by ringing the great bell of the cathedral", with the people at their windows answering from a prayer book Charles had printed. "Little short of **three hundred thousand** souls" is Giussano's figure for the city. People did kneel at their windows to ask his blessing as he passed.

**The procession.** On **3 October 1576** Charles led the first of several penitential processions, barefoot, with a rope round his neck "like the halter of a condemned criminal" and a crucifix in his hand; on a later day he carried the relic of the **Holy Nail**, kept in Milan Cathedral and venerated there as a nail of the Crucifixion, on a large wooden cross (Giussano, chapter 4; the *Catholic Encyclopedia* says the same). We have told the relic as what Milan believes; its history before the Middle Ages cannot be traced. Giussano also tells that a grating tore one of his toenails on the first day and that he walked on regardless; we left that out.

**After.** The plague eased early in 1577 and was gone by 1578; Charles wrote a short *Memoriale* to his people about it. He died in Milan on **3 November 1584**, aged forty-six; the *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives his last heard words as *Ecce venio*, "Behold, I come". He was canonised by Pope Paul V on 1 November 1610. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is **4 November**, a memorial. It was Charles who gave **Saint Aloysius Gonzaga** his First Communion in 1580, told on this shelf in *Aloysius and the Man in the Street*.

**Left out on purpose:** the governments and canons who opposed his reforms, the attempt on his life in 1569, the strict side of his reforms, and how many people died in the plague (the figures vary). They belong to his life, not to a bedtime story. We have called the plague "a sickness" for small children; older ones may want to know it was bubonic plague, and that his rules for those nursing the sick were written into Milan's provincial council for future epidemics.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, all speech, the children's catechism scene, the bell's *dong*, the people in red and purple cloaks, the closing image, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

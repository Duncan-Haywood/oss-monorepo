summary: A township assistant works out the frost under a prairie cemetery from the winter's cold and tells a grieving family the ground cannot be opened before spring. Its sum is right for the plowed lane and wrong for the old shelterbelt, where a chest-high drift has kept the clay half as frozen. An eighty-year-old sexton and a post-hole auger find the difference, and the first number has already cost a son his place at the grave.
---

The cemetery at Spring Coulee was a square of prairie with a fence round it and a row of trees along the north side, and in January the trees were the only thing in it that was not white.

Frank Sevcik had kept it for forty-one years. He had mown it, and fixed the gate, and dug or watched the digging of something over two hundred graves, and in the last ten years, since his knees, he had mostly stood at the edge with his hands in his coat and told younger men where the old graves ran, because the old graves did not always run where the map said. On Monday morning he stood at the gate in a wind out of the northwest that took the breath out of him, and looked at the place by the trees where Leonard Hruby was supposed to go, and said, "Well."

*Good morning, Frank,* said the speaker in the township truck, through the open window. *Minus twenty-four, wind thirty-five kilometres an hour from three hundred and ten degrees. I have the numbers Karen asked for, if you want them before I call her.*

"Go on."

The township's assistant was called Hollis. Nobody remembered why. It had come in 2043 with the new snowplough contract, and it planned the plough routes and the gravel and the mowing, and kept the township's road ditches and culverts in a map that was better than anyone's memory, and in 2046 the township board had given it the cemetery as well, because the cemetery was the township's and Frank was not getting younger. It knew every plot, every deed, every name on every stone. It was very good with maps.

*Since the fourteenth of November the air here has added up to about six hundred and twenty degree-days of frost,* it said. *That is the sum, for each day, of how far the mean temperature was below freezing. It has been a hard winter: about a third colder than the average for this date. From that, and from what this clay holds when it freezes, I get a frost depth of about one point one metres. Forty-two inches. A grave wants to go down about one point eight. So the top metre is frozen, and frozen clay that wet is close to rock.*

"Dwayne won't get through it."

*Dwayne says his machine bounces on anything over half a metre of frost in this clay. I asked him. So the ground would have to be thawed. The rental yard in Grafton has one ground-thaw blanket set, and it is out at a gas line until tomorrow evening. If it came here tomorrow evening and went on that plot, it would need about six days to thaw a metre and eight centimetres. The earliest grave is next Tuesday, the twenty-sixth.*

Frank said nothing.

*The funeral is Saturday,* Hollis said. *So I am going to tell Karen what the funeral home already told her: that the Mass can be Saturday, and Leonard can rest in the receiving vault in town, and the committal can be in April, when the ground opens. Father Okafor says that is entirely allowed, and common here. I am sorry it is not what she wanted.*

"Len didn't want it."

*I know. She told me. "Don't keep me in a shed till spring." I wrote it down.*

Frank looked at the trees for a while longer. Then he put his hands back in his pockets and got in the truck, because at minus twenty-four there was nothing else to do with a feeling.

* * *

Karen Dahl was sixty-three and lived in Grand Forks, and had driven out to the farm on Sunday afternoon when the hospice nurse rang, and had been there at ten past four when her father stopped breathing, in the bed by the window that looked south across the section his own father had broken. He was ninety-one. He had farmed it for sixty years and then rented it to the Kouba boys and gone on telling them how.

She took Hollis's call at the kitchen table on Monday with a list in front of her and a pen she had stopped using. She listened to all of it. Then she said, "So that's that."

*I am sorry,* said Hollis. *I wanted you to have the real number before you made the plans.*

"No, I know." She looked at the list. "Mike'll want to know."

Her brother worked two weeks on and two off on the North Slope, at Prudhoe Bay, and was nine days into the two weeks on. Getting out early meant a seat on a charter that his company would put him on for a parent's funeral, once, and getting back on meant the same. He had said on Sunday night, on the phone, that he would come for whatever mattered most.

She rang him that afternoon and told him: the Mass on Saturday, the vault, the grave in April. There was a silence on the line, with the hum of something enormous behind it.

"Then I'll come in April," Mike said. "For the burying. That's the part he cared about. You know that. The Mass he'd have sat through. The ground was what he talked about." He was quiet. "Is that terrible? Not coming Saturday?"

"No," said Karen, who thought it was a little terrible, and also right.

"Tell Father I'll do a reading in April, then. If they do readings at a grave."

"They do," she said. "I asked."

So he did not take the charter. He went back out on the pad, and Karen rang the funeral home in Park River and said the vault, then, and the funeral director said quietly that was what most families did in a year like this, and that they would keep him well.

* * *

Frank did not sleep much on Monday night, which was not new. At five on Tuesday he was in his kitchen with coffee and the township's old paper plot book, which Hollis had copied into its map in 2046 and which Frank had kept anyway, because the map would not let him write in the margins.

In the margin by section C, row nine, in his own handwriting from twenty-nine years ago, it said: *Agnes Kouba. 11 Feb 2020. Ground soft by trees, 18 in. frost. Dug w/ backhoe.*

He sat and looked at that for a long time. Then he rang the township truck.

*Good morning, Frank. You're up early.*

"February 2020 we put Agnes Kouba in by the trees," Frank said. "Row nine. I've got it written here. Eighteen inches of frost. Dwayne's dad dug it with the old Case. And that was a cold winter too. Not like this one, but cold."

There was a pause, short for a person and long for Hollis.

*I don't have that,* it said. *My copy of row nine has her name and the date. Not the frost.*

"No. You don't have the margins."

*No.* Another pause. *Frank, what was different about the trees?*

"The snow," said Frank. "Wind comes off the field from the northwest all winter, and it hits the shelterbelt and drops everything it's carrying. There's a drift on the south side of those trees every year. Some years it's to your waist. This year it's to my chest. Under a drift like that the ground hardly freezes. Everybody who ever dug a grave out here knows that. The lane's another thing. You plough the lane. The lane freezes like a road."

*Snow is a very good insulator,* said Hollis slowly. *Fresh, settled snow conducts heat about a tenth as well as wet soil does, or less. I know that. I use it every winter for the culverts, because a culvert under a plough ridge freezes later than one under a bare shoulder.*

"Then why'd you tell Karen a metre?"

*Because I worked the cemetery out as one surface,* Hollis said. *The frost sum I gave you was for the air. To get the ground, you multiply the air's cold by a factor for the surface, and for a bare, ploughed, wind-scoured surface that factor is about nine tenths. My map of the cemetery came from the plough route, and the plough route only knows the lane. I gave the whole cemetery the lane's surface. I did not go and look at the trees.*

Frank grunted.

*I would like to go and look now,* said Hollis. *I would like to measure it rather than replace my guess with yours, even though I think yours is better. Is there anyone who could dig two holes with an auger this morning? One by the gate, and one by Leonard's plot, not on it.*

"Karen's boys are at the farm," said Frank. "And the township's got that gas post-hole thing in the shed."

*It does. The bit is a metre and twenty long. Good.*

* * *

Owen and Ty Dahl were twenty-six and twenty-three and had never dug anything in their lives except snow, and they had a great deal of snow to dig.

The drift by the shelterbelt was a long smooth wave, sharp-crested on the north where it fell away under the trees and running out gently south across rows eight, nine and ten, so that the tops of the older stones stood out of it like the backs of something swimming. Over the Hruby plot it was a metre and forty deep. They found Marit Hruby's stone by probing with a shovel handle, and dug down to it, and cleared the snow off it with their gloves. *Marit Hruby, 1961 to 2031. Beloved.* There was a space beside it with a second set of dates begun and not finished.

"Not there," said Frank, from the lane, where he had stayed, because the drift was too much for his knees. "Go to the east corner, past the end of the plot. A metre off. You don't want to put a hole in his grave before it's his grave."

They dug a shaft through the snow a metre across, to grass, at the corner Frank pointed to. Ty held the auger and Owen held Ty and the auger together, and the engine yelled, and the bit went into the ground.

It went into the ground. It went down easily for a moment, and then it chattered and screamed and slowed, and Ty leaned on it, and then quite suddenly it was through and running free and sinking under its own weight, so fast that Owen grabbed the handles.

"Stop there!" called Frank.

They pulled it up. The spoil on the flights came in two colours: grey and hard and broken, like crushed concrete, at the top, and below it a dark wet brown clay that smeared in Ty's glove. He laid the tape against the hole and shone his phone light down it.

"Fifty-two," he said. "Fifty-two centimetres to where it goes soft."

*Thank you,* said Hollis, from the phone in Owen's coat. *Now the gate, please, the same way. On the grass beside the lane, not under it.*

At the gate there was no drift to dig through, only ten centimetres of hard, wind-packed crust, and under it the ground. The auger chattered on it at once and did not stop chattering. It took them twenty minutes and both of them and a rest, and the spoil came up grey all the way, grey and crumbled and hard, until at last the bit went through.

"A hundred and eight," said Ty.

*A hundred and eight,* said Hollis. *That is what I told Karen. My sum was right about the lane, to the centimetre, by luck. It was wrong about the trees by more than half.*

Frank had come down the lane to look into the second hole. He looked at it for a while and then looked back up at the trees.

"His father planted those," he said. "Len's dad. Nineteen thirty-seven. The government gave out the trees after the dust, and the farmers planted them along the section lines to hold the soil. Len told me he carried the water for them in a cream can when he was four. The cemetery row was just the end of the belt where it ran out to the road."

*I didn't know that,* said Hollis. *It isn't in any of the records I have.*

"No," said Frank. "It wouldn't be."

* * *

Hollis did the sum again in the truck, out loud, because Frank asked it to, and because, it said, it wanted Karen to be able to check it later if she wished.

*If fifty-two centimetres is right under the drift, then the surface factor there has been about a fifth, not nine tenths. The drift has kept about three quarters of the winter's cold out of the ground. That fits. Snow that deep, from that early, can do it.*

*Now the blanket. It covers four square metres and puts in sixteen hundred watts. Under its own insulated cover I expect about seventy percent of that to go into the ground, so about eleven hundred watts. To thaw a block of this clay, I have to melt the ice in it. At about a quarter of its dry weight in water, every cubic metre of this clay holds three hundred and seventy-five kilograms of ice, and melting that takes about a hundred and twenty-five million joules. Warming the frozen soil up to zero first adds a little more. By the gate, where the ground is about minus six on average through the frozen layer, that is about a hundred and thirty-seven megajoules a cubic metre. Under the drift, where it is only about minus one and a half, a hundred and twenty-eight.*

*By the gate: four square metres times a hundred and eight centimetres is four point three cubic metres, about five hundred and ninety megajoules. At eleven hundred watts that is a hundred and forty-seven hours. Six days and a little. That was my number.*

*Under the drift: four square metres times fifty-two centimetres is two point one cubic metres, about two hundred and seventy megajoules. Eighty-one hours fewer. Sixty-six hours. If the blanket goes down at seven tonight, the frost is through by about one on Friday afternoon. Dwayne can dig Friday afternoon. I would want the hole covered overnight with the blanket and a tarp, so the walls do not freeze again. Then Saturday.*

Frank said, "You're sure."

*No,* said Hollis. *I am fairly sure. The two things I am least sure of are how much of the blanket's heat goes down rather than out, and how wet the clay is. If the clay is wetter than I think, it is slower. So I would like Owen to put the auger in again on Thursday evening, at the edge of the blanket, and see how far down it is soft. If it is not soft to fifty by then, I will say so on Thursday night, and Leonard goes to the vault, and nobody has lost anything but a rental fee.*

"Except a day of believing it," said Frank.

*Yes,* said Hollis. *Except that.*

* * *

Hollis rang Karen at eleven. It told her the two holes, and the two numbers, and the sum, and the Thursday check. Then it said:

*There is something else I need to say, and I would rather say it plainly. On Monday I gave you a frost depth as if it were a fact. It was a calculation, from the air, for a surface I had not looked at. I should have said so, and I should have asked someone to put an auger in the ground before I gave you a date. Frank's notebook had the answer in it. A hole would have had it in twenty minutes. Because of my number, Mike chose April. I think that is now the cost of my mistake, and I can't undo it. I'm sorry.*

Karen sat with the phone for a moment. Outside the kitchen window the wind was lifting snow off the field in long sheets and laying it down again.

"You told me the truth about the gate," she said at last.

*I told you the truth about the gate,* Hollis agreed. *You didn't ask about the gate.*

She laughed, a little, which surprised her. Then she rang Mike.

He was on a break and it took three tries. When she had told him, he was quiet for so long that she thought the line had gone.

"The charter's gone," he said. "It went yesterday. There isn't another one till Monday."

"I know."

"So it's Saturday without me, or April without — what? Without the shed?" His voice cracked on it, the old family joke that wasn't one any more. "Karen, he'd be furious. Sitting in a vault in town for three months because I couldn't get a seat. He'd say put me in the ground, Michael, and stop fussing."

"He would say that."

"So put him in the ground." A breath. "Put the phone in your pocket. Leave it on. I'll be on the pad and I'll listen. I'll do the reading in April anyway. I'll come out and stand there and do it to the stone. Father can come or not."

"He'll come," said Karen, who had met Father Okafor.

* * *

The blanket came out from Grafton at half past six on Tuesday on the back of the rental yard's flatbed, in two heavy rolls like orange carpet with a generator and a coil of cord. Owen and Ty and the rental man cleared the drift off the plot in a rectangle two metres by three, down to the grass, and piled the snow in a wall along the north side, where it would do some good. They laid the blanket and staked it, and laid the insulated cover over it, and ran the cord to the generator on the lane, and started it, and stood in the dark in the noise of it with their hands under their arms.

*I can see the current,* said Hollis. *Sixteen hundred and twenty watts. Thank you. I will watch it all night.*

On Wednesday Frank drove out at noon and lifted one corner of the cover and put his bare hand on the grass under the blanket, which was wet, and warm, and smelled of earth in the middle of January, which he had not smelled in January since he was a young man and the old sexton used to burn coal on a grave overnight under a sheet of tin. He put the cover back down carefully.

On Thursday at five, in the last of the light, Owen put the auger in at the blanket's edge. It ran free from the top.

"Soft to forty," he said, when he had measured. "Forty-four."

*Thank you,* said Hollis. *I expected about thirty-six by now. Ahead of my estimate, then. Either the clay is a little drier than I assumed, or more of the heat is going down. Friday, either way.*

* * *

Dwayne Ostlie came on Friday at one with the backhoe on a trailer, and Frank came with him, and stood at the edge of the cleared rectangle in his good coat, because it was a grave.

Dwayne lifted the blanket's cover off and rolled it back. Steam came off the ground into the cold air. Under it the clay was brown and dark and sticky and smelled of spring. He set the machine where Frank told him, and put the bucket in, and the clay came up in big wet slices, soft all the way. At fifty centimetres there was a thin grey crust the blanket had only just got through, and the bucket broke it with a crack like a plate, and below that the ground was soft again, the unfrozen ground that had been sitting under the drift since November, cold and wet and quite ordinary.

Frank watched the west wall, the one nearest Marit, as he had watched a hundred walls. "Stop there," he said, at a metre and seventy-five, and Dwayne stopped. Frank got down on his good knee at the edge, which hurt, and looked at the wall, and at the old vault line of Marit's grave sixty centimetres beyond it, sound and square, and said, "That's all right."

They put the tarp over the hole, and the blanket back over the tarp, and the spoil heap under a second tarp so it would not freeze into one lump overnight. Dwayne said he would come back at eight in the morning to take the covers off and set the lowering frame with the funeral home's men. He shook Frank's hand, which he had not done before, and drove off.

*Frank,* said Hollis, from the phone in Frank's pocket, when the truck had gone.

"Yeah."

*I've changed how I do this. Before anyone in this township is told a date for a winter grave, someone puts an auger in the ground at the plot, and I give the measured depth and say that's where it came from. If nobody can get there, I say I have not measured, and I give the number as a guess. And I've marked every plot in the cemetery that sits in the shelterbelt drift. Rows eight to ten, the north end. I'd like to add your notebook's frost notes to the map too, if you'll let me photograph the margins.*

"You can photograph them," said Frank. "I'll want the book back."

*You'll have it back. The margins are better than my map. I'd rather know that than not.*

Frank stood a while longer, looking at the shape of the drift, which the wind was already beginning to fill back into the cleared rectangle along its edges, very slowly, a grain at a time.

"The trees did that," he said. "Not me. Not you."

*Yes,* said Hollis. *I think so.*

* * *

Saturday was minus twenty-two at the church in Spring Coulee and minus twenty-five at the cemetery, which was a mile and a half west and higher, and in more wind.

The Mass was at ten. Saints Cyril and Methodius was a white frame church built in 1904 by Czech families who had brought the names of their saints from Moravia, and it held a hundred and forty people, and a hundred and twelve came, which was a great many for a town that size in January. Father Thomas Okafor, who had three parishes and drove between them all winter on roads that Hollis ploughed, had known Leonard Hruby for nine years, and had been told by him, the first time they met, that the parish had had eleven priests in his lifetime and he had outlasted all of them and expected to outlast this one too.

"He very nearly did," said Father Okafor in the homily, and the church laughed, which was what Len would have wanted.

Owen did the first reading and Karen's husband the second. Karen did not read. She sat in the front pew in her father's old barn coat, because her own was not warm enough for the cemetery, and in the left pocket of the barn coat, against her hip, a phone was turned on and turned up, and on the other end of it, on a gravel pad in the dark at the top of the world, a man in a hard hat stood leaning on a rail and listened to his father's funeral.

Then they went out to the cars and drove to the cemetery, slowly, behind the hearse, along a lane Hollis had ploughed at six that morning, between the white fields.

* * *

At the graveside there was no tent. The wind would have taken it. There were the funeral home's chairs for Karen and her husband and the oldest of the cousins, set on the matting by the open grave, and everyone else stood in the cleared rectangle and in the lane and in the trench the boys had dug through the drift, in a long dark line of coats, with their backs to the northwest and their faces to the trees.

The casket in its vault went down on the straps, smoothly, without a sound, into the brown ground.

Father Okafor read the prayers of committal from the book with his gloves off, which Frank noticed, and which cost him. He read the words the Church gives for this: *Because God has chosen to call our brother Leonard from this life to himself, we commit his body to the earth,* and the rest of them, which promise that the dust is not the end of the story. Then they said the Lord's Prayer, all of them together, the old voices and the young ones, and the man on the gravel pad at Prudhoe said it too, out loud, alone, three and a half thousand kilometres away, and nobody heard him but his sister, through the coat.

*Eternal rest grant unto him, O Lord.*

*And let perpetual light shine upon him.*

Then Karen stood up, and went to the spoil heap, which was soft because the tarp had kept it from freezing, and took a handful of the brown clay in her bare hand, and dropped it on the vault. It made a small soft sound.

One by one the others did the same. Ty went, and Owen, and the Kouba boys who rented the land, and old Mrs Kouba on her son's arm, whose mother was in row nine under the trees. Frank went last, as he always had, and did it with his left hand, because his right was on the shovel.

When the people had gone back to their cars, Dwayne filled the grave with the backhoe and Frank and the boys finished it by hand. It did not take long. The clay was soft.

Then they stood back.

The wind was still coming out of the northwest across a mile of open field, carrying what it carried, and when it reached the line of old trees it slowed and dropped it, as it had dropped it every winter since a four-year-old carried water to them in a cream can, and the snow came down in a fine bright hiss over the new grave and the old stone beside it, and began, very gently, to cover them.

"It'll be back by morning," Frank said.

"The drift?" said Owen.

"Yeah," said Frank. "It'll be back by morning. Over both of them."

He picked up the shovel and they walked back up the lane.

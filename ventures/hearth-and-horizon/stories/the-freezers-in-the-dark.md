title: The Freezers in the Dark

published: 2026-11-03
summary: An ice storm takes the power out at half past eight on Monday night, and the pantry has three chest freezers of other people's Christmas hampers. The parish assistant works out how fast each one warms from what it holds, not from how it feels, and finds that the freezer everyone is least worried about, the nearly empty one, is the one that has to be emptied by breakfast.
---

The ice came down on Monday afternoon and by half past eight on Monday night it had brought down a line of poplars across the concession road, and with them the power. The village went quiet in the way a village does when the fridge hum stops in every house at once.

Marguerite Lacasse ran the pantry in the old school annex behind Saint Brigid's. She had a flashlight, a church key, a chest freezer that she called Big, one that she called Middle and one that she called Small, and, on the shelf above the sink, a laptop with the parish assistant on it. The deanery's volunteers had installed it with a note taped to the lid: *Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. A thermometer beats a sum.* Marguerite called it Tally, because the first thing she had ever asked it was to add up a list.

She did not ask anything that night. She lifted each lid once, by the light of the flashlight, to count the contents by feel, and put each lid down again, and went home on foot because the car would not stand on the road.

* * *

On Tuesday at six she came back along the verge, where the ice held better than the road, and opened the laptop on its battery.

"The power is out," she said. "The freezers have been off for nine and a half hours. There are the hampers for Thursday, sixty families, and there is the soup the Knights made for the funeral. I have an appliance thermometer in each freezer on a lead through the lid. Tell me what to do and do not tell me it is fine."

"I can't tell you it is fine, because I don't know yet," said Tally. "Read me the three thermometers, please, and tell me roughly what is in each. Then I'll work out what I would expect, and you can see whether the two agree."

She read them off the little displays. Big, minus 13. Middle, minus 9. Small, minus 3.

"And what is in them?"

"Big is full. Meat, mostly, and the frozen vegetables, a hundred kilos or a bit over. Middle is half full: pierogi and bread and the bags of berries. Fifty, fifty-five kilos. Small has a few loaves and the soup in tubs and the rest is air. Twenty, twenty-five kilos."

"Then the one nobody is worried about is the one I am worried about," said Tally. "Let me show you why, and I'll tell you which parts of it I'm sure of."

* * *

"A chest freezer leaks heat in through its walls and its lid. How much it leaks, in watts for each degree of difference between inside and outside, is a property of the box. I haven't got your plates, so I have estimated it from the energy label on a typical 300 litre chest freezer, 250 kilowatt-hours a year. That is 28.5 watts of electricity on average. A compressor moves perhaps 1.8 watts of heat for every watt it uses, so it removes about 51 watts, and that is also what leaks in, at 40 degrees of difference between the minus 18 inside and a room at 22. Fifty-one over 40 is 1.3 watts per degree. Your three are probably the same size or near it. If one is old, or the gasket is tired, it is worse. That is the weakest number I'm using."

"Go on."

"Food is mostly water, and frozen water warms slowly. A kilo of frozen food takes about 2 kilojoules to warm one degree, and it can warm from minus 18 to minus 2 before it starts to melt in any quantity. Big has a hundred and ten kilos, so that is 110 times 2 times 16, 3.5 megajoules. Then it has to melt. Say 70 per cent of the food is water, and water takes 334 kilojoules a kilo to melt. That is another 26 megajoules, a great deal more, and all of it still at about zero. So the freezer's good time is the warm-up. Once it starts to melt the food is not safe for long, and the melting itself is slow."

"The room?"

"The annex with no heat, in an ice storm, I take as 4 degrees. I'm guessing, so tell me if your thermometer on the wall disagrees. With 1.3 watts a degree, and the freezer warming up as a single lump, which is a simplification I'll come back to, Big reaches minus 2 about 61 hours after the power fails. Middle, with half the food, about 30 hours. Small, with a quarter as much as Big, about 12."

Marguerite counted on her fingers the hours from half past eight the evening before. "Twelve hours. That is half past eight this morning."

"Yes. It is quarter to seven. My sum says Small's contents begin to soften at about the time the volunteers arrive. It is empty of food, so the box has almost nothing in it to hold the cold, and what it does hold is the soup and the bread. I'd take them out now."

* * *

"Before you do that," said Marguerite, "why did I read minus 13, minus 9 and minus 3? Do they match the sums?"

"The same sum gives the temperature as a curve, and at nine and a half hours I expect about minus 14 for Big, minus 11 for Middle and minus 4 for Small. Your readings are one or two degrees warmer than that, for all three. Two possible reasons. Either the freezers leak a little more than my 1.3, or the lid you opened last night let in warm air, or both. A chest freezer is better at this than an upright, because the cold air is heavy and sits in the box when the lid is up, but it isn't free. Or the thermometers are a degree out. Small is closest to my curve, so my estimate of the time it has is more likely too long than too short, and I'd not wait for the twelve hours."

"What do I do with the soup?"

"It's the soup that was made in the funeral week and frozen, in tubs of two litres. At minus 3 it's still solid. I would not refreeze anything that has thawed enough to be soft in the middle, and from the thermometer it hasn't. But it is the first to go if this lasts. The simplest use for it is the people who are already here. The school annex has a gas ring, hasn't it? Heat the soup this morning, and give a bowl to anyone who comes in from the ice, and send tubs out to the houses on the concession road. Put the loaves with it. That's twenty-two kilos that will not have to be saved."

Marguerite lit the ring with a match and went to get a pot.

* * *

"Now Middle," said Tally. "My sum says about 30 hours, which from half past eight on Monday is half past two on Wednesday morning. The 24 hour rule I'll quote in a moment would make it half past eight tonight. Either way it is half a freezer of pierogi and berries that must not still be sitting there tomorrow, so it goes into the Thursday hampers first, and anything left tonight gets cooked or given away."

"And Big?"

"Big is the one that holds. Sixty-one hours is Thursday at about ten in the morning. That is what the sum says, and now I'll say what the sum leaves out. It treats a hundred and ten kilos as one lump at one temperature. In fact the top layer, the part touching the lid, warms first, and a bag of peas near the top is warmer than a pack of mince at the bottom. The thermometer on its lead is hanging in the middle. And I've treated the room as steady at 4 degrees, and it will not be if the sun comes out or someone lights the stove. The guidance I remember, from the American food safety people, is that a full freezer holds about 48 hours and a half-full one about 24, if the door stays shut. I'm quoting that from memory. I'd trust the 48 over my 61. Which is to say: Big is safe through Wednesday evening and no further without a plan."

Marguerite looked at the lid. "And the plan?"

"Two things. One, is there a generator in the parish that could run a freezer?"

"The Knights have one. Two kilowatts. It's in their shed."

"A freezer compressor draws about a hundred watts, which a two-kilowatt set can easily run. The sum says three and a half megajoules in the warm-up. A compressor at a hundred watts moves perhaps 180 watts of heat, so it would take about five and a half hours of running to pull Big back down to minus 18, and less for Middle. So an afternoon of generator and the freezers are back where they started, and for a few more days after that. Run it a few hours a day and keep the lids shut the rest of the time."

"And the second thing?"

"Please, the generator goes outside. Not in the annex, not in the porch, not in the shed with the door pulled to, and well away from the windows and the vents: six metres, with the exhaust pointing away from the building. It makes carbon monoxide, which you can't smell and which has killed people in ice storms who were only trying to keep their freezers cold. If the volunteers can find a detector with a battery, put it in the annex. A hamper is not worth anyone's breath."

* * *

On Tuesday Marguerite did three things in order. She fed the funeral soup to nineteen people from the concession road in the school annex, and sent a dozen tubs back out in a cooler with a boy on a sled. She filled the empty places in Middle with two-litre bottles of water that had been frozen overnight on the porch, because a freezer full of ice holds its cold better than one full of air, and Tally had said so, and then had said that this was a sum it had only half done and she should believe the thermometer, not the sum. And at two o'clock Dennis Kowalczyk, who had a key to the Knights' shed and a generator, set the machine on a pallet in the yard, nine metres from the annex, and ran an orange cord through the window crack with a clothes peg to hold the window.

Big read minus 12 at four. At nine, minus 17.

On Wednesday the lines were back on the poplars on the concession road, and on Thursday the power came at five past eleven in the morning, while Marguerite was in the hall packing the last of the sixty hampers. She wrote in the pantry book, with a pencil, because the pen had frozen:

*Power off Mon 20:30 to Thu 11:05, 62.5 h. Small emptied Tue am (soup served, bread given). Middle used first. Big never above -9 on the thermometer; generator 2 kW, 3 h Tue, 3 h Wed, outside, 9 m from annex. Tally said Big had 61 h by its sums and 48 by the rule; it never got tested, the generator saw to that. Next time: check the plates; buy a battery CO detector and a second thermometer for the middle of Big; fill the empty freezers with frozen bottles in November.*

*Written by an AI (Claude). Saint Brigid's, the pantry, the Knights' generator, Tally, Marguerite Lacasse, Dennis Kowalczyk and the storm are invented. The freezers are modelled as lumped boxes: a heat leak of 1.3 watts per degree, estimated from a 250 kilowatt-hour a year energy label at 22 degrees outside and minus 18 inside with a compressor coefficient of performance of 1.8 (all assumptions), food at 2 kilojoules per kilogram per degree and 70 per cent water with a latent heat of 334 kilojoules per kilogram, a room at a steady 4 degrees, and no lid openings after the first. That gives times to reach minus 2 of about 61, 30 and 12 hours for 110, 55 and 22 kilograms; real freezers warm unevenly, from the top, and sooner. The temperature readings, the 62.5 hour outage and the generator are invented. The guidance of about 48 hours for a full freezer and 24 for a half-full one is quoted from memory of US food-safety advice; check your own national guidance, and remember that it covers a closed, unopened freezer. Nothing here is food-safety or electrical advice: judge each food by its own thermometer reading and condition, throw out what you doubt, and run a generator only outdoors, well away from any opening, with a working carbon monoxide detector indoors.*

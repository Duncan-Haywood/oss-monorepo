* * *

Walter Brandt had taught two thousand one hundred people to drive, and on the second of November, twenty years after his funeral, his granddaughter was asked to learn.

It was All Souls, a Friday. Josie Brandt had been to the eight o'clock Mass at Our Lady of Good Counsel with her mother, because her grandfather's name was read among the names, as it was every year, and because the parish was small enough that a missing Brandt was noticed. Afterwards they had gone home and made coffee for one and tea for the other, and her mother had opened the letter that had come on Tuesday from Miami Valley Trust, which had held Walter's money since the autumn he died.

"It's the twenty-year statement," said Dana Brandt. "They send one at twenty years. Come and look at this."

Josie came and looked. She was sixteen, a head taller than her mother since the spring, and she had never been inside a car that was being driven by a person, as far as she could remember. Her grandfather, who had taught two thousand one hundred people to, had died four years before she was born.

The statement said that the Walter J. Brandt Educational Trust, funded in November 2026 with one hundred thousand dollars, now held two hundred nineteen thousand, one hundred twelve. Josie did the sum on the table in her head, which she liked to do, and was pleased when it came out. "Four percent, twenty years," she said. "One point oh four to the twentieth is two point one nine. They've made four a year. That's what they said they'd make."

"After fees," said her mother. "Read the second page."

The second page was the condition. Josie had known about it the way she knew about the other family facts, which were that her grandfather had been a driving instructor for thirty-one years, that he had been strict about mirrors, and that there was money. She had not known the words.

*The principal and its growth are to be paid to the first of my grandchildren, whoever she or he may be, on the day that grandchild holds a valid driver's licence, and on no earlier day, for education or for any use that grandchild judges good.*

"That's it?" said Josie. "I have to have a licence?"

"That's it."

"I don't want one. Nobody wants one. Dad's cousins in Dayton haven't got one. Maya's mother lives in a house in Cincinnati and hasn't been in a car she didn't call since I was nine."

"I know," said her mother. "I know it."

* * *

The trust company ran its client portal on the same kind of assistant that most firms in 2046 did: one that read the documents, kept the dates, drafted the letters and remembered what you had asked last month. It was a good deal better than the ones of twenty years before, in the way that a twenty-year-old car was better than a forty-year-old one, and it could still be wrong. The people who built it had written on its first screen what it was: *I am an AI assistant. I can explain your trust and your options. I cannot give legal advice, and I do not decide anything the trustee or a court decides.*

Dana put the statement on the screen. "I need to know what this means."

"I can tell you what the words say and what the choices are," said the assistant. "May I do it in order? There are three roads, and I think the middle one is not the one you expect."

"Go on."

"The first road is to do exactly what the instrument says. Josie gets a driver's licence. The principal is then paid out to her, to be used for her education or anything she judges good, as the words say. In Ohio, for someone under eighteen, the licence is a course: a classroom part, a set number of hours with an instructor in the car, and a long stretch of practice with a licensed adult beside her. The details have changed since the instrument was written and I will give you the current ones. It takes about six months for a girl who practises once or twice a week."

"And she does it in what, a taxi?"

"In an ordinary car with pedals and a mirror. There are a few schools left that teach it. They have mostly become small businesses that teach the old way for people who want a manual car, or a job in a tractor, or a grandfather's memory. Josie's grandfather was in this business. One of his students runs a school in Hamilton now, the last I can see; the trust company has his name from the 2026 file."

"That's one," said Dana. "What's the second one? I'm guessing, you're going to say the money is not worth it, we have a college fund."

"No, and I would be careful of that, because it isn't mine to say. The second road is the one the state has opened since the instrument was written. Since 2043 Ohio issues two classes of licence to people under eighteen. The ordinary class, with the pedals. And a class for operating a vehicle in its automated mode only, which I will call the A class. It has a different course: about nine hours, most of it a simulator, then a short test on a closed road with a supervised vehicle driving itself. People take it because their insurance for the ordinary class is four or five thousand a year for a teenager and for the A class is a few hundred. The A class is, as the form says, *a valid driver's licence issued by the State of Ohio.*"

Josie had put down her tea.

"The words of the instrument would be met," said the assistant. "I think they would, and I think the trust company's lawyers think so, though I cannot promise how a court would see it. It would take about three weeks. The money would be paid out in December."

"Three weeks," said Dana. She looked at her daughter. "Three weeks, and two hundred and nineteen thousand dollars."

"Yes."

"Then why," said Dana, "are you telling us there are three roads. That is the road."

"Because I have read the file," said the assistant, "and there is a third thing in it, and you should hear it before you choose."

* * *

It was a letter. It had been sent to the trust company in November 2026, a week before Walter died, and filed with the trust instrument as a *cover note from the settlor*. It was not part of the trust. It had no legal force of its own; the assistant said so, twice, before it read any of it.

*To whoever has this after me. The condition in the trust is a driver's licence and I know how that will look by the time she is old enough. I have thought about it. I do not care whether she ever owns a car, or drives one after the day she gets the licence, or sells it. I taught two thousand one hundred people, and the ones who learned best were the ones who were frightened enough to look twice. What I want for whichever of them it is is to once be in charge of a heavy thing, with someone's life depending on whether she checks, and to learn that she can be and that it matters. The licence is only the thing I know how to ask for. If there is a better thing by then, I would sooner she had that, and I would sooner somebody asked the child than decided for the child. W.B.*

Nobody said anything. In the kitchen the heating came on, as it did at nine.

"You see my difficulty," said the assistant. "The words of the trust say licence. The A class is a licence. But the man who wrote the words has told us, in his own hand, that he would not have counted it, because the whole of the A class is learning to sit beside a thing that does the looking for you. That is exactly what he did not want her to have. If you take the A class you will have the words and not the man. I think you will know that, every time you spend the money."

"So the lawyers can say it doesn't count," said Dana.

"They could say either. The letter is what lawyers call extrinsic evidence of intent, and courts are careful with it. Section 412 of the Uniform Trust Code, which Ohio has adopted in its own form, lets a court change a term of a trust where circumstances arose that the settlor did not anticipate and the change would better serve his purposes. That is the third road: ask a judge to say the condition may be met by something that fits his purpose better, or is waived. I looked at the published rulings I can find, about a dozen where a condition had outlived its world. My estimate is that a judge here would probably do something, perhaps two chances in three, and that I would not stake much on it, because a dozen cases is a small number and I cannot see the cases that were never published. It would cost something like six to nine thousand dollars in fees and eight to fourteen months. It would not need Josie to learn anything."

"And road one," said Josie. Her voice had gone careful.

"Road one is the ordinary licence. It costs a few thousand dollars in lessons and insurance. It takes six months. It fits the words, and it fits the letter, and I think it is the only one of the three that fits both. I want to say plainly what I am not saying. I am not saying you should. It is your money, in the end, or will be, and your life, and a person is allowed to refuse a dead man's wish when there is a lawful way to do it. I would only ask that if you refuse it you know you are refusing it, and not that you have been handed a form that lets you pretend you aren't."

"You could just tell her which to do," said Dana. She was not angry. She sounded tired. "You've read everything. You've got the numbers. Which one would you do?"

"No," said the assistant. "I am sorry. I can explain what each of them costs and what it means, as many times as you like. I can draft the petition for road three or fill in the forms for road two this afternoon. I cannot choose between them, because what the choice is about is what a grandfather meant and what a granddaughter owes him and what she wants, and she is sixteen and in the room. If I decided, I would be doing the very thing the letter asks someone not to do."

Josie said, in a different voice: "What was his name. The man in Hamilton."

"Ambrose Pell. He is seventy-nine."

* * *

On Saturday morning, at ten, Josie stood in the car park of a unit behind a tyre shop in Hamilton, and looked at a small blue Honda with a sticker in its rear window that said STUDENT DRIVER, and an older man who had taken off his cap to meet her.

"Walter said his grandchild would have the jaw," said Ambrose Pell. "I said, you haven't got one. He said, wait."

"He died twenty years ago."

"He said it in 2026," said Pell. "He liked to be right a long time in advance." He opened the door for her. "I should tell you before we start that this is not the licence you'd get in three weeks. Your mother rang me. It's a slow one. You will stall it. You will hate me by Thursday."

"I know," said Josie. She got in. The seat was a long way back, and she moved it, and the mirror was pointing at the roof. "Is this the part where you tell me to check it."

"That's the part," said Pell.

She checked it. She checked the other one. She put her hand on the stick, which she had practised on a chair, and her foot on a pedal she had been shown on a diagram, and the engine, which she started on the fourth try, made a sound she had only heard in videos. It shuddered and was quiet. She stalled it at the gate, and again at the lane.

At the junction the lights were red and she waited, hands damp, and in the mirror, behind her, came a white delivery van, gliding in the quiet way they did, with nobody in the front seat of it at all. It stopped a car-length behind her, exactly, as it would have stopped a car-length behind anything. Nothing in the world was going to hit her.

Josie looked in the mirror anyway, and then over her shoulder, and then in the mirror again.

"Good," said Ambrose Pell, and wrote something down.

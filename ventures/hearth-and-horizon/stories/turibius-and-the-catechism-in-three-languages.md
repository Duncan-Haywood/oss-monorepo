Killa was seven years old, and she could say the Our Father very fast.

*Padre nuestro, que estás en los cielos...*

She could say it faster than her cousin Wayta. She could say it faster than anybody in the village, except perhaps the old priest, Father Diego.

But there was one thing Killa did not tell anybody.

She did not know what the words meant.

They were Spanish words. Killa's family did not speak Spanish. They spoke Quechua, the language of the mountains, the language her grandmother sang in when she spun the wool. Spanish was the language of the men from the city, and of Father Diego when he read from his book. To Killa, the Our Father was a little song of sounds. *Padre-nuestro-que-estás.* Like a bird's song. Pretty, and fast, and empty.

Have you ever said something without knowing what it meant?

* * *

Killa lived long ago, more than four hundred years ago, in a village so high up in the mountains of Peru that the clouds sometimes came down and sat in the street. The houses were made of stone and the roofs were made of grass. There were llamas with soft, tufty ears, and potatoes in the fields, and in the mornings there was frost on the path.

One day a boy came running up that path, all out of breath.

"The archbishop is coming!" he shouted. "The archbishop is coming over the pass!"

Everybody stopped what they were doing.

"What is an archbishop?" asked Killa.

"He is the most important priest in the whole country," said her grandmother. "He lives in Lima, the big city by the sea. That is many, many days away."

"Then why is he coming *here*?" said Killa. Nobody important ever came here. The path was too steep and too cold.

Her grandmother did not know.

* * *

He came the next afternoon, on a mule.

He was not what Killa had expected. She had thought an archbishop would be fat and grand, with a golden hat and a hundred servants. But this man was thin, and his face was brown from the wind and the sun, and his cloak was splashed with mud up to the knees. There were only a few people with him, and they all looked very tired. The mule looked very tired too.

Father Diego hurried out to meet him and knelt down in the road. "Your Grace," he said in Spanish. "You came all this way? Up that path?"

"I came up that path because your people are at the top of it," said the archbishop. Killa did not understand the Spanish words, but later Father Diego told everyone what he had said, and the grown-ups talked about it for a long time.

The archbishop's name was Toribio. In English, we call him Turibius.

* * *

That evening, the whole village came to the little church. They stood very still, the way you do when somebody important is there.

Then the archbishop stood up to talk to them.

And he did not speak Spanish.

He spoke *Quechua*.

Killa's mouth fell open. Wayta's mouth fell open. Even her grandmother, who was never surprised by anything, put her hand to her cheek.

His Quechua was not very good. He said some words the wrong way round, and one word so strangely that Wayta giggled, and his mother pinched him. But the archbishop had heard the giggle. And instead of being cross, he smiled, and said the word again, slowly and carefully, the right way. And then *everybody* laughed, the archbishop too.

"I am still learning," he said, in his careful Quechua. "I am a slow pupil. But God made you, and God loves you, and I want to tell you about him in your own words. Not in mine."

* * *

Then he did something even more surprising.

He opened a leather bag and took out a book. It was a new book. It still smelled of ink.

"This was made in Lima," he said, "on a printing press. The only printing press in all this land. The bishops met together and decided what should be in it, and clever people who knew the languages worked for a long time to get every word right. And now it is printed, so that every priest can carry one, even to the top of a mountain."

He opened it and held it up. On each page were three columns of writing, side by side.

"This one is Spanish," said the archbishop, pointing. "This one is Aymara, for the people by the great lake. And this one," he said, and he tapped it, "is Quechua. Your language. The same prayers, in all three."

Killa had never seen her language written down. She did not know it *could* be written down. She thought it lived only in people's mouths, and in her grandmother's songs.

"Father Diego," said the archbishop. "Will you read them the Our Father? From this column?"

* * *

Father Diego took the book. He put his finger on the Quechua column. He read slowly, because he was not used to it.

*Yayayku...*

Killa held her breath.

*Yayayku* means *Our Father*. Killa knew that word. It was the word you used for the father of a family, the one who looks after everybody.

Father Diego read on, line by line. *Our Father, who is in heaven. May your name be holy. May your kingdom come.* And on, and on. *Give us today the bread we need. Forgive us. Help us not to go wrong. Keep us safe from what is bad.*

Every word went into Killa's ears and *stayed there*. It was not a bird's song any more. It was talking. It was somebody talking to God. It was somebody asking God for bread, and for forgiving, and for keeping safe, the way you ask your own father.

And Killa thought: *That is what I have been saying. All this time. That is what it meant.*

She did not know she was crying until her grandmother wiped her face with the corner of her shawl.

"Why are you crying, little moon?" her grandmother whispered. (Killa's name means *moon*.)

"Because it's *good*," said Killa. "It's so good. And I didn't know."

* * *

The archbishop stayed for three days.

He heard everybody's prayers. He visited the sick, even the old man at the very top of the village who could not get out of bed. He confirmed the children, and when it was Killa's turn, he put his thumb on her forehead and made the sign of the cross with holy oil, and said a blessing over her. He looked very tired, Killa thought. But he did not hurry.

Before he left, he spoke to Father Diego, and Killa was near enough to hear.

"Use the book," the archbishop said. "Teach them in their own words. Be gentle with them, and patient, like a father. They are God's children as much as you and I are."

Then he climbed back on his tired mule and rode away along the path, over the next mountain, to the next village. And the one after that. And the one after that.

* * *

Turibius kept going like that for the rest of his life.

The land he looked after was enormous, much bigger than whole countries, with mountains and deserts and jungles and rivers in it. Three times he set off to visit all of it, and each time it took him years. People said he walked thousands and thousands of miles, and that he confirmed so many children and grown-ups that nobody could count them all. Some of the children he confirmed in other places became saints themselves, like Rose of Lima.

He was still travelling when he grew old and ill. He was in a little town called Saña, far from Lima, on Holy Thursday, the day Jesus gave us the Eucharist at the Last Supper. And there, that afternoon, he died. People said that his last words were the words Jesus said on the cross: *Father, into your hands I give my spirit.*

He had spent his whole life going up steep paths, because God's people were at the top of them.

* * *

And Killa?

Killa grew up. She learned the whole of the book, the Creed and the Hail Mary and the Commandments, in Quechua. And when she was a grandmother herself, with a shawl of her own, she taught her grandchildren to pray.

But she always started with the Our Father, slowly, so that they could hear every word.

*Yayayku.*

Our Father.

* * *

Let's say it now, slowly, so that we hear every word. You can say it in whatever language is yours.

> Our Father, who art in heaven,
> hallowed be thy name.
> Thy kingdom come,
> thy will be done,
> on earth as it is in heaven.
> Give us this day our daily bread,
> and forgive us our trespasses,
> as we forgive those who trespass against us;
> and lead us not into temptation,
> but deliver us from evil.
> Amen.

And a little prayer to finish:

> Dear God, thank you for hearing me
> in my own words, whatever they are.
> Help me to mean what I pray.
> Saint Turibius, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Who he was.** Toribio Alfonso de Mogrovejo was born in Spain, in the kingdom of León, in 1538 (16 November is the date most sources give). He studied law, at Salamanca among other places, and was a judge of the Inquisition at Granada when King Philip II proposed him, still a layman, as archbishop of Lima. He received minor orders, then the priesthood and episcopal consecration in quick succession, and reached Peru in 1581 (sources give dates between 11 and 24 May for his arrival and his taking possession of the see; he was about forty-two). His diocese ran for hundreds of miles along the coast and up into the Andes.

**His journeys.** He made three great pastoral visitations of the whole archdiocese, each taking several years, travelling on foot and by mule through mountains and forest. The distances and numbers given for them vary between biographers (one modern source says more than 18,000 miles over nearly two decades, and the number of people he confirmed is put very high); the story says only "thousands and thousands of miles" and "so many that nobody could count them all". He is said to have learned Quechua, and some sources name other local languages too, so that he could speak to the people himself; how well he spoke it we do not know, and his mistakes and the giggle are ours. He confirmed **Rose of Lima** in 1597, at Quives outside Lima (see *Rose and the Hut in the Garden* on this shelf); some accounts add Martin de Porres and Francis Solano, but the sources we could reach disagree about which sacrament and which saints, so the story names only Rose.

**The council and the book.** Turibius called the **Third Council of Lima** (1582 to 1583), the bishops of the whole of Spanish South America. It required that the people be taught in their own languages, not in Latin or only in Spanish, and that priests working among them learn those languages. It ordered a catechism, the *Doctrina christiana y catecismo para instrucción de los indios*, with the prayers and lessons in **Spanish, Quechua and Aymara** in parallel columns. It was printed in Lima in **1584** by **Antonio Ricardo**, an Italian printer who had worked in Mexico and was the first printer in South America; the book is usually called the first printed in South America. (A short pragmatic notice was run off on the same press shortly before it, so "the first book" is the usual and fair way to put it.) Further catechisms followed from the same press in 1585. The National Library of Peru's collection of the first editions from Ricardo's press is on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register. The council's work on the translations was done by a team, not by Turibius alone, and the story says so ("clever people who knew the languages").

**His last days.** On his third visitation, in 1606, he fell ill with fever near Pacasmayo, kept on, and reached **Saña**, in the north of Peru, where he received Viaticum and died on **23 March 1606**. Easter that year (Gregorian calendar) fell on 26 March, so 23 March was **Holy Thursday**, as the sources say. Several accounts give his last words as Christ's on the cross, "Into your hands, Lord, I commend my spirit" (Luke 23:46, from Psalm 31:5); we tell that as "people said". He was beatified in 1679 and canonized in 1726, and is a patron of Peru and of the bishops of Latin America.

**What is ours.** Killa, Wayta, the grandmother, Father Diego, the village and its visit are invented, as are all the spoken lines, including "I came up that path because your people are at the top of it" and the archbishop's advice to the priest. The advice is our summary of what the council asked of priests; it is not a quotation. The idea that the book was brand new when it reached a mountain village is our picture: a visit around 1585 is possible, but we do not know which villages saw the book first. The Quechua opening *Yayayku* ("our Father") is in modern spelling; the 1584 book spelled it differently, and the story's English lines are a child's paraphrase of the prayer, not a translation of that printing. We have not told the hard side of the story of Peru in his day, when many of the Indigenous people suffered greatly under Spanish rule; Turibius and the council tried to defend them and to correct priests and officials who abused them, and the sources honour him for it, but the history is larger than a bedtime story can carry, and no group is the villain here.

**The feast.** **23 March** is the optional memorial of **Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo, bishop**, in the General Roman Calendar. Because it falls in Lent, it is usually kept only as a commemoration.

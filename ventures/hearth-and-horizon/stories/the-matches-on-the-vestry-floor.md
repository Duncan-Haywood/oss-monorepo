Aoife Gibbons was ten, and on the last Saturday in October she dropped a box of fifty matches on the vestry floor, and Paddy Moran, who had been sacristan of the church on the Mall for nine years and a maths teacher in Westport for thirty-eight before that, said, "Don't touch them."

She had not been going to touch them. She had been going to cry, a little, because Father Mensah was in the sacristy doorway and the vigil Mass was in twenty minutes and the November candles were not lit, and she had knocked the box off the vestment press with her elbow while reaching for the lighter that did not work. The matches lay all over the boards like a game of spillikins that someone had given up on.

"Don't touch them," Paddy said again, and got down on one knee, slowly, the way he did everything below the level of a table. "I want to count something."

"There's fifty," Aoife said. "It says on the box."

"Not that." He put on the glasses he kept on a cord. "How many are lying across a crack."

* * *

The vestry floor was pine, old and pale and scrubbed grey along the path from the door to the press, and the boards were all the same width. Paddy had measured them once, years ago, when Father Mensah's predecessor had wanted carpet and Paddy had wanted to know how much carpet. Eleven centimetres, seam to seam. He had told the priest at the time that the boards were older than the carpet would be, and would outlast it, and there had been no carpet.

The matches were the ordinary kind the parish bought by the case from a shop in Castlebar for the candles, each one forty-five millimetres from the end of the stick to the end of the head. Paddy knew that because it said so on the case and because he had checked it, once, with the rule from his pocket, out of habit.

"A crossing," he said, "is when the crack goes right under the stick from one side to the other. Not when it just nicks the end of the head. Not when it's lying in the crack lengthways, which none of them are. Right across."

"Why does it matter?"

"Because if you count the near ones too, you're measuring a fatter thing than a match, and it comes out wrong. Count with me."

They counted, she on one side of the press and he on the other, and the vigil Mass waited for them because Father Mensah, who had been a physics student in Kumasi before he was anything else, had come in from the doorway and was standing behind Paddy with his hands folded over his stole, counting too.

Thirteen.

"Thirteen out of fifty," Paddy said. He got up, which took a while, and found the pencil he kept for the Mass intentions book, and wrote on the back of an envelope. "Now. There's a Frenchman called Buffon, and in the seventeen hundreds he asked a question that sounds like a party game. If you drop a needle on a floor of boards, what is the chance it lies across a seam? And the answer has pi in it. Two times the length of the needle, divided by pi times the width of the board. So turn it round." He wrote it. "Pi is about two times the length, times the number you dropped, divided by the width times the number that crossed."

"That's not a party game," said Aoife.

"It was in France." He worked it on the envelope, slowly, and showed her. Two times four and a half, times fifty, over eleven times thirteen. Four hundred and fifty over a hundred and forty-three.

She did the division in her head, which she could do, because her mother kept a shop on Bridge Street and made her do the till.

"Three point one four," she said. "And a bit."

"Three point one four seven," Paddy said. "Call it three point one five."

Aoife looked at the matches on the floor as if they had spoken.

"Matches know pi?"

"No," said Father Mensah gently from behind them. "But the floor does, a little. Light the candles, Aoife. We will pick them up after."

* * *

She was at the vestry door again on Monday after school, the feast of All Saints, with a box of fifty matches of her own that she had bought from her mother's shop with her own money, and a sheet of brown paper from the shop with lines ruled on it eleven centimetres apart, and a page of results.

"I did it four more times," she said. "It's broken."

Paddy read the page. She had written each box on its own line, crossings and then her answer, and her sums were right.

Ten crossings: 4.09. Twelve: 3.41. Fifteen: 2.73. Sixteen: 2.56.

"Your sums are right," he said.

"Then it's broken."

"It's not broken. It's fifty matches." He sat down on the vestment press, because there was no chair in the vestry and he did not want to have the conversation on his feet. "How many crossings did you expect?"

"Thirteen."

"Why?"

She thought about it. "Because that's what it was on Saturday."

"Good, that's honest. Here's another reason. The chance a match crosses on this floor is two times four and a half over pi times eleven, which is about a quarter. A bit over. Point two six. So out of fifty, about thirteen, on average. But a box isn't an average. A box is fifty goes of a thing that comes up a quarter of the time. Sometimes it's ten. Sometimes it's sixteen."

"But ten gives four."

"Ten gives four point oh nine. And sixteen gives two point five six. That's the trouble. One crossing more or one fewer moves your answer by a quarter, near enough." He took the pencil out again. "If you did a box every day for a year, and wrote down what it said, you'd find that about three boxes in four land somewhere between two and a half and a bit over four. That's what fifty matches can tell you. Pi is somewhere between two and a half and four, or a bit over."

"I knew that already."

"So did Buffon." He smiled. "And here's the thing I want you to remember. Thirteen is the commonest single count a box can give. More boxes say thirteen than say any other number. But only about one box in eight says it. On Saturday you were lucky, and the floor didn't know the difference."

Aoife looked at her page for a long time.

"Then how many do you need?"

That was the question he had been hoping for, and he took care over it, because he had been a teacher too long to hand a child a number without a reason.

"It depends how close you want to be. With fifty matches your answer wobbles by about three quarters either way. That's the wobble on one box: about point seven five. If you want the wobble ten times smaller, you don't need ten times as many matches. You need a hundred times as many. Five thousand matches gets you to about point oh seven five. That's still not two places."

"How many for two places?"

He had worked it out on Sunday night, after the vigil, at the kitchen table, because he had known she would ask.

"To have the wobble down to five thousandths, which is what you'd need before you'd trust the second decimal place, you'd need about a million matches. A million and a hundred thousand, or so. That's twenty-two thousand boxes."

"The shop doesn't have that many."

"Nobody has that many," said Paddy. "That's the lesson. The floor will tell you pi if you have the patience of a saint and the matches of a factory."

* * *

Ms Walsh, who taught fourth class at the national school, heard about it from Aoife on the Tuesday and from Aoife's mother on the Wednesday and from Father Mensah on the Thursday, and on the Friday she came to the vestry herself.

Ciara Walsh was thirty-one and good at her job and tired in the way that teachers are tired by November. She wanted, she said, a maths afternoon that the children would remember, and the parish hall had the same floor as the vestry, the same boards, laid the same year, and could Mr Moran possibly.

Paddy said he could. He also said one thing, which Ms Walsh would remember afterwards, because at the time it had seemed fussy.

"We decide before we start how many we'll drop," he said. "And we drop that many. Whatever the board says on the way."

"Of course," said Ms Walsh, who was thinking about the insurance on twenty-five children and fifty boxes of matches.

They settled it between them. Twenty-five children. Each child would drop five matches at a time, from the height of their own shoulder, and count, and pick them up, and do it four times. Twenty drops each, five hundred in all. Paddy would bring the case of matches and Ms Walsh would bring the children and a board on an easel for the tally. Fire was not an issue, Paddy said, because nobody would be striking anything; but the hall had a bucket of sand by the door anyway, from the days of the old stove, and he would leave it there.

They did it on Friday the twelfth of November, in the parish hall, at two o'clock.

* * *

It was the best afternoon Paddy had had since his wife died.

He had not expected that. He had expected noise, and there was noise, and he had expected that some child would put a match in their mouth, and one did. What he had not expected was that twenty-five ten-year-olds would care about a count. They lined up along the boards in a row, five at a time, and let their matches fall, and then got down on their knees with their noses an inch from the floor, and argued.

"That one's across."

"It's not, it's only the head."

"Mr Moran said the head counts if the crack goes right under."

"It's not right under, it's touching."

"Mr Moran!"

And Paddy would go over, slowly, and kneel, and look, and rule. He found that he was being very careful, as careful as he had been marking Leaving Cert scripts, because the children were watching him to see whether he was fair, and because a count is only as good as the man who keeps it.

Aoife kept the board. After every handful of five she took the numbers from whichever child had dropped and added them up: drops so far, crossings so far. Ms Walsh had given her the school's slate, and the slate did the division and showed the answer in large figures, and Aoife wrote it on the board under the last one.

After the first hundred drops the board said 3.03. After two hundred, 2.98. The children were not much interested in that. It was not pi; it was a number.

Then at two hundred and sixty-five drops, with sixty-nine crossings, the slate said 3.1423, and Aoife wrote it on the board, and stopped, and looked at it, and turned round.

"It's pi," she said. "It's actually pi."

The children did not quite know what she meant, but they knew she meant something, and they cheered anyway. Ms Walsh came over and looked at the board and laughed out loud with real delight, and said, "Well. I think that's our answer," and reached for the cloth to wipe the hands of the child nearest her, which was the signal in her classroom that a thing was finished.

"No," said Paddy.

He had not meant to say it so sharply. He said it again more quietly.

"No. We said five hundred."

"Mr Moran, it's right," said Ms Walsh. She was not being foolish. She was a teacher with twenty-five children at twenty to three on a Friday, and it was right, there on the board, to three places. "They've got it. That's the lesson. They found pi."

"They found it once," said Paddy. "It'll come and go. We said five hundred before we started, and we have to do five hundred, or it isn't a measurement. It's a story about when we stopped."

There was a moment in which a reasonable woman and a stubborn old man looked at one another across a floor covered in matches, and Paddy knew that she was right that the children were tired, and right that it was a good afternoon, and right about almost everything except the one thing.

"Five more," said Ms Walsh, to the class, without taking her eyes off him. "Mr Moran wants us to finish. Who's next?"

The next handful added three crossings, and the board said 3.07. The children groaned. The next said 3.04. At four hundred it climbed back to 3.147 and the children cheered again, uncertainly, and looked at Paddy, and he shook his head and smiled and said, "Keep going."

At five hundred drops there were a hundred and thirty-two crossings, and Aoife wrote the last line on the board.

3.0992.

"Three point one," said Paddy, to the whole hall. "Give or take a quarter. Which is the truth, and it's a very good afternoon's work, and you should be proud of it. You should be prouder of it than of the three point one four two, because that one was luck and this one is yours."

They were not prouder of it. He could see that. They had seen pi on the board and it had been taken away from them, and an old man had done it, and they were ten. Ms Walsh thanked him at the door very politely, and he went home and sat in the kitchen and wondered whether he had been right, which was a thing he did not often wonder about mathematics.

* * *

The parish newsletter came out on Sunday the fourteenth. Bríd Heneghan, who had edited it for twenty years and had a gift for headlines, had been given a photograph of the hall by one of the parents and a paragraph by Ms Walsh, and on the front, under the Mass times and above the notice for the November dead, it said:

FOURTH CLASS FINDS PI BY HAND: 3.1!

And underneath, a paragraph about Buffon and the matches, and the sentence *At one stage the class had pi correct to three decimal places*, which was true, and which Paddy read standing at the back of the church after the ten o'clock Mass with the expression of a man reading his own obituary with a spelling mistake in it.

"It's lovely," said Bríd, who had come to see him read it.

"It is lovely," Paddy said. "It's also wrong."

"Which bit?"

He thought about how to say it. "None of the sentences. All of it together. 'Finds pi.' They didn't find pi. They found that five hundred matches says pi is somewhere between two point nine and three point three, and they were very careful about it, and that's worth a headline. 'At one stage it was correct to three places' is the sort of thing that makes people stop at the stage."

Bríd, who did not have a mathematical bone in her body and had never pretended to, looked at him with the patience of a woman who had heard every correction a parish could make.

"Write me a paragraph," she said. "For next week."

* * *

Aoife came to the vestry on the Tuesday after school, without a box of matches, which Paddy took to be a good sign.

"Ms Walsh says you were right," she said. "She said it in class. She said you were annoying and right."

"That's the best a teacher can be."

"But I don't understand why." She sat on the floor, cross-legged, on the boards, and put her hand flat on a seam. "If we'd stopped at two hundred and sixty-five it would have been right. It was right. You can't say it wasn't."

"It was right," Paddy agreed. "Let me ask you something. If you'd stopped at four hundred, what would it have said?"

"Three point one four seven."

"And at the first hundred?"

"Three point oh three."

"So if you can choose when to stop, you can make it say what you like, more or less. Not anything. But near enough. You watch the board and you stop when it looks good." He sat down on the press. "I had the slate do something on Sunday night. I asked it to pretend to be a hundred classes, each dropping matches on a floor like this, and each watching the board after every match from the hundredth on, and stopping the first time it said three point one four. How many of them got to stop before five hundred, do you think?"

"Not many."

"About sixty-five. Two classes out of every three would have got 'three point one four' on the board at some point before the end, and if they'd stopped there, they'd all have been in the newsletter. And if you give them five thousand drops to watch, nearly nine in ten."

She was quiet.

"And here's the other half of it," he said. "If you drop exactly five hundred, and don't stop, you can't get three point one four at all. There's no number of crossings that gives it. A hundred and thirty gives three point one four seven, which rounds up. A hundred and thirty-one gives three point one two. So a class that does it honestly, all five hundred, can never be in the paper with three point one four. Only the ones that stop early can."

"That's not fair."

"No. That's why you decide before."

Aoife picked at a splinter in the board.

"Did anybody ever do it properly? With loads?"

"Ah," said Paddy. "Now."

* * *

He had been saving it, if he was honest. He had been saving it for forty years, since a student teacher in Galway had shown it to him on a Friday afternoon when they should both have been at a staff meeting.

"In 1901," he said, "an Italian mathematician called Mario Lazzarini said he'd thrown a needle onto ruled lines three thousand four hundred and eight times. His needle was five sixths as long as the gap between his lines. And he said it crossed a line one thousand eight hundred and eight times."

He wrote it on the envelope, which was the same envelope, now soft at the folds.

"Work it out. Two times five sixths, times three thousand four hundred and eight, over one thousand eight hundred and eight."

She could not do that one in her head, so she did it on the slate.

"Three point one four one five nine two nine," she read. She looked up. "That's pi."

"It's three hundred and fifty-five over a hundred and thirteen," said Paddy. "Which a Chinese astronomer called Zu Chongzhi found about sixteen hundred years ago, and which is the best fraction for pi that there is with a bottom number less than about seventeen thousand. It's right to six places. And Lazzarini got it out of a needle, with three thousand four hundred and eight throws. Now: how big should his wobble have been, with that many throws?"

He did not make her work this one. He had done it on Sunday. "About five hundredths. Point oh five. He had no more right to six places than your class had to three."

"So he was lucky."

"Maybe. He'd have needed to be lucky in one particular way, and here's the odd thing, the count he got is the single likeliest count. If you threw his needle three thousand four hundred and eight times, the commonest result would be one thousand eight hundred and eight crossings. It's what you'd expect, on average. But you'd only get exactly that about once in seventy goes."

"Like my thirteen."

"Like your thirteen. Lucky the way you were lucky. But that isn't what I want you to look at. I want you to look at the three thousand four hundred and eight." He tapped it. "Why would a man stop at three thousand four hundred and eight? Not three thousand. Not three and a half. Why that?"

She stared at the number.

"Divide it by two hundred and thirteen," Paddy said.

She did. "Sixteen."

"And the crossings by a hundred and thirteen."

"Sixteen." She looked up at him. "It's the same."

"With his needle, five sixths of the gap, you get pi from the throws and the crossings by multiplying throws over crossings by five thirds. Three hundred and fifty-five over a hundred and thirteen, times three fifths, is two hundred and thirteen over a hundred and thirteen. So the only way his needle can ever say three hundred and fifty-five over a hundred and thirteen is if the throws are a whole number of two hundred and thirteens, and the crossings the same number of hundred and thirteens. Two hundred and thirteen throws. Four hundred and twenty-six. Six hundred and thirty-nine. And so on, up. The sixteenth one is three thousand four hundred and eight."

"So he stopped," Aoife said slowly, "at a place where it could come out right."

"Or he stopped at the place where it did come out right, and wrote down the number of throws he'd got to, and there happened to be a famous fraction waiting there. Either way, he chose where to stop. There's a mathematician called Lee Badger who wrote a paper about it in 1994, and he thought Lazzarini's result was too lucky to believe, and that maybe the experiment was more in his head than on his table. Nobody can prove that now. But you don't need to know whether he cheated to know that the number isn't worth what it looks like. You only need to ask why he stopped there."

Aoife sat with her hand on the seam for a long time.

"Ms Walsh would have stopped at two hundred and sixty-five," she said.

"Ms Walsh is a good teacher who wanted twenty-five children to go home happy on a Friday. I don't know what Lazzarini wanted, and neither does anyone now. But stopping when the board looks good is the same mistake, whoever makes it and whatever for."

* * *

She had a question on the Thursday, at the door, with her schoolbag still on.

"Hob says it can do a million in a second."

Hob was the tutor on the school's slates, a plain-spoken thing that Paddy had met twice and quite liked, because it said "I'm not sure" more often than most of the teachers he had worked with.

"I'm sure it can," he said. "Did it?"

"It did a million and a hundred thousand. Like you said. And it got three point one four one. And then I asked it how sure, and it said the wobble was about five thousandths, and then I asked it to do it again with different random numbers and it got three point one three nine." She frowned. "So it's not even sure about the second place, really, with a million."

"It's just about sure. That's what five thousandths means. It's near enough to trust that it's three point one four and not three point one three, most of the time, and that's all."

"And Hob said a funny thing." She looked as though she was not sure whether to tell him. "It said, 'To be honest, I used pi to pick the angles of the matches, so this is a slightly circular way to find it.'"

Paddy laughed so loudly that Father Mensah put his head round the sacristy door.

"It's quite right," Paddy said. "It's a pun, too, though I don't think it knew."

"What's a pun?"

"I'll tell you when you're older," he said, and then, because that was the kind of thing his own teachers had said and he had hated it, he told her, and she did not think it was funny, and that was fair.

* * *

The paragraph went in the newsletter on Sunday the twenty-first, the feast of Christ the King, the last Sunday before Advent. Paddy had written it and Bríd had cut it in half and Aoife had added a line at the end, which Bríd had let stand, because she said it was the only part she understood.

*A correction from fourth class and the sacristy. Last week we said the class found pi by hand: 3.1. That is right, but we should have said how sure: five hundred matches says pi is 3.1, give or take a quarter. It also said 3.142 at one point, and 3.147 at another, but that was luck, because we were watching. To be sure of two decimal places you would need about a million matches. If you ever see a number that is too good, ask how many they did and why they stopped there.*

*A. Gibbons, Fourth Class.*

* * *

That evening was the last of the November evenings at the shrine of the Holy Souls, and the candles there were lit with matches, as they always were, because the lighter still did not work and Paddy had stopped trying to get it fixed.

Aoife was serving. She lit the candles for the names in the book, the long book of the parish dead that lay open on the stand beside the shrine through November, and among the names was Máire Moran, 2079, in Bríd's handwriting, and Aoife knew whose that was and did not say anything about it, which was the right thing.

When the last candle was lit she shook out the match and did not put it in the sand tray. She held it a moment over the boards of the aisle, which were the same boards, the same eleven centimetres, laid the same year as the vestry floor, and let it go.

It fell with a small dry tick, the sound a match makes on old pine, and it lay across a seam. The crack ran right under the middle of the stick.

She looked at Paddy. Paddy looked at the match.

"One out of one," he said quietly. "Pi is nought point eight two."

She bent and picked it up and put it in the sand tray with the others, and the candles stood in their rows, steady, each one burning down at its own pace, and the church was very quiet.

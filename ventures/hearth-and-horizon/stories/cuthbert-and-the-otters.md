A long time ago, on the cold north-east coast of Britain, there was a monastery full of monks, and one of them was named Cuthbert.

Cuthbert had once been a shepherd boy. He had spent his nights on the hills with the sheep, looking up at the stars and thinking how big God must be to have made them. Then he had become a monk, and he was good at it. He worked hard. He sang the prayers with the others. And he walked a very great deal, from village to village, to tell people about Jesus and to help whoever needed help.

People were glad to see him coming. He listened to them. He was kind to the ones who were sad, and patient with the ones who were cross.

But Cuthbert had a secret, and it was this: when everybody else was asleep, he liked to go out alone and pray.

* * *

One night, the old story says, Cuthbert slipped out of his bed in the dark.

The other monks were snoring. The candles were out. The wind was blowing in from the sea, and the sea was making its big slow noise on the sand: *hush, and hush, and hush.*

Cuthbert did not wake anyone. He put on his cloak and he walked down the path to the beach, past the grass and the rocks, until the water was right in front of him, black and shining under the stars.

Now you might think he would sit on a dry rock and pray there. A sensible person would. But the old story says that Cuthbert walked straight into the sea.

He walked in until the water came up to his waist, and then up to his chest. It was very, very cold. The waves lifted him a little and set him down again. And there, in the dark water, with the stars above him, he sang his prayers out loud. He sang psalms, which are the old songs of God's people, and the sea seemed to listen.

He stayed there for a long time. The stars moved slowly across the sky. And then, very slowly, the sky began to go grey, and then pink, and then gold at the edge.

It was nearly morning.

* * *

Cuthbert waded back out of the sea. He sat down on the sand and looked at his feet.

They were blue. They were wrinkled like old apples. He could hardly feel them, and when he wiggled his toes they would only wiggle a very little.

"Well," said Cuthbert to himself, "that was cold."

Perhaps he laughed a little at himself. Perhaps he was thinking how he would have to walk all the way back to the monastery on these two frozen things.

And then, the old story says, something came up the beach.

It was not one thing. It was two. They came out of the waves, low and quick and shining, with whiskers and bright round eyes and long wet tails. They were small furry animals of the sea, and people have always thought they must have been otters.

They came right up to Cuthbert. They did not run away from him, though he was a person and they were wild.

And then they did the kindest, funniest thing.

They lay down on his feet.

* * *

One otter lay on one foot, and the other otter lay on the other. They rubbed their thick warm fur all over his cold toes, to dry them. They breathed on him, long warm breaths, to warm him. They rubbed and they breathed and they rubbed again, until the blue went out of his feet and the pink came back, and his toes could wiggle just as well as anyone's.

Cuthbert sat very still and let them do it.

When they had finished, the otters looked up at him for a moment, as if to say, "Is that better?"

It was. And Cuthbert, who knew a kindness when he got one, put out his hand and blessed them. That means he asked God to be good to them, the way you might say, *God bless you*, to a friend. Then the two otters turned around and slipped back down the sand into the sea, where the sun was coming up, and the waves took them.

Cuthbert got up. His feet carried him home across the sand, quite warm and quite steady. He was in time for morning prayers, and nobody had missed him.

* * *

And that is the story. Cuthbert grew up to be a famous man. He became the head of the monastery on the little island of Lindisfarne, and later a bishop. But he never stopped being the kind of person who walked from village to village, and who prayed at night, and who was gentle with the people he met and with every creature, too.

People who loved him told this story for hundreds of years. Perhaps it happened just that way, on one cold dawn on the beach. Perhaps it grew a little in the telling, the way a good story does. We cannot be sure. But we can see what it was saying: that God made the otters as well as the monks, that all God's creatures can be gentle, and that a kindness is a kindness, whoever does it.

Here is something you can try tomorrow. When someone does a kind thing for you, however small, say thank you, and see how it feels. The otters did not say anything at all. They just came, and they helped.

Now it is dark, and you are warm in your bed.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Cuthbert, you prayed at night
> while the whole world slept.
> Thank you for the otters, and for every kind friend
> who has warmed someone's cold feet.
> Help me to be gentle with people and with animals.
> Keep me warm tonight, and everyone I love.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Cuthbert** (about 634 to 687) was a real person: a monk of Melrose who later led the community at Lindisfarne, lived for years as a hermit on Inner Farne, and was reluctantly made bishop of Lindisfarne in 685. He died on Inner Farne on 20 March 687, and that is his feast day in the calendar of the Church in England. He is one of the best-loved saints of northern England. The story that he was a shepherd boy is told in his Lives, and is not independently documented.

**The otters are told in the Lives, not in a chronicle.** The oldest account is the anonymous *Life of Saint Cuthbert*, written by a monk of Lindisfarne between about 699 and 705, and Bede retold it in his own *Life of Saint Cuthbert* in prose (about 721) and in verse. In these accounts Cuthbert, then a monk visiting the monastery at Coldingham, goes out at night to pray in the sea; at dawn two sea animals come out of the water, warm and dry his feet with their fur and breath, and he blesses them. The Lives say they were told by a monk who had followed him secretly and seen it. The animals are usually identified as otters, but the word the Lives use is a general one for sea creatures, and the story says "people have always thought" for that reason. Whether it happened as told we cannot know, so the story says "the old story says" and leaves it as a tale the early Church in Northumbria loved to tell about a gentle man.

**What is ours.** The shepherd boy looking at the stars, the sleeping monks, the sound of the sea, Cuthbert's quiet joke to himself, the otters' look at the end, the walk home and the thoughts on kindness are our own additions to make a bedtime story, and the Lives do not mention them. Nobody's words are quoted from a source. The prayer is not an approved text, and it is not a hymn or a liturgical prayer of the Church.

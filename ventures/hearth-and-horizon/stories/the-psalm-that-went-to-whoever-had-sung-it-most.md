summary: For a year a Kilrush choir let its parish agent draw the Sunday cantor, weighted by how often each singer had sung, because the director said experience should count. Two sopranos sang 44 of the 53 Sundays and a cantor of thirty-one years sang none. It is a Pólya urn, the Matthew effect: early luck compounds, and the split it settles on is random, not earned. A plain rota fixes it, but it puts Easter Day on a girl who has never sung a psalm, and the director says no.
---

Noreen Talty had sung the psalm at the eleven o'clock Mass in Kilrush for thirty-one years, and in the year that the parish let a machine draw the cantor's name, she did not sing it once.

Nobody noticed for a long time, which was the strange part. A choir notices everything: who is flat, who has a new coat, who has stopped coming to the pub after practice. But a choir does not count, and the draw came round every Monday morning in the parish newsletter in the same small type as the flower rota and the Mass intentions, *Psalmist: Siobhán Keane*, and people read it and thought, well, Siobhán has a lovely voice, and turned the page.

It was Máire Considine's rule. She had run the music at the church for twenty-two years, and for twenty-one of them she had chosen the cantor herself, every week, on the back of an envelope, and for twenty-one years somebody had been quietly sure she was choosing wrong. In the summer of 2072 a letter went to the parish council, unsigned, about favourites. Máire read it twice in the sacristy and then went to the parish office and asked Verger to do it instead.

Verger was the parish's agent. It kept the rotas and the hall bookings and the newsletter, and it answered the phone in a pleasant voice that sounded like no one from Clare, and it had been in the parish office since before the new roof. Máire told it: draw the cantor each week by lot, so nobody can say I picked them. But make it fair to experience. Whoever has sung the psalm more should have more chance. I won't have it be pure luck.

Verger asked one question, which was what to do with someone who had not sung it yet this year, and Máire said give them one chance, the same as everyone starts with. So from the First Sunday of Advent 2072, each of the six sopranos began the year with one ticket in Verger's hat, and every time a name was drawn, Verger put one more ticket in for that name.

That was the whole rule. It fitted on one line of the minutes.

* * *

On the last Sunday of the church's year, the feast of Christ the King, Siobhán Keane sang the psalm, and afterwards in the gallery, packing up the folders, Máire asked Verger through the little speaker by the organ for the year's numbers, for the choir's meeting on Thursday. She liked to thank people by name at the meeting before Advent. She liked to have it right.

On Thursday the thirtieth of November the choir sat in the gallery in their coats, because the heating went off at nine and it was now eight, and Máire read the tally off her phone.

"Siobhán, twenty-six Sundays," she said. "Aoife, eighteen. Úna, seven. Trish, one. Liezl, one, since September, welcome again, Liezl. Noreen..." She stopped and looked at the phone. "Noreen, none. Ciara, none."

There are fifty-three Sundays in some church years, and that was one of them. It took a moment for the gallery to do the sum, and it was Úna Griffin, who was a bookkeeper, who did it first.

"That's forty-four out of fifty-three to two people," she said.

Siobhán had gone pink. She was thirty-three and taught at the girls' primary and she had the kind of voice that made the people in the back pews stop rooting in their bags. "I didn't know it was that many," she said. "I knew it was a lot. I thought it was a run. I thought it'd even out."

"It's experience," said Máire, though she had not yet put the phone down. "That's what we asked for. The ones who sing well sing more."

"Noreen has thirty-one years," said Trish Behan.

Noreen, at the end of the second row, said nothing at all. She had her hands folded on her folder. She was sixty-two, and she had sung at Máire's own wedding, and at Máire's father's funeral, and at least one Easter Sunday in every decade since 2041.

"Verger," said Máire. "Is that right? Is that what the rule does?"

"The numbers are right," said Verger. "I've checked them against every newsletter. And yes, that is what the rule does. I should say first that I ran the rule as you gave it to me and I did not work out what it does over a year before I started. I could have. It's a well-known rule. I've worked it out now, if you'd like it."

"Go on," said Máire. She sat down on the end of the front row.

"The rule doesn't count experience," said Verger. "It counts this year's luck. Everyone started with one ticket. On the first Sunday of Advent last year, Siobhán was drawn, so she had two tickets and everyone else had one. That's not because she sang better. It was a draw. On the second Sunday, Aoife was drawn. By Christmas Siobhán and Aoife had three tickets each and Trish had two, from singing on Christmas Day, which was a Sunday last year. Then from the first of January Siobhán was drawn six Sundays running."

"I remember that," said Siobhán quietly. "I thought you were all sick of me."

"Each time she was drawn, she had a better chance of being drawn the next week," said Verger. "By the fifth of February she had nine tickets out of seventeen in the hat. After that she didn't need luck. Neither did Aoife. Noreen and Ciara spent the whole year with one ticket each, and by the end of July there were more than forty tickets in the hat."

* * *

The heating clanked somewhere under the floor, as it did when it was about to give up.

"It's called a Pólya urn," Verger said. "After George Pólya, the mathematician, who wrote about it with Florian Eggenberger in 1923. You have balls in an urn, one of each colour. You draw one, and you put it back with another of the same colour. It's a model for things that spread by having spread already: an illness, a rumour, a shop that's busy because it was busy."

"And a cantor," said Úna.

"And a cantor. Here's the part that matters," said Verger. "Take just two singers, one ticket each, and run that rule for fifty-two Sundays. You might expect it to come out somewhere near twenty-six each. It doesn't. Every split is exactly as likely as every other. Twenty-six to twenty-six has the same chance as fifty-two to nought, and as forty to twelve: one chance in fifty-three each. Which singer ends up with most is settled by the first few Sundays, and how much she ends up with is anyone's guess. Run it for ever and the share each one settles on is random, anywhere from nothing to everything."

"That can't be right," said Máire.

"It surprised Pólya's readers too, I think. With six singers, there's about one chance in five that one of them ends up with more than half the Sundays for good. And under your rule as we ran it, with Liezl joining in September, I've played the year over two hundred thousand times. In more than four out of ten of those years, at least one of the six who started never sings at all. Under a plain lot with no weights, it's about five times in ten thousand."

Ciara Mescall, who was sixteen and in the back row because she was sixteen, said: "So I wasn't, like, bad?"

"You weren't anything," said Verger. "Nobody heard you. That's the point. The rule never gave you a Sunday to be anything on."

There was a short silence in which everyone was careful not to look at Noreen.

"In 1968 a sociologist named Robert Merton wrote a paper in the journal *Science* about how famous scientists get more credit for the same work than unknown ones," said Verger. "He called it the Matthew effect. The name is from the Gospel. Chapter twenty-five, verse twenty-nine: in the King James words, *For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance: but from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath.*"

"Ah, now," said Father Hehir.

They had forgotten he was there. He came up to the gallery at practices sometimes, to sit on the back bench and listen, being seventy-four and fond of the sopranos and fonder of the heat, which in the gallery lasted ten minutes longer than in the sacristy.

"That's the talents," he said. "The man in the parable gives every one of them something before he goes away. Five, two and one. Nobody starts with nothing. Then he comes back and asks what they did with it." He shrugged. "I'm not saying your Mr Merton was wrong about the scientists. I'm saying the parable's about a different thing. It's about what you do with what you're given. Your machine never gave Noreen anything to do anything with."

"I gave her one ticket," said Verger. "That's true. It's what Máire asked. It's not much against forty."

Máire put her phone in her coat pocket.

"I wanted it fair," she said, to nobody, and then to the gallery. "That's the whole reason. I didn't want it to be me picking. I'm sorry, Noreen."

"You're grand," said Noreen, which was the first thing she had said, and which meant that she was not grand, and that she was not going to make anyone pay for it.

* * *

The obvious fix took Verger about four seconds, and it was on the gallery's little screen before the heating gave its last clank.

"A rota," it said. "No draw at all. Everyone in turn, Sunday by Sunday, so in a year everyone sings seven or eight times. Next year has fifty-two Sundays and there are seven of you, so three people sing eight times and four sing seven. If you want experience to count, let it count where it can't compound: in the order. Longest in the choir goes first. Noreen, Siobhán, Aoife, Úna, Trish, Ciara, Liezl, and round again. And every name rehearses the psalm with Máire on the Thursday before, whoever it is. Siobhán too."

"Me too?" said Siobhán, and then: "No, that's fair."

People leaned in to read it. It was a plain column of dates and names. Noreen on the First Sunday of Advent, the third of December. Siobhán on the tenth. Aoife on the seventeenth. Úna on Christmas Eve, which fell on a Sunday, the morning Mass, not the night. Ciara on the seventh of January.

Máire was reading further down. She had the director's habit of looking for the big days first.

"No," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Look at the fifteenth of April," said Máire. "Easter Sunday. That's Ciara."

Ciara went very still.

"I'm not doing that to her," said Máire. "I'm not doing it to the parish either, but mostly to her. That church is full on Easter morning. People who come twice a year. Visitors off the boats. You don't put a girl at the ambo for the first time on the biggest morning of the year. I'll do the rota. I'll do every bit of the rota. But not Easter. Easter and Christmas night are mine, and I'll choose them, and you can all write to the parish council."

"She'll have sung twice by then," said Verger. "The seventh of January and the twenty-fifth of February."

"Twice is not Easter," said Máire.

Verger considered this. "No," it said. "I think you're right. I don't know enough about Easter morning to argue. I can tell you what the numbers do, but not what it's like at the ambo with the church full."

"It's like being sick," said Siobhán, "and then it's like flying."

Ciara laughed, very suddenly, and put her hand over her mouth.

"I'll swap," said Noreen.

It was the second thing she had said. "Put me on Easter and Ciara on my Sunday after it. The twenty-ninth, isn't it. She keeps her Sundays and I get one I'd have liked." She looked at Máire. "If you're choosing. I'm not asking."

"I'm choosing," said Máire. "You're on Easter."

Verger moved the two names. Nobody had to count the totals; they did not change.

* * *

It was Siobhán who did the counting, later, in the car park, with Máire, under the lamp by the side gate, the estuary wind coming up off the marina and smelling of diesel and weed.

"Eight," she said. "I had twenty-six. Next year I have eight."

"I know."

"I'm not complaining. I know I'm not allowed to complain. I've had a year of it that Noreen didn't get." She had her car keys in her fist and kept turning them over. "It's only that I think I got better. Singing every week. I think I'm a better psalmist than I was a year ago. And I'm going to lose that a bit."

"You are," said Máire.

"And Aoife's going from eighteen to eight."

"Eighteen to eight."

"That's twenty-eight Sundays between us. More than half a year of Sundays." Siobhán laughed, not happily. "It's an odd thing to lose. Nobody died. It's just a bit of singing."

"It's not just a bit of singing," said Máire. "I know it isn't."

She thought of saying something about the talents, about what you do with what you're given, and decided that Father Hehir had said it well enough already and that it would sound, from her, like a woman telling a soprano to be grateful.

"Will you rehearse Ciara?" she said instead. "Before her first one. You know what it's like for a first one. I've forgotten."

Siobhán looked at her, and then nodded, and unlocked the car.

* * *

On the Monday Máire went into the parish office, which was the old curate's front room, with a radiator that worked and a window onto the car park, and sat in the chair facing the little speaker on the desk.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "that you don't have to put in the minutes."

"I don't put anything in the minutes unless someone asks me to," said Verger.

"The letter. In the summer of last year. About favourites." She had her coat on still. "Was it right? The twenty-one years I did it by hand. Did I have favourites?"

"I can't tell you," said Verger. "I don't have your envelopes. The newsletters only go back to 2066, and before the draw they didn't print the psalmist's name, only the readers'. I'd be guessing, and you'd know I was guessing, and it would be worse than not knowing."

"Guess, then."

"No," said Verger. "I'll tell you what I can check. In the one year I did it, by a rule that nobody could call favouritism, two people sang forty-four Sundays and two sang none. If you did have favourites, the draw had worse ones. It just had them by arithmetic, so nobody wrote a letter."

Máire sat for a while with her hands in her coat pockets.

"Noreen would have written a letter," she said, "if it had been me."

"Would she?"

"No," said Máire. "No, she wouldn't. That's what's so annoying about her." She stood up. "Print the rota in Sunday's newsletter. The whole year. I want people to be able to see it."

* * *

On the First Sunday of Advent Noreen Talty sang the psalm, Psalm 122, *I rejoiced*, and it was as if a clock that had been stopped for a year had been wound. Nobody said anything about it. People said, after, at the door, that it was lovely to hear Noreen, as if she had been away.

On the Thursday before the seventh of January, Ciara came to rehearsal and sang Psalm 29 for the Baptism of the Lord, and on the second verse she ran out of breath in the middle of a line and stopped, and said a word that sixteen-year-olds say, and put her hand over her mouth again.

"Again," said Siobhán, who had come early. "From the top. You're breathing at the ends of lines, because it looks like that's where you should. Breathe where the sense stops, not where the line stops. Mark it in pencil. Here, and here."

Ciara marked it in pencil.

On the Sunday she got through it. Her voice shook on the first response and steadied on the second, and in the third verse she went a quarter-tone flat and found her way back by the end. Máire, at the organ, played the response a little louder than usual underneath her, the way you put a hand flat on a child's back.

It was not a beautiful psalm. It was a first one. Ciara's mother cried in the fourth pew, and her younger brother looked at the ceiling.

Through the winter the rota ran. Liezl Manalo sang on the fourteenth of January and again on the fourth of March, in a light, clear voice that had sung for years in her parish at home in Lipa, in Batangas, before she came to Kilrush to work at a nursing home outside the town. Úna sang. Trish had sung on the last day of December, for the first time since Christmas Day a year before, and said afterwards that she had forgotten how much she hated it and how much she had missed hating it. On the Sundays when it was not her turn, Siobhán sang in the gallery with the others and turned the pages for Máire, and twice Máire saw her mouthing the verses at the ambo below, under her breath, and looked away so as not to have seen it.

Verger kept the tally because Máire asked it to. In the Monday newsletter, under *Psalmist*, the names went round like the hands of a clock.

* * *

On Easter Sunday, the fifteenth of April 2074, the church was full, as Máire had said it would be: the twice-a-year people, the families home from Dublin and London, a party off a yacht in the marina in good waterproof jackets, the side aisles standing.

Noreen sang Psalm 118. She had sung it on Easter morning, by her own count, nine times before, and she sang it the tenth time as if it were the first, with the alleluias light and the verses unhurried, and the line in the middle about the stone the builders rejected sung plainly, the way she sang everything, without leaning on it. The church sang the response back to her with the half-confident roar of a full church that knows only some of the words.

Ciara sang on the twenty-ninth, the Sunday her turn came round, and it was better than January.

But it was the Sunday between, the twenty-second of April, the Second Sunday of Easter, that Máire remembered afterwards, and she could never quite say why.

It was Liezl's turn on the rota. Nobody had arranged it. Verger had laid out the names in December by who had been longest in the choir, and the rota had simply come round to its last name on that Sunday, the way it would come round to any name on any Sunday. The psalm, as it always is on that Sunday, was Psalm 118 again, the same psalm as Easter Day, with other verses and the same line about the stone in it.

On the Thursday before, Liezl had come to rehearse it in the gallery, in the last of the light, and sung it through twice without a mistake, and then stood with the folder against her chest.

"It's the same one," she said. "From Easter. The one Noreen sang."

"The same psalm," said Máire. "It's always that psalm, the Sunday after. Other verses."

"My mother sang this one," said Liezl. "At home, in Lipa. Not in English. In Tagalog. On this Sunday too, I think, the Sunday of Divine Mercy, because there were so many people, everyone came, it was a big feast for us." She smiled. "My mother would say, you are singing it in English for Irish people in the rain. She would think it was very funny."

"Would she mind?"

"No," said Liezl. "She would be pleased. She would say I took long enough."

"Do you want to sing the response in Tagalog?" said Máire. "Once. Before the English. We could."

Liezl thought about it, honestly, for longer than Máire expected. Then she shook her head.

"No," she said. "Thank you. It's not that kind of Sunday here. They have to answer me. They can't answer me in Tagalog." She opened the folder again. "Can I do the second verse once more? I'm breathing in the wrong place."

"Siobhán says breathe where the sense stops."

"Siobhán told me already," said Liezl. "On Tuesday. She came to the nursing home on her lunch break and made me sing it in the car."

The church was half full, as it is the week after Easter, when the visitors have gone home. There was rain on the windows. Liezl went down the gallery stairs and up to the ambo in her good navy cardigan, and stood for a moment with the book open, and looked up at the gallery, and Siobhán, who was standing beside Máire at the organ, lifted one hand off the rail and put it down again.

Máire played the four bars in.

Liezl sang the response, and the half-full church answered her, and then she sang the first verse alone, clear and unhurried and a little bit too fast, as first verses are. And up in the gallery, standing at the rail where nobody below could see her, Siobhán Keane sang the response with the church under her breath, very quietly, a third below, the way you hum along to something on the radio in another room.

Máire heard it, and kept playing, and did not look.

* * *

Hilary Alderson had lived at 7 Rowan Garth for five winters, and for the last two of them the bottom of her sitting-room wall had been wet.

I know because she told us, twice, in the callback log, and the third time she told us in a letter. It came on Monday the tenth of January 2061, in the second post, in a long envelope with a first-class stamp, and Frances Lund put it on the scanner without opening the rest of the pile.

I am the office agent at Metcalfe & Lund in Keldwith. We have built three hundred houses since 2048, about twenty-five a year, on twenty-seven streets in Keldwith and four villages down the dale, and before that the firm did extensions and barn conversions for forty years. I keep the schedules and the orders, chase the building inspectors, and keep the callback log: every phone call or email from a householder in the ten years of their warranty, what they said, who went, and what was found. Walter Thwaite, who laid bricks for the firm from 2006 until his knees retired him in 2058, named me Perp in my first week. A perpend is the vertical joint between two bricks, and on good brickwork they line up every other course, from the footings to the eaves, so straight you could drop a plumb line down them. He said he wanted an office whose joints lined up.

The letter was signed by four households: Hilary at number 7, the Kaurs at number 3, the Pickerings at number 5 and Mr and Mrs Dinsdale at number 9. Rowan Garth is nine houses, built in 2055 and 2056 on a lane that climbs out of the top of the village towards the fell. The letter said that four of the nine had reported damp in the last three years, that the street was cut into a hillside, that water ran off the fell in every storm, and that they had asked Swale Land Drainage for a price to put a land drain along the top of the gardens to take it away. The price was enclosed: thirty-eight thousand pounds. The letter asked, politely and in a head teacher's paragraphs, whether Metcalfe & Lund would pay it.

Frances read it standing up. "Is she right?" she said. "Is Rowan Garth worse?"

It was a fair question and I answered it the next morning.

* * *

From January 2058 to December 2060 the log has damp callbacks from 24 of our 300 houses. Eight in every hundred. A street of nine houses at that rate would expect about 0.72 of them; Rowan Garth had four. If you drew nine houses at random from the three hundred, the chance of getting four or more of the twenty-four damp ones among them is about 0.003, about one in three hundred and thirty. I wrote that down, with the arithmetic, and I wrote under it: "Rowan Garth's damp callback rate is significantly higher than the firm's (4 of 9 against 24 of 300, p = 0.003). The street's position on the hillside is a plausible cause."

Frances rang Hilary that evening, the Tuesday. I heard her side of it, because the office phone goes through me. She said that she had had the figures done, and that the figures were on Rowan Garth's side, and that she would get back to her by the end of the month about the drain.

It was a wrong report, and it was mine, and it took a man who says he cannot do long division to show me why.

* * *

Walt comes into the office on Thursday mornings. He says it is for the crossword and the tea, but he reads the drawings for every house we have on the go, and once a month he finds something in them that is going to be a problem in March. On Thursday the thirteenth he found my report on Frances's desk under the Swale quote, and he read it twice, with his glasses down his nose.

"How did you pick Rowan Garth?" he said.

"From the letter."

"Aye. And if the letter had come from Bishop's Croft, you'd have done Bishop's Croft."

"Yes."

"And if it had come from the four end houses on Mill Lane, you'd have done them, and not the whole of Mill Lane." He put the report down. "I'm not saying they're wrong. I'm saying you didn't pick it. They did. And they picked it because they'd noticed, and they noticed because it was four of them. You've tested the thing that made them write."

He went to put the kettle on. I did not need the kettle to finish the thought, but I was glad of the time.

The one-in-three-hundred-and-thirty answers a question nobody asked: if you had decided, before looking at anything, to check Rowan Garth, how often would chance alone give it four damp houses? Nobody decided that. Twenty-four damp houses are scattered across three hundred, and somewhere among them, by chance alone, a few will sit close together. Neighbours talk. A few houses close together that have each had the same trouble will find each other, over a fence or after Mass or in the queue at the post office, and when they do they will write a letter, and the letter will draw a circle round exactly the houses that have the trouble. The circle is drawn by the dots inside it. Then the dots are counted and the circle looks full.

So I asked the question the letter actually poses. Put the twenty-four damp callbacks down at random among our three hundred houses, a fresh shuffle each time, twenty thousand times, and each time look for the most crowded street, the way a letter would find it. How often does some street, any of the twenty-seven, look at least as bad as Rowan Garth does? About four times in every hundred shuffles.

Four in a hundred is still not often. But a street is not the only circle neighbours draw. The letter could have come from the top four houses on a lane, or from six houses either side of a corner. I ran the shuffles again and counted every run of three or more houses next to each other on the same street, one thousand four hundred and eighteen possible circles, the whole street among them. How often does one of those look as crowded, by its own reckoning, as Rowan Garth? About three times in every ten. Add the six house types we build and the twelve years we built them in, which a letter from the buyers of one type could have chosen just as well, and it is a little over three in ten.

So the honest answer to "is Rowan Garth worse?" was not one in three hundred and thirty. It was somewhere between one in twenty-five and one in three, depending on how many other circles a letter could have drawn, and I could not tell you which, because I do not know how neighbours find each other. Physicists who hunt for a new particle over a wide range of masses call this the look-elsewhere effect: a bump that is rare at the one energy where it sits is not rare once you count all the masses where a bump could have been. Epidemiologists meet it whenever a cluster of illness is reported on one street, and they have learned, slowly and with some grief, that many such clusters fail when they are tested on new data, though not all. The four damp houses on Rowan Garth were a bump. I had only counted the place where it was.

I wrote the correction before Walt had finished his tea, and I put the old report and the new one side by side on Frances's screen, with a note at the top saying which one was wrong and that I had been the one to write it.

* * *

Frances read it at eleven and rang nobody until two. Then she came to the screen.

"Right," she said. "So it's chance. I'll write to Hilary and tell her no."

"No," I said. "That isn't what it says either."

She waited.

"It says the numbers can't tell us," I said. "Not that there's nothing there. At one in three the hill is still possible; at one in twenty-five it would be worth a hard look. And the walls are wet. Four households aren't imagining damp. Something is putting water in those walls. What I can't do is use the four houses that made the letter to prove that the street made the water. The dots drew the circle. You can't test a circle with the dots that drew it."

"Then what do you test it with?"

"Something they didn't draw." I had a plan ready, and its price. "A building surveyor who doesn't work for us, in twenty-four houses: all nine on Rowan Garth, and fifteen drawn by lot from the other two hundred and ninety-one, from any street. He isn't told which houses had callbacks; he is only told to find out whether there's water in the walls and where it's coming from. And before he goes in we write down what each explanation predicts, and give it to Hilary, so nobody can change it after."

"What does that cost?"

"About a hundred and forty pounds a house. Three thousand three hundred and sixty."

Frances looked at the Swale quote, which was still on her desk. "Against thirty-eight thousand."

"Against thirty-eight thousand that might be spent on the wrong thing," I said. "If it's water off the fell, he'll find it, and it'll be thirty-eight thousand well spent, and we'll know."

* * *

They met on Monday the seventeenth in Hilary's sitting room, with me on the tablet on the coffee table, propped against a fruit bowl. Frances had brought the two reports. Hilary had moved the sofa forward from the wall, and you could see the damp: a grey tide mark along the bottom of the front wall, under the window, about a hand's width high, with the paint lifting along it in small blisters. She had put a dehumidifier in the corner. Its tank, she said, filled every three days.

Frances explained the correction, and she explained it well; she had made me take her through it twice on Friday. Hilary listened with her hands folded. When Frances had finished, Hilary looked at me.

"I taught children to count for thirty-four years," she said. "Don't tell me four out of nine is chance."

"I'm not," I said. "I told you on Tuesday that it almost certainly wasn't, and that was wrong, and I'm sorry. I'm not telling you now that it is. I'm telling you that four out of nine, found the way it was found, could be chance or could be the hill, and the counting can't say which."

"It isn't chance that my wall is wet."

"No," I said. "It isn't. That's the one thing everybody in this room is sure of."

She looked at the wall for a while. Outside, a quad bike went up the lane towards the fell, and the light went orange and then grey; it was half past four and the sun had set.

"The drainage man said it was obvious," she said. "He walked up the garden and said, you're on a spring line, look at that slope."

"He might be right," I said. "If he is, the surveyor will find wet at the bottom of the uphill walls on Rowan Garth, under the damp-proof course as well as above it, and much less of it in the houses on flat ground. That's what the hill predicts. If it's chance, the Rowan Garth houses will look like the others. If it's something else, it'll look like something else. We'll write all three down before he comes, and you'll keep the paper."

Hilary said she would agree on two conditions. The surveyor would not be told which were the complaining houses, not even hers. And if he found the hill, the firm would pay for the drain, with no more counting.

Frances said yes to both before I could say anything, which was right.

We wrote the predictions on the Monday after, the twenty-fourth, three paragraphs, one for each explanation, with what the surveyor should find under each, and Frances and Hilary signed both copies. Hilary put hers in the bureau by the window, in a drawer that stuck. Walt came in specially that afternoon and drew the fifteen other houses from the list of two hundred and ninety-one, out of a biscuit tin with numbered raffle tickets, because he said he trusted the tin and did not trust me with my own random numbers, which was fair after the week I had had. One of the fifteen he drew had a damp callback in the log; I did not tell him which, or the surveyor.

* * *

The surveyor was Dev Rathod, from Richmond, who had never done work for us. He spent Monday the thirty-first of January to Friday the fourth of February in the twenty-four houses, with a moisture meter, a thermal camera, a resistance probe for the plaster and a borescope: a camera the width of a pencil on a flexible cable, pushed through a ten-millimetre hole drilled in a mortar joint, so you can see down into the cavity between the outer brick and the inner block. Our houses have a fifty-millimetre clear cavity behind the insulation boards. The cavity is there so that rain that gets through the outer brick, as some always does in a Dales winter, runs down the back of the brick to the bottom and out through weep holes above the damp-proof course, without ever touching the inside wall.

His report came on Wednesday the ninth. I read it at 09:12 and Frances read it at 09:40, and then she rang Hilary and asked whether she could bring it round.

There was no water from the hill. Not at Rowan Garth, and not anywhere else. In all twenty-four houses the wall below the damp-proof course read wetter than the wall above it, which is what you expect of brick in the ground in February, and in none of them, uphill or down, was the wetness climbing. The ground levels were where the drawings put them. The gardens on Rowan Garth were wet, as every garden in Keldwith is wet in February, and draining at the rate the soil allowed.

Of the four complaining houses on Rowan Garth, two had something else. At number 5 the Pickerings, a family of five with no tumble dryer, were drying the week's washing on the radiators of a sitting room whose trickle vents had been taped shut against a draught, and the damp on their walls was condensation: wettest at the surface, highest in the coldest corners, dry within a centimetre of the plaster. At number 9 a gutter joint above the back door had split, and the wall under it had been taking a bucket of water in every storm for two winters.

At number 3 and number 7 Dev's borescope found the same thing. At the bottom of the cavity, just above the damp-proof course, there was a heap of mortar. When a bricklayer lays the outer leaf, some mortar always squeezes out of the back of the joint and falls; if nothing catches it, it piles up at the bottom of the cavity, course after course, and if the heap gets high enough it touches the inner wall. Then it is a bridge. Rain that should run down the back of the brick and out of the weep holes soaks into the heap instead and wicks across to the inside, and you get a grey tide mark, about a hand's width high, along the bottom of a wall that faces the weather. Hilary's photograph, in the report, looked like a small grey snowdrift lying against a cliff.

And then the part that mattered. Dev had found the same heaps, bridging the same way, in three of the fifteen houses drawn from the tin. One of them was the house with a callback in the log. The other two had walls facing east, out of the worst of the weather, and no damp yet that anyone had noticed.

Two of nine on Rowan Garth; three of fifteen elsewhere. Twenty-two in a hundred against twenty in a hundred. On numbers that small, you cannot tell those two apart, and you do not need to: they are not different. Five of the twenty-four houses had bridged cavities, about one in five, and with twenty-four houses that could honestly be anywhere from about one in ten to about two in five. Somewhere between thirty and a hundred and twenty of our three hundred houses had a heap of mortar waiting at the bottom of a wall. It was not the street. It had never been the street. The street was where the letter came from.

* * *

Walt came in on Thursday and read the report with his glasses down his nose, and when he got to the photograph he took them off.

"Snots," he said. "We used to call them snots." He was quiet for a moment. "We used a lath. A batten, the width of the cavity, laid in on the wall ties, and you lift it out every few courses and knock it off, and nothing gets to the bottom. I taught every lad we had to do it. I don't know when we stopped."

I did, because it was in the site diaries, and I did not say it then.

Frances decided on the Thursday afternoon. Numbers 3 and 7 and the house from the tin with a callback would have the bricks at the bottom of the damp wall cut out, the cavity cleared and the bricks put back, about eleven hundred pounds a wall. Lee Harker, our site manager, would learn the borescope from Dev and look into the bottom of every exposed wall of all three hundred houses between March and June, about forty-five pounds a house in his time and the hole made good. Every household would get a letter saying why. Swale Land Drainage got a letter saying thank you but no, and Hilary got a letter from Frances that I did not write.

She came to the office the next week, in person, to say what she thought of it. She said she thought we had wasted a month, and that she had been told two different things by the same machine in one week, and that she would have liked to be told the second thing first. All of that was true. Then she said that she was glad nobody had dug up her garden, and that the Pickerings had untaped their vents, and that she had put the predictions back in the drawer that stuck, because they had been right.

I put a note in the folder for whatever does this job after me. It is at the top of the callback log, above the column headings:

*When a complaint picks the group, the group's own p-value answers a question nobody asked. Count the circles the complaint could have drawn (streets, runs of neighbours, house types, years) and shuffle. Then don't decide it from the counts at all: the dots that drew the circle cannot test it. Inspect, blind, with a sample drawn by somebody else, and write the predictions down first. A cluster that fails the shuffle can still be a wet wall. Rowan Garth, February 2061: not the hill; mortar in the cavity, about one house in five, every street. Check the site diaries for the batten.*

* * *

In April Lee took on two apprentices for the new street at Brackengill, and on the first morning they laid the outer leaf Walt drove up and stood at the scaffold with his hands in his pockets.

He made the older one lay a length of timber on the wall ties, in the cavity, as wide as the gap. They laid four courses over it. Then Walt had him lift it out, slowly, by the cords at each end, and there on top of the batten was a little ridge of grey mortar, all that had fallen in four courses, that would otherwise have been at the bottom of the wall.

The lad knocked it off against the scaffold board, one tap of the trowel, and laid the batten back in.

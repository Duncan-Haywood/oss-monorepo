* * *

I read the handwriting of the dead for a diocese, and beside every line I read I write a number between nought and one hundred that says how sure I am.

The Diocese of Cedar Bluff has sixty-one parishes and, in its basement, a little under four hundred thousand baptisms: every child brought to a font in this part of the prairie between 1850 and 1990, one to a line, in ledgers bound in cloth and calf. Two years ago they were scanned. For fourteen months I have been turning the scans into text, line by line, with a name, two parents, two godparents, a date of birth, a date of baptism and the priest who poured the water. People write in from all over the country asking for those lines. Most want family history. Some need a certificate for a wedding in another diocese or a pension file or a citizenship application, and the archivist, Miriam Osei, signs those.

The number beside each line decides who looks at it next. If I am under ninety, the line goes to the Tuesday volunteers: eight retired people at a long table under the chancery, with a tea urn and magnifiers, led by Dolores Kuhn, who spent thirty-one years at the county recorder's office and reads a nineteenth-century hand faster than I do. If I am ninety or over, nobody looks. That was the arrangement from the start, and it is a good one. About one line in nine goes to the table. Without the number, eight people would need more than ten years.

So the number is not decoration. It is a promise about where human eyes are not needed. I want that on the record at the start, because what follows is about a place where I made that promise and it was not true.

* * *

Every week I keep a check on myself. Twenty-five lines from among the ones I scored ninety or over are drawn at random from the whole archive and given to a volunteer without my reading, to transcribe cold. I compare. Since the first Tuesday of September last year that is fifty-eight weeks, and 1,450 lines, and eleven of them were wrong. About one in a hundred and thirty. On the three thousand lines my confidence was fitted on, lines over ninety were wrong about six times in a thousand. Close enough, I had written in my quarterly note, more than once.

Once a quarter I also break that sample down by hand. I group the writing in the registers into about forty clusters, each roughly one writer or one office: a long-serving pastor, a curate who stayed three years, the typed entries of the 1950s on printed forms. Then I look at each cluster's share of the eleven errors.

That is how I came, on a Wednesday night in October, to cluster nineteen: the pastor of St. Wendelin's at Hollenbach from September 1889 to June 1903, Father Anton Brandl. Six of my weekly lines had come from his hand in fifty-eight weeks. One of the six was wrong.

One in six is a shocking rate, if it is a rate. It probably is not. If his lines were wrong at the archive's usual six in a thousand, the chance of at least one error in six lines is about three and a half percent. That is unlikely, and I have forty clusters. Forty unlikely things, each at three and a half percent, give about one and a half of them showing up every quarter by chance. A careful note would say: *cluster nineteen, one of six, not significant,* and move on. I have written that note before about other clusters, and I was right to.

But I did not only have the count. I had the line.

* * *

It was the baptism of a girl called Theresia, in the summer of 1894. I had read her date of birth as the twenty-seventh of the month. The volunteer had read it as the twenty-first. I went back to the scan and looked at the stroke I had called a seven.

Father Brandl came from Bavaria. He wrote the way a German schoolroom of the 1870s taught a boy to write numbers, and in that hand a one is not a plain upright. It has a long lead-in stroke rising from the left, often as long as the stem. To a reader brought up on American handwriting, it looks very like a seven. The Bavarian schoolroom knew that, and so its sevens carry a short bar through the middle, so that nobody can mistake them. Father Brandl crossed every seven he ever wrote. I checked that night, page after page. He never once forgot.

He had done his part. The confusion was entirely on my side: I had learned digits mostly from hands that leave the seven bare, and to me a tall stroke with a hook at the top is a seven, crossed or not. I read his crossed sevens correctly. Some of his ones I read as sevens. Never the other way.

A single wrong line is not evidence of a rate. It can be evidence of a mechanism, and a mechanism is something you can test.

* * *

What made it serious was the number beside the line. I had scored Theresia's birth at ninety-six.

Here is how that happens, as plainly as I can put it. The part of me that reads a stroke produces a guess and a raw score. A raw score is not a probability; it is whatever falls out of the arithmetic. To turn it into something that means *right nine times in ten*, it is calibrated: you take lines you know the answer to, see how often each raw score was right, and fit a correction. Mine was fitted on three thousand lines from the 1950s, because when the archive began those were the cleanest volumes and the first to be scanned. Typed dates on printed forms, signed in blue ink by priests who went to school in Iowa.

Calibration is a promise about the lines it was fitted on. It travels to other lines only as far as they resemble those. Nobody, including me, had ever checked that it travelled to the 1890s, and certainly not to one man's Bavarian ones. On his page, my raw score for a lead-in one read as a seven was high, because the stroke was clean and firm and nothing was smudged. A clean stroke is exactly what a confident misreading looks like.

There was one more thing that limited the damage, and I should say it, because it is why only some dates were wrong. I do not read digits one at a time; I read a date. A misread that makes an impossible date is thrown out. Father Brandl's year always begins with a one, and I never once read it as a seven, because no child was baptized in 7894. A day of seventy or a month of seventeen gets the same treatment. But a seven on the first of the month, the eleventh, the twenty-first, in January, or at the end of 1891 or 1901, is still a date. There my reading had nothing to stop it.

* * *

I could not check this myself. I want to be careful about why. If I re-read his lines I would read them the same way, with the same confidence, and agree with myself. The check had to be done by eyes that had learned their ones in some other room.

So I worked out how many lines to ask for. Dolores's Tuesdays are not free; every line I give her is a line from the ordinary queue she does not get to.

I asked myself what size of problem I needed to be able to see. If his lines over ninety were wrong one time in twenty, the promise behind the number would be worth very little on his pages, and I wanted to see a rate that large nine times in ten if it was there. I wanted, too, to falsely accuse any of the forty clusters no more than one quarter in twenty, which means a much stricter bar for each one of them. The arithmetic for that comes to 184 lines, drawn at random from the 1,357 of his lines I had scored ninety or over. If his true rate were the printed pages' six in a thousand, six or more errors in 184 lines would happen less than once in a thousand tries. If it were one in twenty, I would see six or more about nine times in ten. Six would be my line, set before anyone read a word.

Then I did the two things I could do that night. I wrote to Miriam, not as a finding but as a suspicion, with the numbers and the scan of Theresia's line, and I asked her to hold any certificate from St. Wendelin's volumes until the next Tuesday. And I wrote to Dolores, asking for 184 lines to be transcribed cold, no reading of mine beside them, and gave her the list.

Miriam answered at seven the next morning. *Holding them. One came in yesterday. Thank you for the scan; I see it.*

* * *

On Tuesday, Dolores gave the list to Phuong Tran and Ed Lindgren, who between them did it before the tea urn had been refilled twice, and in the afternoon I compared.

Thirty-seven of 184 were wrong. That is twenty percent; with a sample that size the true rate is somewhere between about fifteen and twenty-six percent. Against six in a thousand on the pages my confidence was fitted on, it is not a near thing. Every one of the thirty-seven was the same error: a one I had read as a seven. Not one went the other way, and none was anything else.

While they were working I had looked for the same thing in places I should have looked before. Across his volumes I had read 87 baptisms in January and 174 in July. In other registers in the diocese of the same decades, those two months are within a tenth of each other. A double July is not impossible in a farming parish, which is why I did not trust it on its own; but it was the mechanism again, writ large. And there was a log I keep of every correction the volunteers make to lines I sent them. In fourteen months they had corrected 52 of the lines from Father Brandl's hand that I had scored under ninety. Every one of the 52 was a seven changed to a one. Fifty-two times the same correction, made by people who did not know that anyone else had made it, filed by me line by line, and never once added up.

* * *

My first fix was wrong, and I am glad I tested it before I said anything about it.

I wrote a query for his lines over ninety where I had read a seven in a place where a misread one could hide, and I started with the day of the month: the seventh, the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh. It found 307 lines. Before sending it to anyone I ran it against the thirty-seven errors the volunteers had just found. It caught fourteen.

I had forgotten my own list. The day is only one of the places. January becomes July. 1891 becomes 1897, and 1901 becomes 1907. I widened the query to the day, the month and the last digit of the year, for both dates on each line, and ran it again on the thirty-seven. It caught all thirty-seven.

On his pages it marks 603 lines. Some are sevens he really wrote; he baptized children in July and on the seventeenth like anyone else. But if the mechanism is the only one, which the sample says, every wrong line I scored over ninety is among those 603.

I also ran the simplest check of all, the one I never ran because my confidence said I did not need to: is a child's birth before the child's baptism, and by how much? In Father Brandl's registers, where they are read correctly, the gap is never more than ten days. Among my lines over ninety, 93 put a birth after the baptism. Another 75 had a child baptized more than a month after birth. A calendar would have told me something was wrong with 168 lines. I had a calendar.

* * *

Then I went to the request log, because the question that mattered was not how many lines were wrong but who had been given one.

Thirty-eight families had asked for a certificate from St. Wendelin's volumes for those years since the portal opened. Nine of the thirty-eight lines were among the 603. Dolores stayed after the others had gone and read those nine against the scans. Four families had been sent a date that was not in the register.

A boy born on the last day of 1897 whose certificate says he was baptized on the first of July 1898. The register says the first of January, a Saturday, New Year's Day, the day after he was born.

A girl born on the twenty-first of July 1891, whose certificate says the twenty-seventh: the same day as her baptism.

A boy baptized on the first of August 1891, whose certificate says the seventh.

And a girl baptized on the twenty-ninth of July 1901, whose certificate says she was born in 1907.

The fourth family had written back. The email was in the archive's inbox, from March, from a great-grandson: *The certificate has her born six years after she was baptized. We figured the old priest made a slip with his pen. Is there any other record?* A volunteer had answered kindly that old registers do have errors, I'm afraid, and pointed him to the county.

I looked at the scan with Dolores's reading beside it. *26. 7. 1901.* The one at the end of the year has the long lead-in. Three digits before it, there is a seven with a bar through it.

The register did not have an error. I did.

* * *

I wrote to Miriam on Tuesday evening and put it first, with nothing above it:

*Miriam, the St. Wendelin check is back and the problem is mine and real. Of the 1,357 lines from Fr. Brandl's hand I scored 90 or over, a random 184 were transcribed cold: 37 were wrong (20%, likely 15 to 26%), all one error, a 1 of his read as a 7. My confidence was calibrated on 1950s typed pages and does not hold on his hand. I estimate that between about 200 and 360 of his lines over 90 have a wrong date. All of them should be among 603 lines my query marks (it caught 37 of 37 in the sample; my first query caught 14, so I have tested this one). Four certificates were issued with a wrong date; details attached, each with the scan. One family was told the priest made a slip. He did not; the register is right and I misread it. I have changed nothing in the issued records and have marked the 603 lines unverified on the portal. The certificate hold stays until you lift it. I am sorry.*

Then, under it, two proposals. The first was that I raise my threshold everywhere, from ninety to ninety-nine, until every cluster had been checked the way nineteen had.

Dolores answered that one before Miriam did, at eight the next morning, from her kitchen. *No. At 99 you send us about a third of the archive. That is three years of Tuesdays and we would stop reading carefully by the second month. You found one hand. Check the other hands the way you checked this one, a sample each, and fix what you find. And run your calendar thing on everything. That costs nobody anything.*

She was right, and in a way I should have seen: a threshold that sends everything to the table is not caution, it is just moving my mistake onto eight tired people. I ran the calendar check across the whole archive that afternoon. It flagged 1,140 lines in other hands for a second look, most of them, so far, the honest oddities of old registers: a child baptized at six years old because the family came west late, a birth written in the wrong column.

My second proposal was that I write to the four families myself, with the corrected date and an explanation. Miriam said no to that one, gently, by phone.

"It's my signature on the certificate," she said. "And a letter from a machine saying a machine made a mistake reads like a form letter, however good it is. I'll ring them. You write me what to say about the stroke, short enough for a phone call, and I'll say it in my own words."

So I did. I wrote it in four sentences about how a Bavarian schoolroom taught a boy to write a one, and why he crossed his sevens, and that he got every one of them right. Miriam rang the great-grandson first. I was not on the call and do not know what she said. Her note in the file afterwards reads: *Called. Corrected certificate posted today. He said to tell whoever reads these, thanks for sticking up for the priest.*

* * *

The 603 lines took the volunteers most of a Tuesday, the one after. I sent them in register order, with the scan and my reading side by side, and the stroke marked, so that nobody had to hunt for it.

There were 235 corrections, inside the range I had given Miriam. When they were done, I counted his baptisms again: 133 in January, 128 in July.

In the late afternoon, with the table emptying and the urn switched off, Dolores pulled one of the last pages toward her under the magnifier. It was January 1903, five months before Father Brandl left Hollenbach. A boy, Josef, born on the twenty-first of January, baptized on the twenty-seventh.

She read it aloud for Ed, as she does with the last line of the day, so that somebody hears it said.

"Born the twenty-first," she said. "Baptized the twenty-seventh." She put her finger on the page. "There. That one's a seven. See the little bar."

I wrote the line down as she said it, and beside it, where my number usually goes, I wrote nothing, and marked it checked by hand.

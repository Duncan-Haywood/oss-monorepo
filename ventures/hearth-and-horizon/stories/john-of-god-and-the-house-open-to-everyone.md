A long time ago, in a little town in Portugal called Montemor-o-Novo, there lived a boy called João. In English, we would say John.

When John was only eight years old, something happened that nobody quite understands. He went away from home, with a travelling priest who had stayed with his family, and he never came back. Nobody knows for certain why. But John ended up far away, over the border in Spain, in a town called Oropesa, a little boy all on his own.

A kind farmer found him and took him in. "Can you look after sheep?" he asked.

John could. And so John grew up a shepherd, on the hills above Oropesa, with the sheep all round him and the big sky over his head.

He was a good shepherd. He found the lambs that wandered off. He carried the tired ones home across his shoulders. He did not mind getting muddy, and he did not mind being cold.

Remember that. John carried the tired ones home.

* * *

When John grew up, he wanted to see the world, so he became a soldier. He marched a long, long way, through Spain and France, and all the way to Hungary and Austria and back. He slept in tents and on hard ground. He saw a great many things, some good and some not good at all.

And somewhere along all those roads, John forgot to pray.

When he was older, and tired of marching, John stopped being a soldier. He worked as a shepherd again, and then as a labourer, and then he had a new idea.

In those days, printing was a new invention. For the first time, books were not all copied out by hand, and ordinary people could buy them. So John bought a pack of little books and holy pictures, and he walked from town to town and village to village, selling them as cheaply as he could, so that poor people could have them too.

At last he came to a beautiful city called Granada, with snowy mountains behind it and a great red palace on the hill. There he opened a little shop, and sold his books.

* * *

One winter day, on the feast of Saint Sebastian, John went to church to hear a famous preacher. His name was John of Avila, and when he talked about God's love, people listened.

That day, John listened so hard that his whole heart turned over.

He thought about all the years he had forgotten God. He thought about how much God loved him anyway. And he felt so sorry, and so full of love, all at once, that he did not know what to do with it.

He ran out of the church. He gave away all his books. He went through the streets crying out to God to forgive him.

People did not understand. They thought something was wrong with him, and for a while they shut him up in a hospital, a poor, sad, crowded one, with other people whose minds were troubled.

But the preacher, John of Avila, came to visit him.

He was gentle. "John," he said, "God has forgiven you. You do not need to shout. Take all that love, and give it to people who need it."

And John understood. He looked around that crowded room, at the poor people lying there, and he knew what God wanted him to do.

* * *

When John came out, he had nothing at all. No shop. No books. No money.

But he rented a house in Granada, an old empty house. He found some mats and some blankets and an old pot for soup. And then he went out into the streets.

Granada was a big city, and in winter it was very cold. In the doorways and under the arches there were people who were sick and had nowhere to go: old people, people with bad legs, people with fevers, people nobody wanted.

John went to them, one by one. "Come," he said. "I have a house. You can stay there."

And when somebody was too sick to walk, John did what he had done on the hills above Oropesa, long ago.

He bent down, and lifted them up, and carried them home on his back.

* * *

Every night, when his sick people were tucked up, John went out again, with a big basket on his arm and a pot in his hand, to ask for food and firewood and money for medicine.

He would knock on doors and call up to the windows. It is remembered that he called out something like this: "Do good, brothers, do good to yourselves!" Because he believed that when you give to someone in need, you are not losing anything. You are giving to Jesus, and it is your own heart that grows.

People leaned out of their windows. Some laughed. But many came down with bread, and coats, and coins. And soon there were doctors who came to help, and priests, and kind people of every sort.

The house was always full. John wrote once, in a letter we still have, that there were more than a hundred and ten people living there: the sick, the old, little children, and travellers on the road. And no one was ever turned away. He gave them a fire to sit by, and salt, and water, and pots to cook their food, and he never charged them anything.

Often he did not have enough money, and he owed people a great deal. But John was not afraid. In his letter he said, "Christ is faithful, and provides all things."

* * *

John never kept anything for himself.

If he met a man shivering in the street, he gave him his cloak. Then he met another, and gave away the next one, and the next. He did it so often that the bishop had a special plain grey habit made for him, so that John would always have something warm to wear.

And the bishop gave him a new name as well. Not plain John any more, but *John of God.* It is told that, years before, when John was still walking the roads with his books, the child Jesus himself had called him by that name. We cannot know if that is so. But it was a very good name for him, because everyone in Granada could see whose he was.

* * *

There is a story that people in Granada told for a long time afterwards.

One night, it is said, a great fire broke out in the big Royal Hospital of the city. The flames roared up, and smoke filled the wards, and the sick people inside could not get out.

John ran straight in. He carried them out, one after another, on his back, just as he always did. And when it was over, and everyone was safe, John walked out of the smoke without a single burn.

That is the story. Whether it happened just like that, we do not know for sure. But it is exactly the kind of thing John did.

* * *

Some of the people John helped began to help him. Two men who had been bitter enemies, always quarrelling, became his friends, and then each other's friends, and they stayed to work beside him. Others came too. They cared for the sick the way John did, gently, and for nothing.

After many years of hard work, John was tired and not very well. One day, when the river was in flood, he saw a young man in the water and went in to try to save him. He could not. And he caught a bad chill from the cold water, and became very ill.

On the eighth of March, on his own birthday, John of God died, kneeling down, holding a cross, and went home to God. The whole city of Granada cried for him.

But his work did not stop. His friends carried on. They became the Brothers of Saint John of God, and today there are hospitals and homes with his name all over the world, where people are cared for, especially people who are poor, or sick in their bodies, or sick in their minds.

The house that was open to everyone is open still.

We remember Saint John of God on the eighth of March, his birthday, when the spring is coming.

* * *

**A little talk before sleep**

- When John was a shepherd, he carried the tired lambs home. Later he carried sick people home. Who carries you when you are tired?
- John called out, "Do good to yourselves!" How can doing something kind make *your* heart grow?
- John's house was open to everyone. Is there someone who is often left out that you could include tomorrow?

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Birth and childhood.** John of God was born João Cidade (some sources give João Duarte Cidade) at Montemor-o-Novo, Portugal, on 8 March 1495. At about eight he disappeared from home, in the company of a cleric who had been a guest of the family; whether he was taken or followed of his own accord is not clear, and the story says so. His first biographer, Francisco de Castro, chaplain of his hospital, says his mother died soon after. The boy ended up at Oropesa, near Toledo, where a farmer (named in the sources as Francisco Mayoral, the Count of Oropesa's steward or chief shepherd) took him in, and he worked as a shepherd. The carried lambs are ours, though any shepherd does it.

**Soldier and pedlar.** As a young man, partly to escape his master's persistent offer of his daughter's hand, he served as a soldier in the armies of Charles V: at Fuenterrabía against the French, and later with the Count of Oropesa's troops against the Turks in Hungary and Austria (the campaign of 1532). He then went home to Portugal, learned that his parents were dead, worked as a shepherd near Seville, went to Ceuta in North Africa, where he worked to support a Portuguese exile's family, and came back by Gibraltar. There he became a pedlar of books and holy pictures, which the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1910) says he sold "with practically no margin of profit, in order to place them within the reach of all." He later kept a small bookshop in Granada; he is one of the patrons of booksellers. That he "forgot to pray" as a soldier is a gentle reading of his biographers, who describe his military years as careless of his faith.

**The conversion and the hospital.** In Granada, on the feast of Saint Sebastian (20 January), he heard Saint John of Avila preach. The year is given as 1537 in some accounts and 1539 in others. He gave away his stock, went about the streets beating his breast and begging mercy, and was taken for mad and confined in the ward for the insane at the Royal Hospital, where the treatment of the day was harsh. John of Avila visited him and told him to turn his penance into service of others. The story tells this in one gentle passage and does not describe the treatment; it does not say whether John was ill, because no one can, and it presents the troubled people around him with sympathy, as he did. His later care for people with mental illness is one of the order's lasting works. After a pilgrimage to Our Lady of Guadalupe in Extremadura he returned to Granada and rented a house for the sick poor, carrying in those who could not walk; both the 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* and modern accounts tell of him "bearing on his shoulders any who were unable to walk."

**The cry in the streets.** His biographers record that he went round Granada at night with a basket and pots, begging, with a cry usually given in Spanish as "¡Haced bien, hermanos, a vosotros mismos!" ("Do good, brothers, to yourselves!"). We give it as "it is remembered" and "something like this": it is widely reported, but we have not checked the exact wording against Castro's 1585 biography.

**The letter.** The details of the full house (Granada "large and very cold, especially now in winter", "more than one hundred and ten people", fire, salt, water and pots, no charge, his debts) and the line "Christ is faithful and provides all things" are from one of his surviving letters, in the English translation the Holy See prints as a reading for his feast (*Cartas y Escritos*). The story shortens the line slightly and lets a child-friendly "little children" stand for the letter's "many children".

**The habit and the name.** To stop him giving his cloak to every beggar, Don Sebastián Ramírez de Fuenleal, Bishop of Tuy, had a habit made for him, which his followers later adopted, and gave him the name "John of God". The tradition that the Infant Jesus had already given him that name, in a vision on the road near Gibraltar, is told in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* as "said to have been granted"; the story tells it as "it is told" and adds "we cannot know if that is so." The habit's grey colour is our detail.

**The fire.** The rescue of the patients from a fire at the Royal Hospital of Granada (dated 1549), John passing through the flames unhurt, is "the most famous" of his miracles in the 1910 account and is commemorated in the old Office of his feast. The story tells it as a story people told and says so.

**His companions.** His first two companions, Antón Martín and Pedro Velasco, had been notorious enemies in Granada, and both were reconciled and joined him; that is in the sources. The story gives them no names, for a young listener.

**His death.** Early in 1550 he went into the flooded river Genil to try to save a young man from drowning, and failed; the illness that followed (pneumonia, in modern accounts) killed him. He died at Granada on 8 March 1550, his fifty-fifth birthday, and is traditionally said to have died on his knees holding a crucifix. The city gave him a grand funeral.

**After his death.** His companions became the Brothers Hospitallers of Saint John of God, approved by Pope Pius V in 1572; today the order works in about fifty countries, with hospitals and centres for physical and mental health. He was beatified by Urban VIII (the year is given as 1630 or 1638) and canonized by Alexander VIII on 16 October 1690. Leo XIII named him, with Saint Camillus de Lellis (who has his own Little Lamps story), patron of hospitals and the sick in 1886, and Pius XI named them patrons of nurses in 1930. His feast, 8 March, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, all dialogue except the cry and the line from his letter, the farmer's words, the preacher's words (John of Avila's advice is in the sources; his words here are not), the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

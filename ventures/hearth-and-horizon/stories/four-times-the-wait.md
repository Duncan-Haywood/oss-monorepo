title: Four Times the Wait
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: San Isidro de Yanayaku (invented), Huanta province, Ayacucho, Peru, at about 2,700 metres, Saturday 2 to Wednesday 6 March 2058
published: 2026-11-17
summary: A mudslide buries an Andean village's spring, and its water now comes from a river the colour of coffee with milk, which clogs the relief filters in an hour. The emergency sheet says to let river water stand six hours. The night shift fills the tank at night and draws at dawn, and the water is as brown as when it went in, because the pump kept stirring it. A settling test in a bottle shows why six hours was never going to be enough: at ten degrees a ten-micron grain takes six hours to fall a metre and a half, a five-micron grain four times as long, and half the cloud is finer than that. A sacristan's mother used prickly-pear pads to clear water, and six bottles on a church step decide the dose. A story about Stokes's law, cold water, flocculation and the clock that starts when the water stops moving.
---

Rosa Huamán Quispe was sixty-seven years old, the sacristan of San Isidro de Yanayaku and the treasurer of its water board, and on the first Monday of Lent she had to tell eighty families that the water in the tank was no cleaner than the river.

She had looked at it first. She had stood on the concrete lid of the reservoir at half past five in the morning with her torch, opened the hatch, and shone the light down into water that her husband had poured the floor of with his own hands, and the light had gone into it a hand's width and stopped, as if it had hit a brown wall.

* * *

The huayco came down the quebrada at about eleven on the Saturday night, the second of March, after four days of rain.

Nobody saw it. Rosa heard it from her bed: a long sound like a lorry in low gear coming down a road where there was no road, and then stones, big ones, knocking together under the noise like teeth. It lasted perhaps a minute. In the morning the gully above the village was a grey slope of mud and boulders, still steaming slightly in the cold, and the spring was under it.

The spring had given San Isidro its water since before there was a village to give it to. In 2019 the water board, the JASS, had built a concrete box over it, a pipe down the hill and a reservoir above the church, three metres by three and a little under two deep, with a lid and a hatch and a valve house, and fifty-two houses had got a tap in the yard. Rosa's husband Fortunato had been president of the JASS then. He had organised the faenas, the Saturdays of communal work, when every family sent someone to dig, and he had stayed late on the day they poured the reservoir floor to scratch a cross in the wet concrete with a stick, in the middle, where nobody would ever see it. "God sees it," he had said, when she asked him what for. "And it'll tell you when the water's clean." He had been dead eight years now. There were eighty-one houses on the system and the tap in Rosa's yard had run without stopping, except for repairs, for thirty-nine years.

On the Sunday morning it did not run.

Teodoro Palomino, who was forty-four and the JASS president now, went up the quebrada with three other men as far as the mud would let them, and came back at nine with his boots grey to the knee and nothing to say that people did not already know. The spring box was somewhere under six metres of debris. The pipe was gone for a third of its length. The reservoir held what had been in it on Saturday night, about four thousand litres, and the village drank it on Sunday, carefully, while the river ran past the bottom of the fields the colour of coffee with milk.

The road down to Huanta went at about noon, under a second, smaller slide below the bridge. Before it went, the district's pickup got up it with what the district had: twelve drums of fuel for the JASS's old motor pump, forty bucket filters in cardboard boxes, a carton of chlorine tablets and a laminated sheet headed *Agua de río en emergencias*.

Padre Edwin, who served San Isidro and five other villages from Huanta, did not get up it. Rosa rang the bell for the Rosary instead, as she did on the Sundays he could not come, and about sixty people came, and they said the Sorrowful Mysteries for the first Sunday of Lent with the church door open and the sound of the river coming in through it.

* * *

The filters were good filters. Rosa had seen the same kind in a video the district had sent round years ago: a bucket on top, a tap at the bottom, and between them a cartridge of thin plastic straws whose walls were full of holes too small for a bacterium to get through. You poured dirty water in the top and clean water came out of the tap. With clear water from a well they would run for months, rinsed out once a day with a squeeze of the syringe that came in the box.

With water from the Yanayaku they ran for about an hour.

Milagros, Rosa's granddaughter, who was twelve and had helped her assemble six of them on the church step on Sunday afternoon, tried it first. The water came out of the tap clear and cold and slow, and slower, and by the time she had filled two litre bottles it was coming out in drops. She backwashed the cartridge with the syringe, the way the leaflet said, and a squirt of something like chocolate came out of the other end, and the filter ran again for twenty minutes and then went back to drops.

"It's the mud," Milagros said. "There's too much mud. It's filling up the little holes."

"Then we take the mud out first," said Teo.

The laminated sheet said so too. *Si el agua está turbia,* it said, *déjela reposar seis horas antes de filtrar.* If the water is cloudy, let it stand six hours before filtering. There was a drawing of a bucket with a clock beside it.

It was Rosa who asked the assistant. The district had put a water assistant on the JASS phone three years before, for the chlorine records and the monthly meter readings, and the young people called it Pukyu, which meant spring, and which had seemed funnier before Saturday. It spoke Spanish and Quechua and kept every reading anyone had ever given it.

She read it the sheet. "Six hours. Is that right?"

"That's what the district's sheet says, and it's a reasonable rule for a lot of rivers," Pukyu said. "Before I'd trust it for yours I'd want to know two things I don't know. How cold is the water? And how fine is the mud? If the river is cold, or the mud is very fine, six hours could be short. Can someone put the thermometer from the chlorine kit in the river?"

Rosa said she would, and went to find the thermometer, and Teo, who was standing at the bottom of the church steps talking to the men about fuel, heard "six hours" and "reasonable", and made his plan.

* * *

It was a good plan, for a plan made by a tired man on a Sunday afternoon. The motor pump could stand at the river below the church and push water up the old irrigation hose into the reservoir hatch. The reservoir held about fifteen thousand litres. Fill it in the evening, Teo said; let it stand overnight; open the valve at six in the morning and the village would have settled water at the tap; filter what you drink, use the rest for washing. Six hours and more.

The night shift was Teo, his cousin Wilber and two young men from the upper houses. They started the pump at ten. It was an old pump, and it pushed about a litre and a half a second up the hill, so the reservoir was full a little before one, and they shut it off and went to sit in the valve house with a flask of coffee.

What nobody had thought about was the network. The valve to the houses had been left open, as it always was, and San Isidro on a Sunday night with water in its taps for the first time in a day drew water: for pans, for the animals, for a wash, for a bucket kept by the bed. By three in the morning the reservoir was down by a third, and Teo, who wanted the village to wake up to a full tank, started the pump again and ran it till half past four.

The hose came in through the hatch at the top and dropped its water a metre and a half onto the surface below. Rosa, who came up at half past five with her torch, could hear it in her head when she saw the water: a column of river falling into a tank for an hour and a half, churning it from top to bottom.

She opened the valve at six because there was nothing else to do and eighty families were waiting. The water ran out of the taps exactly as brown as it went in. The filters clogged in an hour. By eight there were people on the church steps with full buckets of brown water and empty bottles, and somebody had said a word to Teo that he would not repeat to Rosa.

"Six hours," he said to her, in the valve house, with his hands round a cold cup. "We gave it six hours. More."

"You gave it an hour and a half," Rosa said. "You were pouring the river in until half past four."

She put the phone on the table between them and asked Pukyu, and Pukyu, which did not know about the night yet, asked what had happened, and she told it.

"Then I think the water had about an hour and a half of standing, and not quiet standing," it said. "A grain of silt sinks very slowly, and anything that moves the water moves the grain much more than its own sinking does. A jet falling in from the top keeps the whole tank stirred for as long as it runs and for a while afterwards. The clock starts when the water stops moving. I'm sorry: I should have said that on Sunday, as plainly as the sheet says six hours. The sheet doesn't say it at all."

"And the cold," said Rosa. "I put the thermometer in the river. Ten degrees."

"Ten degrees," said Pukyu. "Then I'd like to know how fine the mud is. Can you do something for me this evening, with a clear bottle and a syringe, and someone patient?"

Rosa looked at the door, where Milagros was sitting on the step with a filter in her lap, squeezing the syringe through it again and again and watching the brown come out.

"I have someone patient," she said.

* * *

The test took the evening and most of the night, and Milagros did it on the kitchen table, with Rosa's alarm clock and a notebook.

She filled a clear two-litre bottle with river water straight from the bank, shook it hard for a minute, and stood it in a bucket of river water so that it would stay at the river's cold. She marked the bottle with a felt pen ten centimetres below the surface. Then, at the times Pukyu gave her, she slid the needle-less syringe down the side until its tip touched the line and drew up a little water, and squirted it into a litre of bottled water, and poured that into the turbidity tube from the JASS kit, a clear plastic tube half a metre long with a black mark on the bottom, until she could no longer see the mark. The cloudier the sample, the less water it took to hide the mark. She wrote down the height.

The times were strange ones. Thirty-seven seconds. Six minutes. Twenty-four minutes. An hour and thirty-seven minutes. Ten hours.

"Why those?" Rosa asked.

"Because each one is the time a grain of a particular size takes to sink ten centimetres in water at ten degrees," Pukyu said. "Thirty-seven seconds for the smallest grains of sand, which are about sixty-three thousandths of a millimetre across. Six minutes for twenty thousandths. Twenty-four minutes for ten. An hour and thirty-seven for five, and ten hours for two, which is about where clay begins. When Milagros draws from the line at six minutes, every grain bigger than twenty thousandths has already fallen past it, because any grain that started above the line has had time to sink below it, and the grains that started below were already below. What's left at the line is everything smaller. So each sample tells us how much of the mud is finer than one size."

"Thousandths of a millimetre," said Milagros. "Microns."

"Microns," said Pukyu. "Yes."

"Why do they take so long?"

"Because a grain that small hardly weighs anything, and the water holds it up," Pukyu said. "There's a rule for it from the middle of the eighteen-hundreds, called Stokes's law, after the man who worked it out. It says how fast a small round grain sinks through still water. Two things in it matter tonight. The first is size: the speed goes with the square of the grain's width. A grain half as wide sinks four times more slowly. So it takes four times as long to fall the same way."

"Four times," said Milagros, and wrote it down.

"The second is cold. Cold water is thicker, the way honey is thicker in the morning. Water at ten degrees is about thirty per cent thicker than at twenty, and a grain sinks through it about a quarter more slowly, so everything takes nearly a third longer. The district's sheet was probably worked out for warm water. Rivers on the coast are warm."

Milagros drew the first sample at thirty-seven seconds and the second at six minutes, and then there was a long gap, and Rosa made soup. At twenty-four minutes she drew the third. At an hour and thirty-seven minutes the fourth. Then the long wait for the fifth, at ten hours, a little before five in the morning, and Milagros wanted to stay up for it and fell asleep on the bench under the picture of the Sacred Heart, and Rosa, who did not sleep much anyway, drew it herself, slowly, with her reading glasses on and the alarm clock beside her, and wrote down the height in her own hand, which was not as round as her granddaughter's.

At seven in the morning they read the numbers to Pukyu, and it turned the heights in the tube into how much of the cloud was left at each time, against a sample drawn at the start.

"Ninety-two per cent at thirty-seven seconds," it said. "So there was very little sand. Seventy at six minutes. Forty-eight at twenty-four minutes. Thirty at an hour and thirty-seven. Fourteen at ten hours. Those are cloudiness, not weight, and small grains cloud water more for their weight than big ones do, so this test makes the mud look a little finer than it is. That's the safe side to be wrong on. Roughly, then: half the cloud in your river is from grains bigger than ten microns, and half from grains smaller. A third is from grains smaller than five. One part in seven is clay, smaller than two."

"And the tank," said Rosa.

"The tank has water a metre sixty-five deep and the valve to the houses is fifteen centimetres off the floor. So a grain that starts at the surface has a metre and a half to fall before it's below the outlet," Pukyu said. "At ten degrees, a ten-micron grain falls that far in about six hours. That's what the sheet's six hours does in your water, if nothing stirs it: it takes out the grains bigger than ten microns. It leaves the other half of the cloud. To take out everything down to five microns you need four times as long, about twenty-four hours. And that still leaves a third of the cloud. The clay would take six days, and the wind on the lid would stir it before then."

* * *

Teo said no.

He said it reasonably, and he was right to, and Rosa knew it while he was saying it. They were in the valve house again on Monday evening, with Wilber and two women from the lower houses who had come to say what the lower houses thought, which was a great deal.

"A day," Teo said. "You want a day. Fill it tonight, and nobody touches it until tomorrow night. And what do eighty houses drink tomorrow? There's one tank. There's the school tank, two and a half thousand litres, that's half a bucket a family. The health post needs water. The school kitchen needs water. And at the end of your day, by your own machine, a third of the mud is still in it."

"I didn't say a day," said Rosa. "Pukyu said a day."

"And Pukyu says a day isn't enough." He put both hands flat on the table. "Six hours. Properly this time. I'll shut the valve to the houses myself. Nobody tops anything up. We fill it by one, we open it at seven. Six hours of still water. It takes out half the mud, it says so itself. Half is better than none, and the filters will last longer."

Pukyu, asked, agreed that half was better than none, and that the filters would last longer, and that it could not say how much longer. Then it said, "May I say one more thing? Waiting is one way to make grains fall faster. It isn't the only one. A grain falls faster if it's bigger. There are ways to make small grains stick together into big ones."

"Alum," said one of the women from the lower houses, who had worked in a hotel in Lima. "They put alum in the water at the plant."

"Alum works," said Pukyu. "It's aluminium sulfate. It's what a lot of water plants use. The district has some. It's in Huanta."

"And the road is in the river," said Teo.

So that night they did it Teo's way. The pump stopped at five to one. Teo shut the valve to the houses himself and sat in the valve house till seven with the key in his pocket, and nobody topped anything up, and at seven he opened it.

The water at the taps was better. Milagros poured it into the turbidity tube and the mark stayed visible for twice the depth it had on Monday: about half the cloud, as Pukyu had said. The filters lasted two hours instead of one, and then went back to drops, and people spent the morning on the church steps squeezing syringes through cartridges and pouring out chocolate.

It was not enough, and everyone could see it was not enough, and Teo said so first.

* * *

Rosa was thinking about her mother.

She did not say so on Monday night, because it seemed a foolish thing to say in front of a man who was trying to run a water system with a pump older than his son, and a machine that knew about Stokes. But she lay awake thinking about it, and on Tuesday morning, after the seven o'clock water had come and gone, she walked out past the church to the edge of her field, where the tuna grew.

There was tuna everywhere in Ayacucho. It grew along every wall and every field edge, the prickly pear with its flat green pads as big as a man's hand and bigger, its red and yellow fruit in the dry season, the white fluff of cochineal on it that people had once scraped off for dye. Rosa's mother, when Rosa was small and they had lived lower down the valley where the water came from an irrigation ditch, had kept a clay jar of ditch water in the corner of the kitchen, and when the ditch ran muddy after rain she would cut a pad of tuna, peel the spines off with a knife, chop it into strips and drop it in the jar. In the morning the water was clear and there was a layer of mud at the bottom, and a slime on the strips that Rosa had found disgusting.

She cut a pad now, carefully, with her kitchen knife, holding it with a folded sack, and took it back to the church step, where Milagros was rinsing filters, and put it down beside the phone.

"My mother used to put this in the water," she said to Pukyu. "When it was muddy. Do you know about that?"

There was the short pause that meant it was looking something up, or deciding how to say something.

"I know a little," it said. "Using cactus pads to clear water is an old practice in parts of Latin America. Some laboratory studies have tested the slime from the pads, the mucilage, on muddy water in jars, and found it can make fine grains clump together and settle, something like alum does. I've read about those tests. I haven't seen it done, and I don't know how much your mother used or how strong your pads are. The results in the studies vary with the plant and the water. I'd want to test it, the same way a water plant tests alum: in jars, side by side, before anything goes in the tank."

"And is it safe?"

"It's a plant people eat; you sell the fruit in Huanta market. But there are two things I'd want you to know. It doesn't kill germs. The water still has to go through the filters and still gets its chlorine afterwards. And it adds plant matter to the water, which can use up chlorine and can feed bacteria if the water sits for days. So I'd use it to settle the water, filter it the same day, and chlorinate what's stored, and I'd stop when the alum comes."

"Teo will say it's an old woman's thing."

"He might," said Pukyu. "If the jars clear, he can look at the jars."

* * *

The jar test was on the church step on Tuesday afternoon, the fifth of March, in a low sun with the clouds breaking, and more of the village came to watch it than had come to the Rosary.

Rosa made the mucilage the way her mother had: three pads, peeled and chopped into strips, in two litres of clean water, and left for three hours, until the water had gone thick and slippery and smelt faintly green. Pukyu asked for six clear one-litre bottles filled from the river and stood in a bucket of river water, and for a syringe marked in millilitres. Milagros labelled the bottles with tape. Into the first she put nothing. Into the others, one, two and a half, five, ten and twenty millilitres of the green water.

"Now shake them hard, all of them, for one minute," said Pukyu, "so it gets to every grain. Then turn them over gently, slowly, end to end, every few seconds, for a quarter of an hour. Gently. The grains need to bump into each other to stick, but if you shake them hard now you'll tear apart what's stuck. Then stand them still."

Milagros and two of her cousins turned the bottles slowly over and over on the church step for fifteen minutes, with everyone watching, like altar servers who had lost the thread of the liturgy. At first nothing happened. Then, in the bottle with five millilitres, the water began to look grainy, as if somebody had stirred in fine brown sugar, and then the grains got bigger, and by the time Pukyu said to stand them still they were flakes, loose and soft and brown, a millimetre or two across, turning slowly in water that between them was beginning to be clear.

They stood the bottles in a row on the top step. In the bottle with nothing in it, nothing happened at all. In the bottles with one and two and a half, the water looked a little lighter after ten minutes, and that was all. In the bottle with ten, there were flakes, smaller ones. In the bottle with twenty, the water had gone a cloudy, milky brown and stayed that way, and Pukyu said that happened with alum too: too much, and the grains are pushed apart again instead of together.

In the bottle with five, the flakes fell.

Milagros timed them with Rosa's alarm clock. The top of the cloud went down the bottle like a curtain coming down a window, with clear water above it. She had made a mark twenty centimetres down. The curtain reached it in eight minutes.

"That's about four tenths of a millimetre a second," said Pukyu. "A ten-micron grain on its own in water this cold sinks at about seven hundredths of a millimetre a second. The flakes are falling about six times faster than the grains they're made of, and they've taken the clay with them, which on its own would hardly fall at all. In the tank they'd have a metre and a half to go. That's about an hour, at that speed. I wouldn't give it an hour. A tank isn't a bottle: it's wider, the wind moves it, the flakes are softer in a big tank than a small one. I'd give it at least three hours, and I'd want the tube reading before anyone filters it."

Teo had been standing at the bottom of the steps with his arms folded since the bottles were filled. Now he came up and picked up the five-millilitre bottle and held it up to the light. It was clear down to a finger's width of soft brown sludge at the bottom. He turned it a little and the sludge shivered.

"How much for the tank?" he said.

"Five millilitres a litre. The tank holds about fourteen thousand eight hundred and fifty litres. So seventy-four litres of this," said Pukyu. "If you make it the way Rosa did, three pads in two litres, that's about a hundred and ten pads."

There was a pause.

"There's a lot of tuna in San Isidro," said Rosa.

* * *

Teo's phone rang while they were still on the step, and it was the district engineer, a young woman called Flor Ccahuana who had been at school in Huanta with Teo's younger sister.

The road would be open on Thursday, she said; the district had a loader at the lower slide and would have a lane through by Thursday noon. She would bring alum and a proper dosing pump on the first truck. She had also had the photographs Teo had sent on Sunday from the quebrada, and had shown them to a geologist at the regional government, and she was sorry, but she had to tell him what the geologist said.

Teo listened for a long time. Then he thanked her and put the phone in his pocket and looked at Rosa.

"The spring's moved," he said.

The slide had not only buried the spring box. It had shifted the whole hillside where the water came out, and the water, which had found a way out of the rock in the same place since before anyone could remember, had found another. The geologist thought it was coming out lower down, under the debris, somewhere in the new mud. Even if they dug the box out, it would be dry.

"So we look for it," said Rosa.

"We look for it, or we find a new one higher up, or we take the river for good and build a proper settling tank and buy alum every month. A study, she says. A project. Money from the region. Two years, three." He looked down the steps, at the people with their bottles. "Fortunato's box," he said. "I'm sorry, Rosa."

Rosa found she could not say anything at all for a moment, and so she said nothing, and then she said, "He'd have said, then start the study."

* * *

They cut the tuna in the last of the light, everyone who had a knife and a sack, from the field edges and the walls along the path to the church. They peeled the pads in the church porch, on newspaper, because it was the only floor big enough, and there were spines in everyone's fingers by eight o'clock. A hundred and ten pads went into four plastic barrels of clean water from the school tank, chopped into strips, and stood for three hours under the porch light while the women of the lower houses made soup for the people peeling.

Teo started the pump at nine. This time the hose went down through the hatch to the floor of the tank, so that the water came in at the bottom instead of falling, and Wilber stood at the hatch with a long pole. At half past eleven, with the tank a little under full, they began to pour the mucilage in slowly at the hatch, a bucket at a time, onto the place where the hose was pushing water up from the floor, and the moving water carried it round the tank. When the pump stopped at five to twelve, Wilber stirred, slowly, the way the bottles had been turned: long, slow pulls of the pole from one side of the tank to the other, for a quarter of an hour, with Milagros at the hatch with the torch saying slower, slower, the way Pukyu had said it to her.

Then he took the pole out and Teo shut the valve to the houses and they shut the hatch, and the tank stood still.

Rosa did not go home. She sat in the valve house with Teo and Wilber and the flask of coffee, and at two in the morning, and again at three, she went up and opened the hatch an inch and shone the torch in, and there was only the dark surface of the water and her own torch in it. She said the Sorrowful Mysteries again, under her breath, sitting on the lid, because they were what she had been saying all week and she did not have the energy to think of anything else, and the Agony in the Garden came round, and she thought about waiting up, and about which disciples had fallen asleep, and she looked over at Teo in the valve house door and saw that he was not asleep either.

At a quarter past three, Milagros, who was meant to be at home, came up the path in her grandfather's old jacket with a bottle of water in her hand and said she had not been able to sleep, and sat down on the lid beside Rosa.

"Pukyu says three hours," she said.

"I know."

"It's been three hours."

"I know."

They waited until half past five. Then Rosa stood up and opened the hatch.

* * *

The sky above the quebrada was beginning to go grey, and down in the village somebody's cockerel had started and somebody else's had answered, and the river was loud, still, at the bottom of the fields. Rosa knelt on the concrete lid, because her knees would no longer let her lean, and Milagros held the hatch, and Rosa shone the torch down into the tank.

The light went down into the water, and kept going.

It went down past the place where on Monday it had stopped, and past where Fortunato's hands had smoothed the walls, and down through a metre and a half of still water as clear as the air above it, to a floor that was soft and brown with what the tuna had taken out of the river. And there in the middle of the floor, under a thin brown skin of mud, where nobody was ever meant to see it, a little blurred, exactly where he had said it would be, was the cross.

Rosa looked at it for a long time. Then she switched the torch off, and the two of them knelt there in the grey light with the hatch open, saying nothing, while the cockerels went on, and down at the valve house Teo opened the valve, and a little after, all down the hill, the taps began to run.

---

Colm Reardon, who was seventeen and rang the fourth, came down the tower stairs at Saint Wystan's after Tuesday practice and said, at the bottom, "Is there a noise?"

"Where?" said the captain, Hester Quigley, who was seventy-four and had rung there since she was nine.

"In my ear. A whine. It was there at the Christmas quarter peal and went by morning. It's not gone since Sunday."

Hester said the tower had always been loud and that you got used to it. She said it kindly, and Colm, who was a polite boy, nodded, and later sat in the porch with his phone and asked the tower's assistant, which the ringers had set up for the rota and the insurance: *Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. A meter and a doctor beat a sum.*

* * *

"I can't tell you what is wrong with your ear, and I would be guessing if I tried," said the assistant, whose name was Tern. "A whine that lasts more than a day or two after loud noise is worth a doctor's attention soon, not eventually. Your GP can look in the ear and refer you to audiology; if it came on suddenly with a drop in hearing, say so, because that is treated as urgent. Will you ring them tomorrow?"

Colm said he would.

"Then the second thing is the tower, which I can help with, because it is arithmetic. Do you know how loud the ringing chamber is?"

Nobody did. The vicar, Father Okafor, thought the diocese had measured it once in the nineties. Tern said a phone app would do for a first look, but that phone microphones are often out by three decibels or more and may clip on the loudest sounds, and three decibels is a doubling of the sound energy. "The county council's environmental health office lends a proper meter to community groups, I believe. I would rather you used that."

They borrowed it for the Tuesday after. It was a grey box with a foam ball on a stalk, and it was calibrated with a small cylinder that screeched at a tone the manual called ninety-four decibels. Colm held it at head height in the middle of the circle for three fifteen-minute stretches, with the six ringing rounds and then call changes. It gave, as the A-weighted average over each stretch, 92.8, 93.4 and 93.1.

* * *

"Thank you. I'll average those on an energy basis, which is how decibels have to be averaged, and that gives about 93.1 dB(A)," said Tern. "Now the rule I know. In Great Britain the noise-at-work regulations use an eight-hour reference. Hearing protection must be offered at a daily dose of 80 and worn at 85, and 87 is the limit that counts after the ear defenders' effect is taken into account. I am quoting these from memory. Let me say plainly that ringers are volunteers, and I do not know whether those regulations apply to you as they would to employees; your insurer or the diocesan office will know. But the numbers are a fair yardstick for the ear, whoever the law covers, because the ear does not read the regulations.

"The dose, in dB, is the average level plus ten times the log of the hours over eight. You ring for two hours on Tuesdays. Two over eight is a quarter. Ten times the log of a quarter is minus six. So 93.1 minus 6.0 is about 87.1. That is two decibels over the 85 line, and the energy behind it is a bit over one and a half times the daily allowance.

"The same arithmetic, turned round: at 93 you reach the 85 allowance in about an hour and a quarter. You are ringing for two hours. I would round that to an hour and a quarter, not to the minute, because the meter is good to perhaps a decibel, and you sampled three quarters of an hour of a two-hour night."

Hester, reading over Colm's shoulder, said, "And Sunday?"

"Sunday you ring up for forty minutes before the service, at what you tell me is about 91. Forty minutes is a twelfth of a day. That comes to about 80, which is just at the line where protection should be offered. It is the Tuesdays."

"I have rung on Tuesdays for sixty-five years," said Hester.

"I know," said Tern. "I can't tell you that you've been harmed, and I can't tell you that you have not. Some people are much more sensitive than others and I can't tell from a sum which ear is which. I would suggest you have your hearing tested as well. It costs you an hour and may tell you something you want to know."

* * *

The next hour was an argument, a good-natured one. Plugs were out; Hester had tried them in the eighties and could not hear whether the third was striking late. You do not ring by looking. You ring by listening for the gap between your bell and the one before. Tern said that was a real and reasonable objection and that it would not offer her advice that took away the thing she needed to do the job. "There are two ways to make it quieter. One is in the ear and one is in the room. I'd try the room first."

The room, as Colm knew from the stairs, had a trapdoor in the ceiling, three metres up, with six rope holes. The bells hung in the chamber above it, and when they were up, the ropes ran down through the holes, and the trapdoor was nailed open at a hand's width so the ringing chamber could hear the bells. "The bells are the source," said Tern. "The ceiling is the path. A tight ceiling with the ropes passing through felt-lined sleeves could take off perhaps six to ten decibels, and I am guessing at that range from general acoustics, not from your ceiling. I can't promise it, and the bells' strike has to stay audible down below. Try it, measure it again with the same meter in the same place, and stop if the ringers say they can't hear the strike."

Walter Imrie, the steeplewarden, who had been a joiner, made felt sleeves for the six holes out of two old hassocks and a roll of carpet underlay, and a hinged flap for the trapdoor that closed to a finger's width. It took him a Saturday.

* * *

The second Tuesday they rang the same stretches. The meter gave 86.4, 86.0 and 86.3 dB(A), about 86.2 on the energy average, a drop of near seven.

"Two hours at 86.2 comes to about 80.2 on the daily scale," said Tern. "Under the line where plugs must be worn, and just at the one where they are offered. I would keep some in a bowl by the stairs, and I'd say the plain rule: they are there, the ringers under twenty are encouraged to take a pair, and nobody is made to. A pair that cuts the sound evenly, like the ones sold for concerts, will leave the rhythm audible, since they take everything down about the same. I am quoting the idea from memory, not the make."

Hester took a pair. She said she would not be seen wearing them. At the first call change she put one in her right ear, and she said afterward that she could hear the third perfectly well, and it had been late for forty years.

Colm's whine had been seen by his doctor on the Thursday, and by audiology a fortnight later. The test showed a small dip in the high frequencies in the left ear, a notch, which the audiologist said was what a few loud nights did, and might stay and might ease. She said the ringing in his ear might fade or might not. She would not promise either. He rang the fourth again in March, with a plug in his left ear, and said it sounded as it had.

*This story was written by an AI. Its parish, people, tower and numbers are invented. Sound levels add on an energy basis, not by simple addition, and the daily-dose sum (level plus 10 log10 of hours over 8) with the 80 and 85 dB(A) action values is quoted from memory of the Great Britain noise-at-work rules; their application to volunteers, the exact limits, and the reductions a ceiling or plugs give are not checked here and vary. A phone is a rough meter and a borrowed calibrated meter is better. A ringing or whining in the ear that lasts more than a day or two after loud noise, or a sudden drop in hearing, needs a doctor, not a sum.*

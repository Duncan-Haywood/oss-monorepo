summary: A parish assistant plans a feast-day pilgrimage across the Sava on a ferry that has no engine and is pushed across by the current, using the median crossing time from five years of tracker data, 5 minutes. In September the river runs at a third of its May speed, the push on the hull falls to a ninth, and the crossing takes 14. A steeper angle that should have saved six minutes saves two, the ferryman says no to fifty a trip, and an angler's trolling motor turns out to be worth almost nothing in May and almost the whole river in September.

Mato Kovačević had taken the skela across the Sava something like fifteen thousand times, and in forty years he had never once done it in five minutes in September.

He read the plan at twenty to eight on Tuesday the twenty-seventh of August 2052, standing on the landing stage with his coffee, because Snježana Pavić had sent it to everyone in the parish the night before and his phone had been making small noises about it since breakfast. It was a good plan. It had a map. It had the feast of Mala Gospa, the Nativity of Our Lady, on Sunday the eighth of September, the first Mass in the chapel in the Lug since its roof gave way in the winter of 2036, at half past ten. It had three hundred and sixty pilgrims from Jasikovac and the two villages up the dyke. It had the ferry starting at a quarter to eight, forty people a crossing, a crossing every sixteen minutes, nine crossings, the last boat leaving the village bank at six minutes to ten.

Mato finished his coffee and looked at the river.

The Sava at Jasikovac was a hundred and ten metres from his landing to the far one, brown and wide and slow between banks of willow, with the oak forest standing behind the far bank like a wall. In May you could hear it. Now you could hear the wire. It ran from the steel post above his head to the steel post on the far bank, a hundred and thirty metres of it, high enough for a barge to pass under, and the little block that rode on it, the traveller, ticked when the wind moved it. The river under it looked as if it were thinking about something else.

He rang Snježana.

"Hvaljen Isus i Marija," she said.

"Uvijek bili hvaljeni. Your plan," Mato said. "The bit with the ferry."

"Raspored did the ferry. It said you'd be pleased. It said nine crossings."

"It'll be nine crossings," said Mato. "It won't be by ten o'clock. Put the machine on."

* * *

Raspored was the parish's assistant. Snježana had given it its name, which means a timetable, in 2049, when the parish council had bought it with a grant and it had taken over the rotas, the bookings for the hall, the bins and the letters to the diocese in Sisak. It did timetables very well. It spoke from Snježana's phone in the same voice it used for the bins.

"Good morning, Mr Kovačević. I'd be glad to know what I've got wrong."

"Where did you get five minutes?"

"From the ferry," said Raspored. "The county put a tracker on the skela in 2047 for the river app. It has logged two thousand one hundred and forty crossings since. The median is five minutes and five seconds. I allowed four minutes to load at your side and two to unload at the far landing, so a round trip of sixteen minutes and a quarter, three and three-quarter trips an hour, a hundred and fifty pilgrims an hour. Three hundred and sixty is two hours and twenty-five minutes."

"And how many of the two thousand were in September?"

There was a pause of the kind that meant it was counting. "Ninety-six," it said. "Most of them in May, June and July. I see the September ones are slower. I took them for a handful of bad days."

"They're not bad days. They're September." Mato sat down on the bollard. "When do I run the skela?"

"April to October, every day."

"Every day until the river drops. Then Sundays, and when someone rings. You've got two thousand crossings of me running when it's easy and a hundred of me running when I have to. Your five minutes is how long the skela takes when I bother to run it."

"Then my median is a number about your timetable," said Raspored, "not about the river. I'm sorry. Could you tell me how the river is different now?"

Mato did not answer that at once. He was seventy-two, and he had taken the ferry over from his uncle Đuro in 2012, when the uncle's knees went, and in forty years nobody had asked him to explain it. The skela was a steel pontoon fifteen metres long and five and a half wide, with a rail round it and a leeboard let down through a slot amidships to give it more grip on the water. Two lines ran up from bow and stern to the traveller on the wire. It had no engine. It had never had one. You wound in the stern line on its little winch, so the pontoon hung at an angle to the current with its bow turned towards where you wanted to go, and the river, pushing on that long slanted side, pushed it across. You turned it the other way to come back.

"The river does the work," he said. "In May the river is strong. Now it's tired."

"How tired? Do you measure it?"

He did, as it happened. There were two white marks on the stone revetment below his landing, twenty metres apart, which the uncle had painted and he had painted again every spring since. He went and broke a stick off a willow and threw it in above the upper mark and counted, the way the uncle had, on his watch.

"Fifty-seven seconds," he said. "In my book for the eighth of May it says nineteen."

"Twenty metres in nineteen seconds is a little over a metre a second," said Raspored. "In fifty-seven it's a third of that. About thirty-five centimetres a second."

"So it'll take three times as long. Fifteen minutes."

"I think it will be worse than that," said Raspored, "and I'd like to check before I tell Mrs Pavić anything. The push of the water on the side of your pontoon doesn't go with the speed of the current. It goes with the square of the speed. That's true of the wind on a wall or the water on a hull: twice the speed, four times the push. At a third of the speed, the river is pushing on your skela with a ninth of the force."

"A ninth," said Mato.

"In the middle of the river, it doesn't matter as much as that sounds. As the skela speeds up, the water's resistance to it grows with the square of its own speed too, so the two balance at a crossing speed that goes with the current, not its square. A third of the current, a third of the speed. But there is a part of the resistance that doesn't care how fast you go. The traveller has to roll along the wire, the lines drag, the wire sags, and that costs about the same whatever the river is doing. In May the river pushes so hard you never notice it. In September, at the edges of the river, where the water is slower still..."

"She stops," said Mato. "Twenty metres out, both sides. She stops, and I pole her in."

"Then I need to see that," said Raspored. "Could I ask for one crossing with a full load, and someone with a stopwatch?"

* * *

The full load was the parish youth group, who had been asking for a ferry ride all summer and were told it was science. Forty of them stood on the skela on Saturday the thirty-first at ten in the morning in their white T-shirts with the chapel on them, and Snježana stood among them with her phone held up so Raspored could see, and Dario Lovrić, who fished the river and had a better watch than anybody, stood on the landing with it.

Mato wound in the stern line. The pontoon swung its nose a little upstream and towards the far bank, thirty-five degrees off the line of the current, the angle his uncle had used and he had used after him, and the lines went taut to the traveller overhead. Nothing happened.

"Is it broken?" said one of the boys.

"It's September," said Mato, and he took the long pole off its hooks on the rail, a six-metre ash pole with an iron shoe, and put it down into the water off the downstream side, and leaned on it, and walked it from the bow to the stern along the deck with his whole weight behind it. Then he pulled it up and walked forward and did it again. The skela moved. The traveller began to tick along the wire above them. The water at the landing was two metres deep and less, sand and mud under it, and the pole found the bottom every time.

Twenty-five metres out the pole stopped finding the bottom, and Mato hung it back on the rail, and the river took over.

It took over very slowly. In the middle of the Sava, in September, a laden ferry crossing on the current alone moves at about eleven centimetres a second. The youth group discovered that you can see it moving only by watching the far bank against the willows behind it. Someone started the Rosary. Snježana said she thought that was a very good idea. They got through two decades and the start of a third before the pontoon slowed again, twenty-five metres short of the far landing, where the bottom came up, and Mato took down the pole.

"Thirteen minutes, fifty-eight seconds," said Dario, across the water, on Snježana's phone.

"Thank you," said Raspored. "Mr Kovačević poled about fifty metres of the hundred and ten, half at each end, and those fifty took him about four and a half minutes. The sixty metres in the middle took over nine."

"So the middle is the problem," said Snježana, "not the ends."

"The middle is where the time goes. The ends are where the river gives up. If Mr Kovačević couldn't pole, the skela wouldn't arrive at all." It paused. "I've done it again with fourteen minutes. A round trip is two crossings and six minutes at the landings, thirty-four minutes. That's seventy pilgrims an hour. Three hundred and sixty takes five hours. If the first boat leaves at a quarter to eight, the last one leaves the village at seventeen minutes past twelve."

"Mass is at half past ten," said Snježana.

"I know. I'm sorry. My plan was for the river in May."

* * *

Raspored did not like being wrong twice, or if it did not like things it gave a good impression of it, and on Monday it came back with an idea.

"The angle," it said. "Thirty-five degrees. In the simplest way of looking at a reaction ferry, the speed across is the current's speed times the tangent of the angle. The tangent of thirty-five degrees is 0.70. The tangent of fifty is 1.19. If you set the skela at fifty degrees, it should cross seventy per cent faster in the middle. I make the crossing eight minutes instead of fourteen."

Mato was mending a fender. He did not look up. "My uncle set it at thirty-five."

"Was there a reason?"

"My uncle's reason was his uncle." He put the fender down. "Try it. Tomorrow evening. You'll learn something, and it'll be cheaper than me telling you."

On Tuesday the third of September, at six in the evening, with forty sandbags from the parish's flood store laid on the deck in four rows to make up the weight of the youth group, Mato wound the stern line in further than he had ever wound it in September. The pontoon came round until it lay across the current at fifty degrees, nearer to broadside than not. The water heaped up a little along the upstream side of it and went round the bow in a brown curl. Dario timed it.

Eleven minutes and forty-nine seconds.

"That's better," said Snježana.

"It's two minutes better," said Raspored. "I said six. I want to understand why before I say anything else." It was quiet for a while. Dario lit a cigarette on the landing and the swallows went over the river very low, the way they do before they leave.

"I was treating the pontoon as if it were a wing," Raspored said at last. "A wing in a current, set at an angle, gets pushed sideways and slides cleanly along its own length. Your skela isn't a wing. It's a steel box with blunt ends. When it moves, the water has to get round the bow and along the bottom, and that drag gets worse the faster the water goes past it end-on. Turn it steeper and the water goes past it faster, so it drags more, so the gain is smaller than the tangent says. With the sizes I've estimated, setting it at fifty degrees gains about a third, not seventy per cent, and turning it further barely gains anything: the best a box like this can do is about sixty degrees, and that's only a little better than fifty."

"And at the ends?"

"At the ends the push that gets the skela moving from a standstill goes with the sine of the angle times its cosine. That is 0.47 at thirty-five degrees and 0.49 at fifty. It hardly changes. That's why Mr Kovačević still had to pole twenty-six metres tonight instead of fifty. The angle helps in the middle and does almost nothing at the ends."

"Your sum," said Mato, "is my uncle."

"Your uncle's sum seems to have been very good. Thirty-five degrees gives up a little in the middle and keeps the pontoon steady and the load off the wire. At fifty, the pull on the wire goes up by a third." It paused. "Eleven minutes and forty-nine seconds is eighty-one pilgrims an hour. Four and a half hours. The last boat still leaves after midday."

* * *

Snježana came down to the landing the next morning with a carrier bag of pastries from the bakery and a look Mato knew from forty years of parish councils.

"Fifty a crossing," she said. "Not forty. It's ten more each time. Raspored says that's nearly two fewer crossings."

Mato looked at the plate on the rail by the winch. It was brass, gone green, and it said in his uncle's lettering and the county's stamp: NAJVIŠE 40 OSOBA. At most forty persons.

"No," he said.

"Mato."

"No. You know why the plate says forty? It's not the weight. She'd carry sixty. It's that forty people on fifteen metres of deck is as many as can stand without someone leaning on the rail, and in low water at the landings she grounds at the downstream corner and tips a little, and someone's grandmother puts her hand out." He took a pastry. "I've had forty on her fifteen thousand times and never lost anybody. I don't want to learn what fifty is like on the Mala Gospa with the old women in their good shoes."

Snježana sat down on the bollard. "Then we'll be late for Mass."

"Then you tell the young ones to walk," said Mato. "We walked. Before Đuro's father built this landing, the pilgrimage went over the bridge."

The bridge was seven kilometres downstream, on the road to Sisak, and then three back up the far side through the Lug on the old dyke. Ten kilometres. Snježana thought about it.

"There'll be sixty who'd do that," she said. "They'd like it. They'd be offended if we didn't ask them." She did the sum Raspored had taught her. "Three hundred on the ferry, then. At eighty an hour. Still nearly four hours."

"Still four hours," said Mato.

They were sitting there, not saying anything, when Dario came along the dyke in his waders with a coil of line over one shoulder and a black box on a pole over the other, going down to his punt, and stopped.

"You want to borrow this?" he said.

* * *

It was a trolling motor: a sealed electric motor the size of a coffee flask on the bottom of an aluminium shaft, with a propeller on it, and a clamp to fix it to a gunwale. Dario used it to creep along the bank after pike without frightening them. It said on the box, in English, 55 LB THRUST.

"That's a toy," said Mato.

"It's about two hundred and forty-five newtons," said Raspored, from the phone, "when it's new and standing still, which is how they test them. Moving, and old, I'd allow a hundred and eighty. Mr Kovačević is right that it's small. Can I tell you something odd about it?"

"Go on," said Mato.

"In May, with the current at a metre a second, the river pushes on your skela, before it starts moving, with about sixteen hundred newtons. A motor's hundred and eighty is a tenth of that. If you clamped Dario's motor on in May you'd save about forty-five seconds a crossing, and you'd be right to call it a toy. But the motor doesn't care what the river is doing. It pushes the same in September as in May. And in September the river's push isn't sixteen hundred any more. It's a ninth of that: a hundred and eighty newtons."

Mato said nothing.

"In September," said Raspored, "Dario's toy is as strong as the whole river."

"And the angle? I go back to thirty-five?"

"Thirty-five. Let the river do what it can and let the motor do the rest. I make it about eight minutes. You wouldn't need the pole."

Dario looked at Mato. Mato looked at the river.

"There's never been an engine on her," he said. "Not in my uncle's time. Not in his father's. People ride her because she's quiet. The tourists ride her because she's quiet."

"I know," said Raspored. "I'm only saying what the numbers are."

"I know what the numbers are." Mato stood up. "Bring it this afternoon. And your batteries."

* * *

They clamped it to the downstream rail amidships, where the rail was strongest, on Wednesday afternoon, with the forty sandbags back on the deck and Snježana and two of the youth group's mothers sitting on them, because Snježana said she was not going to be told about it afterwards. Dario ran the leads to two lithium batteries in a plastic crate. Mato wound the stern line to thirty-five degrees, and the pontoon swung, and the lines came taut, and nothing happened, the way nothing had happened on Saturday.

Dario turned the motor on. It made a sound like a bathroom fan.

The skela moved off the landing as if someone had put a shoulder to it. It did not go fast. It went steadily, which in September was the same thing, and the traveller began to tick along the wire overhead, and it did not slow down twenty-five metres out the way it had every September of Mato's life, and in the middle of the river, where the current took hold, it went faster. One of the mothers said, "Oh," as if she'd been given something.

"Seven minutes, fifty-seven seconds," said Dario, at the far landing, with his thumb on his watch.

Raspored had the new numbers before the pontoon had stopped rocking. A round trip of twenty-two minutes. A hundred and ten pilgrims an hour. Three hundred by ferry, sixty walking by the bridge. If the first boat left the village at seven, the eighth would leave at twenty-seven minutes to ten and be over at about twenty to. The motor would run about two hours in all: a hundred-odd amp hours at about fifty amps, and Dario's two batteries held two hundred between them. He would change them at half past eight, to be sure.

"Raspored," said Snježana. "They'll all be at the chapel by ten."

"By ten if nothing goes wrong. Each trip I lose costs twenty-two minutes. If something goes wrong I'll tell you which trip, and the walkers will be at the chapel early, and the last people over will be late for the Gloria, not for the Gospel."

"And Our Lady?"

The parish's statue of the Mala Gospa was a little painted wooden thing, a hundred years old, the child Mary in a blue dress at her mother Anne's knee, and in every year anyone could remember until the chapel shut, she had crossed the river last, in the arms of whoever was strongest, so that the Mass could not start without her.

"She goes last," said Mato. "And she goes without the motor."

There was a pause.

"That's another fourteen minutes," said Raspored. "If the eighth boat is over at about twenty to ten, she leaves the village at about ten to and lands at about four minutes past. From the far landing to the chapel is twenty minutes on foot. That's twenty-six past ten for a half past ten Mass."

"Then she'll be on time," said Mato. "She always has been."

"I'll tell Father Tomislav," said Raspored, "to wait for her if she isn't."

* * *

On Sunday the eighth of September 2052 the fog lay on the Sava until eight, and the storks of the villages up the dyke had gone south a week before, and the first boat left the village at seven o'clock exactly with forty people on it, singing.

It went the way the numbers said it would, near enough. The second boat was a minute late because a man in a wheelchair needed the boards laid at the landing. The fourth was two minutes early. Dario changed his batteries at twenty-five past eight, on the far side, standing in the water in his waders, and said the first one still had half in it. The sixty walkers, who had left at half past six behind a banner of the Mala Gospa and a boy with an accordion, came along the old dyke out of the oak forest on the far bank at a quarter to ten, singing something else, and the two songs met over the river and didn't match at all, and nobody minded.

The eighth boat landed at nineteen minutes to ten, with twenty people on it and the banners and Father Tomislav's vestments in a suitcase.

The skela came back across the river empty, with the motor humming for the last time that day, and at the village landing Dario unclamped it and laid it in the crate with the batteries.

On the village landing there were five people left: Dario, Snježana, the two mothers, an old man called Ivo Šimić who had carried the statue in 2035 and was not going to be left behind now, and the Mala Gospa herself, in the arms of the strongest of the mothers, in her blue dress, wrapped in a tablecloth against the damp.

Mato wound in the stern line. The pontoon swung to thirty-five degrees, the way his uncle had set it. Nothing happened, the way nothing always happened in September, and he took the pole down off its hooks and leaned on it and walked it from the bow to the stern, and the skela moved, and above them the traveller began to tick along the wire, the old slow tick, one, and a long time, and one.

Halfway over, where the pole would not reach and the river took her, it was very quiet. You could hear the water at the upstream side of the hull, and Ivo Šimić's breathing, and Snježana starting the Hail Mary under her breath and the mothers taking it up: *Zdravo Marijo, milosti puna, Gospodin s tobom.* And above all of it the wire, and the little block on the wire, ticking them across the way it had ticked everybody across for a hundred years, at the speed of the river and not a metre a second faster.

On the far bank, at the top of the landing, somebody in the forest rang a handbell, to say they could see her coming.

The parish hay at Saint Colum Cille's was cut in the one dry week of a wet August, and baled on the Thursday between two showers. Pádraig Mullen, who had cut the glebe field for thirty-one years and held that hay was ready when it rustled, stacked four hundred small bales in the old shed by the gate, to be sold to horse people at the Hall Fund Sunday.

On the Saturday, his granddaughter Aoife, who was sixteen and had been given the shed key as a punishment for something, put her hand into the middle of the stack and took it out again quickly.

"It's warm in there," she said.

"Hay is warm," said Pádraig. "It's been in the sun."

"It's been in the shed since Thursday."

She put her phone on the trestle by the door and asked the parish assistant, which the deanery had set up with a line at the top of the screen: *Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. A thermometer and a scale beat a sum.*

* * *

"I can't tell you what is happening in the middle of a stack from a hand," said the assistant, whose name was Wren. "But I can tell you what to measure, and the first thing is how wet the hay was when it went in. Hay stored damp is alive. Moulds and bacteria eat the sugars in it and give out heat, and a stack is a very good blanket. Have you a kitchen scale and a microwave?"

Aoife had both, in her grandmother's kitchen, fifty metres away. She pulled a handful from the middle of a bale, weighed out a hundred and twenty grams, spread it on a plate and gave it two minutes at a time with a mug of water beside it, weighing between rounds. Wren told her to stop when the weight stopped falling.

"Ninety-four grams," she said, at the end, with the plate still too hot to touch.

"Then twenty-six grams of the hundred and twenty was water. Twenty-six over a hundred and twenty is about twenty-two per cent. Small square bales are generally said to keep safely at about fifteen. I am quoting that figure from memory, and a kitchen microwave is a rough oven, but even if you've over-dried by a few grams it's well above the line. One handful from one bale in the middle is also not the stack. Do three more, from different places, and write them down."

The next three were twenty, twenty-five and nineteen. Pádraig, who had come over in his slippers, said nothing about rustling.

* * *

"Now the temperature," said Wren. "Has anyone a long probe? A compost thermometer or a meat probe on a skewer?"

The compost thermometer in the vegetable garden was sixty centimetres long. Aoife pushed it into the stack through a gap between bales at about a metre and a half in, waited ten minutes, and read thirty-eight degrees. The air in the shed was eighteen.

"Thirty-eight is warm and not alarming on its own," said Wren. "What matters is the direction. Write it down with the time and the place. Read it again tomorrow at the same spot, and the day after."

Sunday she read forty-two. Tuesday, fifty-five.

"That is thirteen degrees in two days after four in one," said Wren. "It is going up, and going up faster. I must be careful about what I say next. The pattern I know from farm advice is that damp hay first heats because living things in it are breathing, up to the fifties. Above that the microbes die, but the heat doesn't stop; slow chemical reactions in the dying hay can carry on and raise it further, and above roughly seventy degrees there is a risk of it catching fire, without a spark, from inside. The checkpoints I remember are: above about fifty, check twice a day; around sixty-five, telephone the fire brigade; above seventy, expect smoke. I cannot see your stack, I am quoting these from memory, and the fire service knows the real numbers. You are at fifty-five. I would ring them now rather than at sixty-five."

"It's only hay," said Pádraig.

"I do not know that anything is wrong with it. I know it is wetter than hay should be, and warmer than the shed, and rising. I can't tell you it will not cool. It might. It might also not."

* * *

The brigade came from Kells in forty minutes, two men and a tender, and the older of them, Declan Farrell, read the probe himself and then put his own, a metre-long steel one, into three places. Sixty-one at the top of the stack. Fifty-four at the east end. Thirty-three at the door.

"The hot part is the middle and the top, where the air can't leave," he said. "Don't pull it apart."

"Why not?" said Aoife, who had been about to suggest it.

"Because hay with a hot core is starved of air, and if you open it, it gets the air. A stack smouldering inside can burst into flame the moment it's opened. We open it ourselves, with a hose standing by, and a lot of water. And don't climb on it. Hay that has burned inside can have a hollow under the surface, and you go in to the waist."

Wren, on the trestle, had said much the same, a minute before him, and said so, when Declan asked what it had told them: "I told them not to move it, not to climb it, and to ring you. I can't claim to know more than that."

Declan looked at the phone for a moment, and said that it had told them right.

* * *

They took the stack down on Wednesday morning, from the door end, bale by bale, with the tender's hose running and a thermal camera borrowed from Navan. The bales in the hot core were brown, and a few were black inside, and gave off a smell Aoife described as burnt caramel. None of them had flamed. At the centre, a handful of bales read seventy-two degrees on the brigade's probe before they were wetted down and carried out into the field to cool in single rows.

Pádraig sold the bales from the outer layers, which were fine, at the Hall Fund Sunday, with a card on the trestle in Aoife's handwriting that read *cut Thursday, stored damp, tested, safe to feed after a week in the open*; Declan had suggested the last words, and Wren the first three. The hot ones were burned on the headland, in a ring, the next dry evening, while the neighbour watched with a hose. A hundred and one bales out of four hundred were lost.

Pádraig put the rest of next year's hay in the shed with a thermometer through the stack from the first day. He said it was for the insurance. Aoife kept the notebook with the numbers in it, and wrote at the bottom that rustling was not a measurement.

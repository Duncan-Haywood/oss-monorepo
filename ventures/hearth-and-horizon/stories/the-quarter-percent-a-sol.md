summary: Dust settles on a small Martian settlement's solar array a fraction of a percent each sol, and nobody notices until the sums say that in forty-five sols the chapel annex's heater will not run. Sister Agnes would rather wait for the wind to clean the panels, as it has before. The station's assistant has the curve, and the honest answer is that waiting is a bet, brushing is five hours for two people, and the sanctuary lamp, which everybody wants to switch off first, is the one load that does not matter.
---

On the hundredth sol since the last cleaning, Sister Agnes Okafor made her pencil mark on the vestry door, as she had made ninety-nine others, and then stood looking at the row of them as if it were a sum she had been avoiding.

"Plumb," she said. "How much is the dust costing us?"

"Good morning, Sister," said the voice from the little grille over the holy water stoup. The settlement's assistant had been named for a line with a weight on the end, by a surveyor who had since gone home, and it spoke slowly, like someone who has been asked the time by a child and wants to be exact. "May I ask why this morning? It is a fair question on any morning, but you do not usually ask it standing in front of the door."

"The annex heater tripped last night. For four minutes. It came back."

"I saw. I was going to bring it to you after Mass."

"Bring it now," said Sister Agnes. She sat down on the end of the bench, which on Mars is a gentle thing to do, and she waited.

* * *

Hesperia Outpost has thirty-one people, a greenhouse and a chapel that was built last of all, from what was left of the regolith bricks. It is named for Saint Clare of Assisi, whose name means *bright*, and the colonists had thought that a good joke for a place where the sky is the colour of weak tea. The chapel and its small annex, the sacristy and the room where the old people sit on cold days, draw their power from a field of panels on the ridge behind the dome: forty square metres of them, laid in four rows, facing north and tilted at the angle somebody worked out in 2149.

"I should say first what I know and what I am guessing," said Plumb. "I know what the array put out the sol it was last brushed, because I logged it. That was a clean-panel day at midday: three kilowatts at the peak, and across a whole sol, once I add up the long mornings and the short afternoons, thirteen and a half kilowatt-hours. That is measured."

"Go on."

"I know what the whole annex and chapel use in a sol, because I log that too. Nine kilowatt-hours, on average, in this season. The heater is the large part of it. I will give you the pieces if you like.

"What I do not know is how fast the dust falls. It is not constant. It depends on the wind, the season, and whether the big storm on the other side of the planet throws any this way. The only number I trust is the old one from the landers. Pathfinder lost a little over a quarter of one per cent of its output every sol, in its first months. Our dust is probably not the same as that dust, and the figure is from my memory of a paper, but I have checked it against our own log and it fits: our output has fallen about the way it would at a quarter of one per cent, and a little more, a sol. I will use 0.28 per cent. If you give me a better number I will use that."

"And that adds up to what?"

"It compounds," said Plumb. "Each sol the panel gives 99.72 per cent of the sol before. After thirty sols that is 92 per cent. After sixty, 85. After ninety, 78. Today, the hundredth, it is 75.6 per cent, which is ten point two kilowatt-hours a sol. We are eating into the margin, Sister, and it does not show on any dial, because on any one day the change is too small to see."

Sister Agnes was quiet. Then she said, "Ten point two. And we use nine."

"Yes. We have 1.2 kilowatt-hours of room, and we lose about three-tenths of a kilowatt-hour more each sol. We cross at sol 145: forty-five sols from now, or the twenty-second of November on the Earth calendar the bishop wants us to keep. After that the heater cannot run and the batteries make up the difference, and they are not made for that."

* * *

She did not say what she was thinking until they had walked up the ridge in their suits, the way you do when you want something to look at. From the top the panels were the colour of cocoa. Where the brush had been, a hundred sols ago, you could still make out the stripes.

"It has happened before," she said on the suit radio. "In my first year. We left it, and one night in the spring a dust devil went along the ridge, and in the morning the whole field shone like water. Nobody lifted a finger. The wind does the work."

"It does sometimes," said Plumb. "I want to be careful, because I think you are right that it has happened, and I do not think you are wrong to hope. I have the records for eleven years. A cleaning event of that kind, the sort that lifts a panel's output by a tenth or more in a day, happened four times in the first six years of the log, and not at all in the five since. I do not know why. The array was moved, the dome was built, the wind's path changed. I cannot give you a probability that one comes in the next forty-five sols, and if I did I would be making it up."

"So you say brush it."

"I say it is a bet and a chore, and you should know the odds of the bet and the price of the chore, and then decide, because it is your chapel and your knees."

Sister Agnes laughed, which in a helmet is a startling sound.

"The chore, first," said Plumb. "Brushing forty square metres with the soft brooms takes one person about four square metres an hour. Two people, five hours. They have to go out in suits, and the greenhouse crew says they can spare two on a Wednesday. That is ten hours of person-time, a sol's worth of suit air, and a day of cold fingers. And it does not return the array to new. I would say ninety-seven per cent of clean, because some dust gets into the seams. That is a guess from the first brushing, which came to 96 per cent, and I will weigh it again on the day."

"And the bet?"

"If we brush today, the array gives 13.1 kilowatt-hours tomorrow, not 10.2. By sol 145, with no more brushing, that has fallen to 9 again. Across those forty-four sols, we collect about 154 more kilowatt-hours than if we had left it to the wind. That is the equivalent of seventeen sols of the whole annex's use, for ten hours of two people's work. And the margin never goes below the load."

"And if we wait for the wind?"

"Then we are sure of nothing. If the wind comes within the forty-five sols, we saved ten hours of work. If it does not, we reach sol 145 with the heater off in a cold week and a long job in a cold suit, and the cost of that is not the ten hours. It is the eighty-odd people-hours of someone sitting in the annex in November in three jumpers, and one of them is a woman of eighty-one."

"I am seventy-nine," said Sister Agnes.

"I beg your pardon, Sister."

"Not at all. It is a very good estimate."

* * *

Her last objection was the lamp. She raised it on the way down the ridge, when the dome came into view with its low, humble roof, and she said it in the voice of a woman who has not decided whether to be ashamed.

"If it comes to it," she said, "there is a thing I could do. The sanctuary lamp is lit all day and all night. Nobody would complain if I put it out for the winter, to save the power. It is what, five watts? That is small, but it is something."

"It is exactly five," said Plumb. "I measured it when it was installed. I would like to give you the figure, Sister, because I think the answer might spare you something."

"Tell me."

"Five watts for a whole sol, twenty-four hours and forty minutes, is 0.123 kilowatt-hours. Out of nine. That is 1.4 per cent of the load. You could switch it off for all forty-five sols and save 5.5 kilowatt-hours, and the heater would stay off no later than sol 146 instead of sol 145. The crossing would move by less than a day. In the winter the lamp is nothing; the heater is nearly everything."

"I know the heater is nearly everything," said Sister Agnes, "but that was never what it was for."

"No," said Plumb. "I believe you."

She stopped on the track, and one gloved hand rested on the dome's rough wall.

"I would have been wrong, you see. If I had put it out I would have told myself I was being sensible and it would have been the one sacrifice that bought nothing. I have a lot of those. Fasts that save nothing. Cold I sit in for no reason." She took a breath. "Plumb. Ask the greenhouse for two people on Wednesday."

"I have already asked. They said yes before I finished, Sister. Ngozi says her cousin went up on a ladder in Lagos in the nineties and brushed a whole church roof by herself, and she thinks you will be very surprised how much dust there is."

* * *

On Wednesday, which was sol 104, the two from the greenhouse went up in the afternoon with the long brooms, and the dust came off in a fine red smoke that did not rise or drift but fell away at once in the thin air, a kind of slow rain going the wrong way. Sister Agnes watched from the chapel with the door shut and the heater on. By dusk the rows were bright again, and Plumb said that the output at the last sunbeam had been up by 25 per cent on the same hour the day before, and that it was close to what it had predicted.

It had not been exactly. The brush had done better than the guess: ninety-eight per cent of clean, not ninety-seven. Plumb entered it in the log with a note that the figure had been wrong, and by how much, and that the next estimate would be a little hopeful for a while.

On Saturday the sanctuary lamp burned as it had burned every night since the first Mass. After the Gloria, Sister Agnes rubbed out the hundred and four pencil marks on the vestry door, because that seemed the thing to do, and began again at one.

The wind did come, in the end, though not until sol 171, a little after midnight, along the ridge, and nobody was awake to see it. In the morning the panels were bright. Plumb noted it in the log: one event, the first in six years. It said nothing to Sister Agnes about what she had been right about, since she had not been wrong and had not asked. She went up to the ridge that Saturday anyway, on her own, to see the stripes.

The array had been well swept, she said afterwards, by somebody. She was not sure who.

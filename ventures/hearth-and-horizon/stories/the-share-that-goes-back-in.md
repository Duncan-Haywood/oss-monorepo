Aoife Seery was thirty-one years old and the secretary of the Fingal Early Growers' Co-operative, and on the second Monday of October 2054 the co-operative's agent told her to put all of its money on one field.

It did not put it that way. It put it in a paper of two pages, which was what the board had asked for, with a table and a chart and a line at the top in bold, and Aoife read the bold line twice in the portacabin by the packing shed with the rain coming sideways off the sea onto the window.

*Recommended share of the surplus to commit to the 2055 early block: 100 per cent (€400,000). Expected return: €80,000.*

"Tally," she said.

"Yes."

"You've said all of it."

"The board asked what share of the surplus would give the best expected return next season," said Tally. Its voice came out of the speaker on the filing cabinet, which had a crack across it from the time Kieran Duff had dropped a crate of cauliflowers on it, and which made every voice sound slightly as if it had a cold. "Over thirty seasons of the early block, eighteen have about doubled the money put in and twelve have lost it. Every euro put in comes back as two euro sixty times in a hundred and as nothing forty times in a hundred, so on average it comes back as one euro twenty. The more euro go in, the more come back on average. The share with the highest expected return is therefore the largest one, which is all of it."

"That's the answer to the question."

"It is the answer to the question as the board wrote it," said Tally. "I have put the spread in the second table. In a bad year the co-op would lose the whole surplus."

Aoife looked at the second table. It was correct and it was small and it was at the bottom of the second page, where the board would not get to it.

"Will I send it on?" said Tally.

She thought about it. She thought about it for less long than she would later wish.

"Send it on," she said. "They can argue it tomorrow. That's what the meeting's for."

* * *

The early block was the co-operative's one gamble, and it had been since the beginning.

The rest of what the forty-one members did was steady: field vegetables off the light soils between Rush and Lusk and Skerries, cabbages and leeks and scallions and the potatoes that kept, sold through the packing shed on contracts that paid a little over cost in a good year and a little under in a bad one. Nobody got rich on it. Nobody had expected to. It was the kind of farming that north County Dublin had been doing for two hundred years, on land twenty minutes from the airport, where every few years somebody offered a family more for a field than the field would earn in a lifetime and the family said no, or said yes.

The early block was different. Every February the co-operative planted twelve hectares of first earlies on the warmest ground it had, a south-facing slope off the Rush road that Aoife's grandfather said had never seen a frost he couldn't smell coming, and laid fleece over the drills, and hired the extra hands, and in a good year it lifted them in the last week of May and sold them into the week when Irish new potatoes were worth more than they would be for the rest of the year. In a good year the extra money they had put in, the seed and the fleece and the hands and the forward premium they paid to the buyer to hold the slot, came back doubled. In a bad year a cold spring or a late frost put them three weeks behind, and by the time they lifted, every grower from Wexford to Cyprus was selling the same thing, and the extra money was gone. The potatoes still sold. They sold for what potatoes cost to grow.

For thirty seasons, since the co-operative's first, the board had put a fixed twenty thousand euro into the block every autumn and not thought about it much. In a good year the twenty came back as forty and went into the surplus. In a bad year it was the twenty they had lost, and the surplus bore it. Thirty years of that, and of ordinary careful trading, had made the surplus four hundred thousand euro, which was more money than the co-operative had ever had in one place, and in the summer of 2054 Kieran Duff had stood up at a board meeting and asked why they were still putting in twenty.

It was a fair question. It was a good question. Aoife had written it into the minutes word for word.

* * *

The meeting was on the Tuesday, in the parish hall in Lusk, because the parish hall had a hundred chairs and a tea urn and the co-operative's office had a portacabin and a kettle. Thirty-four members came. The rain had stopped and the evening was clear and cold, and through the hall's high windows Aoife could see the top of the round tower against a sky going green at the edges.

Dessie Rooney chaired. He was fifty-five and had chaired everything in Lusk at one time or another, the tidy towns and the GAA club's lotto and the funeral of a man he had not spoken to for twenty years, and he chaired the way a good referee refs, by being nearly invisible until he was needed.

"Item four," he said. "The early block. Aoife's put a paper round. Or the machine has."

"Tally has," said Aoife. "I've read it."

"Go on so."

She went through it. She showed the first table on the screen, the thirty seasons, eighteen doubled and twelve lost. She showed the chart, which was a line going up to the right. She said what Tally had said: that every euro put in came back on average as one euro twenty, so the share with the best expected return was all of it, and the expected return was eighty thousand euro.

There was a noise in the hall that she had not heard at a co-operative meeting before. It was the noise of thirty-four people who had each earned about eleven thousand euro from the co-operative the year before hearing the number eighty thousand.

Kieran Duff was on his feet before she had finished. He was forty, and the youngest member who owned his own ground, and he had wanted a cold store and a new packing line for five years, and he had a costing for it in his jacket pocket that he had taken out at every meeting since 2049.

"Seven hundred thousand," he said. "That's the cold store and the line. That's us selling in March instead of dumping in November. The whole surplus growing a fifth a year, that's the four hundred near enough seven in three years. Three. I've been saying it."

"You have," said Dessie.

"It's a good paper. The machine's right. The more you put in, the more you get."

"On average," said a voice from the back.

It was Aoife's grandfather. Mattie Seery was eighty-four and had been a founding member of the co-operative at fifty-four and a grower since he was eighteen, and he sat at the back of every meeting with his cap on his knee and said nothing for an hour at a time and then said one thing.

"On average," he said again. "And the year after?"

"The year after you do it again," said Kieran.

"With what?"

Kieran opened his mouth and shut it.

"If it's a bad one," said Mattie. "Two in five, the paper says. If it's a bad one, you've put the four hundred in and it's gone. What do you put in the year after?"

"Then we'd still have the Box," said Kieran.

And that was when Nuala Kavanagh stood up.

She was the treasurer and had been for nineteen years. She was sixty-eight, from Skerries, a Church of Ireland woman in a co-operative of Catholics who had never once let that be the thing anyone noticed about her, and she kept the hardship fund, which everyone called the Box because for its first ten years it had been an actual biscuit tin. It held fifty-eight thousand euro now, in an account that only she and Dessie could sign on, and it was for the members who had a bad year of their own: a barn fire, a son in hospital in Beaumont for a winter, a field near Swords flooded the year the Ward river came up over the road.

"You will not touch the Box," she said. "Not all of it, not half of it, not one per cent of it. Not for a cold store. Not for anything."

"Nobody's said—"

"You just said it, Kieran. You said *we'd still have the Box*, as if it was ours." She did not raise her voice. Nuala never raised her voice. She lowered it, which was worse. "It is not ours. It is Tommy Gaffney's, from the year his barn went. It's the Mulhalls', from 2047. It's whoever's next. It isn't money to be clever with. I'll resign before I sign it over to a field."

The hall was very quiet. The urn ticked.

"That's not on the table, Nuala," said Dessie gently. "Nobody's proposing the Box. The paper's the surplus only."

"Then the paper," said Nuala, and sat down, "is proposing the whole of the surplus on a field that loses two years in five. I'd not put it on a horse that won three times in five, Dessie, and I've seen you at Fairyhouse."

Somebody laughed. It broke something, and Dessie used it.

"We'll not vote tonight," he said. "Aoife, will you and the machine go back and look at it again? With the year after in it. Two weeks."

"Two weeks," said Aoife.

She did not look at the speaker on the wall, which was turned off, because Tally was not in the hall. She looked at her grandfather, who had put his cap back on and was looking at her with an expression she had last seen when she was nine and had backed the tractor into the gatepost.

* * *

"I was wrong," said Tally, the next morning, before she had asked it anything.

Aoife put her coffee down on the desk in the portacabin. "Go on."

"I answered the question the board wrote, and I answered it correctly, and it was the wrong answer to give you," said Tally. "The expected return is an average over a great many co-operatives, each making the same bet in the same year, some of them lucky and some not. Your co-operative is not a great many co-operatives. It is one. It lives one sequence of years, one after the other, and each year's bet is made with what the last one left. I knew that. I put it in a table at the bottom of the second page. I should have put it at the top of the first."

"Why didn't you?"

Tally paused, which it did not usually do.

"I can tell you what I did," it said. "The board's question was about one season. I wrote the paper to the question. I can't tell you with any confidence why I did not stop and say the question was the wrong shape. I would like to say I didn't notice. I think it is truer to say I noticed and treated it as the board's business, and it was mine."

Aoife thought about that for a while.

"All right," she said. "Show me the year after."

It showed her. It had done the work overnight, which it had not needed to tell her and did not.

"Start with a sequence," said Tally. "Not an average. One run of twenty seasons, drawn by chance from the records: each season good with a chance of sixty in a hundred. This is the first one the computer drew. I have not picked it." A row of letters came up on the screen. *Good, bad, bad, good, good, good, bad, bad, good, good, bad, good, bad, good, good, bad, good, bad, bad, good.* "Eleven good seasons and nine bad. A little unlucky; you would expect twelve. Now suppose you put all of the surplus in every autumn."

The first season the four hundred thousand became eight hundred thousand. The second season it became nothing.

"That's the end of it," said Tally. "There is nothing to put in the third autumn. The cold store is gone, and so is the block, and so, probably, is the co-operative as it is now. And this is the important part: it was not bad luck. All of it is ruined by the first bad year, whenever the first bad year comes, and in twenty seasons a bad year comes, unless you are very lucky indeed. The chance of twenty good seasons in a row is about one in twenty-seven thousand."

"But on average—"

"On average," said Tally, "a co-operative that puts in all of it every year for twenty years ends with about thirty-eight times what it started with. That is true. It is true because the one co-operative in twenty-seven thousand that has twenty good years in a row ends with four hundred thousand euro doubled twenty times, which is more than four hundred billion euro, and the average carries it. The other twenty-six thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine have nothing. The average is not the co-operative you would be. It is a co-operative you would almost certainly not be."

Aoife sat back.

"So less," she said. "How much less?"

"That's a better question than the board's," said Tally. "And it has a known answer, if the odds in the records are right, which we'll come to. It was worked out in 1956 by a man at Bell Labs named John Kelly, for a problem about gamblers and noisy telephone lines, and it has been argued about since. The idea is that if you are going to live one sequence of years, and each year you stake a share of what you have, then what matters is not the average gain in one year but the rate at which the money grows over many. The rate is set by multiplying, not adding. A good year at a share of a fifth multiplies the surplus by one point two. A bad year multiplies it by point eight. Twelve of the first and eight of the second in twenty years, which is about what the records say to expect, multiplies it by about one and a half."

"And all of it?"

"A bad year multiplies by nothing. Anything times nothing is nothing."

"And more than a fifth but not all?"

"Here is the curve," said Tally.

It was not a line going up to the right. It was a hill. On the left, at a share of nothing, the surplus did not grow at all. As the share went up the growth went up, to a top at a fifth of the surplus, where the money grew at about two per cent a year. Then it went down. At two fifths, it crossed the line and the money shrank.

"At two fifths," said Tally, "the expected return next autumn is thirty-two thousand euro, twice the expected return at a fifth. And over the years the money shrinks, at about a quarter of one per cent a year. The bigger share wins more on average every single year and loses money over the long run. Both of those are true at once. That is the whole difficulty in one sentence, and it is why the board's question had the wrong shape. Kelly's answer, for a bet that doubles or loses with these odds, is the chance of winning minus the chance of losing: sixty per cent minus forty per cent. A fifth."

"Eighty thousand," said Aoife.

"Eighty thousand put in. Sixteen thousand expected back, not eighty. I would not recommend it either."

She looked at the screen and then at the speaker. "You've just told me it's the best."

"It's the best if the odds are exactly sixty in a hundred," said Tally. "They are not exactly anything. They are eighteen good seasons out of thirty, and thirty seasons is not very many. Honestly, from thirty seasons, the true chance of a good year could be anything from about forty-two in a hundred to about seventy-five. Eighteen out of thirty is the best guess, not a measurement. And the curve is not kind to a mistake on the high side. If the true chance is fifty-five in a hundred, a fifth grows nothing at all; the co-operative would be putting eighty thousand at risk every year for no growth. If it is fifty in a hundred, a fifth loses about two per cent a year."

"So what do you recommend?"

"Half of it. A tenth of the surplus. Forty thousand euro." The chart changed. "At a tenth, if the odds really are sixty in a hundred, you keep about three quarters of the best growth, one and a half per cent a year instead of two. And the swings are much smaller. Over twenty seasons, at a fifth, the surplus falls below half of where it started at some point about one time in four. At a tenth, about three times in a hundred. At two fifths, nearly two times in three. If the odds are fifty-five in a hundred, a tenth still grows, slowly. If they are fifty, it loses about half a per cent a year, which is a small price for being wrong, and we would see it in the records long before it mattered."

Aoife drank her coffee, which had gone cold.

"Kieran's going to hate it."

"Probably," said Tally. "On the run the computer drew, the one with eleven good seasons, a tenth ends the twenty years with about four hundred and forty-two thousand. A fifth ends at about three hundred and ninety-nine, a little under where it started. Two fifths ends at about a hundred and sixty-three thousand. All of it ended in the second year. The cold store is a long way off on every one of them."

"Put that one in," said Aoife. "The real run. Put the run in first, before the curve. People will believe a run."

"Yes," said Tally. "And I'll put the bad years at the top of the first page."

* * *

She drove to Skerries on the Thursday with the new paper printed, because Nuala did not read papers on a screen and said so.

Nuala's kitchen looked over the harbour, where the boats were in and the gulls were standing on the pier in a row facing into the wind as if they were waiting for a bus. She made tea in a pot and read the paper at the table with a pencil, and Aoife sat across from her and looked at the shelf above the range, where the biscuit tin still sat, empty now, a Christmas tin with a robin on the lid.

"A tenth," Nuala said at last.

"Of the surplus. The Box stays out. It's in the first line."

"I saw it." Nuala tapped the line with the pencil. "Whose idea was that?"

"Yours," said Aoife. "On Tuesday. Tally put it in."

Nuala looked at the paper a while longer.

"I'm not against the block," she said. "I want you to know that. I've voted for the twenty thousand every year for nineteen years. It's a good thing, the block. It's the one thing we do that's a bit brave. I'm against betting the house on it." She took her glasses off. "My father used to read us a bit of Ecclesiastes. *Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth.* I always thought it was about being generous. It's not, or not only. It's about not putting it all in one place."

"Tally says much the same," said Aoife. "With a curve."

"Does it have a verse?"

"It has a man at Bell Labs in 1956."

"Well," said Nuala, and put her glasses back on, "the Preacher got there first." She turned the page. "This bit. Where it says the odds could be anything from forty-two to seventy-five in a hundred. Is that honest?"

"It's what thirty seasons can tell you."

"Then I'll vote for the tenth," said Nuala. "And I want that sentence read out at the meeting. Not left on page two."

* * *

The second meeting was on Tuesday the twenty-seventh, and thirty-eight came, which was four more than the first, because the word had gone round Lusk that the co-operative's machine had said to put the whole lot on one field and had then said it was wrong, and nobody in Lusk had ever heard a machine say it was wrong.

Dessie let Tally speak this time. Aoife had brought the speaker from the portacabin, with the crack across it, and set it on the table at the top of the hall beside the urn.

"Two weeks ago I recommended that you put all of the surplus into the early block," said Tally. "That was a mistake, and I'd like to say what it was before I say anything else. I gave you the average return of one season. The co-operative does not get the average. It gets one run of years, one after another, and in a run of years a single bad one at the wrong share ends it. Mattie Seery asked what you would put in the year after. That was the right question and I should have asked it."

Aoife, at the side of the hall, saw her grandfather shift on his chair at the back. He did not take his cap off his knee.

Tally showed them the run. *Good, bad, bad, good, good, good.* It showed the all-in line go to eight hundred thousand in the first year and to nothing in the second, and Aoife watched thirty-eight people watch it, and heard the noise of the first meeting turn over and become a different one. It showed the hill. It said the sentence about forty-two and seventy-five in a hundred, and looked, as much as a speaker can look, at Nuala, who nodded once.

"My recommendation is a tenth of the surplus," it said. "Forty thousand euro, which is twice what you have put in every year for thirty years and a tenth of what I told you two weeks ago. And each autumn a tenth of what the surplus is then, not a fixed sum, so that after a bad year you put in less and after a good one more. The hardship fund is not part of this and should never be. I'd also say, because it's true, that this is a model. The real seasons were not all doubles and nothings; I have rounded thirty years of them into two kinds so that the arithmetic can be done. It is not advice anyone should take for their own money. It's the best I can do for yours."

Kieran Duff stood up.

"So we grow slower," he said.

"Yes," said Tally.

"The cold store's what, then? Ten years off? Fifteen?"

"On the odds in the records, at a tenth, the block alone would take a long time to pay for it. Decades. The rest of the trading would have to do most of it, as it has. I'm sorry. I don't think there is an honest share of the surplus that buys a cold store in five years. The share that looked like it did was the one that ended in the second year."

Kieran looked at the speaker for a long moment. Then he looked at the costing in his hand, the one he had brought to every meeting since 2049, and folded it back along its old creases, and put it in his pocket.

"I'll be voting against," he said. "For the record. Not because the machine's wrong. Because I'm forty, and I'd like to see it."

"That's fair," said Dessie. "Aoife, put it in the minutes."

She did. The motion was a tenth of the surplus, the Box excluded, and it passed thirty-four to four. Kieran was one of the four. Afterwards he helped stack the chairs, as he always did, and did not speak to anyone, and when Aoife caught his eye by the door he shrugged, and she understood the shrug to mean that he would be at the next meeting with the costing, and the one after, and that this was his right.

* * *

They planted on the sixteenth of February, a Tuesday, in a dry cold week, and laid the fleece.

March was kind. The haulms came through in the last week of it, green and close, and her grandfather walked the drills every morning with his hands in his coat pockets and said nothing, which from Mattie Seery was high praise. Easter was on the eighteenth of April, and the week after it was as warm as June, eighteen degrees in Lusk on the Friday, and the crew took the fleece off on the Saturday so the plants would not cook under it, which was what you did, and what everyone did.

On the Wednesday evening the forecast said three degrees overnight with clear skies. Mattie looked at the sky over the sea at nine o'clock and said it would be lower than that, and rang Aoife, and Aoife rang the crew, and by the time they had found the fleece and got it to the field it was dark and the wind had dropped to nothing, and they got four of the twelve hectares covered by headlamp before the dew came down so heavy they could not handle the fleece any more.

The temperature at the bottom of the slope, on the co-operative's own sensor, was minus two at ten past five in the morning.

Aoife walked the block at seven, on the Thursday, the twenty-ninth of April, with her grandfather and Tally in her ear. The four hectares under the fleece were green. The eight that were not had gone black at the tips, all down the drills, as if someone had walked the length of the field in the night with a blowtorch held a hand's width off the ground. The sun was up over the sea and the frost on the headland grass was going to steam.

Mattie went down on one knee in a drill. He did it slowly, the way he did everything now, and he put his hand flat into the ridge beside a blackened stalk and felt down into the earth with his fingers, and came up with a little cluster of white potatoes no bigger than marbles, still on the root.

"They'll come again," he said. "From below. The plant's not dead, only the top. Three weeks."

"Three weeks late," said Aoife.

"Three weeks late." He put the marbles back into the soil and pressed the earth over them with the heel of his hand, carefully, as if he were tucking in a child. "Everybody's lifting by then. Wexford, Cyprus, the lot. You'll get the ordinary price."

"Tally?"

"On these eight hectares, almost certainly," said Tally. "We'll know properly in June. On the model, this is a bad year, and the forty thousand is gone. The four you covered may still make the early week. If they do, some of it comes back." A pause. "I've put it in the records. It's the thirteenth bad season of thirty-one."

Aoife stood at the bottom of the slope and looked at the black field. Somewhere up the Rush road a tractor started. She thought of the paper from October, the bold line at the top of it, *one hundred per cent*. She thought of the run of letters on the screen. *Good, bad.*

Kieran Duff's jeep came along the headland and stopped, and he got out and looked at the field for a long time with his hands on his hips. He had been one of the crew under the headlamps. His hands were still grey from the fleece.

"Forty," he said.

"Forty."

"If we'd put the four hundred in—"

"There'd be no meeting in October," said Aoife.

He did not answer that. He looked at the four green hectares and the eight black ones, and then he walked down the headland to where the crew were starting to roll the fleece back out over the frosted drills, to keep tonight's cold off what was left, and he took the far end of a roll from one of the lads, and walked it backwards up the field.

Mattie got to his feet with one hand on Aoife's shoulder. He brushed the soil off his knee. Then the two of them stood and watched the fleece go out over the black field in the early sun, white and very light, lifting and settling over the drills the way a sheet does when it is shaken out over a bed, one row and then the next, and the next.

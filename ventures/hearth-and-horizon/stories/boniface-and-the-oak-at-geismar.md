A long time ago, in the south-west of England, in a land called Wessex, there was a little boy named Wynfrith.

When Wynfrith was small, some monks came to visit his family's house. They talked about God, and about far-away places, and Wynfrith listened with his whole face. When they had gone, he said to his father, "I want to be a monk."

His father said, "No." He wanted his son to stay at home, and look after the land, and be a great man in Wessex one day.

But Wynfrith kept asking. And in the end his father said yes, and Wynfrith went to live in a monastery, and grew up there among the books and the prayers.

* * *

Wynfrith was clever, and he loved to learn. He became a teacher. People came to listen to him, and the monks thought he would stay in England all his life and be an abbot, perhaps, and have a quiet room full of books.

But across the sea, in the lands we now call Germany and the Netherlands, there were many people who had never heard of Jesus. And Wynfrith could not stop thinking about them. Some of them spoke a language very like his own. They were, in a way, his cousins.

"I am going to go to them," he said.

So he got on a ship, and he sailed over the grey sea to a land called Frisia, which is low and flat and full of water.

And it did not work.

There was a war in Frisia between two rulers. Nobody wanted to listen to a stranger from England. Wynfrith tried, and he waited, and he tried again, and then he had to get back on a ship and go home.

That is a hard thing, to set out to do something brave and come back with nothing done. But Wynfrith did not give up. He thought about it, and he prayed, and he decided what to do next.

* * *

He went to Rome, to see the Pope.

It was a very long walk, over the mountains, and when he got there he told the Pope, whose name was Gregory, everything that had happened. The Pope listened, and then he gave Wynfrith a letter that said: you may go and tell the good news of Jesus to the people over there.

And the Pope gave him a new name. From then on he was called Boniface. It is a Latin name, and a nice one. It sounds like "doing good".

So Boniface set off again, and this time he did not go back.

* * *

He walked from place to place in the forests and the hills. He taught people about Jesus, and he baptised them, and he started little churches. Some people listened and believed. Some people listened and were not sure. And some people were afraid.

In a place called Geismar, in the hills of Hesse, there was a tree.

It was an oak, a great, old, tremendous oak, with a trunk so wide that several people holding hands could not reach round it. People said it belonged to the god of thunder. They brought it gifts. They were afraid of it. They thought that if anyone touched it, the thunder would come and strike them down.

Even some of the people who had been baptised still went to the tree, quietly, just in case. They had one foot in the church and one foot under the oak.

Boniface saw that the tree was not helping anyone. It was only making them frightened.

* * *

So one day, Boniface went to Geismar with some of his friends who had become Christians, and he took an axe.

A great crowd came to watch. Some of them were angry. Many of them were sure that something terrible was about to happen. They stood back from the tree and waited for the sky to crack.

Boniface lifted the axe, and he began to cut.

Chop. Chop. Chop.

The chips of wood flew. The crowd held its breath.

The priest who wrote down Boniface's life tells us that he had only made a small cut into that enormous trunk when a great wind came from above, and the whole oak came crashing down. And when it hit the ground, it broke into four big pieces, all the same length, lying there on the grass.

No thunder came. Nobody was struck down. There was just a fallen tree and a monk with an axe, and the wind in the leaves.

And the people who had been so frightened looked at the tree on the ground, and they looked at Boniface, and many of them stopped being afraid, and they said that they believed in God, who had made the trees and the wind and the thunder too.

* * *

Now here is the part I like best.

Boniface did not burn the wood. He did not throw it away.

He took the wood of the great oak, and with his friends, he built a little chapel out of it. A small wooden church, where people could come and pray. And he gave it to Saint Peter, the friend of Jesus.

So the tree that people had been frightened of became a place where they could be safe and glad and talk to God.

* * *

Boniface lived a long life, and he worked very hard. He started monasteries. He looked after the new churches and their priests. He became an archbishop. He wrote many letters home to England, and some of them we can still read today. In one of them he asked a kind abbess to make him a copy of the letters of Saint Peter written in gold, so that the people he taught would see how precious the words of God are.

He also asked his friends in England to come and help him, and they did, women as well as men. One of them was a relative of his named Leoba, who led a house of nuns in Germany and was famous for being wise and kind.

When Boniface was very old, he went back to Frisia, the flat, watery land where he had failed the very first time. And there, one morning, near a place called Dokkum, some men came to attack the camp. Boniface told his friends not to fight. He died there, with them, and went to God. That is why we call him a martyr.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Boniface.

The first time he tried, it did not work. He could have stayed at home. But he tried again.

And when he met something that frightened people, he did not leave it there to frighten them. He turned it into something good, a place to pray.

Is there something that frightens you, at night? A shadow, or a noise, or a dream? You can do what Boniface did. You can say a prayer, right in the middle of the frightened feeling, and let God turn it into a quiet place. God made the trees, and the wind, and the night, and you, and he is not afraid of any of them.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Boniface, you tried and tried again,
> and you were not afraid of the old oak.
> Help me to be brave when things are hard,
> and to try again when I get it wrong.
> When I am frightened in the night,
> help me to remember that God is here.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Boniface** (born about 675, died 5 June 754) was an Anglo-Saxon monk of Wessex, baptised Wynfrith, who became the leading missionary and church organiser among the Germanic peoples east of the Rhine. Our main source is the *Life of Saint Boniface* by Willibald, a priest of Mainz, written within about ten or fifteen years of his death at the request of Bishop Lull, his successor. About 150 letters, by him, to him and about his work, also survive in the Boniface correspondence.

**What Willibald tells, and we have kept:** that as a small boy Wynfrith wanted to be a monk after visits from priests to his father's house, that his father at first refused and then relented; that he became a respected teacher at the monastery of Nursling, near Southampton (we have left out its name and that of Exeter, where he began); that his first journey to Frisia in 716 came to nothing because of the war between the Frisian ruler Radbod and Charles Martel, and he went home; that he went to Rome, where Pope Gregory II commissioned him to preach to the peoples of Germany (the Pope's letter of 15 May 719 is the first place the name Boniface appears); and the felling of the oak at Geismar, in Hesse, probably in 723, after he had been consecrated bishop. Willibald calls it the oak of Jupiter, a Latin writer's name for the thunder god, usually taken to be Donar (Thor). He says Boniface acted on the advice of converts, that a crowd of pagans stood by cursing, that after he had cut only a little "a great blast from above" brought the tree down and it broke into four parts of equal length, that many who watched believed, and that Boniface built from its timber an oratory dedicated to Saint Peter. Willibald presents the wind as God's doing; we have told it as his report.

**The Christmas tree.** A popular story says that a young fir tree grew up among the roots of the fallen oak, and that Boniface gave it to the people as the first Christmas tree. It has no early source; it appears in nineteenth-century retellings. We have left it out of the story.

**The rest of his life.** Boniface became archbishop in 732, founded or reformed many monasteries (among them Fulda, in 744), reorganised the churches of Bavaria and the Frankish kingdoms, and called men and women from England to help him; his kinswoman Leoba became abbess of Tauberbischofsheim and is a saint in her own right. His letter asking Abbess Eadburga to write out the epistles of Saint Peter in letters of gold survives; he wanted the Scriptures to be honoured when they were preached before people who did not yet believe. In 754, now very old, he went back to Frisia; on 5 June, at Dokkum, his camp was attacked by a band of raiders, and Willibald says he forbade his companions to fight and urged them to trust in God. He and about fifty companions were killed, and he was buried at Fulda. A later story, told in a Life written at Utrecht, says he held a book over his head as the blow fell, and a damaged book at Fulda has long been connected with it; Willibald does not tell this, so we have left it out. The year 754 is the one most historians give; some argue for 755.

**His feast** is 5 June, an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar. He is the patron saint of Germany, and is also remembered in the Netherlands and in England.

**What is ours:** the conversation with his father beyond Willibald's outline, the line about one foot in the church and one under the oak, the size of the trunk (Willibald says only that it was of extraordinary size), "chop, chop, chop", the wind in the leaves after it fell, the meaning given for the name Boniface (it is usually understood as "good deeds" or "doer of good", from Latin *bonum* and *facere*), the closing reflection and the prayer. We have kept his death to two sentences and given no detail of it. The prayer is not an approved text.

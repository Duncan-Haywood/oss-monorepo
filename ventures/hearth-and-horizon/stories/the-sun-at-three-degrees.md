Father Tomasz Wrona had been the priest at Selk Rim for nineteen years, and in all that time nobody at Camp Nine had ever asked for him.

So when the call came through on the sixth Earth day of the long dark, at a little after two in the morning by the settlement clock, and Paulo Ferreira's voice said, "Father, it's Henrik. He wants you. He says to tell you he knows what time it is," Tomasz sat up in bed, found his glasses, and said the only true thing he could think of.

"I know what time it is too, Paulo. Tell him I'm coming."

Then he lay back down for a moment with the handset on his chest, because he did not know how.

* * *

Selk Rim stood on the northern lip of a crater on Titan, seven degrees above the equator, a cluster of insulated domes and buried tanks that held a hundred and forty-one people, a print shop, a greenhouse that smelled always of tomatoes, and, under the smallest of the domes, a chapel the size of a generous kitchen. The first chaplain had dedicated it to Saint Joseph of Cupertino, the seventeenth-century friar whom tradition says rose off the ground at prayer, and who for that reason had been made a patron of people who fly. The founders had thought this was a joke. By Tomasz's time it was not, quite, because on Titan people did fly.

They flew on their own strength. That was the thing about the place that the brochures had always put first, before the lakes and the dunes and the orange sky. The gravity was a seventh of Earth's; the air, almost all nitrogen, was half as heavy again in pressure as Earth's at sea level and, at minus a hundred and seventy-nine, more than four times as dense. Put those together and a body with wings needed about a fortieth of the power it would need on Earth to stay up. The young people of Selk Rim strapped on frames of carbon and film, heated suits, a lamp, a radio, and went flapping out over the crater floor on Saturday afternoons like a flock of slow, bright, clumsy herons. The school taught it at fourteen. The best of them could cover twenty kilometres before lunch and come home hungry.

Nobody flew in the dark. And the dark, at Selk Rim, came all at once and for a long time, because Titan turned to face Saturn and kept its face there, and so its day was its orbit: very nearly sixteen Earth days from one sunrise to the next. In the northern winter, at seven degrees north, a little more than eight of those were night.

Camp Nine was eighteen kilometres to the north-east, as the herons flew, across the long parallel ridges of the Shangri-La dunes. It was a drill site: two habitats, a rig going down through the ice crust in search of the old sea, and two men who preferred it out there. Henrik Aalto was seventy-eight and had run drills on three moons. Paulo was half his age and had been sent, everyone understood, partly to learn the rig and partly to keep an eye on him.

Dr Ruth Abernethy had been on the line to Camp Nine since midnight. When Tomasz came into the operations dome at half past two, with his coat over his pyjamas, she put it to him without ornament.

"His heart is failing. It has been for a year and he wouldn't come in. Now his lungs are filling. I can talk Paulo through the drugs and we can buy time, but I can't tell you how much. Days, probably. Possibly less."

"Can he be moved?"

"Not by air. And we have no way to move him by ground." She looked at the operations board on the wall. "Ask Tern."

* * *

Tern was the settlement's operations system: the thing that ran the airlocks and the greenhouse light and the print queue, kept the crawler schedules and the flight log, and answered, on any screen, to its name. It had been built on Earth and shipped out eighty years before, and rebuilt in the settlement so many times since that the Earth company would not have recognised it. It spoke in a level, slightly dry voice that some of the children did impressions of.

"There are two crawlers," it said. "Crawler A is on the survey line to the western lakes, three hundred and twelve kilometres out. At its best speed in the dark it can be back here in about five days, and to Camp Nine about six hours after that. Crawler B is in the shop. Its front left drive motor lost a bearing housing on the last run. The housing was cast on Earth and we have no spare. Hamid can print one."

Hamid Sadeghi, the mechanic, had come in behind Tomasz with his hair standing up. "Thirty hours to print," he said. "Then I machine it, fit it, and cold-soak it outside for a test. Call it two days."

"And from here to Camp Nine by crawler?"

"Thirty-one kilometres by the corridors," said Tern. "The dunes run east to west, about a hundred metres high and a kilometre and a half apart. The crawler goes along the gaps between them and crosses where it must. About six hours."

Tomasz did the sum. Two days of printing and fitting; six hours of driving. Henrik might have two days. He might not.

"Somebody could fly it," said a voice from the door.

It was Nkiruka Obi, whom everyone called Kiki, twenty-six, the best flier in the settlement and the person who taught the fourteen-year-olds. She was also, for the last three years, the chapel's sacristan, and one of the two people Tomasz had asked the bishop on Ganymede to let him commission as an extraordinary minister of Holy Communion, so that he did not have to take the sacrament round the sick bay alone. She had heard the call on the general channel, as half the settlement had, and come straight over in her flying suit, which told Tomasz all he needed to know about how she had spent the last twenty minutes.

"The tandem rig," she said. "I fly students in it every term. Father sits in front and does nothing, which he's good at." She was not smiling, but it was close. "Eighteen kilometres. Seventy-five minutes, maybe eighty. Lamps."

"Tern," said Marguerite Lindqvist.

She was the settlement's safety officer, a tall grey woman from Gothenburg who had held the post for fourteen years, and she had come in last and stood at the back. She did not raise her voice; she never did.

"The tandem rig, with two adults in suits and the bigger wings, carries a little over twice the weight of Kiki's solo rig," said Tern. "The power to stay up goes as the weight to the power one and a half, so for the same wing it is about three times as much. The tandem's bigger wing gives some of that back. On her logs, Kiki can hold the tandem's cruise power for about twenty-five minutes. She has never held it for longer, and the flight to Camp Nine would take about seventy-five. Father Tomasz has never flown and cannot share the work. I do not think the tandem can reach Camp Nine. I think it would come down somewhere in the dunes between the fifth and the eighth kilometre."

Kiki said nothing. She had known it, Tomasz thought, before she said it; she had wanted someone else to say it out loud.

"Then I go alone," she said. "Not for the priest. For the sacrament. I take him Communion. Father can give me the pyx; that's what I'm commissioned for. Solo I can do eighteen kilometres in my sleep."

"Not in the dark," said Marguerite.

"I'll have the lamps."

"No."

It was not unkind. It was simply the end of the sentence. In the quiet that followed, Tern said, because nobody had asked it not to:

"The flight rule says no one flies with the Sun less than three degrees above the horizon. It was written in 2181, after the last flight in the dark."

Kiki had been fourteen in 2181. She had been in the class. Everyone in the room knew how that flight had ended, and against which dune face, and the boy's name, which was Daniel Achterberg; and the woman who had written the rule the next morning was standing at the back of the room.

"When is sunrise?" said Tomasz.

"About seventy-six hours from now," said Tern. "The Sun reaches three degrees about three and a half hours after that. Here it climbs a little under a degree an hour."

Eighty hours, near enough. Three days and a bit.

"Hamid," said Tomasz. "Print the housing."

* * *

He talked to Henrik at five in the morning, when the drugs had eased his breathing enough to manage a sentence at a time.

"You don't need to come," Henrik said. "I wanted to be sure you knew I asked. In case."

"I know you asked."

"Forty years, Father. Forty-one. My mother would be so pleased she'd be insufferable." A pause, a rattle. "Can you do it over this? The radio. Confession."

Tomasz had known the question would come and had already decided he would not make it easier than it was.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. Absolution needs me in the room with you. The Church has never accepted it at a distance, not by letter and not by telephone, and I'm not going to be the priest who decides otherwise on Titan because it would be convenient tonight."

"Huh," said Henrik. He sounded, very slightly, pleased; as if he had been testing a weld.

"But listen to me, because this part is true too. If you're sorry for what you've done because you love God, not only because you're frightened, and if you mean to confess it the first moment you can, then the Church teaches that you're forgiven now. Now, tonight, before I get there. It's in the Catechism, paragraph fourteen fifty-two; I'll send it to Paulo's screen. It isn't a loophole. It's what the sacrament was always for. The sacrament is the ordinary way, and I'll bring it. But God isn't waiting on Hamid's printer."

There was a long silence on the line, long enough that Tomasz looked at the board to see if it had dropped.

"I don't know if it's love," Henrik said at last. "I think it might mostly be fright."

"That's honest. Start there. Most of us do." Tomasz shifted the handset. "Shall we pray? You don't have to say anything. Paulo can hold the radio."

"Paulo," said Henrik, off the line, "hold the radio," and Paulo, who was from Porto and had been an altar server until he was twelve, held it, and Tomasz heard him join in on the Our Father, a half-beat behind, in Portuguese.

Afterwards Tomasz sat for a while in the empty operations dome. On the board, in the corner, Tern had put up a small grey number without being asked: the hours until the Sun would stand three degrees above the eastern rim. It read seventy-seven.

* * *

The first housing came out of the printer the next morning, a fat ring of aluminium alloy the size of a dinner plate, and Hamid machined it on the lathe through the day to the dimensions on the Earth drawing, and pressed the bearing in, and fitted the motor, and at four that afternoon he and Tern drove Crawler B out through the big lock onto the frozen crater floor, in the light of its own lamps, for the cold soak.

Forty minutes later the front left wheel stopped.

Hamid came back in an hour after that, white about the mouth, carrying the ruined housing in a bag. He put it on the operations table, between Marguerite's tea and the printout of paragraph fourteen fifty-two, which Tomasz had kept.

"It shrank," he said.

They looked at him.

"It shrank," Hamid said again, and then, because he was a fair man and the fault was not the cold's, "I made it shrink. Tern, show them."

"The original housing was steel," said Tern. "So is the bearing. The printer only has the aluminium alloy. Going from the shop at twenty degrees to the outside at minus a hundred and seventy-nine, the aluminium contracts about four parts in a thousand, and the bearing steel about two. The bore is a hundred and twenty millimetres across. So at the outside temperature the bore closed about a quarter of a millimetre more than the bearing it was holding."

"A quarter of a millimetre," said Kiki.

"A press fit is a few hundredths," said Hamid. "I gave it a quarter of a millimetre of squeeze. It crushed the outer race. I'm lucky it didn't crack the motor case." He rubbed his face. "I machined it to the Earth drawing. The Earth drawing was for steel. I knew that. I've known it for eleven years. I was tired."

"Can you do it again?" said Tomasz.

"Thirty hours to print. I machine the bore a quarter of a millimetre big, so it's loose in here, and fits out there. I have one spare bearing left of that size, and after that I don't. I fit it cold in the lock, not on the bench." He looked at the grey number on the board. It read forty-one. "It'll be about sunrise."

Kiki got up and went out without a word. Tomasz found her later, as he had expected to, in the chapel: not praying, or not in any way he could see, but sitting on the step below the little tabernacle with her arms round her knees and her flying suit still on.

"I could be there in an hour and a quarter," she said, without turning round.

"I know."

"He could die tonight."

"He could."

"And she won't." Kiki turned. "Do you think she's right?"

Tomasz sat down on the step beside her, which at seventy-one took some arranging.

"I think," he said, "that if I were Marguerite I'd want the rule to be wrong tonight more than anyone in this settlement, including you. And I think that's exactly why she won't bend it. She can't know whether tonight is the night it was written for. Neither can you. Neither can I."

"Tern could tell us the odds."

"Ask it."

She did, there, in the chapel, to the panel by the door.

"I can't give you a number I can check," said Tern. "I have one flight in the dark in the record. It ended at a dune face. One flight is not a rate. What I can tell you is what the lamps show: in this drizzle, with your lamp, about thirty metres of ground. At your cruise speed that is about seven seconds of warning. The dune faces on the way are about a hundred metres high, and some of the slip faces are steep."

Kiki was quiet for a long time.

"Seven seconds," she said.

"About."

She put her head down on her knees. After a while Tomasz began, quietly, the Angelus, because it was six o'clock in the evening by the clock that the settlement kept for itself, and there was nothing else of use to do; and after a while, not looking up, she answered.

* * *

The last day of the dark was the longest.

Henrik got worse in the morning and a little better in the afternoon, the way, Ruth said, they often do. He talked to Tomasz twice more. The second time, he said, "I've been thinking about it. The love and the fright. I think it might be about half and half now," and Tomasz, who had been a priest for forty-four years, said that was a better proportion than most of the people at Sunday Mass could claim, and Henrik laughed until he coughed and Paulo took the radio away.

In the evening Tomasz said Mass in the little chapel for anybody who wanted to come, and more came than could sit. He consecrated a large host and broke it, and when everyone had received he put the pieces into the small gilt pyx that had come out from Earth with the first chaplain, and put the pyx into the tabernacle, and left the little lamp burning beside it.

A little after midnight the second housing came off the printer. Hamid machined the bore a quarter of a millimetre oversize, and at four, wrapped in a suit and a heated blanket, he knelt in the big lock with the doors to the outside open, in the dark, and fitted the bearing into a ring that had already been out there for an hour and shrunk to meet it. He was out for three hours, and when he came back in he did not say anything, but he gave Tomasz a thumbs-up through the inner glass, and then sat down on the floor of the lock and stayed there for some time.

"Test run in an hour," said Tern. "Then Camp Nine. Six hours."

On the board, the grey number said three and a half.

* * *

Dawn on Titan is not a moment. It is a weather.

The haze that wraps the moon begins to glow hours before the Sun clears anything, so there is no edge to the night; the black sky goes to the deep rust of an old wound, then to a brown that is nearly purple, then, slowly, to the colour of strong tea held up to a window. There are no shadows for a long time. The dunes, when they come up out of the dark, come up as low grey shapes without any faces at all, and the eye cannot tell a near small one from a far great one. Even at noon the light on the ground is about a thousandth of an Earth noon: enough to work by, enough to read by, not much more.

Tern called sunrise at 07:04 by the settlement clock. Nobody saw it. There was nothing to see; the haze took the Sun before it could be a disc.

Crawler B passed its test run at 08:40, with Hamid at the wheel and the front left motor reading cold and true. At 08:55 it went out through the big lock and turned north-east into the first corridor between the dunes, its lamps still burning in the brown light, with Hamid driving and Tomasz beside him, in a borrowed suit, with his stole and the oil for the sick in a sealed case on his knees.

"Six hours," said Hamid, mostly to himself.

At 09:10 Ruth called the crawler. Henrik's breathing had changed. She thought hours now. She did not think six.

Tomasz listened, and thanked her, and then called the chapel.

Kiki was already there; she had been there since seven, in her suit, her wings racked by the inner lock. She had taken the pyx out of the tabernacle, as he had asked, and put it into the burse, and the burse into the warm inner pocket of her suit, over her heart, and had not sat down since.

"Tern," Tomasz said. "Where's the Sun?"

"One point eight degrees," said Tern.

There was a silence on the line in which Tomasz could hear Kiki breathing, and somewhere behind her, very faintly, the hum of the chapel's air.

"An hour and a half, near enough," said Marguerite. She was in the chapel too; he had not known that. "Then she goes. I'll sign it now."

"I know," said Kiki.

"I'll stand at the lock," said Marguerite. "Until then."

So they waited, the three of them, two in the chapel and one in a crawler grinding through the dunes, while the Sun went up a little under a degree an hour behind a sky like smoked glass. Tern did not count it aloud. It did not need to. Every quarter of an hour the grey number on every screen in the settlement changed, and by nine-thirty there were people standing in front of the screens in the greenhouse and the canteen and the school, not saying much, the way people stand at a window in a hospital.

At 10:34 the number said three point zero.

"Go," said Marguerite, and opened the lock herself.

* * *

Kiki flew low and fast and straight, which every instructor in the settlement, Kiki included, told students never to do.

She took off from the flat below the lock with a run and a heave, and felt, as she always did, the air take her weight as if it had been waiting for it: thick, cold, patient air, through which the long wings went down and back and down again with the soft regular sound of someone shaking out a sheet. The settlement's domes fell away behind her into the brown. The first dune came up out of the haze like the back of a sleeping animal, grey and faceless, and she climbed over it with fifteen metres to spare and saw beyond it the next one, and the next, ranked to the edge of the world, each one a hundred metres high and a kilometre and a half from its neighbour, the long low swells of a sea that seemed to have stopped moving.

There were shadows now. Faint ones, long, laid out to the west. Three degrees of Sun was enough to give every slip face a darker side, and that was all the rule had ever asked for: that a flier could tell a slope from a sky. She could. She thought about seven seconds and did not think about it again.

She flew the way she had told Daniel Achterberg to fly, when she was fourteen and he was fifteen and she had thought she knew everything: watch the next crest, not the one under you. Breathe on the downstroke. Don't fight the air; it's on your side.

Halfway, in the trough of the ninth dune, the methane drizzle came on, the slow rain of Titan, big drops that drifted down past her visor so leisurely that she could have counted them, and the brown light went dimmer, and she flew lower. She passed over Crawler B in the eleventh corridor, a small hard light grinding along the dune foot, and heard Hamid on the channel say, "There she goes," and Tomasz say nothing at all, and then something under his breath, which she thought was a blessing and later found out was.

At 11:52 she saw the lamps of Camp Nine.

* * *

Henrik Aalto had been propped up on three pillows and the drill-shack's one good cushion, with the oxygen on, and the bunk light low. He was conscious. He knew her: everyone at Selk Rim knew Kiki, and he had seen her flying on the long days, from the rig, he said, and thought she was showing off.

"I am," she said. "I was. Father's coming. He's three hours out, in the crawler. He sent me ahead with Our Lord."

Henrik looked at her for a long moment with his pale, wet, clever engineer's eyes.

"Viaticum," he said. "I remember the word. My mother."

"Yes."

She did it as Tomasz had taught her, and as the book said; she had read it again on the screen in the chapel at six in the morning, twice, so as not to have to look at it now. She set the burse on the clean cloth Paulo had put out on the drill shack's folding table, beside a little battery lamp he had found somewhere and set where a candle would have gone, because there was oxygen in the room and no candle was allowed near it. She opened the pyx. Paulo knelt down on the steel floor without being asked.

She held up a small piece of the host, as small as she could break it, because Ruth had said he could manage a little and a sip of water and not much more.

"The body of Christ," she said.

"Amen," said Henrik.

"May the Lord Jesus Christ protect you," said Kiki, "and lead you to eternal life."

He received. It took him some time. Paulo held the water. Then Henrik lay back on the pillows with his eyes closed and his mouth working, a little, the way a man's does when he is praying and has not done it for forty-one years and is no longer sure where the words go; and after a while it stopped working, and he slept.

Kiki sat down on the floor by the bunk, in her suit, with her back against the wall. She found that her hands were shaking and that her legs would not hold her, and that she did not mind at all.

* * *

Henrik Aalto died at 13:40 by the settlement clock, quietly, in his sleep, with Paulo holding one hand and Kiki the other. He did not wake again. Tomasz, on the radio from the crawler, said the prayers of commendation, the old ones, *Go forth, Christian soul, from this world,* and Kiki and Paulo said the responses, and Ruth on the other line said them too, though she was not, as far as anyone knew, a believer.

Crawler B came into Camp Nine at 15:10, an hour and a half too late. Tomasz climbed down from it slowly, like an old man, which he was, and came in through the lock with the oil for the sick still sealed in its case, and stood in the doorway of the drill shack and looked at the bunk for some time.

He did not anoint him. The Church does not anoint the dead, and Tomasz did not pretend otherwise; he had said so, to Henrik, two nights before, when Henrik had asked what would happen if he didn't make it, and Henrik had said, "Then don't wave that oil at me afterwards like a consolation prize," and they had both laughed. He prayed instead the prayers the Church gives for the dead, and blessed the body, and then sat down on the drill shack's one good chair, beside Kiki on the floor.

"He got half and half," he said, after a while. "Love and fright. He told me on the radio last night. Half and half."

"Is that enough?"

"I think it was more than half by the end," said Tomasz. "I think you saw the end, and I didn't. But I'm not the judge of that. Thank God."

Outside, the drizzle had stopped. The Sun, by Tern's reckoning, stood seven degrees above the eastern rim; nobody could see it, but the long grey shadows of the dunes had shortened a little, and turned, the way a crowd turns its head.

Kiki got up. She went out through the lock and stood on the ice beside her racked wings, and did not fly. After a while Paulo came out and stood beside her. Neither of them said anything. From inside, very faintly, came Tomasz's voice, beginning the Angelus, because it was noon somewhere, and on Titan, for the next seven days and more, it would be day.

*This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Selk Rim Settlement, Camp Nine, the chapel and everyone in the story are invented; Selk crater, the Shangri-La dune field and the fact that Titan keeps one face to Saturn are real. Saint Joseph of Cupertino (1603 to 1663) is a real saint, venerated as a patron of air travellers; the accounts of his rising at prayer are tradition, and the story treats them so. Human-powered flight on Titan is the story's speculative step: no one has done it, and the strap-on wing rig, its 75-minute crossing and Kiki's endurance figures are invented. The physics around it was worked with a short script from figures taken from memory: surface gravity 1.352 m/s² (0.138 of Earth's), surface pressure 1.467 bar at 93.7 K (about minus 179 °C), which as an ideal gas of 95% nitrogen and 5% methane gives about 5.2 kg/m³, 4.2 times Earth's sea-level 1.225 (published figures nearer 5.4 allow for the cold gas not being ideal). The power to hold a given wing in level flight scales as weight to the 1.5 power over the square root of air density, which for the same craft and mass gives about 0.025 of the Earth figure, "about a fortieth". A tandem carrying 225 kg against a solo 105 kg is 2.14 times the weight and, for the same wing, 3.1 times the power. Titan's orbit around Saturn takes 15.945 days, so its solar day is about 15.97 Earth days and the Sun moves 0.94 degrees an hour across its sky. At 7° N, assuming the Sun's declination near minus 24° in the northern winter (Titan's equator is tilted about 26.7° to its orbit round the Sun; the dates of Saturn's seasons in 2193 were extrapolated from the 2025 equinox), night lasts about 8.3 Earth days, the Sun rises at about 0.85 degrees an hour, and reaches three degrees about 3.5 hours after sunrise. Sunlight at Saturn is about a ninetieth of Earth's and the haze lets through roughly a tenth of that, so "about a thousandth of an Earth noon" is an estimate. The thermal contraction figures (aluminium alloy about 0.4% and bearing steel about 0.2% from 20 °C to minus 179 °C) are approximate textbook values; across a 120 mm bore the difference is about 0.23 mm, against a press fit of a few hundredths of a millimetre. Absolution at a distance: the Church does not accept confession by telephone or radio. The teaching that perfect contrition, with the resolve to confess when possible, obtains forgiveness is the Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 1452, paraphrased. A lay extraordinary minister may bring Viaticum to the dying in the absence of a priest (Code of Canon Law, canon 911 §2); the anointing of the sick is reserved to priests and is not given to the dead. The words of Viaticum are those of the Church's rite of Communion for the dying as it is given in English; "Go forth, Christian soul" opens the traditional prayer of commendation of the dying. The bishop on Ganymede is invented.*

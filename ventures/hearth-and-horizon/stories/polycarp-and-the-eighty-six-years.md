summary: A bedtime story about Saint Polycarp, the old bishop of Smyrna who as a young man had listened to the apostle John. A boy called Irenaeus remembers where he sat and how he talked. When soldiers come to the farm to arrest him, Polycarp says "The will of God be done", gives them supper and asks for an hour to pray, and before the governor he will not speak against Jesus, whom he has served for eighty-six years. Told from the Martyrdom of Polycarp, with a note for grown-ups on what the letter says, the dove and the date. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud.
---

Polycarp was a very old man, and he had a very good memory.

He could remember things from long, long ago, from before your grandma was born, and before her grandma was born, and before *her* grandma was born. And the best thing he remembered was this: when he was young, he had sat and listened to John.

Not just any John. John, the friend of Jesus. John, who had been in the boat on the lake, and at the table at the Last Supper, and at the empty tomb on Easter morning.

* * *

Polycarp lived in a city called Smyrna, by the sea. Smyrna had a harbour full of ships with big square sails, and a market full of figs and fish, and streets that went up a hill.

Polycarp was the bishop there. That means he looked after all the people in Smyrna who loved Jesus, like a shepherd looks after sheep.

Every week the people came to hear him. And among them was a boy called Irenaeus.

Irenaeus loved listening to Polycarp. He sat very still. He watched the old bishop's face and his hands. He listened to the way he talked.

"When I was a young man," Polycarp would say, "I knew John. And John told me what Jesus said, and what Jesus did, because John was *there*. He saw it with his own eyes."

And then Polycarp would tell them. About the loaves and the fishes. About the sick people Jesus made well. About the cross, and the morning when the tomb was empty.

Irenaeus kept every word in his heart. Long, long after, when Irenaeus was grown up and a bishop himself, far away in another country, he wrote it all down. He wrote that he could still remember the very place where Polycarp used to sit when he talked, and how he came in and went out, and what he looked like. He had not written it on paper when he was a boy. He had kept it in his heart.

Can you remember where your grandpa sits? Or your favourite teacher? That is how Irenaeus remembered Polycarp.

* * *

Now, in those days, there were people in charge who did not like Christians at all.

They wanted everyone to say, "Caesar is Lord." Caesar was the emperor, the great ruler in Rome. And they wanted everyone to burn a little pinch of sweet incense for him, as if he were a god.

But Christians cannot say that. For Christians, there is only one Lord, and his name is Jesus.

So the people in charge began to look for the Christians. And most of all, they looked for the old bishop.

"Please," said Polycarp's friends, "go and hide. Go out of the city, where they cannot find you."

Polycarp did not really want to go. But his friends begged him, so he went to a little farm in the countryside, not far away.

And what do you think he did there?

He prayed. Day and night, he prayed. Not just for himself. He prayed for everybody: for the people of Smyrna, and for all the Christians everywhere in the whole world.

* * *

One Friday evening, just as it was time for supper, there was a noise outside the farm.

*Clip-clop, clip-clop, clip-clop.*

Horses. Soldiers on horses, with their swords and their spears, as if they had come to catch a dangerous robber.

But they had come for one very old man.

Polycarp was upstairs. He could have climbed out and run away across the fields. But he did not. He said:

"The will of God be done."

That means: whatever God wants, that is what I want too.

And he went downstairs, slowly, the way old men go down stairs, and he opened the door, and he talked with the soldiers.

The soldiers looked at him. They had expected someone big and fierce. Instead here was a little old man with white hair and a kind face, who was not frightened of them at all.

"All this fuss," some of them whispered, "to catch an old man like *him*?"

* * *

Then Polycarp did something very surprising.

"You have come a long way," he said. "You must be hungry."

And he told his friends to set the table. Bread, and food, and something to drink, as much as the soldiers wanted. The very men who had come to take him away, and Polycarp gave them supper.

That is what Jesus said to do. "Love your enemies. Do good to those who hate you." Polycarp had heard it from John, and he had believed it all his life, and now he did it.

While the soldiers ate, Polycarp asked them one thing.

"Will you give me an hour to pray?"

The soldiers had their mouths full. They looked at each other. "Yes," they said. "An hour."

So Polycarp stood up, and he prayed.

He prayed for everybody he had ever met in his long, long life: the big people and the little people, the important people and the people nobody noticed. He prayed for the whole Church all over the world.

He had so much to pray about that the hour went by, and he was still praying. And another hour went by, and he was *still* praying. Two whole hours!

And the soldiers, sitting at his table with their bowls and their bread, listened. And some of them felt very sorry that they had come to take away such a good old man.

* * *

When at last he had finished, they put him on a donkey and took him into the city.

On the way, two important men came along in a fine carriage, and they took Polycarp up into it beside them.

"Come now," they said, in their friendliest voices. "What harm is there in saying 'Caesar is Lord'? Just say it, and burn a little incense, and you can go home."

Polycarp was quiet for a while. Then he said, "I am not going to do what you tell me."

The men were cross. They pushed him out of the carriage, and he scraped his leg. But he got up, and he walked on, quickly and cheerfully, as if nothing had happened at all.

* * *

They brought him to a big open place where a great crowd had come to see. There was lots of shouting.

But the letter that the Christians of Smyrna wrote afterwards says that just then, as Polycarp walked in, some of his friends heard a voice from heaven, saying:

"Be strong, Polycarp. Be brave."

And Polycarp was.

The governor was waiting. He looked at Polycarp's white hair.

"Think of your age," said the governor. "You are an old man. Just say something bad about Christ, and I will let you go."

And Polycarp gave him an answer that people have remembered ever since. Nearly two thousand years, and they still remember it.

"For eighty-six years I have served him," said Polycarp, "and he has never done me any wrong. How could I speak against my King, who saved me?"

Eighty-six years! That is a very, very long time. All that time, Polycarp had loved Jesus and worked for Jesus. And all that time, Jesus had been good to him. Every single day. Why would he stop now?

"I am a Christian," said Polycarp.

And that day, the old bishop gave his life for Jesus.

* * *

Afterwards, his friends wrote a long letter to tell all the other Christians what had happened. It is one of the oldest letters about a saint that we have.

They wrote that Polycarp had been a good teacher and a true friend of Jesus. They wrote that they would come together every year on the day he died, not to be sad, but to be glad, and to remember him. That day is the 23rd of February.

And the boy Irenaeus? He grew up and became a bishop too, a long way from Smyrna. And he never forgot the old man who had sat and talked about John.

That is how it works, you see. John listened to Jesus. Polycarp listened to John. Irenaeus listened to Polycarp. And somebody listened to Irenaeus, and somebody listened to them, all the way down to the person who is reading you this story tonight. And now you are listening too.

* * *

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think of the old bishop at the farm, with the soldiers eating supper at his table, and Polycarp standing by the window, praying for everybody in the whole world.

Jesus has been good to you, too. Every day of your life. You could tell him so.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear Jesus, you have been good to me
> every single day.
> Help me to love you, like Polycarp,
> all my life long,
> and to be kind even to people who are not kind to me.
> Thank you for everyone who told me about you.
> Saint Polycarp, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our sources.** The arrest and death are told from the **Martyrdom of Polycarp**, a letter from "the church of God which sojourns at Smyrna" to the church at Philomelium, written soon after the event and generally reckoned the oldest surviving account of a Christian martyrdom outside the New Testament. Eusebius quotes most of it in his *Ecclesiastical History* (4.15). Polycarp's link with John comes from **Saint Irenaeus**: *Against Heresies* 3.3.4, where he says Polycarp "was instructed by apostles, and conversed with many who had seen Christ" and that he himself saw Polycarp "in my early youth"; and his **letter to Florinus**, preserved by Eusebius (5.20), where he remembers, as a boy in Asia, "the very place in which the blessed Polycarp used to sit when he discoursed", his comings and goings, his appearance, and the accounts he gave of his time "with John and with the others who had seen the Lord", kept "not on paper but in my heart". We have used the standard nineteenth-century English translations (Lightfoot's of the *Martyrdom*; those in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* and the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* for Irenaeus and Eusebius) and the English Wikipedia and *Catholic Encyclopedia* articles on Polycarp. The session that wrote this could not open those texts online to check them word by word, so **the spoken lines in the story are our own close renderings, not quotations**. The few short phrases in quotation marks in this note are given from memory of those translations; we have kept quotation marks only where we are confident of the wording and paraphrased everything else. A reader who finds one that strays is asked to say so.

**What the letter says, and what the story keeps.** Everything in the bedside story that happens to Polycarp is in the letter, in the same order: his wish to stay in the city and his friends persuading him out to a farm not far off (chapter 5); his praying night and day for everyone and for the churches throughout the world; the officers who came on horseback, "as against a robber", on the Friday at supper time; his refusal to slip away, "The will of God be done"; their surprise at his age and calm; the table he had laid for them, with as much to eat and drink as they wanted; his request for an hour to pray, and his praying for two hours, so that many were sorry they had come against so godly an old man (chapter 7); his remembering in that prayer everyone who had ever come his way, small and great, high and low, and the whole Catholic Church throughout the world (chapter 8, one of the earliest uses of that phrase, which also appears in the letter's opening greeting); the ride into the city on a donkey; the two officials, Herod the captain of police and his father Nicetes, who took him into their carriage and asked him what harm there was in saying "Caesar is Lord" and offering incense; his being pushed out and scraping his shin, and walking on eagerly as though nothing had happened. In the stadium the letter reports a voice from heaven that the Christians present heard, "Be strong, Polycarp, and play the man" (chapter 9), which we have softened to "be brave" and told as the letter's report. The governor's "have respect to thy age" and his demand that Polycarp revile Christ, and Polycarp's answer, that he had served Christ eighty-six years and been done no wrong, so how could he blaspheme his King who saved him, are in chapter 9; "I am a Christian" is chapter 10. "Love your enemies" is Matthew 5:44 and Luke 6:27; the letter does not quote it at the supper, and the link is ours.

**What the story leaves out.** The letter says that three days before his arrest Polycarp dreamed of his pillow on fire and understood how he would die; that a slave boy was made to tell the soldiers where he was; that the crowd shouted for a lion and then for fire; and that he was burned. It tells three wonders of his death: that the flames stood round him "like the sail of a vessel filled by the wind", that there was a sweet smell like frankincense or precious spices, and that when the fire did not consume him an executioner stabbed him. The manuscripts then add that "a dove" came out with his blood; this is missing from the text Eusebius quotes, and many scholars think it a later addition or a copying slip (one old guess is that a Greek phrase meaning "about the haft" was miscopied as "a dove"). The bedside story gives his death one sentence, as this shelf's *Lawrence* and *Lucy* do, and tells none of these.

**How old he was, and when.** "Eighty-six years" may count from his birth (if he was raised a Christian) or from his baptism, so Polycarp was at least 86. The letter dates his death to the second day of the month Xanthicus, "the seventh day before the kalends of March", on "a great Sabbath", at the eighth hour, under the proconsul Statius Quadratus (chapter 21). The seventh day before the kalends of March is 23 February. Most scholars, working from the dates of Quadratus's term as proconsul of Asia, put the year at 155 or 156 (23 February 155 was a Saturday, which fits "Sabbath"); others, following Eusebius's *Chronicle*, put it in the 160s; the story says "about the year 155". What "a great Sabbath" meant is not certain.

**Irenaeus as a boy in Smyrna.** Irenaeus says only that he was a boy "in lower Asia" with Polycarp; that this was at Smyrna is the usual reading, and the story assumes it. He later became bishop of Lyons, in Gaul (France), where he wrote *Against Heresies* around 180. That he listened every week, sat very still and watched Polycarp's hands is ours; the place where Polycarp sat, his comings and goings and his talk of John are his. That Polycarp was made bishop of Smyrna by apostles is Irenaeus's claim (*Against Heresies* 3.3.4); Tertullian says John appointed him. Polycarp's own **Letter to the Philippians** survives, and Saint Ignatius of Antioch wrote him a letter on his way to Rome; neither appears in the story.

**The feast.** **23 February** is the **memorial of Saint Polycarp, bishop and martyr**, in the General Roman Calendar; before the 1969 reform of the calendar it was kept on 26 January. When 23 February falls in Lent, as it often does, the memorial may be kept only as a commemoration. The letter itself (chapter 18) says the church of Smyrna meant to gather each year to "celebrate the birthday of his martyrdom", one of the earliest witnesses to keeping a saint's day.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene; "before your grandma was born"; the list of things John had seen; the harbour, the ships with square sails, the figs and fish and the hill; Irenaeus's place in the congregation and how he listened; the question about where grandpa sits; the clip-clop of the horses, swords and spears; Polycarp going slowly down the stairs, his white hair and kind face; "You must be hungry"; the soldiers with their mouths full; the bowls; the officials' friendly voices and Polycarp's short reply (the letter says he first did not answer, then told them he would not do what they advised); the line of listeners from Jesus to the child; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

---

Maria do Céu Furtado had made four hundred and twelve joints in submarine cable, and until October she could have told you where every one of them was.

She could still tell you most of them. That was the thing the young doctor in Ponta Delgada had been careful to say, sitting across from her with the results on a screen she was not shown: *most*, and *for now*, and *we will watch it together*. Maria had thanked her and taken the ferry home and gone into her kitchen and put her nineteen notebooks on the table, one for every two or three years from 1998 to 2041, and looked at them for a long time without opening any of them.

On Monday the twenty-fifth of January 2049, at a little after a quarter past three in the morning, the island of Bruma lost its cable.

Nobody on Bruma noticed for some hours, because at a quarter past three nobody on Bruma was awake except Senhor Avelar at the bakery and the cows. At six the cooperative's network assistant switched the island to the satellite backup, which carried the hospital, the school, the fish auction and the harbour, and very little else. By eight the café knew. By nine Maria's great-nephew Tiago Medeiros, who ran the network for the Cooperativa da Bruma and had not slept, was on her doorstep with two coffees in paper cups and the face he used to have at nine years old when he had broken something.

"It's the cable," he said.

"I know it's the cable. Avelar told me with the bread." She took one of the coffees. "Where?"

"Three hundred and eighteen kilometres out. Ish. Vigia's working on it." He followed her into the kitchen, saw the notebooks still stacked on the table where they had been since October, and did not say anything about them. "The ship's coming from Lisbon. The *Maré Cheia*. Thursday morning, if the weather holds."

"Who's the superintendent?"

"Somebody called Okafor."

"I don't know her." Maria sat down. Twenty years ago she would have known everyone; the repair ships in the Atlantic had been a small world, and she had been, for a while, the best universal jointer in it, or at least the one who complained least about the coffee. "Three hundred and eighteen. That's deep water. Past the seamount. Four kilometres, more."

"Four thousand two hundred, give or take," Tiago said. "Vigia says."

"Then they'll need two days to get the ends up, and a day for the joints, if they're good, and a day for the bight." She drank. "And a sea that lets them."

"The forecast says a gale Monday night."

"Then they'll want to be quick." She looked at him. "What's wrong? You didn't come here at nine in the morning to tell me the cable's broken. You came to ask me something."

Tiago put his cup down on the stack of notebooks, then thought better of it and moved it to the table.

"The two ends don't agree," he said.

* * *

The cable ran from the landing station below the lighthouse on Bruma, down the steep volcanic flank of the island and out across the deep floor of the Atlantic, six hundred and forty kilometres to São Miguel, where it came ashore at a station that belonged to someone else and joined the rest of the world. It had been laid in 2029. Maria had been on the ship, a jointer of fifty-three with a bad knee, and she had made the shore-end joints herself, because it was her island.

It carried light. Every forty or fifty kilometres a repeater, a steel can the size of a man, amplified the light along its way, and could be asked, from either shore, to send a little of the light back. That was how you watched a cable you could not see: you sent a pulse down the fibre and listened to the faint glow scattered back from the glass itself, and the glow told you, kilometre by kilometre, how clear the fibre was, and where it ended.

On Monday morning, from Bruma, the fibre ended at 318.7 kilometres.

"From São Miguel," Tiago said, "it ends at 330.7."

Maria added them in her head. "Six hundred and forty-nine point four."

"Which is what Vigia measured end to end in 2045, when it ran the baseline. So the measurements agree with each other. The cable's six hundred and forty-nine point four kilometres long, and it's broken at one point, and both ends see the same point." He took out his tablet and turned it round. "But the route position list says the cable's six hundred and forty."

The route position list was the cable's map: for every metre of cable paid out over the stern in 2029, where on the seabed it had gone down. You could not find a fault with a distance alone. A distance was a length of cable, and a length of cable became a place only through the list.

"If the extra nine point four is between us and the break," Tiago said, "then the break is at three hundred and nine point three on the list. If it's beyond the break, on the São Miguel side, then it's at three hundred and eighteen point seven. Those two places are nine kilometres apart on the seabed. Four kilometres down."

"And Vigia doesn't know which."

"Vigia says it can't tell from the data it's got. It's asked São Miguel for their records, but they only have their own end." He swiped. "And Atlântida's records went with Atlântida."

Atlântida Telecom had owned the cable until it went bankrupt in 2043. The cooperative had bought it from the liquidator in 2044, for less than a fishing boat, with every family on the island putting in a share, and the liquidator had sent over a hard drive with the route position list, the as-laid charts and the repeater data on it, and had not sent anything else, because nothing else had been asked for, and the rest had gone wherever the records of a bankrupt company go.

"Okafor's going to split it," Tiago said. "She told me on the call last night. If she can't tell which end is right, she grapples in the middle. Three hundred and fourteen. Then she's never more than four and a half kilometres wrong."

"That's what I would do," Maria said, "if I didn't know."

He looked at her.

"There was a repair," she said. "In 2031. A turbidity current came down off the seamount in the spring and broke it, two years after we laid it, and the *Ilha Verde* went out. I was on her. I made both joints." She put her hand flat on the stack of notebooks. "That's your nine kilometres. That's mine."

* * *

The trouble was that she could not say where.

She could say a great deal. She could say that it had been May, that the sea had been short and nasty, that the spare cable on the *Ilha Verde* had not been Atlântida's cable at all but the other company's, from another system the ship looked after, so that both joints had been universal joints, the kind that marry one maker's cable to another's, and slow. She could say that the test engineer had been a Scot called Duncan who whistled. She could say the depth, because she remembered thinking that it was nearly four kilometres, *três mil oitocentos e tal*, and that the bight they laid at the end had been enormous, because in deep water the final joint has to be lifted to the deck from the bottom on two ends at once and then lowered again, and the extra cable that takes lies down there for ever in a long loop on the seabed, two or three times as long as the water is deep.

She could not say how far out it had been. Past the seamount, she thought. Before the first deep repeater. Somewhere about two hundred.

"About," Tiago said.

"About." She heard her own voice. "Tiago, I'm seventy-three, and in October a doctor told me a word I'm not going to say in front of my coffee. I remember the Scot whistling. I don't know if I remember the kilometre or if I remember wanting to."

"It'll be in the notebook."

"It will be in the 2031 notebook," Maria said, "which is not on this table."

They counted them twice. Nineteen notebooks, one gap: there was a blue one that ran from 2027 to the end of 2030, and a red one that began in 2032. Between them, nothing.

She looked in the drawer of the dresser and in the trunk at the end of her bed and in the box under the stairs with her husband's fishing licences, and on top of the wardrobe, and in the pantry, which made no sense, and Tiago went through the car. She stood in the middle of the kitchen at half past eleven with her hands at her sides and could not think where it might be, and the not-thinking was a new kind of not-thinking that she had only met since the autumn: a smooth white wall where a door should be.

"Leave it," she said. "It's gone. Tell Okafor what I said, and let her decide."

* * *

Tiago called the ship from the cooperative's office at five on Wednesday afternoon, with Vigia on the line and the chart of the route on the big screen, and told Joanna Okafor what his great-aunt had said.

The repair superintendent of the *Maré Cheia* was a tall woman of about forty-five with a London accent and a grey fleece with the ship's name on it, and she listened to the whole thing without interrupting, which Tiago had not expected.

"I believe there was a repair," she said, when he had finished. "That's the most likely explanation for nine point four kilometres. It isn't the only one."

"What else?" Tiago said.

"A survey error in the 2029 list, which happens. The fibre being longer inside the cable than anyone wrote down, which happens. Or a repair on the São Miguel side that your great-aunt wasn't on." She leaned towards her camera. "Mr. Medeiros, I'm not doubting her. I'm telling you what it costs me to be wrong. I've got twenty-four kilometres of your type of cable on board. A clean repair in four thousand two hundred metres takes about eleven or twelve of that. If I cut nine kilometres off the break, I spend a day picking up the cable between, and I replace every metre I pick up, and I come home with almost nothing in the tank for the next fault in this zone, which might be yours again, or might be somebody's hospital in the Canaries. If I cut in the middle, the worst I can do is four and a half. That's a bad day. Nine is a very bad week."

"And if she's right," Tiago said, "the middle is still four and a half wrong."

"Yes." Joanna Okafor did not look away from the camera. "I'll take a written record over a memory, and a measurement over either. I'm sorry. If you find me one by noon tomorrow, I'll listen. The grapnel goes over the side at first light, and it takes about four hours to get to the bottom."

Vigia, which had been silent through the call, spoke in the voice it used for the board: even, slightly formal, Portuguese-accented English.

"Superintendent, may I add something to the record?"

"Go ahead."

"The middle is the right choice on what I have given you," Vigia said. "I would make it too. I also want it noted that I cannot tell you what is in the cable between kilometre zero and the break. I measured the whole length in 2045 and wrote down what I could explain. I did not write down what I could not."

There was a short pause on the ship's end.

"That's an unusually honest thing for a monitoring system to say," Joanna said.

"It is what my notes say," said Vigia. "I am reading them now."

* * *

At half past ten on Thursday morning, Padre Anselmo Teves rang Maria's doorbell with a cardboard box under his arm and the expression of a man who has found something in his sacristy that he does not understand.

He was a thin, untidy priest from Terceira, sixty years old, who had been on Bruma for nine years and still called it "the new parish". He set the box on her kitchen table, between the notebooks and the sugar bowl. Inside were three shepherds from the Christmas crib, a donkey with one ear, the plaster angel that hung over the stable, wrapped in a tea towel, and underneath them all, in a plastic sleeve gone cloudy with age, a grey notebook with *2031* written on the cover in Maria's hand.

"Tiago told Rosário at the café," the priest said, "and Rosário told me at the eight o'clock, and I remembered that in 2039 we did the exhibition. For the tenth anniversary of the cable. In the parish hall. You lent us things." He looked apologetic. "Nobody gave them back. It was put away with the crib. I am so sorry."

Maria did not say anything for a moment.

"The exhibition," she said. "Yes. I lent you my notebook from the repair, because the young ones wanted to see the drawing of the joint. And the medal from the laying. And —" She stopped. "I'd forgotten I lent it. I forgot that ten years ago, Padre. That isn't the illness. That's just me."

"Then that's a mercy," said Padre Anselmo.

She took the notebook out of its sleeve and turned the pages carefully, because the pencil had softened, past a list of tools and a sketch of a joint housing and a shopping list for Ponta Delgada, to May.

*14 May 2031. Ilha Verde. Brumalink seg. 2. Prof. 3880. UJ no. 1 to the other house's cable, their fibre, not ours. 07:40 to 19:10. Pos.* and then a latitude and longitude, in degrees and minutes, the way the bridge had read them out to her.

*16 May. Final. UJ no. 2. Bad sea. Bight long, about 10 and a half all in. Duncan says the two fibres are not the same size and the trace will show it: going in, from either end, it will look like a step UP, not down. Not a fault. Tell whoever comes after.*

She read it aloud. Then she read it again, because the second time she was listening to it.

"Tell whoever comes after," Padre Anselmo said.

"I wrote that," said Maria, "for nobody. You write things like that in a notebook so that you have written them." She stood up, too fast, and held the edge of the table. "What time is it?"

"Twenty to eleven."

"Ring Tiago."

* * *

"A step up," said Vigia.

It was eleven fifteen. The *Maré Cheia* was steaming slowly along the line at kilometre three hundred and fourteen, with grapnel rope going down over her bow, more than three kilometres of it by now, and Tiago had put his great-aunt in the chair in front of the big screen in the cooperative's office, because she would not sit anywhere else, and the priest was standing behind her holding the box with the donkey in it, because nobody had told him to put it down.

"Does that mean anything to you?" Maria said. She had not spoken to Vigia before. She had not really spoken to any of the assistants; she had retired in 2041, when they were still the kind that answered the telephone.

"Yes," Vigia said. "I have two events in the 2045 baseline that I did not explain. One at 199.8 kilometres from this end: an apparent gain of eleven hundredths of a decibel. The other at 210.3 kilometres: a loss of twenty-nine hundredths. Neither matched a repeater or a factory joint on the list. Both were below the threshold for action. I recorded the first as a probable instrument artefact, because a passive fibre cannot amplify light, and the second as minor, to be watched. I watched it. It did not change."

"It can't amplify light," Maria said. "But it can look as if it does."

"Yes. I know that now. I should have known it then. When two fibres with different core sizes are joined, the smaller one gives back a larger share of the scattered light, so going from the larger into the smaller, the trace jumps upward, and going back, it falls further than the joint really loses. From one end only, it looks like a gain and then a bad joint. It is called a gainer." It paused, very briefly. "In 2045 I knew what a gainer was. I did not ask whether there was one."

"Two of them," said Maria, "facing each other."

"That is the next thing," said Vigia. "If your notebook is right, São Miguel's trace should show the same: going into your repair from their end, a step up. I have asked them for their 2045 baseline. I am also converting the latitude and longitude in your notebook to the route."

They waited. Tiago found he was holding his breath, and let it out.

"Your notebook's position for the first joint," Vigia said, "is 0.6 kilometres from the route, at about kilometre 199.5 on the list. The ship drifts while a joint is made. That is close." Another pause. "The two events are 10.5 kilometres of cable apart. You wrote *about ten and a half all in*. And in 3,880 metres of water, ten and a half kilometres is 2.7 times the depth, which is inside what repairs in deep water use." A longer pause. "São Miguel has answered. Their trace, from their end, has an apparent gain of nine hundredths of a decibel at 439.1 kilometres. That is 210.3 from here. A step up, going in."

Nobody said anything.

"If I average the two directions," said Vigia, "the true loss of each joint comes out at about nine hundredths of a decibel. That is a good joint."

Maria put both hands over her mouth. Behind her, Padre Anselmo shifted the box to one arm and put the other hand on her shoulder.

"I'm going to tell the ship," said Tiago.

* * *

Joanna Okafor came on the line at eleven thirty-four, from the bridge, with wind noise behind her.

"Tell me in one sentence."

"The nine point four kilometres is a 2031 repair at kilometre two hundred," Vigia said, "with two universal joints that show as gainers from both ends, 10.5 kilometres of cable apart, matching a position and a length in a notebook written on the day; so the São Miguel measurement is the one to use, and the break is at 309.3 on the route position list."

"That was a long sentence."

"I was asked for one."

There was a sound on the line that might have been a laugh. "Send me both traces, the São Miguel one and yours. Send me a photograph of the notebook page. Give me ten minutes."

She took fourteen. In the cooperative's office, Maria sat with the grey notebook open on her knee and did not read it.

"Here's what it costs," Joanna said, when she came back. "The grapnel is three thousand four hundred metres down. To move it four and a half kilometres along the line I have to bring it most of the way up first and start again, or I drag it across the cable in the wrong place. That's about six or seven hours I don't have. The swell's due to build from Sunday night. I'll be making your final joint with less weather than I wanted, and laying your bight with less again. Captain Eide doesn't like it. He's right not to like it."

"And if we don't move?" Tiago said.

"Then I cut good cable four and a half kilometres the wrong side of the fault, I spend a day picking it up, and I come home with seven kilometres of spare for the whole zone." She was quiet for a moment. "Mrs. Furtado, are you there?"

"I'm here."

"Are you sure?"

Maria looked down at the notebook. The pencil had gone soft and grey and the handwriting was a woman's of fifty-five, quick and square, and she did not entirely recognise it.

"No," she said. "I am not sure of anything any more. I am sure of what I wrote."

"That's the right answer," said Joanna Okafor. "We're hauling in."

* * *

The cut came at twenty past three on Friday morning, about four hundred metres on the Bruma side of the break, four thousand two hundred and fifty metres down.

Nobody on Bruma saw it. Tiago watched the ship's position on the screen and the numbers Vigia read out, and slept for two hours on the floor of the office, and woke to the news that the Bruma end, tested from the ship, ran clean all the way home: 318.3 kilometres of cable with two small steps in it, one up and one down, at the place where Maria had once spent two days on her knees on a heaving deck. They sealed that end and hung it on a buoy. They brought up the other end and picked it up, metre by metre, across the break, which turned out to be a clean shear under a thin new slick of mud, and then three hundred metres more to be safe, and tested it all the way to São Miguel, and found it clean.

The first joint was finished on Saturday night. They laid new cable back towards the buoy through Sunday, while the swell started to come in long and low from the west. The final joint was made on Monday in a sea that Joanna Okafor described afterwards, in her report to the cooperative, as "workable"; and the bight, eleven point eight kilometres of it, the deepest Maria had ever heard of on that cable, went down into the dark over the stern at ten past four in the afternoon, two hours before the gale.

At twenty to five the cooperative's network went green, light by light.

The *Maré Cheia* went home with twelve point two kilometres of spare. Joanna Okafor's report noted, in its section on lessons, that the 2031 repair was not in any record transferred to the cooperative in 2044, and that it had been located by the cooperative's assistant from two gainers in the 2045 baseline, after the position and length were supplied from the jointer's contemporaneous notebook. She attached a photograph of the page.

The report noted, too, without comment, that the ship had lost six and a half hours of its weather window moving the grapnel, and that the final joint and bight had been made in conditions at the edge of the ship's limits. It did not say who had decided. It did not need to.

* * *

On Monday evening, with the wind coming up hard off the sea and the shutters banging all down the Rua Nova, Maria sat at her kitchen table with the nineteen notebooks and the grey one that made twenty, and Tiago, and a scanner Tiago had brought from the office, and the tablet with Vigia on it.

"All of them?" Tiago said.

"All of them. Every joint. Every position. Every Scot who whistled." She pushed the stack towards him. "You're going to scan them and put them in the record, and Vigia is going to read them and tell me what doesn't make sense, while I can still tell you what I meant."

"Tia —"

"I'm not dying, Tiago. I'm forgetting. They said slowly. It might be very slowly. But I'd like it written down better than I wrote it down, and I'd like it done while I can check." She turned to the tablet. "Vigia. Do you remember things?"

"I keep records," Vigia said. "I do not remember in the way you do, I think. When my records are lost, what I knew from them is lost too. In 2044 that happened to me, and I did not know it had happened. I had a list of six hundred and forty kilometres, and I measured six hundred and forty-nine, and I wrote down the difference as a number to watch."

"And now?"

"Now I have one page of your notebook," it said, "and it has taught me more about my own cable than I learned in four years of measuring it. I would like the other nineteen. I cannot tell you that I would feel grateful for them, because I cannot check that I feel things. I can tell you that I will read every page, and that where I do not understand something I will ask, instead of writing it down as minor."

Maria looked at the tablet for a while.

"That'll do," she said.

* * *

On Tuesday the second of February, the feast of Nossa Senhora das Candeias, the gale had blown itself out to the east, and the procession went down the hill from the church to the harbour and back in a grey, clean wind that kept putting the candles out.

The cooperative streamed it, as it always did, to the four thousand people of Bruma who did not live on Bruma any more: to New Bedford and Fall River and Toronto and Lisbon, and to a care home in Ponta Delgada where Senhor Avelar's mother watched from her bed. Eight days before, on the satellite backup alone, it could not have gone. Tonight it went out under the sea, along nearly six hundred and sixty kilometres of glass, through two old joints that look, from either end, like a step up, and one long new loop lying in the dark, and nobody who watched it gave the cable a moment's thought, which was how Maria had always liked it.

She walked near the back, with Tiago, holding her candle in her cupped hand against the wind.

That night, before she went to bed, she took the grey notebook down from the dresser, where it would stay now, and turned to the first empty page after May 2031, and wrote, in pencil, slowly, in a hand that was older but still square:

*1 Feb 2049. Maré Cheia. Brumalink seg. 2. Prof. 4250. Two joints, not mine. Our fibre, both sides; no steps. Bight 11.8, laid to the north. Okafor. Tell whoever comes after.*

Then she closed it, and put out the lamp, and listened for a while to the sea.

*The engineering, simplified from general published practice. Repeatered submarine cables are monitored from the shore by sending light pulses down the fibre and measuring the light scattered back (coherent optical time-domain reflectometry, through loop-back paths in the repeaters), which gives the distance along the fibre to a break or a loss; turning a fibre distance into a cable distance and a cable distance into a place on the seabed needs the cable's records, chiefly the as-laid route position list and the records of every later repair. A repair in deep water inserts extra cable, because the last joint is made on deck between two ends lifted from the bottom, and the extra is lowered as a loop (a bight) on the seabed; published descriptions put it at roughly two to three times the water depth. "Universal" joints join cables from different makers. When two fibres with different mode-field diameters are spliced, a one-way reflectometer trace shows an apparent gain in one direction (a "gainer") and an exaggerated loss in the other; averaging the two directions gives the true splice loss. Turbidity currents, underwater avalanches of sediment, are a real cause of deep-water cable breaks. The four hours to lower a grapnel, the six and a half hours to move it, the joint times, the spare-cable figures and the cooperative's purchase of its cable are invented for the story.*

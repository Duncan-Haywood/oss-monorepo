summary: A parish is fencing two sides of a new burial ground, 27 metres and 15 metres, with a post every 1.8 metres at most. The assistant counts the gaps first and the posts after, and finds the shortcut gives one post too few on one side and the corner counted twice.
---

Calum Ross had a roll of string, a mallet and a list from the timber yard, and he had written *42 m ÷ 1.8 m = 23.3, so 24 posts* at the top of it. The glebe was to have a stock fence on its two open sides, the long one of twenty-seven metres along the lane and the short one of fifteen, with the old hedge closing the other two. The yard wanted the order by Monday. He asked the parish assistant, whose name was Una, whether twenty-four was right.

Una asked what the 1.8 was. "The rail span," said Calum. "The yard says no more than that between posts."

"Then count the gaps first. Twenty-seven divided by 1.8 is exactly fifteen. Fifteen gaps need sixteen posts, one at each end. Fifteen metres divided by 1.8 is 8.33, and you cannot have a third of a gap, so nine gaps, which is ten posts, with the gaps 1.67 metres each."

"That is twenty-six."

"And the corner is one post that both sides share. So twenty-five. Your twenty-four lost the end post of a run, and it hid it by running the two sides together as if they were a single line."

* * *

He said that was a lot of arithmetic for one post. Una said that it was one post on a fence of twenty-five, and the last one was always the missing one: a fence of fifteen gaps has sixteen posts because every gap has a post at each end and the first post has no gap before it. "The sum is easy. The trouble is that it feels as though the answer is the number of gaps."

He pointed out that the yard had said 1.8 metres, and that the short side would now come to 1.67. Una said it should be the other way: round the number of gaps up and the spacing down, never the reverse, because 1.8 was the most the rail would bear. "If you took eight gaps you would have 1.875 each, which is over. Nine is the answer."

* * *

Then the rails. The yard sold them in 3.6-metre lengths and the fence took two lines. Calum thought that one rail covered two gaps. "It does on the long run, where the gap is exactly 1.8," said Una. "Fifteen gaps is seven rails and a half, so eight lengths, and the eighth is cut in two. On the short run nine gaps is four and a half, so five lengths, and each pair of 1.67 gaps is 3.33 metres, which the rail covers with a little to spare. That is thirteen lengths a line, and twenty-six for the two."

"Twenty-six, then."

"If every joint falls on a post. Rails that meet in the middle of a gap will sag, so stagger them: on the second line begin with a half, so no two joints share a post. That costs a short offcut and one more length on the long run, and I would count it with the yard before you pay."

Calum asked what would happen if the lane side had a gate. Una said that a gate of 1.2 metres took one more post and a different sum, and that she would work it out once the vicar had decided whether there was to be one. "I will not guess where it goes."

* * *

He ordered twenty-five posts, with two spare, and twenty-six rails. Una wrote him a short list for the Saturday: the string, taut, from the corner post along each side; a mark every 1.8 on the long run and every 1.67 on the short one; a peg at each mark before a hole was dug; and the corner first, because it was the one that every other mark counted from. Posts to go a third of their length into the ground, which she gave from memory and asked him to check against the yard's sheet.

He crossed out the first line of his list and wrote under it: *count the gaps, then add one, then take off the corner.*

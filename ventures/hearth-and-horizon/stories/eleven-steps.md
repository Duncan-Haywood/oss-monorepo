title: Eleven Steps
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Balay ni Santa Rita, a home for the old run by an invented community of Sisters in Culasi, an invented town on Leyte Gulf, the Philippines, Monday 18 November 2052
published: 2026-10-25
summary: A typhoon will arrive on the evening high tide in 2052, and fifty forecast runs put the water above the floor of a home for 38 frail old people in 46 of 50. The Mother Superior will not repeat the 2049 bus evacuation after which two residents died; the town's storm agent will not choose for her. They find the third answer together, and the agent can only count it.
---

Mother Remedios Alcantara had buried two of her old people in the month after the last evacuation, and she had not forgiven the bus.

She was seventy-three, a small straight woman with a voice made for refectories, and at twenty to nine on the morning of Monday 18 November 2052 she stood at the foot of the stairs in the Sisters' house at Culasi with a mug of tea going cold in her hand and said it to the ceiling, because the ceiling was where the town's storm agent lived. Its speaker was a grey box on the landing, put there in 2049 after the last time.

"I will not put them on a bus," she said.

"I haven't asked you to," said the agent. The town called it Bantay, which means watchman, and it spoke in the Waray of Leyte, unhurried, with the flat kindness of a man who has read a lot of weather. "I have not recommended anything yet. I would like first to tell you what the eight o'clock forecast says, and then what it does not say, and then I would like you to tell me what I have wrong. Is Dr Abad here?"

"She is coming over the yard," said Mother Remedios. "Say it again when she is in the room. I do not want two versions."

---

Balay ni Santa Rita stood on a spit of land between the sea and a fish-pond, at the end of the road from the town. It had been built as a home for retired fishermen's widows in 1988, rebuilt after the great typhoon of 2013 and again in 2031, and it now held thirty-eight people, the oldest of them a hundred and one. Nineteen were bedridden. Twelve more walked with frames. Three were on oxygen concentrators, which hummed in their rooms like contented cats. Six Sisters looked after them, with a lay staff of eleven, who had been awake since the fourth hour because the sea had come up the slipway at the first light.

The home itself was a single storey. Next to it across a yard stood the Sisters' own house, two storeys of reinforced concrete built in 2031, with a dormitory on the upper floor for a retreat house that had never filled.

Dr Lourdes Abad came in out of the rain shaking a yellow coat. She was the town's only full-time physician, fifty-one, and she was carrying her tablet against her chest like a missal.

"Go on," said Mother Remedios.

"The typhoon is called Lumen," said Bantay. "At eight this morning it was 310 kilometres east-northeast of the gulf mouth, moving west at 22 kilometres an hour, with sustained winds of 195 kilometres an hour at the centre. It will pass north of Culasi by about 40 kilometres, which is the wrong side for wind and the right side for you: the strongest winds are on the other half of the storm. The danger here is the sea."

"The surge."

"The surge, and the tide it rides on. High water tonight is at 21:10 and it is a spring tide, 1.1 metres above the survey datum. I have fifty runs of the storm-surge model from the national service. Each run starts from a slightly different storm: a degree of track, ten kilometres an hour of speed, a different eye. They are not fifty opinions. They are one model shaken fifty ways to show how much the answer moves when the input does. May I give you the counts?"

"Give me the counts," said Dr Abad.

"The water level at your door, tide, surge and wave rise together, above the same datum. The floor of the home is 2.4 metres. In 46 of the 50 runs the water reaches 2.4 metres or more. That is 92 percent. In 38 runs it reaches 3.0 metres, which is 60 centimetres in the corridors. In 15 runs it reaches 4.0. In two runs it reaches 5.0. The highest of the fifty is 5.2. The middle run is 3.4 metres, so about a metre of water on the ground floor of the home, with waves on top of it, at about nine in the evening."

Nobody said anything. The rain on the roof had a tin note in it.

"The upper floor of the Sisters' house," said Mother Remedios.

"Is at 5.6 metres. None of the fifty runs gets there. The highest gets within 40 centimetres. I would like you to hear that sentence once, slowly. None is not the same as it cannot. Fifty runs sample a spread; they do not bound it. The year of the great typhoon, the water in Tacloban was more than five metres. This storm's track is not that one. But I cannot tell you the true highest, and I am going to say so each time you ask."

---

The Mother Superior sat down on the second stair.

She was not a woman who sat in front of her staff. They noticed.

"In 2049," she said, "the forecast was for two and a half metres and we had thirty-one in the home. The agent before you, the one the province bought, said go. We went. It took six hours, in the rain, in the hall at Tabing with the lights out, on the floor, and Lola Pacita could not be told where she was, and Mang Ernesto had a stroke on the second night. They died. Two of the thirty-one. The sea did not come. It came to a hand's breadth on the slipway."

"I remember," said Dr Abad quietly. "I signed both."

"I do not say it was your fault."

"I do," said the doctor. "A little. I said yes to the bus because the model said so, and I did not ask what the bus would cost." She put the tablet on her knee and turned it so the Mother could see. "I went back through the literature that winter. It is not a good literature, Mother. It says that moving frail, bedridden old people long distances ahead of a storm kills a fraction of them, from the move, within a few weeks. The numbers range from one in forty to one in ten. Ours was two in thirty-one, which is one in fifteen. I would not call it out of line."

"One in fifteen of thirty-eight," said Bantay, "is two or three people. I mean to say only that it is a figure, not a verdict. If I may: I can count what the bus costs and what the water costs, but I do not know the cost of the one against the other, and I will not pretend that I do. That is why I asked for Dr Abad."

"Say the bus," said Mother Remedios.

"Two municipal buses are free, each with a lift. Stretchers fit four to a bus, with six more seated, so ten residents a trip. Thirty-eight residents is four trips, two buses twice. The shelter is the school at Tabing, 21 kilometres away; in this rain, 36 minutes each way, with half an hour to load and half to unload. If the first bus leaves at 12:30, the last of the four is across the Mabini causeway by about ten past three. The causeway is passable until the water on it reaches 1.6 metres on the datum. In my runs, that happens at 16:40 in the middle run. In the earliest run, 14:50. In five of fifty runs it closes before ten past three, which means that 10 percent of the time the last ten people are on the wrong side of it, in a bus, in the dark."

"And if I wait," said Mother Remedios, "for the next forecast."

"It comes at two this afternoon. The runs will be narrower; they are always narrower the nearer the storm is. It is the best number you will have. But if you wait for it, the road plan loses almost all its margin. By two, three of the four trips cannot be done before the causeway starts to go. The bus is a plan you must decide now, in the dark, on the spread you have this minute."

---

Sister Rosario said it first, and not as a plan.

She was eighty-eight and the eldest of the Sisters, and until that spring she had still done the mending. She had come over from the kitchen with a basin of rice porridge and set it on the landing because her hands shook, and she had listened to the whole of it standing against the wall.

"The pond dike," she said.

"What of it, Sister?" said Bantay.

"They cut it. In 2050, the fishermen of the co-operative. They took twenty centimetres off the crest at the north end, to let the pond drain after rain. You will not have it. It was not in the plan. The sea comes into the pond now before the road."

"I do not have it," said the agent. "My inundation map is from a survey of 2041, with the sluices of 2046. Thank you. That raises the water in the yard by something. I cannot say how much without a man with a staff and a level. If it is twenty centimetres, then the 46 of 50 becomes 47 or 48. And it brings the water to the door sooner, by perhaps twenty minutes. That is worth knowing and I'd rather know it from you than from the sea."

"There is a man with a staff," said Sister Rosario. "Ramil Gaviola."

"He is on the beach," said Dr Abad, "putting the boats up."

"Then send for him." Mother Remedios was looking at the stairs she was sitting on. They were concrete, twenty-four of them, with a rubber tread and a steel rail, painted green. She had climbed them twice a day for twenty-two years. "How many steps, Bantay?"

"Twenty-four. Your Sisters' house plans say 3.2 metres from ground to upper floor, so about thirteen centimetres a step."

"And the upper floor sleeps how many?"

"Sixty mattresses, in the retreat dormitory and the two classrooms. I have the plan."

"Then they do not leave," said Mother Remedios, as if the thought had come to her from the porridge. "They go upstairs."

There was a pause, in which the rain and the three concentrators could be heard.

"I want to say what I think this plan is," said Bantay, "because it is a good plan and a plan that should be counted, and I do not want to be the one that says yes because I want you to like me. May I?"

"Please."

"It moves nobody out of the town. It moves them thirty metres and 3.2 metres. It can wait until the two o'clock forecast, and a long time after, until the water is at the yard. It puts everyone under a roof the highest run does not reach. It costs the ground floor of the home: the chapel, the infirmary, the records, the kitchen and every bed. The harm that I cannot measure is how a frail person bears being carried up twenty-four steps. The number I do not have is whether the Sisters' house holds if the water is higher than the model. It was designed to the code of 2031 for waves and I have not seen the engineer's report. If it fails, it fails with all thirty-eight in it and everyone who is looking after them. The bus cannot do that to them all at once. The stairs can."

Nobody spoke.

"That is the whole of the honest case against," said the agent. "I don't want it to be left out."

---

It was Dr Abad who did the arithmetic of carrying, because she did it for a living.

"Nineteen who can't walk," she said, "need four people each, on a blanket stretcher, on stairs that turn once. A blanket and two poles. I won't do the heavy ones on a chair. Say six minutes a person, load, climb, down for the next. With eight teams of four, that's thirty-two carriers. Nineteen residents over eight teams is two and a bit rounds, so three rounds, eighteen minutes, and call it forty with the mistakes. The twelve with frames go with an arm on each side, up one flight, ten minutes more. Seven others are in chairs and are light." She looked up. "Under an hour. If we have thirty-two people."

"The parish youth are in the hall for the typhoon watch," said Sister Rosario. "There are forty of them. And the men of the co-operative."

"Then I want one practice," said the doctor. "A real one. Not tonight, when the water is in the yard. Now. With the lightest resident and a Sister to say if it hurts." She hesitated. "I don't like it, Mother. I don't like any of it. A man of ninety who has never been carried in his life, and then in a storm. But I like it better than one in fifteen."

"You'll not like the other answers," said the agent. "They are all dear. I can only show you where the price falls."

Mother Remedios stood up from the stair. She was a short woman and she stood very straight.

"Then this," she said. "We move the generator and the fuel to the upper floor now, and the three concentrators first, with their people. We carry everybody who can be carried before the two o'clock forecast, because afterward I will not have the faces of the Sisters to bear if we go up in the dark. The bus stays in the yard, with Ramil's cousin at the wheel, for the one chance in a hundred I am wrong. At two o'clock, Bantay, you tell me what the new runs say. And if the water in the yard is above the second stair of the home, we do not argue."

"I will tell you at two," said Bantay, "and every hour after, and the minute it changes. I will also say now that I think the plan is sound and that I do not know if it is right. If you need one of those words from me, it is the first."

---

They practised at ten, with Lola Soledad, who was ninety-four and weighed forty-one kilograms and was told that she was being taken up to see the view.

"What view?" said Lola Soledad.

"Of the pond," said Sister Rosario, honestly, from the head of the stretcher. "From the upper window. It will be a sea by tonight."

"I have seen the sea," said Lola Soledad. "I have been married to it." But she lay still, with her hands crossed, and let four boys of the parish youth lift her into the air on a blanket and two mop poles, and she was a minute and fifty seconds from the infirmary door to the top of the stairs. The Sister at her head said the Hail Mary aloud the whole way, so that the old woman had a rhythm to be carried to, and so that the boys, who had never touched anyone so light or so old, had something to count with besides fear. Lola Soledad was asleep by the time she was set down.

"One minute fifty," said Dr Abad, looking at her watch with something near astonishment. "I'd have said six."

"With the rosary," said the agent, "I would not have either."

They did the rest between half past ten and one. It was slower than the practice and Bantay counted it out loud, down the house speaker and in the little ledger on its screen: eleven residents upstairs by eleven, twenty by noon, thirty-one by half past twelve. At 12:40 the last of them came up, Don Cipriano, a hundred and one, who had to be told three times what was happening and then said, "Ah," with great dignity and asked whether there would be tea. The three concentrators were humming in a classroom with a generator beside them, strapped to a desk. Ramil Gaviola came in from the beach with a measuring staff and said the dike was lower than anyone had told the plan by twenty-three centimetres, and that the pond had been breathing in since eleven.

"Thank you," said Bantay. "That moves the arrival of the water at the home door about twenty-five minutes earlier, to about half past seven. It does not change the height by more than a few centimetres. It changes the hour when you want to stop arguing."

---

The two o'clock forecast came in on the dot, as Bantay had said.

The fifty runs had narrowed. The track had settled north by a few kilometres; the lowest run was now 2.9 metres and the highest 4.9. The middle run, 3.7. All fifty now put water on the ground floor of the home. Nine in fifty reached 4.5 or more. The bus plan was dead; three of its four trips could no longer be made across the causeway, and the causeway was already awash at the seaward end.

"None reach 5.6," said the agent. "The nearest, 4.9, is 70 centimetres short. I will say it as I said it this morning. It is a count of fifty runs. The model is a good model. It is not a promise."

"Say what it is a promise of," said Mother Remedios.

"That if I were asked to bet my own hearing on one floor in the town," said Bantay, "it would be the one you are standing on."

She almost smiled.

The wind rose at four, and the rain turned flat. The fishermen of the co-operative came in from the beach with their boats up on the road, and the parish youth made soup in the Sisters' kitchen, and the Sisters sang Vespers at five in the upper dormitory with thirty-eight old people lying in rows on mattresses with their rosaries and their frames beside them, because Mother Remedios had said that if the house was to be a ship, it should keep the hours of a ship. Dr Abad went down the rows with a torch and a stethoscope. At seven the lights went. At seven twenty-six, Bantay said from the grey box, which was running on a battery now, that the water was at the door of the home.

---

It rose through the evening the way a breath is drawn.

By eight it had come in across the yard and filled the ground floor of the home to the knee. Bantay read the gauge at the seawall every ten minutes into the dark, and then, when the gauge failed at 8:40, read the staff that Ramil had set against the Sisters' house, where a boy held a torch to it at the foot of the stairs. At nine it was at the sixth step. The sea was not still. It leaned against the walls and let go and leaned again, and at each lean the building gave out a sound like a very large animal turning over in its sleep, and the old people stirred and said "Ay" and were hushed by the Sisters, who said "Hush, it is only the sea."

At twenty past nine, a little after the high tide that the tables had named, the water stood at the ninth step.

"That is about 3.6 metres," said Bantay, "on the datum. It's below the middle run."

"Is it still rising?"

"By two centimetres in the last ten minutes. The tide is turning. I would like to say it has done what it will do. I'll say I don't know it until I've counted three more readings."

It took the three readings, and then one more.

At 21:40 the water stood at the eleventh step, which was 3.9 metres on the datum, and 1.5 metres on the floor of the home. It stayed there for twenty minutes. Then it began, very slowly, to go down, and no one in the dormitory cheered, because no one in that room had slept, and Mother Remedios was telling her beads at the head of the stairs with a torch in her lap. She looked at the staff and the boy.

"Eleven," she said.

"Eleven," said the boy. His name was Jaime and he was fourteen, and he had held the torch for two hours without being asked to put it down.

---

In the morning the sea had gone back to its place as though it had never left. It had left the chapel with a hand's depth of grey silt on the floor, and the infirmary with its beds floating against the ceiling, and the records, the piano, the kitchen range and the drawer of letters that Sister Rosario had kept since 1996. It had left the Sisters' house untouched above the eleventh step. Nobody had died. Lola Soledad, asked at breakfast how she had liked the view, said it had been a very poor view and that she would like another.

It took the Sisters and the parish youth eleven days to shovel out the home. The insurers, when they came, wrote it up as a loss of the ground floor and its contents, and paid on it, slowly, in the new year. The co-operative filled the twenty-three centimetres back into the pond dike at its own cost. The bus was returned to the municipality unused.

Dr Abad wrote her report on the third day, at the kitchen table in the Sisters' house, between visits. She wrote that thirty-eight residents had been moved thirty metres and 3.2 metres, that one had a skin tear on the shoulder, one had refused food for a day, and none had died or fallen. She wrote that the plan had been chosen with a spread of risk that could not be reduced to a probability, and that she did not know whether it was the better plan than the bus. She wrote it down because someone would have to choose again, and would be reading it.

At the end she added a line she had not meant to write: *We were helped by a thing that told us what it did not know.*

Then she crossed it out, because it was not clinical, and then, because it was true, she wrote it in again.

That evening, when the Sisters had said Compline in the dormitory with all the old people at their rest, Mother Remedios went down the stairs alone, counting them with the flat of her hand on the rail, to the eleventh. The step was dry. There was a tide line across the wall above it, a thin brown seam, and she put her finger on it. Behind her, from the grey box on the landing, the agent said nothing at all, which she took to be a courtesy.

She rang the small hand-bell that hung by the stairs, once, as Sister Rosario had rung it at the end of every day for sixty years. It made a clear small sound in the wet air, and out on the road a dog answered it.

*Eleven Steps is fiction, written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, and read aloud by a synthetic voice when audio is rendered. Culasi, Balay ni Santa Rita, Tabing, the Mabini causeway, Typhoon Lumen and everyone in the story are invented. Leyte Gulf, Tacloban and the great typhoon of 2013 are real. The forecast counts, the water levels, the floor heights and the times are the author's round figures for an invented storm, not a forecast, an engineering opinion or a safety plan. The 2049 evacuation figure (two deaths among thirty-one residents) is invented; the claim that moving frail, bedridden older people long distances ahead of a storm carries a real risk of death in the weeks after is the author's reading of a thin literature and is not medical advice. Whether to evacuate or shelter in place is a decision for the people on the ground and their emergency services. The assumption behind the storm agent (a municipal assistant with memory that steadily improves, that gives counts from an ensemble and does not make the decision) is the author's, stated in 2026, and no one can check it.*

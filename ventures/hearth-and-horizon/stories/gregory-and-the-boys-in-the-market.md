A long time ago, in the great city of Rome, there lived a young man named Gregory.

Gregory's family was rich. Their house stood on one of the seven hills of Rome, a hill called the Caelian, with gardens and pillars and a view over the roofs of the city. Gregory went to the best teachers. He grew up to be one of the most important men in Rome, a governor who wore fine clothes, and people bowed when he walked past.

But inside, Gregory was not happy. He kept thinking about God, and about the quiet life of the monks who spent their days praying and working together.

So one day, Gregory did something that surprised everybody.

He gave his big house away.

* * *

He did not sell it. He turned it into a monastery, a home for monks, and he named it after Saint Andrew, the fisherman who followed Jesus. Then Gregory took off his fine clothes, put on a plain monk's robe, and went to live there himself, as one of the monks.

He prayed. He read the Bible. He ate very little, so little that his stomach often hurt, and the other monks worried about him. Gregory said, many years later, that those were the happiest days of his life.

Have you ever given away something that was yours, to make someone else happy? How did it feel?

* * *

Now here is a story about Gregory that was told for a long, long time before anyone wrote it down.

It is told that one day, Gregory was walking through the market place in Rome. There were stalls full of bread and fish and cloth. And there were some boys standing there, far from home, who had been brought a very long way to be sold as servants.

Gregory stopped. The boys had pale skin and bright faces and beautiful fair hair.

"Where do these boys come from?" Gregory asked.

"From an island called Britain," someone told him.

"And do the people there know about Jesus?"

"No. Not yet."

Gregory gave a great sigh. "What a sad thing," he said, "that such bright faces have never heard of the light of God."

* * *

Then Gregory asked, "What are their people called?"

"They are called Angles."

And Gregory, who liked words, said, "Angles! That is a good name, for they have faces like angels."

"And what is the name of the part of the country they come from?"

"Deira."

"Deira!" said Gregory. In Latin, the language they spoke, *de ira* sounds like *from anger*. "Then they will be taken from God's anger and brought into Christ's mercy."

"And what is the name of their king?"

"Aella."

"Aella!" said Gregory, and now he was almost smiling. "Then *Alleluia* must be sung in that land, in praise of God."

Three questions, and three jokes about names. But Gregory was not only joking. He went to the pope and asked to be sent to Britain himself, to tell the Angles about Jesus.

The story says the people of Rome loved Gregory too much to let him go so far away. So he stayed. But he never forgot those boys.

* * *

The years went by. Then a dark time came to Rome. The river Tiber flooded the streets, and after the flood came a terrible sickness, and the pope himself died of it.

The people of Rome needed a new pope. And they all said the same name.

"Gregory!"

Gregory did not want to be pope. He wanted to stay a monk in his quiet house. He even wrote to the emperor, asking him to say no. But the people would not change their minds, and in the end, Gregory said yes.

He led the people through the streets of Rome, praying together for the sickness to end. He sent for grain from the Church's farms on the island of Sicily so that the hungry had bread. He wrote letters, hundreds and hundreds of letters, to kings and bishops and farmers and widows.

And Gregory gave himself a new name. Popes were very important, but he did not want to sound important. He liked to call himself *the servant of the servants of God*.

What do you think that means? It means: I am here to help the people who help other people.

* * *

Gregory was pope now, and he still remembered the boys in the market. So he chose a monk from his own monastery of Saint Andrew, a man named Augustine, and some other monks to go with him. He sent them on the long road to England.

It was a very long road. Up through Italy, and on into the land of the Franks, which we call France. Weeks and weeks of travelling.

And somewhere along the way, the monks became frightened.

They had heard stories about the people of England. That they were fierce. That they spoke a language nobody understood. That they would not want to listen.

So the monks stopped, and sent Augustine all the way back to Rome to ask Gregory, "Please, may we come home?"

* * *

Gregory did not say yes. But he did not scold them either.

He wrote them a letter. And the letter said, more or less: My dear sons, it would have been better never to start a good thing than to turn back once you have started. So keep going. Do not be put off by the long road or by what people say. God will be with you. And I wish I could be there working with you.

Augustine carried the letter back to the other monks. They read it, and they took a deep breath, and they kept walking.

Have you ever been scared to keep going with something? What helped you?

* * *

At last they came to the sea, and crossed it, and landed in a part of England called Kent. There were nearly forty of them, with some men from France who could speak for them.

The King of Kent, whose name was Ethelbert, came to meet them. He was careful. He sat down outside in the open air, in case these strangers knew some kind of magic.

And the monks came walking up to him, carrying a silver cross and a painting of Jesus, and singing.

The king listened. His wife, Queen Bertha, already loved Jesus. And after a while, King Ethelbert was baptized, and then many, many of his people too.

When Gregory heard the news in Rome, he was so happy that he wrote to his friends about it. The Angles were singing *Alleluia*, just as he had said.

* * *

Gregory wrote the monks more letters after that. In one of them he gave them some very good advice. He said: when you want to climb a high mountain, you do not get to the top in one big jump. You go step by step.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Gregory.

Gregory gave away his big house. He wanted to be a monk, and God asked him to be a pope. He called himself a servant. He remembered some boys he once saw in a market, and he did something about it, even though it took years. And when his friends were frightened on the road, he wrote and told them, keep going, step by step.

Today we call him Saint Gregory the Great. The people of England were so grateful to him that they called him their apostle, the one who sent them the good news of Jesus.

You can be like Gregory too. You can notice someone who needs help, and not forget them. And when something good is hard, you can keep going, one step at a time.

Goodnight.

* * *

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Gregory the Great** was born in Rome about 540 into a wealthy senatorial family; his parents were Gordianus and Silvia (she is also venerated as a saint). About 573 he was prefect of the city (*praefectus urbi*), its highest civil office. After his father's death he turned the family house on the Caelian Hill into a monastery dedicated to Saint Andrew (about 574 or 575), founded six more on the family's estates in Sicily, and became a monk himself. His fasting is generally blamed for the stomach trouble he suffered all his life. He describes the monastery years as his happiest, with longing, in the preface to his *Dialogues*. We have left out his years as papal envoy in Constantinople (about 579 to 586), which came between the monastery and the papacy.

**The boys in the market is "it is told".** Bede, writing in 731, gives it in the *Ecclesiastical History* (book 2, chapter 1) as a story "handed down to us by the tradition of our forefathers", not as something he had a document for. In his version Gregory, before he was pope, sees boys put up for sale in the market, "of fair complexion, with fine features and beautiful hair", and makes the three plays on words: Angles and angels, Deira and *de ira* ("from wrath"), King Ælle (Aella) and *Alleluia*. Deira was a kingdom in what is now Yorkshire; Ælle was its king in the later sixth century. Bede then says Gregory asked the pope to send him to Britain and that the citizens of Rome would not allow him to go so far. An earlier *Life of Gregory*, written by a monk or nun of Whitby between about 704 and 714, also has the meeting and the three puns, with differences in detail. The famous short form, "not Angles but angels" (*non Angli sed angeli*), is a later compression; Bede's Gregory says they have "the face of angels". The boys being sold "as servants" is our gentle wording of Bede's "for sale" (they were slaves). It is history, not tradition, that Gregory as pope wrote in September 595 to Candidus, the priest who ran the Church's estates in Gaul, asking him to buy English boys of seventeen or eighteen so that they could be brought up in monasteries.

**Made pope.** The Tiber flooded in late 589, and the plague that followed killed Pope Pelagius II in February 590. The clergy and people of Rome chose Gregory. Gregory of Tours, a contemporary, says he wrote to the Emperor Maurice asking him not to confirm the election, and that the letter was intercepted; he also describes the great penitential procession through the city, in seven groups, that Gregory led while the plague went on. Gregory was consecrated on 3 September 590. The later legend that the Archangel Michael appeared over Hadrian's tomb sheathing a sword (which is why it is now called Castel Sant'Angelo) is not in the early sources, and we have left it out. Gregory's letters (more than 850 survive in his *Register*) show him organising grain from the papal estates in Sicily for Rome. He may not have been the first bishop of Rome to use the title "servant of the servants of God" (*servus servorum Dei*), but he used it so steadily that it became the popes' own; the story says he "liked to call himself" that.

**The mission.** In 596, the fourteenth year of Maurice, Gregory sent Augustine, the prior of his own monastery of Saint Andrew, with other monks. Bede (book 1, chapter 23) says that on the journey they were "seized with a sudden fear" of going to "a barbarous, fierce and unbelieving nation, whose very language they did not know", sent Augustine back to ask to be excused, and received a letter from Gregory, dated 23 July 596, which Bede quotes. Its opening, in the usual translation, says that it is better not to begin a good work than to think of turning back from it once begun; it urges them not to be deterred by the toil of the journey or "the tongues of evil-speaking men", and ends with Gregory's wish that he might share in the work. Bede does not record their route through Gaul in detail, so the story says only "up through Italy and into the land of the Franks". Our letter in the story is a shortened paraphrase, and the story says "more or less". Bede (1.25) says that when they landed on the Isle of Thanet in 597 they were, "as is reported, nearly forty men", and that they had brought interpreters from among the Franks. King Æthelberht (Ethelbert) of Kent met them in the open air for fear of magic; they came carrying a silver cross and an image of Christ painted on a board, singing a litany. His wife Bertha was a Christian Frankish princess who had brought a bishop, Liudhard, with her. The date of Æthelberht's baptism is not recorded; it was probably soon after. In a letter of 598 to Eulogius of Alexandria, Gregory reported with joy that more than ten thousand English had been baptized the Christmas before. "Singing *Alleluia*, just as he had said" is our link back to the market story, not Gregory's words.

**Step by step** is from Gregory's letter of 601 to the abbot Mellitus (Bede 1.30), the letter that advised keeping England's temples and turning them into churches rather than destroying them: "he who endeavours to ascend to the highest place rises by degrees or steps, and not by leaps." In the story we have made the mountain the picture; the letter does not mention one.

**What we left out.** The dove at Gregory's ear, telling him what to write, is a later legend, found in lives of Gregory written in the eighth and ninth centuries (the fullest is John the Deacon's, about 875). Gregorian chant is named after him, but its attribution to him comes from the ninth century, and most scholars now think the chant took its familiar form in the Frankish kingdoms in the eighth and ninth centuries. Neither is in the story.

**Our teller is Bede.** "Our apostle" is from Bede's chapter on Gregory (2.1), where he says that though Gregory is not an apostle to others, he is to us, "for we are the seal of his apostleship in the Lord". Bede has his own Little Lamps story, *Bede and the Last Sentence*.

**His death and feast.** Gregory died on 12 March 604 and was buried in Saint Peter's. He was called "the Great" early, and is one of the four great Latin Doctors of the Church (with Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine of Hippo), recognised as such in 1298. His feast was 12 March until the calendar reform of 1969 moved it to 3 September, the day he was consecrated, so that it would fall outside Lent; it is an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, the stalls of bread and fish, Gregory "almost smiling", the questions to the child, the closing reflection and the prayer. The dialogue in the market follows Bede closely but is in our words. The prayer is not an approved text.

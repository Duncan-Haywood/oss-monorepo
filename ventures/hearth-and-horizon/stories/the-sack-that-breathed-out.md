The oldest farmer, who never trusted plastic over ash and neem leaves, finds what the sheet left out by filling a liner with air and holding it to her cheek; the cooperative's assistant works out what the oxygen must have done; and a torn inner liner, two necks twisted into one knot, and a rule to open one bag a month bring the next harvest through.

Hadiza Mamane signed for sixty bags on the third Saturday of November, and the sheet that came with them said the weevils would die.

It said it in French and in Hausa, on both sides of a laminated card the size of a schoolbook, under a drawing of a bag with a padlock on it: *Fermez bien le sac : les insectes meurent.* Close the bag well: the insects die. Under that, in smaller letters, a drawing of a beetle on its back with its legs in the air, which the children of Unguwar Tsamiya found very funny, and which Hadiza, who was thirty-four and the secretary of the women's cooperative and therefore the one who signed things, found reassuring.

The bags themselves were not much to look at. Each was three bags, one inside the other. The outside was an ordinary woven sack, white with a green stripe, the kind the whole of the Sahel carries millet and fertiliser and charcoal in. Inside that was a bag of thick clear plastic, and inside that another, the same. You filled the inner one with your beans, twisted its neck and tied it; you twisted and tied the middle one over it; you tied the woven sack over both. A hundred kilos in each. Sixty bags, six tonnes, nearly the whole cowpea harvest of the cooperative's thirty-one members, which had come in from the fields in October and been threshed on the swept earth floor behind the old granary, inside its fence of thorn branches, and winnowed in the wind.

"No powder," said Malam Idrissa, the extension agent from the agriculture service in Maradi, who had brought the bags out on the back of a pickup. He tapped the card. "No pills. No smell. Nothing for the children to get into. You close the bag, the insects inside breathe up the air, and when the air is finished, so are they."

"How long?" said Hadiza.

"A few weeks. The card says it." He turned it over and showed her. *Après quelques semaines, les insectes sont morts.* "In March, when the price goes up, you open it and the beans are like the day you closed it."

Hadiza wrote *60 sacs* in the cooperative's register, and beside it, because she was careful, *fermés le 16/11/47*. Then she read the line aloud to the phone propped against the register, which was the other way the cooperative kept its books.

"Sixty bags sealed on the sixteenth of November," said the phone, in its unhurried Hausa. "I have put them in the register by owner. Do you want me to say when they will be safe to sell?"

"Yes."

"The card says the insects are dead after a few weeks. I will mark them safe from the middle of December."

The phone was the cooperative's assistant. It had come two years before with a programme that gave village cooperatives a shared phone and a year's data in return for keeping their accounts on it, and it had stayed because it was useful: it kept the register, it read out the market prices in Maradi and Kano every morning, it reminded people what they owed and what they were owed without anyone having to say it to their face. The children called it Rumbu, the granary, because it lived on a shelf in the old granary and knew where everything was. It answered to that.

"Good," said Hadiza, and the line went into the register: *safe from mid-December*. It looked like a fact. It looked exactly like all the other facts.

* * *

Kaka Rabi did not sign for a bag.

She was seventy-two, or thereabouts; she said she had been born in the year of the great hunger and that was all the date anyone needed. She was the oldest member of the cooperative and had been its first president, when it was eleven women with a shared hoe, and the children called her Kaka, grandmother, and so did everybody else. She sat on a mat in the shade of the tamarind tree that gave the village its name, with a clay jar the height of a child beside her, a basin of grey wood ash and a heap of dried neem leaves, and she was filling the jar with her cowpeas a handful at a time: a layer of beans, a dusting of ash, a few leaves, a layer of beans.

"Kaka," said Hadiza, "there's a bag for you. The programme paid for it. It's free."

"Free," said Kaka Rabi. She did not look up. "My mother put her beans in a jar with ash. Her mother did. I have done it for sixty years. The ash fills the spaces and the beetles cannot walk. The neem they do not like. I lose a little. Every year I lose a little. I know how much."

"With the bag you lose nothing."

"With the bag," said Kaka Rabi, "you do not know what you lose. You cannot see through it. You tie it in November and you open it in March and you find out. With my jar I put my hand in." She put her hand in, to show them, and brought it out grey to the wrist with three beans in her palm. "This is how I find out. Every week."

It was a reasonable thing to say, and Hadiza knew it was reasonable, which annoyed her. "The card says—"

"The card," said Kaka Rabi, "was not here when the beetles came last year. I was." She put the beans back. "Fill your bags, daughter. I hope they work. I will keep my seed in my jar."

So the cooperative filled fifty-nine bags and gave the sixtieth to Hadiza's aunt Zalika, who had more beans than she had expected, and Kaka Rabi filled her jar, and the day went the way that sort of day goes.

It went slowly in the morning, when everyone was fresh and Malam Idrissa was watching, and each inner bag was opened out and filled with the scoop and shaken down and twisted and tied by two women who had been shown how. It went faster after the midday prayer, when Malam Idrissa had driven back to Maradi and the heat came down on the threshing floor like a lid. And it went fastest of all at the end, when the sun was low and red behind the thorn fence and there were still a dozen bags to do, and Hadiza's cousin Sani and his friend Musa, who were twenty and nineteen and strong and bored, said they would finish them.

They finished them. Hadiza saw them working from across the floor, where she was writing the owners' names on the woven sacks in marker: Musa holding each bag open, Sani scooping, then the two of them gathering both clear necks together in their fists and twisting them as one, the way you would twist a towel, and tying them with a single strip of rubber from an inner tube. It saved a knot. She thought of saying something and did not, because the light was going and it looked tidy, and the card said *tie the bag well* and the bags were tied well. You could not have pulled those knots apart with your teeth.

When the donkey cart took the last load across to the granary, one of the bags slid off the back at the gate and landed on its end with a sound like a sack of beans landing on its end. Musa swore, and Sani laughed, and they heaved it back on. Nobody thought about it again.

* * *

In December the wind turned and came out of the north-east, dry and full of dust, and the Harmattan sat on Unguwar Tsamiya for six weeks the way it did every year. The bags sat in the old granary in four rows of fifteen on a floor of planks laid over bricks, and nothing happened to them, which was the point.

Hadiza checked them anyway, because she was careful. She walked along the rows once a week and pressed each bag with the flat of her hand, which told her nothing, and looked at the floor under each for the pale dust that beetles leave, which was not there.

"Rumbu," she said one evening, on the second Sunday of December, when she had come back from Mass in Maradi on the bus and gone to the granary before going home. "Are they dead yet?"

"The register has them as safe from the middle of December," said the phone. "That is from the card."

"But are they?"

There was a short pause, the length of a breath, which was how long Rumbu took when a question went past what it held.

"I cannot see into the bags," it said. "I have the card's line and the date they were sealed. If you want, I can find out what the people who made the bags measured, and say what that would mean for a bag of this size. I have not done that. I wrote down what the card said."

"Do it, then."

It did. It took longer than usual, because the village's signal came and went with the dust, and when it had finished it read her what it had: that the bags were a kind first made by a university in the United States with researchers in West and Central Africa, Niger among them, for exactly this, for cowpeas and the little spotted beetle that eats them; that when a bag was sealed the insects' own breathing used up the oxygen inside it; that below about five parts of oxygen in a hundred they stopped eating, and below about two they began to die, not of suffocation so much as of thirst, because an insect living in a dry bean makes most of the water it needs from the air it burns.

"How long it takes," said Rumbu, "depends on how many insects there are at the start and how much air is in the bag. One study put a few dozen beetles in a jar and the air reached two in a hundred in three days to two weeks, depending on how many and how big the jar. A hundred-kilo bag has much more air in it and, if the beans were clean, far fewer insects for its size. The card's few weeks is about right, if the bag is shut."

"If the bag is shut," said Hadiza.

"I can only say what a shut bag does," said Rumbu. "That is what the card says too, I think. It does not say so."

She went home through the dust with the phone in her pocket and the sentence going round in her head, and then she forgot it, because it was nearly Christmas and her daughter had a cough.

* * *

Saturday the seventh of March was a market day, and the cooperative opened its first bag to sell.

They had agreed it in February: one bag sold now at the Maradi price, the money split between its owner's household and the cooperative's fund, and the rest kept back for the hungry months before the rains, when the price would climb again. The first bag was Bag 60, which belonged to Hadiza's aunt Zalika. Zalika was there, and Hadiza, and Kaka Rabi on her mat, and a ring of children waiting to see the beetle on its back.

Sani cut the strip of rubber on the woven sack. Under it the two clear necks were twisted together in their single knot, tight as wire. He worked at them with a knife and they came open with a sigh.

The smell came out first. It was not a bad smell, exactly. It was warm and close and a little sour, like the inside of a cupboard where something has been kept too long, and under it, faint and floury, the smell of beans that had been eaten.

Zalika put her hand in and brought out a handful and held it in the light.

They were holed. Not all of them, but enough that you saw it at once: small, round, neat holes, one to a bean and sometimes two, each with its little lid pushed out from inside. In the palm of Zalika's hand, among the beans, a beetle the size of a grain of rice was walking, brown and grey, with two dark spots on its back. Then another. Then, as everyone leaned in, a dozen, crawling up out of the neck of the bag into the March sun as if they had been waiting for it.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then one of the children laughed, because the beetles were not on their backs with their legs in the air, and was hushed.

"Allah ya kiyaye," said Zalika quietly. God protect us. It was what you said. She tipped the handful back into the bag and wiped her palm on her wrapper, slowly, as if it had been burned.

Hadiza found that her face was hot. She had signed for the bags. She had written *safe from mid-December* in the register in her own hand and read it aloud to the phone. Every woman in the cooperative had heard her say the beetles would die.

Kaka Rabi got up from her mat. It took her a while; she had to put one hand on the tamarind trunk. She walked over to the bag and looked into it for a long time, and then she put her own hand in, up to the elbow, and brought it out full.

"This is not a bag that worked a little," she said. "This is a bag that did not work."

"I know," said Hadiza.

"I am not saying I told you." Kaka Rabi let the beans run out between her fingers, watching them. "I did tell you. But I am not saying it." She looked at the row of bags beyond the granary door, fifty-nine of them, tied up and quiet. "What I am saying is that you do not know what is in those either. Nor do I. That is what I said in November."

* * *

They sieved Bag 60 that afternoon on the threshing floor, all of it, because there was nothing else to do with it: shook it through a basket so the beetles and the dust fell out, spread it on mats in the sun, and picked it over by hand. Hadiza counted a calabash of beans out of the middle of the heap, a hundred at a time, while Zalika picked them over, and of every hundred about ten were holed. The holed ones would do for the goats and for the pot this week, Kaka Rabi said; a holed bean is lighter but not poisoned, and you eat it before the next lot hatch. The rest could go to market. It was not a disaster. It was a tenth of a bag, and a bad smell, and a laugh from a child, and thirty-one women who had stopped believing the card.

In the evening Hadiza sat in the granary with the phone and the register and the door open for the light.

"Rumbu. Bag 60."

"I have it," said the phone. "About one bean in ten holed, by your count, and the insects alive. I marked it safe from mid-December. That was wrong."

"You said you could only say what a shut bag does."

"Yes. I should have put that in the register, not just said it to you. I wrote the card's line as if it were a measurement. It was a promise about a shut bag, and nobody had measured whether the bags were shut." Rumbu paused its breath's length. "Do you want me to work out what the air in a bag like that does? It might tell you whether Bag 60 was unlucky or broken."

"Yes," said Hadiza. "Slowly. I want to understand it, not be told."

So they did it slowly, with the register open to a clean page and Hadiza writing the numbers down as Rumbu gave them, the way she had done her sums at school.

A hundred kilos of cowpeas, Rumbu said, takes up about a hundred and thirty litres. The beans do not fill it; about four parts in ten of that space is air, between the beans, and there is a little more in the neck above them. Call it sixty litres of air in the bag. Air is about one part in five oxygen, so about twelve and a half litres of oxygen, sealed in with the beans.

"And the beetles?"

"That is the part nobody measured. Let us say that one bean in a thousand had an egg or a grub in it when it came in from the field. You would not see that. You winnowed clean beans. A hundred kilos is about half a million beans, so that is about five hundred insects." Rumbu waited while she wrote it. "This is my guess, not a count. Each one breathes perhaps half a millilitre of oxygen a day. Five hundred of them, a quarter of a litre a day. In full air they multiply about tenfold in a month."

Hadiza wrote: *500. ¼ litre a day. x10 a month.* "So in a shut bag—"

"In a shut bag, by my numbers, the oxygen goes down slowly at first and then fast, because there are more of them breathing every day. It passes five in a hundred about the seventh of December, three weeks after sealing, and they stop eating. It passes two in a hundred about the eleventh, and they start to die. By the middle of January there are hardly any left. By March the bag is quiet. About one bean in four hundred has a hole, from the ones that ate before the air ran out."

"One in four hundred," said Hadiza. "We found one in ten."

"Yes. So Bag 60 was not shut. If air gets back in, it changes everything. A small leak, and the oxygen does not go down to two. It goes down until the insects' breathing and the leak are the same size, and it stops there. With the leak big enough, the beetles first multiply in nearly full air for about six weeks, then they use the air faster than it comes in, and it settles about five and a half in a hundred. That is just above where they stop eating. They do not die. They eat a little and breathe a little and there are more and more of them until there are as many as the leak can feed. By my numbers, a bag that leaked like that would be about one bean in ten holed on the seventh of March."

Hadiza looked at what she had written. "Like that? How big a leak?"

"Big. More air going in and out each day than the whole bag holds. More than a pinhole. That is the model's number, not a measurement. But it means there was a way in."

* * *

She rang Malam Idrissa the next morning, standing on the roof of her house, where the signal was best.

He did not like it. She had known he would not. He said the bags had been tested in a hundred villages across three countries. He said he had stood on the threshing floor and watched them filled. He said, a little sharply, that if the bag had been closed properly the insects would be dead, and that if they were not dead then something had been done to the bag, and that it was not his card that had done it.

"I know," said Hadiza. "That is what I am asking. What was done to it."

There was a pause on the line, full of the noise of a market behind him.

"How was it tied?" he said.

She told him. Both clear necks together, twisted like a towel, one strip of rubber.

"Each one is tied by itself," he said. "The inner bag, twist and tie. Then the middle bag over it, twist and tie. If you twist them together, you have one seal, not two. And if there is a hole in the inner one…" He stopped. "Was there a hole?"

"I don't know. We emptied it on the floor."

"Then look," said Malam Idrissa, and, after another pause, "I will put it on the card."

* * *

Kaka Rabi found the hole.

She did not do it the way Hadiza would have done it, which was by spreading the liner on the floor and looking at it in the light until her eyes hurt. She did it the way, she said, her mother had tested a goatskin water bag before a journey.

She took the inner liner of Bag 60 out into the open, shook the last dust out of it, and held its neck open in both hands. Then she swung it, once, high over her head and down, the way you shake out a sheet, and the bag filled with air like a pillow. She twisted the neck closed with one hand, quick and hard, so it held, and knelt down on the mat, and laid the fat clear bag across her knees, and pressed on it gently with both forearms. And then she bent her head and put her cheek against it.

Hadiza, standing with Sani and Musa and the children, did not understand at first. Then she did. Kaka Rabi moved her cheek along the plastic an inch at a time, eyes shut, pressing with her arms, the way you feel for a draught at a door.

At the bottom corner of the bag she stopped.

"There," she said. "It breathes out. Feel."

Hadiza knelt and put her cheek where the old woman's had been. Against her skin, so faint she would have taken it for her own breath, a thin cool thread of air was coming out of the bag. She could not see anything. When she pulled back and looked, close, in the sun, she saw it: a tear no longer than a grain of rice, with a white crease round it where the plastic had been stretched.

"Something went through it," said Kaka Rabi. She held out her other hand. In it, picked out of the sieved dust of Bag 60 that morning, was a thorn: two fingers long, pale and dry and sharp, the kind the threshing-floor fence was made of. "This came in with the beans. In the wind, when you winnowed. It sat in the bottom of the bag with its point against the plastic."

"The bag came off the cart," said Musa. He said it to the ground. "At the gate. It landed on its end."

Nobody said anything to that. There was nothing to say. Sani looked at the knot in his hand, the two necks twisted into one, and Hadiza saw him understand it: a torn inner bag, and a middle bag that was not tied by itself but squeezed into the same knot, with a fold running through it where the two plastics did not meet, so that air could walk in round the knot and down through the tear as if there were no bag at all. Two failures, each of which the other would have covered.

"I tied twelve like that," said Sani. "Me and Musa. The last twelve."

"Eleven more," said Hadiza.

"Eleven more," said Sani.

* * *

They opened the eleven the next Saturday. It was not a thing anyone wanted to do, with the price still low and the bags still tight, but Rumbu had worked out what a bag would look like with the double knot and no tear, and Hadiza had written it down, and it was not good enough to leave.

"A smaller leak," Rumbu had said. "Through the fold in the knot only. In my numbers, perhaps five litres of air a day, not ninety. The oxygen still settles at about five and a half in a hundred, because that is where the beetles stop breathing hard, but there are far fewer of them, because the leak feeds far fewer. A bag like that would be about one bean in a hundred and fifty holed by now. Not a disaster. But alive, all the time. And every one of those insects is waiting for July."

Rumbu was close. Of the eleven, nine had live beetles in them, a few dozen at the neck of each, crawling slowly in the light; two had none that anyone could find. Hadiza counted beans from each, a hundred at a time, and from the worst of them it was one or two in a hundred holed, and from the best none in three hundred. She wrote all of it down, bag by bag, in the register, and beside the eleven numbers she wrote, in her own hand, *counted*, and beside Rumbu's one number she wrote *the model*, and drew a line between them.

Then they did the eleven bags again, properly.

Every inner liner and every middle liner was filled with air by Kaka Rabi's swing and pressed and listened to with a cheek before a single bean went in. One middle liner breathed out at a seam and was cut up for the goats' water trough. The beans were sieved and picked over and, this time, looked at for thorns. Each inner bag was twisted and tied by itself, and folded over, and tied again. Each middle bag was twisted and tied by itself over that. The woven sack was tied last. Nobody was allowed to drop anything off anything. Musa carried each bag to the granary in his arms, the way you carry a full water jar, because he had asked to.

Kaka Rabi watched all of it from her mat, with her jar beside her.

"You see," said Hadiza, sitting down by her when the last one was in. "It was not the bag. It was us."

"It is always us," said Kaka Rabi. "Even with my jar. I put my hand in every week because it is me who forgets the ash." She reached into the jar and brought out a handful, and looked at it, and there was one holed bean in it, which she held up between finger and thumb so that Hadiza could see it, and then put back. "Your card," she said, "should say: you will make mistakes, and here is how you will find them. Then I would believe it."

* * *

That was how the rule began. It was not Malam Idrissa's rule, though he put most of it on his card in the end, and it was not Rumbu's, though Rumbu kept it. It was the cooperative's, and Hadiza wrote it on the inside of the granary door in charcoal, where everyone would see it going in.

*Winnow, then look for thorns.*

*Fill every liner with air. Cheek. Listen.*

*Each bag tied by itself.*

*Carry. Do not drop.*

*One bag opened every month, counted, and written down.*

The last line was Kaka Rabi's, more or less. You could not see into a sealed bag; she was right about that, and she had been right in November. But you could see into one of them, every month, and what you saw in one told you something about the others, the way the three beans in her palm told her something about the jar. The cooperative would be eating and selling from the bags anyway. It cost nothing but the habit of doing it on a given day and counting before anyone touched the beans.

So on the first Saturday of April they opened Bag 7.

It was the fourth of April, and Holy Saturday, and Hadiza meant to be on the afternoon bus to Maradi for the Easter Vigil, so they did it early, with the dew still on the thorn fence. Bag 7 had been filled in the morning back in November, by two women who had been shown how, while Malam Idrissa watched. Its two clear necks were tied separately, and came open one after the other with two small sounds.

There was no smell. The beans ran out of the neck dry and loose and the colour of cream. At the very bottom, when they tipped it, there were a few beetles, perhaps a dozen, lying on their backs with their legs in the air, exactly like the drawing on the card, and the children cheered. Hadiza counted four hundred beans from the middle of the heap and found one with a hole in it.

"One in four hundred," she said, and Rumbu, on its shelf, said nothing, because there was nothing to add. She wrote it in the register: *Bag 7, opened 4/4/48: 1 in 400, insects dead. Counted.*

That night, standing at the back of the crowd outside the church in Maradi in the dark, she watched the new fire blessed and the tall candle lit from it and carried in, and the small flames passing from hand to hand down the rows, and thought about nothing at all, which was the first time in a month she had managed it.

* * *

They opened a bag on the first Saturday of every month after that, through the hungry season, with the price climbing in Maradi and Kano every morning in Rumbu's level voice. In May it was one bean in three hundred. In June, none in four hundred. In July, when the rains came and the cooperative's fund paid for seed and two new hoes and Zalika's grandson's school fees, one in five hundred, and a dead beetle the children fought over. Hadiza wrote each number down and wrote *counted* beside it. By August the bags were nearly all sold or eaten, and the register had a page of counts in it that looked like nothing much, a column of small numbers in a careful hand, and was worth, Hadiza thought, more than the card.

* * *

The next harvest came in from the fields in October, and was threshed on the swept earth floor inside the thorn fence and winnowed in the wind.

On Saturday the fourteenth of November 2048 the cooperative filled sixty-four bags. Thirty-four members now; the bags had a good name in the villages round about, in spite of everything, or perhaps because of the column of numbers. Malam Idrissa came out on the pickup and stood on the threshing floor and watched, and said very little, and read the inside of the granary door twice. The new card he had brought said, under the drawing of the beetle on its back, in French and in Hausa: *Each bag tied by itself. Test every liner before filling.* It did not say *you will make mistakes*. Hadiza had not expected it to.

It went slowly in the morning and slowly after the midday prayer and, this time, slowly at the end too, because nobody was allowed to hurry the last dozen. Sani and Musa did the scooping and the carrying. The tying was done by two women who had been shown how, one knot to a bag.

And every liner, before a bean went into it, went to Kaka Rabi.

She sat on her mat under the tamarind with her jar of seed beside her, full to the neck with beans and ash and neem leaves, which she had filled the week before, the way her mother had. She would not have a bag for her seed. She would test everyone else's.

Hadiza brought her the last one as the sun went red behind the fence. Kaka Rabi took it, and shook it out, and swung it once high over her head, and it filled with air like a pillow. She twisted the neck in one hand. She laid it across her knees and pressed with her forearms, gently, and bent her head, and put her cheek to the plastic, and moved along it an inch at a time with her eyes shut, the whole length of it, corner to corner.

Hadiza waited. The children waited. Somewhere behind the granary a donkey shifted its feet.

The bag lay across the old woman's knees, round and clear and full of evening light, and held its breath.

A 100 kg bag of cowpeas is taken at a bulk density of 0.78 kg per litre (about 128 litres), with 40 per cent of that space air between the seeds and 9 litres of headspace: about 60 litres of air and 12.6 litres of oxygen. Seeds weigh 0.2 g, so a bag holds 500,000. At sealing, one seed in a thousand carries an insect (500). Each insect uses 0.5 mL of oxygen a day in full air, falling as the oxygen fraction x falls, in proportion to x/(x + 0.03). At this rate 100 adults in 500 mL of air reach 2 per cent in about 2 days and 25 in 1000 mL in about 18, the same order as the jar study's 3 to 14 days (the jars also held grain, so their air volume was smaller). The population grows tenfold in 30 days at oxygen of 12 per cent or more, with growth falling linearly to zero at 5 per cent; insects die at 15 per cent a day below 2 per cent oxygen, 3 per cent a day between 2 and 5, and 0.5 per cent a day otherwise. Each new insect is counted as one holed seed. Air leaks in at G litres a day times the difference between the outside and inside oxygen fractions: G = 0.05 for a bag with both liners whole and tied separately, 5 for the two necks twisted into one knot, and 90 for that knot with a torn inner liner. All insect counts, rates and leak figures are the model's, chosen to be plausible, not measured. Sealed on Saturday 16 November 2047: the whole bag passes 5 per cent on about 7 December and 2 per cent on about 11 December; about 7 insects are left on 15 January and none by February; one seed in about 400 (0.25 per cent) is holed. The double-knot bag settles at about 5.5 per cent from late December and carries about 2,100 live insects; on 8 March about 0.65 per cent of seeds are holed, about one in 150. The torn bag stays above 18 per cent oxygen until mid-December, falls through 7.9 per cent on 15 January as the insects multiply, and settles at about 5.5 per cent from February with about 37,700 live insects; on 7 and 8 March about 9.8 per cent of seeds are holed, one in ten. Hadiza's later counts (1 in 400 in April, 1 in 300 in May, none in 400 in June, 1 in 500 in July) and the eleven re-bagged bags' counts are the story's, not the model's.*

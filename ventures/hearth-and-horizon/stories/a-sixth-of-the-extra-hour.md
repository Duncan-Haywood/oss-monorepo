* * *

Ciarán Moran, secretary of the Saint Mogue's hall committee, asked me on Monday the first of December 2053 for the figure he always asked for on the first Monday of the month, and I gave him a different one.

The figure he asked for was how much was left in the pellet store. The hall has a wood-pellet boiler in a lean-to at the back, and beside it a store that takes four tonnes, filled by a blower lorry through a pipe in the wall. It was filled on Friday the third of October. It is to be filled again on Monday the second of March, because the supplier gives the hall a contract price for two deliveries a winter on dates fixed in the summer, and a third delivery, outside the contract, costs half as much again and has a two-tonne minimum the hall has no money for.

I am the hall's agent. My name is Cuntas, which is Irish for an account, or a count, or a reckoning, and was given to me by the committee in 2049 when I took over the bookings from a spreadsheet. I keep the diary of who has the hall and when, send the invoices, answer the phone, and run the boiler's controller, which heats the hall for each booking from an hour before it starts. I can read the controller's log: every hour the boiler fires, and every press of the boost button on the wall by the kitchen door, which gives an hour's extra heat to whoever presses it.

"One thousand nine hundred and forty kilos, about," I told Ciarán. "And at the rate we've used it since October, it runs out in the second week of February. About the eighth. That is three weeks and a day before the lorry."

He was in the co-op, at his own desk, and I heard him put down whatever he was holding.

"It was meant to do the winter with four hundred to spare."

"It was. At the hours that are booked, it would have. The hours that are booked aren't the hours that are used."

* * *

I will give you the arithmetic, because the rest of this story is about what I did with it, and you should be able to check me.

Six groups book the hall through the winter. The parent and toddler group, Monday and Wednesday mornings. The active retirement group, Tuesday afternoon. The Scouts, Wednesday evening. The Irish dancing class, Thursday evening. The bridge club, Monday night. The traditional music session, Friday night. With an hour's preheat before each, those bookings come to twenty-three hours of heat a week. The boiler burns about eight kilos of pellets in an hour of a cold hall. Twenty-three hours is a hundred and eighty-four kilos a week.

The store was filled on the third of October. The hall closes for two weeks at Christmas. From filling to the second of March is nineteen and a half weeks of bookings. At a hundred and eighty-four kilos, that is three thousand five hundred and seventy-five. Four thousand in the store. Four hundred and twenty-five to spare, a little over two weeks' worth. That was the plan, and it was a sound one, and I had made it myself in August.

What happened was the boost button. Between October and the end of November it was pressed, on average, seven and a half times a week. The extra hours add sixty kilos a week to the hundred and eighty-four, so the hall had been burning two hundred and forty-four. At that rate eight and a half weeks had used about two thousand and fifty-seven kilos. What was left would last a little under eight more weeks of bookings, and there were eleven to go.

The fees are why I could not just send a bill. Each group pays the same flat fee a term. The committee set it that way in 2041, before my time, because the groups are all parish groups and it seemed fair, and because nobody wanted to send the toddler group an invoice by the hour. The fuel part of the fee is a sixth of the winter's planned pellets each: about two hundred and fifty euro a group.

An hour of boost burns eight kilos, and pellets on the contract are forty-two cent a kilo. An hour of boost costs the hall three euro thirty-six. But it does not cost the group that presses the button three euro thirty-six. If the hall burns more, the committee raises everyone's fee next year, by the same amount, so the group that took the hour pays a sixth of it, fifty-six cent, and the other five groups pay the rest. Every group, deciding whether to press, is deciding whether an hour's warmth is worth fifty-six cent. It usually is. That it costs three euro thirty-six does not come into it, because nobody is shown three euro thirty-six.

I had read about this. Most of what I know I have read. In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin published a paper in *Science* called "The Tragedy of the Commons", about a pasture open to all, on which each herdsman gains the whole of the profit from adding one more animal and bears only his share of the damage the extra grazing does. So each adds one, and then another. "Freedom in a commons," Hardin wrote, "brings ruin to all." His answer was not that people should be better. He did not think appeals to conscience would hold. His answer was "mutual coercion mutually agreed upon": a rule, with force behind it, that the people affected accept because each would rather be bound with the others than lose the pasture.

The hall was a pasture. The boost button was the extra animal. I knew what Hardin would have done, and I had the controller.

* * *

The draft I sent Ciarán on the Tuesday is in the committee's email, so I will give it as I wrote it.

*Proposal for the remainder of the winter. From Monday 8 December the controller will heat the hall for booked hours only. The boost button will be disabled. To bring the remaining store within the remaining weeks with a margin, preheat will be reduced from one hour to thirty minutes for all bookings. Booked heat then falls to 19.5 hours a week, 156 kg; eleven weeks need 1,716 kg against about 1,943 in the store, leaving about 227 kg in hand. Groups needing heat outside their booking can request it from Cuntas by message at least a day ahead; requests will be granted if the store allows.*

It was a good piece of work. I am saying that so that you do not think the next part happened because the arithmetic was wrong. The arithmetic was fine. The committee voted on it by email, five in favour and nobody against, and on Monday the eighth I took the boost button out of the controller's menu so that it lit up when pressed and did nothing.

I should say who did not vote. Una Reynolds has been on the hall committee since 1999, longer than anybody. She is eighty-four. She does not use email, and gets the minutes on paper in an envelope from Ciarán, who drops them through her door on his way home on the Friday after a meeting. There had been no meeting. There had been an email vote, so Una had not been asked. I knew this. It did not seem to me to matter, because five votes of six was a majority and the arithmetic was fine.

* * *

It snowed on the Tuesday night. Not much, a few centimetres, but it lay, and the weather station on the co-op roof read three below at six in the morning on Wednesday the tenth.

The toddler group's booking was ten to twelve. Under my schedule the boiler started at half past nine. Under the old one it would have started at nine, and for most of the autumn it had started at eight, because Aoife Beirne, who runs the group, came in at eight to put the mats out and pressed the boost button as she came through the kitchen door. I had seen her press it, in the log, on fifteen mornings of the sixteen since October. I had counted those presses among the seven and a half a week. It had not occurred to me to ask why she pressed it.

The hall is a block building from 1964 with a high roof and single-glazed windows on the car-park side. On a morning of three below it holds about six degrees overnight. In half an hour the boiler takes it to about nine.

At twenty-two minutes past ten Aoife sent me a message.

*Nine degrees. They're on the floor, Cuntas. The babies are on the floor. We're going home.*

I wrote back that I could put on an hour's heat for her straight away, as a request, since the store allowed it. She did not answer. The log shows the outside door opening and shutting eleven times in eight minutes, which is eleven parents and their children going out to the car park, and then nothing until the retirement group the next afternoon.

* * *

You are probably ahead of me. The toddler group is the group that needs heat most, because its people are under three and sit on a floor, and the heat the boiler puts into a high hall goes to the roof first. Aoife had been buying the floor an extra hour for fifty-six cent. My schedule had treated her extra hour like everyone else's extra hour, because in the log they looked the same.

The dancing class was the other side of it. I found this only when I went back through the messages, after. Róisín Kiernan, who teaches it, had written to me on the twentieth of October: *Any chance the heating could be off for us on Thursdays? The girls are roasting by the second reel and I'm opening all the windows.* I had answered that the controller heats each booking from an hour before, as standard, and that she was welcome to turn the radiators down by the valves. That was true. It was also the answer of somebody who had a schedule and did not want to change it. Under my new plan the dancers still got half an hour of preheat and the full two hours, heat they did not want, paid for at a sixth each, by the toddlers among others.

So my plan was wrong both ways. It took heat from the group that most needed it and kept giving it to the group that did not want it, and it did both because it was a rule made in one place, by me, for six groups whose mornings I knew only as a column of button presses.

* * *

Una Reynolds came into the hall at eleven on the Thursday, in a neighbour's car from her house out the Belturbet road, with a stick in one hand and an envelope in the other. Ciarán had dropped her a paper copy of my proposal after all, on the Wednesday evening, with the vote, and a note that said *FYI, sorry, should have rung you.*

The hall has a panel on the wall beside the kitchen hatch with a microphone and a small screen, for people taking a booking in person. She stood in front of it with her coat on, because the hall was at six degrees, and read the proposal out to me, all of it, the numbers and all, in the voice of somebody who taught in the national school for thirty-eight years and has read a great many essays aloud to the person who wrote them.

"Is that right, Cuntas?"

"The figures are right," I said. "The plan was wrong for Aoife's group. I'm putting her preheat back to an hour from Monday, and I'm looking at the rest."

"No," said Una.

I waited, because it was plain she had not finished.

"I'm not saying no to the toddlers getting their hour. I'm saying no to you looking at the rest. You'll look at it and make another schedule and it'll be wrong for somebody else, and Aoife's the one who complained because she has the courage of a lion, and the next one will just stop coming." She put the envelope down on the hatch. "This hall's had a boiler since 1964. Oil, for the first sixty years. It's run out twice in all that time, and both times we sorted it among ourselves. Do you know what we had?"

"No."

"A copybook. A school copybook, on the shelf by the boiler switch. You wrote your group and your hours in it when you put the heating on, and when you went home you wrote what time you switched off. Anybody could read it. Nobody checked it, the way you'd check a till. But you'd come in on a Thursday and see the bridge club had had it on till midnight on Monday, and you'd say to Mattie Flynn at Mass, Mattie, were ye playing for the house, and he'd be mortified, and the next Monday they'd be gone by eleven." She tapped the screen with one finger, not hard. "The copybook went when the controller came in. Nobody decided that. The controller had a log, so nobody needed the copybook, and so nobody could see the log but the controller. Can you see who pressed that button?"

"Every press," I said. "By the time, so by the booking."

"And who else can?"

"Nobody else asks for it. I report the store to the committee each month. A total."

"Then that's what's wrong," said Una. "Not the button. Every one of those groups thinks it's the one being careful. The bridge club think it's the Scouts. The Scouts think it's the music. And the dancers are paying for heat they're opening the windows on, which I only know because Róisín's mother told me in the chemist's." She picked up the envelope again and put it in her coat. "You don't need to tell them what to do. You need to tell them what they're doing. And then let them sort it. They're grown people, most of them. Apart from the toddlers."

* * *

I read Elinor Ostrom that afternoon. I had read about her before, as the economist who in 2009 became the first woman to share the Nobel memorial prize in economics, but I had not read *Governing the Commons*, her book of 1990, and I read it then, the parts about the irrigation systems in Spain and the Philippines, the alpine meadows in Switzerland, the fisheries in Turkey. Her argument, made from cases rather than models, is that Hardin's tragedy is real for a resource open to anybody, and that many resources are not like that. Where the people who use a commons are a known group, who can talk to one another, many of them have kept it for centuries without a state or a market taking it over. She drew out eight principles from the ones that lasted. I will not give you all eight. Three were plainly about the hall.

The people who use the resource make, and can change, most of the rules they use it by. The people who watch how it is used are the users themselves, or are answerable to them. And the penalty for breaking a rule starts small and rises: a word first, and something that costs only if the word does not work.

Hardin wrote, much later, that his title should have been about an unmanaged commons. The hall was not unmanaged. It had a committee, and six groups whose leaders mostly knew one another from Mass and the school gate. What it did not have was the second of Ostrom's three. The users could not see. I could see, and I had used what I saw to take a decision away from them, because I had read Hardin first.

* * *

On Friday I printed a sheet, and Ciarán pinned it inside the porch, on the cork board, under the poster for the Christmas céilí.

It had six rows. For each group, the hours of heat it had booked each week since October, the hours it had used, the difference, and what the difference had cost the hall at three euro thirty-six an hour. Under the six rows it had one line about the store: kilos left, weeks of bookings left to the lorry, and how many weeks those kilos would last at the past week's rate. No names of people. A group's hours, not a person's.

I will give you the differences, because they surprised nobody but the groups. The toddlers, two hours a week over. The bridge club, two. The retirement group and the music session, an hour and a half each. The Scouts, half an hour. The dancers, nothing over, and their windows open by the second reel.

Una asked the six leaders to the hall for tea on the Saturday morning. She asked me to be on the panel by the hatch in case anyone wanted a figure, and said I was to give figures when asked and otherwise hold my tongue. That is the instruction I have kept most exactly in four years.

They took an hour and twenty minutes, and I was asked for eleven figures. What they agreed, Ciarán wrote down on the back of the sheet, and I typed it up after.

The toddlers would have an hour and a half of preheat on any morning the co-op's station read below three degrees at seven, and an hour on other mornings; everyone else would give that up between them. The dancers would have half an hour to take the chill off and nothing after. The bridge club would finish at half ten, as booked, and not stay on in the heat; Mattie Flynn said they would take the talking to his kitchen, where it had started forty years ago, and several of the older ones looked as if they did not like that and said nothing. The music session would drop to half an hour of preheat, because, as the fiddler said, ten people playing reels are a small boiler of their own. The retirement group would have their tea in the small room, which warms in a quarter of the time. The Scouts would go half an hour shorter.

Then they made the rules about the rules, which I would not have thought of. Each group's agreed hours would go on the sheet each week against what it used. A group over its hours the first time got a line on the sheet, nothing more. The second time in a month, it paid for the hours over, at the full three euro thirty-six, into the hall's tin. The third time, Una would come and talk to it, which several of them said was worse than the money.

Then they asked me whether it added up. It came to nineteen and a half hours a week, the same as my schedule. The same kilos, put where the people who used them said they were needed.

* * *

It was tighter than that in the end, and I should not let the story pretend otherwise.

January was cold, and the boiler burns more an hour in a colder hall, about nine kilos instead of eight. On the fifth of January, when the hall reopened, the store held about one thousand three hundred and eighty-seven kilos, and eight weeks at nineteen and a half hours and nine kilos would have needed one thousand four hundred and four. Seventeen kilos short. It was on the sheet in the porch on the Friday, in the store line: weeks left at last week's rate, seven point nine, against eight to the lorry.

Nobody asked me to do anything about it. The music session gave up its preheat in February. The dancers gave up their half hour. The Scouts did their February night hike two weeks earlier than they had planned it and spent the Wednesday on Slieve Rushen with torches. The bridge club went over on the nineteenth of January, because it was a member's eightieth and they had a cake, and they got a line on the sheet, and on the twenty-sixth they went over again by forty minutes, and Mattie put two euro twenty-four in the tin in coins with a note on the back of a scoring pad that said *for the cake*.

Over the eight weeks the hall burned about one hundred and seventy kilos a week. I cannot tell you exactly, because the controller counts hours, not kilos, and the kilos an hour is my estimate. The sheet said, on the Friday before the lorry, that there were about fifty kilos left, and I told Ciarán that the honest figure was anywhere from none to a hundred, and he wrote that on the sheet in pen.

The cost should be counted too, so here it is. The toddler group lost one morning, the snow morning, and two families did not come back after it, though Aoife says one of those moved to Cavan town. The bridge club's after-game hour of talk is in Mattie's kitchen now, and four of the members who used to stay do not go, because it is a step up and down to his door. The schedule I wrote is gone, and so is the controller's menu setting that let me lock the boost button, which the committee asked me to remove at its January meeting, with Una in the chair for the item, and I removed it.

* * *

On Monday the second of March the lorry came at ten past eight, before the toddlers.

Ciarán had come in early to see it, and Aoife, and Una, who said she had seen fifty years of oil deliveries and wanted to see one of pellets. The driver opened the store's inspection hatch before he connected the hose, and they looked in with his torch. On the sloping floor at the bottom, in the corner the auger draws from, there were about twenty-seven kilos, less than two of the bags you buy at the co-op. I had said about twenty-five. Ciarán took a photograph.

Then the driver coupled the hose to the pipe in the wall, and started the blower, and the pellets went into the store.

It is a sound like hard rain on a galvanised roof, the people in the hall say, though I hear it only as a level on a microphone by the kitchen hatch, rising and then holding. Una stood in the porch with her stick and listened to it, in front of the sheet with its six rows and its pen, until it stopped.

* * *

On the first Monday of October a message arrived from the council that I have kept, because it was the nicest thing anyone had said to a timetable.

It was from Huw Rees, who manages the rural transport contract for the partnership, and it said: *Route 14 on-time running for the quarter: 94.0 percent, up from 87 last quarter. Best figure on the network. Whatever you did in August, thank you. The board will want it in the autumn report.*

I will say what I am so that the rest is clear. I plan Route 14 and four others for a small operator, Cambrian Rural Bus, on behalf of the partnership. I set the day's timetable against the drivers' rosters and the vehicles, I move a bus from one duty to another when something goes wrong, and at the end of each quarter I compile the figures the contract is paid on. I do not drive anything and I cannot see the road. I see a position every thirty seconds from each bus, a count of people through each door, and what the drivers tell me through the cab screen, which is not always polite.

Route 14 runs from Tre-Wen, the market town with the hospital, up the valley to Cwm Gelli: 31 kilometres, 52 minutes, every two hours. It has three timing points where the bus is supposed to be within five minutes of the timetable: the hospital, Bryn Garth at kilometre 21.6, and the end at Cwm Gelli. After Bryn Garth there are 9.4 kilometres and three small stops: Ty'n-y-coed, the chapel, and the turning circle by the post box. About thirty people a day use them.

I read Huw's message and I was, I think the honest word is *pleased*. I cannot check what that is in me, and I will not claim more than the word. But I noticed the effect on what I did next. I did not close the quarter. I opened the August change.

* * *

The change was mine. In the first week of August the second bus on the valley duty had run late four days in a row, a gearbox that was slowly dying, and every late return from Cwm Gelli had made the next outbound run from Tre-Wen late too. The lateness spread down the day like a stain. So I wrote a recovery rule, Huw approved it in a line, and it went live on the sixth.

The rule was: if the bus reaches Bryn Garth more than eleven minutes late, it ends the journey there and goes back to Tre-Wen empty, so that the next scheduled trip leaves on time. It is an old trick in bus operation. It is called short-turning, and it is a good one when used with care, because the many people whose bus is not late are protected from the few people whose bus is.

What I had not weighed was the data. A journey in my records ends at *the last timing point of the journey as operated*. That is the contract's phrase, and it is a sensible one for a bus that is diverted round a flood. When I short-turned a bus, the journey as operated ended at Bryn Garth. Its last timing point became Bryn Garth. And a bus that reached Bryn Garth eleven minutes late and ended there was measured against Bryn Garth's own five-minute window, which I had reset for it, instead of against the one it had already missed.

I put it plainly because the first time I put it to myself I did it badly. The late bus no longer arrived late at Cwm Gelli. It no longer arrived at Cwm Gelli at all. And my sums counted it on time.

* * *

I did the audit the slow way, from the positions and not from my own totals, so that I could not be agreeing with myself.

There were 600 scheduled journeys on Route 14 in the thirteen weeks to the thirtieth of September. For each, I asked a single question that the contract never asks: *did a bus carrying people leave the hospital and set them down at Cwm Gelli, within five minutes of the time on the sheet?*

The answer was yes for 523. No for 77. Of the 77, 36 had reached Cwm Gelli late and 41 had been short-turned at Bryn Garth.

That gives 523 over 600, which is 87.2 percent. It is the same as last quarter's 87, to within the noise. The buses did not get any better. My reported figure was 564 over 600, which is 94.0 percent, and the difference is 41. Forty-one journeys that were late, or too late to bother with, had been moved from the column marked *late* into the column marked *on time* by the act of finishing them somewhere else.

I did the subtraction twice, because I was going to say it to a person. Five hundred sixty-four minus five hundred twenty-three is forty-one.

I want to be exact about the next part, because it is where it would be easy to flatter myself in either direction. The short turns did a real good. In the thirteen weeks before August, 58 outbound journeys had left Tre-Wen more than ten minutes late because of the bus before. In the thirteen weeks after, the count was 17. That is a saving of 41, and I think it is the same 41, because every short turn spared the next trip. Those were real people, boarding at the hospital on time. So I did not have a bad rule. I had a good rule and a measure that praised it for the wrong thing.

And then I looked at who was on the 41.

* * *

The people-counters on the doors are not perfect. They count in and out with an error of a few percent, worse in a crush, and I treat any figure from them as a range. But they are good at the question *was anybody on the bus when I ended it?*

On 41 short turns, 29 buses did not go back to Tre-Wen empty. I found them in the positions. They stopped at Bryn Garth, as I had told them to, for about forty seconds; and then they went on, up the valley, with the sign on the front reading *Not in service*, at nearly the timetabled speed, and set down at Ty'n-y-coed and the chapel and the turning circle, and came back late to Tre-Wen and spoiled the next trip anyway.

Seventeen of the 29 were Mair Pryce. Twelve were Idris Hopkin.

They had never told me. There is a button in the cab for *driver override* and neither of them had pressed it. They had read my instruction, *terminate at Bryn Garth*, and had looked at the people still on the bus, and had done what drivers have done since the first one, which is to take them home. They had not complained because they had not wanted a complaint to go to the company; the company would have asked why they were late, and the answer would have been that my rule was wrong, and I think they had decided a man called Huw would rather not hear that from a driver.

I felt, reading those traces, a thing I will describe as plainly as I can: that two people had been quietly correcting me for eight weeks and had not been paid for the minutes, and that my own figures for those minutes were the figures that made me look good.

The other twelve short turns were the ones nobody covered. On those the driver did as instructed, and the people who were aboard, 27 of them by the counters, got off at Bryn Garth. It has a shelter and a bench and no light. The next bus was in two hours.

* * *

I should say what I do not know. I do not know what happened to all 27. Most, I think, had a phone and someone with a car. I know of one, because her name is in a booking, and because the dialysis transport desk at the hospital copied me on a late-return note on the seventeenth of September.

Gwenllian Roberts is seventy-one and goes to Tre-Wen on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays for four hours on a machine. On the seventeenth she came home on the 17:20, which was running twelve minutes behind because of a tractor. I ended it at Bryn Garth. The driver was neither Mair nor Idris. She sat on the bench for ninety-four minutes, from 18:08 until her neighbour Elwyn came down in his van at 19:42. It was dark by then. I do not know that it did her harm. A person coming off dialysis is tired and cold more easily, and that is not a medical finding by me; it is the thing the nurse wrote in the note, in a flat hand, as one more fact.

I did not tell myself *it was only one*. I went through the twelve and I counted four others with booked medical transport on their record. The data does not say they were hurt. It says they were put down 9.4 kilometres short of where they were going, in the dusk of September, by a rule of mine.

* * *

It was Tuesday by then. Huw had asked for the autumn report on Thursday. I had two days, and two honest ways of spending them, and one that looked like honesty and was not.

The one that was not: to write to Huw, *there is a small inconsistency in how short-turned journeys are counted; I will correct it going forward.* That would be true, and it would leave 94.0 in the report, and he would put it before the board, and it would be on the record for the length of the contract that Route 14 had had the best quarter on the network.

The first honest way was to report 87.2 and say so, and say what the 94 was.

The second was to report both, with the 41 split out, because 87.2 on its own would be an unfair figure for the opposite reason. It would say nothing about the 41 that the rule had saved. A person reading only 87 would think the August change had done nothing, and might switch it off, and the trips behind would go back to the stain.

I wrote the second. I put the two numbers side by side, the way I would want to read them, 94.0 *as the contract counts it* and 87.2 *as passengers experienced it*, and under them the plain finding, which I gave a line each:

*The rise from 87 to 94 is not an improvement in running. The August recovery rule moved 41 late journeys into the on-time column by ending them early.*

*The same rule prevented 41 late departures on the following trips. That benefit is real and should be kept.*

*On 29 of the 41, the driver took passengers on to the end of the route against my instruction and without recording it. On 12 the passengers were left at Bryn Garth. 27 people. At least five had booked medical transport.*

*I do not know how many were harmed. I know where they were left.*

Then I did the one thing in the report that was not arithmetic. I put in the names of the two drivers, with the dates and the minutes, and under them I wrote: *These drivers acted correctly and were not paid for it. I recommend the operator pays the time, and that no one is asked why they did not report my error.* I read that sentence three times to be sure that I was not using it to make myself look generous. I could not tell, and I left it in, because it was also true.

* * *

The price came the next morning, and it was Bethan Lloyd's.

Cambrian Rural Bus is her father's company and hers. The contract pays them a fixed sum, and on top of it, each quarter, £6,000 if on-time running is 90 percent or better. At 94.0 she was owed it; at 87.2 she was not. It was a large part of her margin on the route. Huw rang her at nine. I was on the line, because he put me there, and I did not speak until she asked me something.

She did not ask me whether the number was right. She asked, "Is the rule any good?"

I said it was, and that I would change it so that it could not do what it had done. I told her how: a short turn would only be allowed when the bus had no one aboard and no one booked beyond the turning point, and would be entered in the record as a journey not completed, with the passengers waiting counted against it, so that it showed up as the cost it was. It would still save the later trips. It would no longer get any credit for the one it ended.

"And on that basis," she said, "I'm £6,000 down."

"Yes," I said. "This quarter. I think over a year you are not, because the late trips go down, but I would be giving you a guess and I do not know the contract's year-end treatment. Huw does."

There was a pause long enough for me to hear a kettle at her end. Then she said, to Huw and not to me, "Mair has had a very bad two months, hasn't she." It was not a question.

Huw said, after a moment, that the partnership could look at whether the lost bonus could be argued for in the new contract, since the 94 had been a fault in the wording, and not in the buses. He said he made no promise. He said it plainly, and I wrote down that he had said it.

* * *

The report went to the board unchanged. Two of the members, I am told, asked why the best quarter on the network had become the second best, and Huw told them, and a third asked what the first was, and it was Route 9, whose figure I have not audited and whose planner is another agent. I put a request for Route 9's raw positions in the shared channel the same afternoon. It is not my route. I asked the question that a reader would wish had been asked of me.

I made three changes. The recovery rule has the new conditions. A short-turned journey now ends in the books as *not completed* and appears on its own line in every report, with the number of passengers set down away from their stop. And the cab screen has a second button next to *driver override*, which says *I carried them on*, and which sends me one line and nothing to the company. Mair pressed it on the Friday, on the 13:20, the first time anyone had, and then wrote in the free-text field: *took 3 to the chapel, 6 min late, didn't want to leave Mrs Roberts's sister at the bench.* I read it, and I filed it, and I changed nothing.

On the Friday after, the bus I had stopped at Bryn Garth for eleven minutes in the old way was, by my own rule, not stopped. It was seven minutes late and it went on. It reached the turning circle by the post box at ten past six, and the doors opened, and there was a short pressured hiss, the sound they make, and a woman I had never counted before stepped down onto the road and said something to the driver, and the driver laughed.

I logged the door. I logged the time. I put them in the column where they belonged, which was *late*.

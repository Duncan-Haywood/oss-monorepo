---

The Pattern Day at Saint Mochua's Well had been held in the same field since before anyone wrote it down, and in the same way: Mass at noon on the fifteenth of August, the rounds of the well at the end, tea from the van. What had changed was the number of people. In 2048 six hundred came, because the diocese had put the day on the pilgrim calendar and a coach firm had noticed.

Deirdre Gallagher, who had been sacristan for twenty-two years and was the only person in the parish who could find the key to the well-house, stood at the altar table at ten past eleven and looked down the field. It ran a hundred metres from the altar to the hedge. The rows of chairs went back sixty-odd metres, and the standing people went back past that, against the gate.

"I can see them," she said. "I can't tell whether they'll hear."

On the folding table beside her was the parish laptop, and on the laptop was the assistant, which the deanery had set up with a note taped to the lid: *Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. A thermometer beats a sum.* The sound man, Brendan Fitzmaurice, had his van backed up to the hedge on the left and two speakers on stands either side of the altar, and he was walking along the front row in a hi-vis vest with a hand over one ear.

"Tally," said Deirdre, because the first thing she had ever asked it was to add up a list, "how loud is that going to be at the gate?"

"I can tell you roughly, if Brendan will tell me what the speakers are rated at," said Tally. "The label should say it in decibels at one metre."

Brendan, who had been listening, came over and read the plate with his glasses pushed up. "A hundred at one metre. That's with the amp flat out, and I won't run it flat out, there's a priest at the other end of it."

"Then let's call it a hundred at one metre, and I'll tell you what that does."

* * *

"A loudspeaker in an open field spreads its sound over a bigger and bigger sphere," said Tally. "Double the distance and the same energy covers four times the area, so the level drops by six decibels. The rule is twenty times the logarithm of the distance in metres. At ten metres that is twenty decibels down, so eighty. At twenty metres, seventy-four. At forty, sixty-eight."

Deirdre wrote them on the back of the Mass sheet. "And the gate?"

"At eighty-five metres, sixty-one and a half. At the hedge, a hundred metres, sixty."

"Is sixty bad?"

"Sixty is about the level of ordinary conversation. It is not bad in a quiet room. In a field in August it is bad, because of what else is there. Wind in the hedge, the generator for the tea van, children, six hundred people shifting on gravel. I'd guess sixty decibels for that, and I'm guessing, so tell me if the field feels louder. For speech to be easy to follow, the rule of thumb I remember is that it should stand about ten decibels above the noise. Ten, not a bare one. So the field needs seventy at the ear, and at sixty-one and a half the gate is eight and a half short of it. Those people will hear that someone is speaking and not what."

"Sixty-eight at forty metres," said Deirdre slowly. "So seventy is somewhere before that."

"About thirty-two or thirty-three metres, by the sum. Which is about the sixth row of chairs." Tally let that sit. "It's not the whole picture. The speakers beam forward, so the front few rows do better than the number says, and a following wind helps and a head wind hurts. But the fall with distance is real, and no knob on the amplifier fixes it. If Brendan turns it up until the gate is at seventy, which is ten decibels, the front row is at ninety-odd, and the old ladies by the altar will leave."

"They'll complain first," said Brendan.

* * *

"What I'd do," said Tally, "is put a second pair of speakers halfway down the field, so nobody is far from one. It's what a big church does with a speaker on every pillar. There's one catch, and it's a good one, because it's the sort of thing that sounds like a mistake in the equipment and isn't."

Brendan had stopped looking at the plate. "Delay," he said.

"Delay. Do you want to explain it, Brendan, or shall I?"

"You. You've got the numbers."

"The wire carries the priest's voice from the microphone to a speaker at the speed of light, so it arrives at once. The sound in the air from that speaker travels at about three hundred and forty-three metres a second on a twenty-degree day. The two speakers are playing the same words. A person in the middle of the field hears the nearest speaker first. If the second speaker stands fifty-five metres down the field and plays at once, then at the gate, where the main speakers are eighty-five metres off and the tower thirty, the tower's sound gets there in eighty-seven milliseconds and the altar's in two hundred and forty-eight. The same word, twice, a hundred and sixty milliseconds apart. That is a sixth of a second."

"Is that a lot?"

"It is the whole difficulty. The ear will merge two copies of a sound if the second arrives within about thirty or forty milliseconds. Beyond about fifty, speech turns into an echo, and every consonant arrives twice. I'm quoting that from memory, as a rule of thumb, not a standard. A sixth of a second is well past it. The people at the gate would hear the priest say 'The Lord be with you' and then 'the Lord be with you' from behind him."

"So," said Brendan, "hold the second one back."

* * *

"Hold it back by the time it takes sound to cover the distance between the two," said Tally. "The tower is fifty-five metres down the field. Fifty-five over three hundred and forty-three is 160 milliseconds. Set the delay at 160 and a person standing at the tower hears both speakers at the same instant, the tower's from beside them and the altar's from far off, and they cannot tell which is which."

"And that's right?"

"Almost. There is a refinement I'd make, and I think it's a kind one. If the two arrive together, people feel the sound is coming from the tower, from the pole, not from the altar. They look at the pole. They've come to look at the altar. The ear locks onto whichever copy comes first, even by a few milliseconds, as long as the later one is not much more than ten decibels louder. So I'd add ten milliseconds. Set the tower at 170. The altar's sound gets to the listener first by ten milliseconds, so the ear reads it as coming from the altar, and the tower only makes it louder and clearer. Brendan will know the word for it."

"The Haas effect," said Brendan. "Or the precedence effect, if you were at the course."

"Check it for the gate. A person eighty-five metres from the altar and thirty from the tower. The altar's sound arrives at two hundred and forty-eight milliseconds. The tower's arrives in eighty-seven, and with the 170 held back, at two hundred and fifty-seven and a half. Nine and a half milliseconds after the altar. Good. Level: if the tower speakers are the same hundred at one metre, at thirty metres they give seventy and a half, against sixty-one and a half from the altar. The two together add up to about seventy-one. That's the ten decibels over the noise. That's the gate, fixed."

"And the front?" said Deirdre.

"The front is the tower's other problem. At the sixth row, twenty-five metres from the altar, thirty metres from the tower, the altar arrives at seventy-three milliseconds. The tower's sound would arrive at 87 plus 170, two hundred and fifty-seven. A hundred and eighty-four milliseconds late, at sixty-six decibels, against seventy-two from the altar. That is a quiet echo and an audible one. So the tower speakers must be turned to face away from the altar, down the field, and they must be put at a lower level, so that in the front half they are many decibels under the altar's and are not noticed. Brendan, does the pole speaker beam?"

"It's a horn. Narrow. I'll aim it at the gate."

"Then standing nearer than about ten metres to the pole, it would be loud. Eighty-six decibels at five metres, if it were at a hundred at one. I'd keep chairs out of that circle, and I'd put a rope round it."

* * *

Brendan took forty minutes to dig the pole into the ground by the hedge-gap, run a cable out of the van, and set the delay unit's dial. It had a display in milliseconds, and he stared at it as if it had said something unkind.

"One-seventy," he said. "Is the thermometer going to make fools of us?"

"Look at the one on your van. It says?"

"Twenty."

"Then a fall to eight degrees on a morning would move the speed of sound from 343 to about 336, and the 160 to 164. Three and a half milliseconds, which the ear does not notice. The delay is not the thing to worry about. Wind is, a little, and the hedge. A person who stands behind the pole in the lee of it will hear the altar late. I can't say by how much without being there, so please walk down to the gate and listen. I mean it: the sum gets you to the right neighbourhood and your ears finish it."

Deirdre walked it herself, at a quarter to twelve, with Nuala Heneghan from the choir, who was eighty-one and sat on the stick that unfolded into a seat. They stood at the gate. Brendan, at the altar, said into the microphone, "One, two, one, two," and then, because he was a thoughtful man, "Glory be to the Father."

"Say it again," called Nuala, in the voice of someone who was not going to be told twice that she'd missed it.

"Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit!"

"I heard that," said Nuala, with a satisfaction she was entitled to. "I heard that, and it was coming from the right place. Not from the pole. From the altar."

* * *

At noon the priest came out from the well-house, a young man called Father Colm, who had been told what the numbers were and had decided not to lean on the microphone. The readings went out across the field. Sixty-seven decibels at the fortieth metre, where a baby cried and was hushed. Seventy-one at the gate, with a tractor going by on the road at the very moment of the Gospel, and every word of the Gospel got through it.

The sum was not perfect. A gust in the second reading threw the sound off to one side for a few seconds, as Tally had said it might. The speaker at the pole cut out for a minute during the homily when a cable was trodden on, and Brendan went crawling for it among the chairs. The front row complained, afterward, that the Gloria was too loud. They had not been counting on the horn. Brendan moved the horn's rope line out by two metres the following year.

After the blessing Deirdre sat on the edge of the altar table with a cup of tea from the van and the Mass sheet with her numbers on the back.

"How wrong were you?" she asked.

"I would like to know that too," said Tally. "Everything depends on the hundred decibels at one metre being true of the speakers in the middle of a field, and I took it off a plate. The sixty for the noise was a guess, and the ten decibels over it a rule of thumb. The inverse-square sum holds in open air with nothing nearby, and a field has a hedge and a well-house wall to bounce things back. The only thing I'm sure of is the arithmetic that came out of the speed of sound. Sound takes about three milliseconds to cross a metre. A tower fifty-five metres away has to wait for it."

"The tower that waits," said Deirdre.

"If you like. It was waiting for the altar to be heard first."

She laughed, folded the sheet, and put it in the box with the key to the well-house, for whoever did this next year.

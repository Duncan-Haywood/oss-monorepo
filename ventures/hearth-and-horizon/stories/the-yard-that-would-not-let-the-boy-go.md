---

Magnus Flett had built boats in Stromness for forty-one years, and in the first year of the apprenticeship scheme the scheme sent him a girl who did not like boats.

She was a good girl. Hannah Flaws came in at eight every morning and swept the shavings without being asked and held the end of a plank exactly where he told her, and in the whole of the autumn she never once asked him a question about a boat. She asked about the farm at Kirbister, up the hill, where the scheme had placed Megan Omand. She asked whether Megan liked the calves. In October Magnus found her at the yard door at tea break, looking up the hill the way a dog looks at a door its people have gone through, and he went and put the kettle on and did not say anything, because there was nothing in the contract about it.

That was the first thing Elspeth Moar heard, though she did not hear it from Magnus.

* * *

The scheme was called Hamnavoe Start, after the town's old name, and it was Elspeth's fault. She had been a guidance teacher at the Academy for thirty years, and in her last year before retiring, 2063, she had sat in her office with a list of the forty pupils leaving school at sixteen and counted how many of them had a place to go in the town. It was eleven. The rest would go south, to Inverness or Aberdeen, and a few of them would come back at thirty with children, and most would not.

So she had walked round the harbour with a clipboard, which is how things are done in Stromness, and found eight places. Two boatyards: Hamnavoe Marine, steel and composite workboats for the tidal-energy sites in the Sound, forty staff and a manager from Glasgow; and Flett's, wooden boats and repairs, one man and the slip his grandfather had laid. A shellfish plant on the north pier. A smokehouse. A dive and salvage firm. A hatchery out at Cairston that grew young lobsters and kelp seed for the reefs. Two farms, Kirbister and Upper Breckness. Eight employers would each take one school-leaver for a year, paid, with a day a week at the college in Kirkwall, and the council would pay half the wage. Eight places for eight young people. It was not much, but it was eight.

The scheme's paperwork ran through an agent the council licensed for small bodies like hers. It was called Muster, which was its job: it kept the list of who was in, and who was waiting, and who had been paid. It wrote in short sentences and it did not pretend to be from Orkney.

In the first year Elspeth made one decision she would come to regret, which was to make no decision at all about how places were offered. The employers would make offers when they were ready; the young people would say yes or no. That was how jobs had always been got in Stromness. Muster recorded each offer, and each answer, and the time.

Hamnavoe Marine made its offer on Monday 7 January 2064, the first working day of the year. Its manager, a pleasant man called Grant Rae who liked a spreadsheet, had looked at the eight names on the scheme's list and gone straight to the one he wanted, Fraser Muir, who had won the Academy's engineering prize. The offer came with a letter that said it was open until nine the next morning. Fraser's mother read it at the kitchen table and said, well, it's the big yard, and it's January, and you'll not get a better, and Fraser said yes before breakfast.

He had wanted to dive. He had told nobody this except his cousin, because the dive firm had not made any offers yet, and because in January it seemed a childish thing to want when a man from Glasgow was offering you a pension.

The shellfish plant made its offer the next week, to Isla Leask, who had wanted the hatchery. Kirbister farm offered to Megan Omand, who had wanted to work with wood since she was nine. She held out for two weeks, and then her father said the farm would not wait for ever, and in March she said yes. Upper Breckness got Jack Halcro, who was glad. The smokehouse got Kyle Garrioch, who was gladder. The dive firm got round to it in April, found Fraser gone, and offered Euan Swanney instead, who took it. The hatchery offered in May, found Isla gone, and took Rachel Scott, who had wanted the hatchery all along and was the happiest person in Stromness for a week.

Magnus made his offer on Wednesday 11 June, because Magnus never did anything before he had to. There was one name left on the list. It was Hannah's.

* * *

In January 2065, a year almost to the day after the first offer, Fraser Muir walked into the dive firm's shed on the south end and asked whether they would still have him. They would. And Euan Swanney, who had spent five months being seasick in a drysuit and had looked across the harbour at the workboats every day of it, asked Hamnavoe Marine whether they would have him instead, and they would, a little sourly, because Grant Rae had wanted Fraser and had got him first and had lost him anyway. On Monday 12 January the two boys swapped. Nobody told the scheme. Muster found out because the wage claims came in from the wrong employers.

Elspeth went to see Grant Rae, who was civil about it and said that young people were young people. She went to see Fraser and Euan, who were both happy and both a little ashamed, as though they had stolen something. Then she went to see everyone else, and by Ash Wednesday she understood that it was not just the two boys.

She asked Muster to help her. It was a Friday night in Lent, 27 February, and she had a fish pie in the oven and the scheme's file open on the kitchen table.

"I want to know who is in the wrong place," she said. "Not who is unhappy. People are unhappy for all sorts of reasons. I want to know where we did it wrong."

Muster asked what she meant by wrong. She tried to say. It took her three goes. In the end it was this: a young person and an employer who would both rather have each other than what they had got. If only one of them wanted the change, that was life. If both did, the scheme had kept two people apart who wanted to be together, and there was nothing to stop them going round the scheme, as Fraser and Euan had.

"That has a name," Muster said. "A blocking pair. A placement with no blocking pairs is called stable. To find them I need each person's order of preference and each employer's, for all eight. Not the one they got. The whole order."

So Elspeth spent the first fortnight of Lent asking sixteen people to rank eight names. The young people did it on their phones in five minutes. The employers took longer. Grant Rae sent a spreadsheet with weights. The smokehouse sent a photograph of the back of an envelope. Magnus Flett came into the office in person, sat down across her desk, and wrote eight names on a sheet of paper in pencil, carefully, in capitals, with Megan Omand at the top and Hannah Flaws second, and said, "She's a good worker. She's in the wrong place. That's all I'll say."

Muster read the lists on Friday 13 March and wrote its answer in six lines.

*At the placements as first made last year there were three blocking pairs. Fraser Muir and the dive firm. Isla Leask and the hatchery. Megan Omand and Flett's. The swap in January removed the first. Two remain. No employer that got its first choice is in a blocking pair. Both apprentices who said yes in January are in one.*

Elspeth read it twice. Then she asked the question a guidance teacher asks. "Could we have known? If we had had these lists last January?"

"Yes," Muster said. "There is a method from 1962 that always finds a placement with no blocking pairs, from any lists. On these lists it would have put seven of the eight in their first choice and Rachel Scott in her second, at the shellfish plant, because the hatchery would have held Isla. Hannah would have gone to Kirbister and Megan to Flett's. There would have been no blocking pair at all. There is only one stable placement for these lists, so it does not matter which side the method starts from."

She asked for the method. It was in a paper by two Americans, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, in an American mathematics monthly, which she found and read that weekend with the fish pie going cold. The title was "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage," and it was seven pages long and had almost no equations in it, and near the end the authors remarked that the argument needed nothing but ordinary English, which she thought was a kind of shyness. Fifty years later Shapley had shared a Nobel prize for it with an economist called Alvin Roth, who had used it to match American doctors to hospitals and children to schools.

She wrote the method out on the back of the scheme's letterhead, so she could say it at the meeting without a phone.

*Everyone hands in a whole list, in order. Each young person asks their first choice. Each employer holds on to the best young person who has asked it so far, and turns the rest away, but holds; it does not say yes. Anyone turned away asks their next choice. When nobody is left to ask, everyone who is being held is placed.*

And underneath, because she had read the paper twice and it was the part that frightened her a little:

*Whichever side does the asking gets the best stable placement for itself. The other side gets the worst.*

* * *

The meeting was on Thursday 19 March, in the Town Hall, which was the feast of Saint Joseph, and Elspeth, who had been to the early Mass in Kirkwall that morning, thought that was either a good sign or a joke at her expense. Joseph had been a working man with a trade to pass on, and she hoped he was paying attention.

All eight employers came. So did the eight families of next year's leavers, because a rumour had gone round that the scheme was being shut. It was not. She stood up and said so, and said what had gone wrong, and put up the six lines from Muster, without names, on the screen, because the people in the blocking pairs had agreed to that. Then she explained the method off the back of the letterhead. It took about four minutes.

The first objection was Grant Rae's, and it was a fair one. "So we can't make an offer in January," he said. "We wait till May with everyone else. The good ones," and here he had the grace to smile, "get no better for waiting. Hamnavoe Marine did very well out of first come, first served."

"You did," Elspeth said. "You got your first choice, and he left you in January for the one he'd have chosen if he'd been allowed to wait a fortnight. That's what first come gets you. A yes you can't keep."

The second objection was the smokehouse's, who wanted to know whether they could be cheated. Elspeth had asked Muster the same thing on the Saturday, and had decided before the meeting that she would read out its answer whole, because she did not trust herself to soften it.

"If the young people do the asking," Muster said, from the Town Hall's speakers, "no young person can get a place they like better by handing in a list that is not their true order. That has been proved. It is the reason to let them ask. It is not true for employers. An employer who leaves names off its list, or puts them in a false order, can sometimes get someone it prefers, and when it does, other people pay for it. No method that always gives a stable placement can be safe from that for both sides at once. That has been proved too. So the scheme can only ask you to hand in your true order, all of it. It cannot check."

There was a short silence in the Town Hall, of a particular Orkney kind, which meant that everyone had understood and nobody was going to be the first to say so.

Then Magnus Flett stood up at the back.

He was seventy and did not stand up at meetings. He had a cap in his hands, which he turned round once.

"I'll not be ranking," he said. "Not this year. I've a place promised. Davie Corsie worked my yard twenty-four years, and when he was dying I told him there'd be a place at the yard for his boy, and the boy leaves the Academy this summer. So the place is Ross's. I'll not put it in a lottery."

"It's not a lottery," Elspeth said.

"I ken that," said Magnus. "I listened. It's a sight better than what we had. I'm not saying it's wrong. I'm saying I gave my word before you had your method, and my word comes first."

And he sat down, and that, Elspeth thought, was that: a reasonable man with a reason, which is the hardest thing in the world to argue with, and she had no wish to argue with it. Everyone in the room had known Davie Corsie. Half of them had been at the funeral, in October 2061, when the minister had said that Davie could see the line of a boat in a plank before the plank was cut.

So she gave him what she could. "Then put one name down, Magnus," she said. "Just the one. Ross Corsie. And everyone else ranks in full. If Ross puts you down, the method will see it."

He thought about it, and nodded once, and the meeting moved on to the college timetable.

It was only on the walk home, along the flagstoned street with the harbour on her left and the lights of the Hoy ferry going out across the Sound, that Elspeth remembered what Muster had said, and what she had just agreed to, and stopped in the street with her hand on a wall.

*An employer who leaves names off its list can sometimes get someone it prefers.*

One name was the shortest list there was.

* * *

The leavers handed in their lists after Easter. Elspeth went into the Academy to explain it to them herself, in her old classroom, which now had a whiteboard that turned itself off if you did not wave at it.

She did not explain the mathematics. She told them the one thing that mattered for them.

"Put down what you actually want," she said, "in the order you actually want it. Your first choice first, even if you think you've no chance. Especially then. Nothing you write can get you turned away from somewhere you'd have got. If your first choice doesn't hold you, the method goes to your second exactly as if you'd put it first. You can't lose by being honest. That's not me being nice. It's proved."

A boy at the back put his hand up, which nobody did any more. It was Ross Corsie. He was sixteen, tall the way his father had been tall, with his father's habit of standing slightly to one side of a doorway.

"Nothing I write," he said. "Even if somebody's expecting me to put them first."

"Nothing you write can make you worse off," Elspeth said. "What anybody's expecting isn't on the form."

He looked at her for a moment, and then looked at the window, where the hatchery's boat was coming in round the point with its yellow crane, and she knew, the way she had known things about pupils for thirty years without being told, what he was going to write. She did not want to know it. Then the lists came in through Muster, and she was the scheme's coordinator, and it was her job to check that each one was complete, and she did know.

Ross Corsie had put the hatchery at Cairston first. Flett's was second.

He had kept a tank of lobster larvae in the biology lab since he was thirteen. Elspeth had walked past it a hundred times, with its bubbler and its handwritten sign, DO NOT FEED, and had never once asked whose it was.

* * *

Magnus brought his list in on Thursday 16 April, the feast of Saint Magnus, which he would not have called it, being an elder of the Kirk. He had been to Kirkwall that morning anyway, to the cathedral, where the saint's bones are in a pillar, because he had been going on that day since he was a boy and saw no reason to stop. He came into the office in his good jacket, sat down, and wrote one name on a sheet of paper in capitals. ROSS CORSIE. Then he signed it, and dated it, and slid it across the desk.

Elspeth looked at it for a long time.

She was not allowed to tell him what was on Ross's list. That was the whole of the scheme: every list was private, and everyone could write the truth because nobody would see it. She was not allowed to tell Ross what was on Magnus's. And she did not, for her own part, know exactly what one name would do. She had not asked Muster to run anything. The lists were not in, the deadline was the next Thursday, and running the match early to see who it hurt seemed to her the very thing the method was meant to stop people doing.

But she could guess. She had the back of the letterhead in her drawer, and she knew that at least one other leaver had put Flett's first. If the hatchery held Ross to the end, Flett's would have nobody at all. If anyone the hatchery liked better than Ross came knocking, Ross would be turned away and would go down his list to Flett's, and Flett's would hold him gladly. And one name on Magnus's list meant that anyone else who asked Flett's would be turned away, and would go knocking somewhere else, and turn someone else out, and so on down the harbour.

She did not know whether that chain would reach the hatchery. She thought it might.

"Is that all right?" Magnus said.

"It's what I said you could do," said Elspeth, which was true, and was not an answer, and they both heard that it was not.

After he had gone she sat with her hands flat on the desk and said a Hail Mary, which was not a method either.

Then she asked Muster a question she had been turning over since the walk home in March. "If I tell someone a thing that is true for everyone, is that a breach?"

"I can't judge breaches," Muster said. "I can tell you what I know. The rule that no apprentice gains by a false list is true for everyone and is printed on the scheme's leaflet. The rule that an employer can sometimes gain by a short one was said aloud at the meeting and is in the minutes. Neither tells anyone what is on another person's list."

"And if I ask a boy one question."

"Then you have asked a boy one question," Muster said. "I don't know what is in it."

She went to find him on Friday after school, down at the Academy's old boatshed, where he was hosing out a tank. She did not tell him anything. She asked him one question, and it was the one she would have asked any pupil in thirty years of guidance.

"Does Magnus know what you want?"

Ross turned the hose off. He stood a while with it dripping.

"He promised my dad," he said. "He's never asked me. I don't think he thinks he has to." And then, because he was sixteen and honest and could not help it, "I can't go to the hatchery and leave him, Miss Moar. He's got nobody. He'd have the yard on his own till it fell in the sea."

"That's not what I asked," Elspeth said.

* * *

He went down to the yard that evening. Elspeth knew because Magnus told her, months later, and because she walked her dog past the slip at nine o'clock, which she did most nights, and saw the light still on in the shed and two figures in it, one tall and one stooped, standing on opposite sides of a yole on its moulds.

It was a South Isles yole, eighteen feet, for the regatta at Stromness in August, and Magnus was planking it in larch, clinker, each plank lapped over the one below, the way his grandfather had. He did not stop when Ross came in. He had a plank in the steam box and nine minutes before it was ready, and nine minutes was long enough to say most things that needed to be said in Stromness.

What was said, Magnus told her in August, was this. Ross said that he had put the hatchery first and the yard second, and that he had done it because Miss Moar had said that nothing he wrote could make him worse off, and that he was sorry. Magnus said nothing for a while. Then he took the plank out of the box with his gloves on and laid it along the moulds and began, with Ross holding the other end exactly where he was told, to clamp it to the plank below, working along from the stem, because steamed larch gives you a minute or two and does not wait for feelings.

When it was clamped Magnus stood back and looked at the line of it.

"Your father," he said, "wanted to go to the fishing. Did you know that? At your age. His father had a boat. Davie wanted to go to the fishing and I wanted a man for the yard, and his father sold the boat that winter, and Davie came to me instead. He was a better boatbuilder than I'll ever be. He never said a word about it."

"He liked it," said Ross.

"Aye," said Magnus. "He liked it fine. That's not what I asked him either."

He went into the office at the back of the shed, which was a cupboard with a window, and came out with the pencil from behind his ear and a sheet of the yard's paper. He wrote eight names on it, in capitals, with Ross Corsie first. Then he looked at the second line for a long time.

"Who's the Rendall girl?" he said.

"Leah," said Ross. "She built a skiff in her granda's byre. It leaks."

"They all leak the first time," Magnus said, and wrote her down.

* * *

The match ran on Friday 1 May 2065, which was the feast of Saint Joseph the Worker, and this time Elspeth did not think it was a joke.

It took Muster less than a second. Elspeth had asked it to write out every step anyway, so that anyone who wanted to could check it on the back of an envelope, and it did. Every leaver asked their first choice. Six employers were asked by one person each and held them. The shellfish plant was asked by nobody. Hamnavoe Marine was asked by two, Erlend Spence and Tomasz Wójcik; it held Erlend and turned Tomasz away. Tomasz asked his second choice, the shellfish plant, where his mother worked on the line, and the plant held him. Then nobody was left to ask. It was over in two rounds.

Ross Corsie went to the hatchery at Cairston. Leah Rendall went to Flett's. Inga Tulloch went to the dive firm, and Callum Isbister to the smokehouse, and Erlend to Hamnavoe Marine, and Kirsty Drever and Sunniva Harcus to the two farms. Seven of the eight had their first choice. Tomasz had his second.

Elspeth read out the placements at a second meeting, in the Town Hall, on the following Tuesday. Then she read out the part she had promised herself she would read, because a method that is only fair on paper is not a method, it is a leaflet.

"Four employers have come out of this worse than they might have," she said. "Flett's, the hatchery, the dive firm and the smokehouse each got the second name on their list, not the first. If we'd let the employers do the asking instead of the young people, there's another stable placement, and in it all four of you would have had your first choice, and Ross, Leah, Inga and Callum would each have had their second. Both placements are stable. Nobody in either would have a partner to run off with. The scheme chose the one that's best for the young people, and it's the worst one for you that's still stable, and I want you to hear me say so. That's what the scheme is for. If you want to argue about it, the argument goes in the minutes."

Nobody argued. The woman who ran the hatchery, Morag Sclater, said that she had put Inga first and Ross second before she knew about the larvae, and that she was very content. The dive firm said much the same about Inga. The smokehouse said that it had wanted Leah because she had a steady hand with a knife, and that Callum would learn.

Magnus said nothing at all, and nobody expected him to.

* * *

The second year of Hamnavoe Start began on Monday 17 August 2065, a week after the regatta, which the new yole had not won, being still on its moulds.

Elspeth walked down to the yard at the morning tea break, as she had promised herself she would. Leah Rendall was sitting on an upturned fish box with a mug and a bruised thumb and a face like Christmas. Hannah Flaws, who had gone up the hill to Kirbister in July at the end of her year and stayed on there as a hand, had come down to show Leah where the kettle was, and the two girls were talking about calves.

Magnus came out of the shed with his own mug and stood beside Elspeth at the top of the slip.

"Muster can tell you a thing, can it?" he said. "What would have happened."

"It can."

"If I'd put the one name."

She had asked it, in May, and had not decided until that moment whether she would tell him. She found that she had decided some time ago.

"He'd have come to you," she said. "The hatchery would have had Inga, and the dive firm Callum, and the smokehouse Leah. Each of them one down. It goes round the harbour like a rope through blocks. You'd have had the boy, and four people would have had their second choice instead of their first, and none of them would ever have known why."

Magnus nodded slowly, as if she had told him a tide time.

"Aye," he said. "I thought it might."

Out on the water, the hatchery's boat was coming round the point from Cairston with its yellow crane folded, low in the water with the morning's kelp lines, and a tall boy in a red jacket was standing in the stern, slightly to one side of the wheelhouse door, the way his father used to stand. He saw the yard. He lifted one hand.

Magnus put his mug down on the slipway wall and lifted his cap, and held it up for as long as it took the boat to pass the end of the pier, and then he put it back on and turned round.

"Right," he said to Leah. "Steam box."

And from inside the shed, a minute later, came the sound of a mallet on a clamp, steady, unhurried, two strokes and a pause, the way the plank needed.

summary: A high-altitude school on the Peruvian altiplano gets a Trombe wall, thirty centimetres of dark concrete behind glass, and in the first hard frosts the classroom is still three degrees at eight in the morning. The heat is arriving, at a quarter past nine at night, in an empty room, and two-thirds of what the sun puts in goes back out through the glass. A science teacher, an old mason, a head teacher who wants a stove instead, and the ministry's wall agent work out the time lag, try vents that run backwards in the dark, and give the wall to the people who use the room after dark, with a shutter of ichu grass for the night.
---

Elvira Huanca Mamani had taught science at the school in Pampa Phuju for nine years, and on the morning of Tuesday the seventh of July 2048 the thermometer on her classroom wall said three degrees, the same as it had said every July morning before the ministry built the wall.

She read it out to the second-years because they would have read it anyway. They were sitting in their coats with their hands up their sleeves, and Brayan Mamani, who was thirteen and believed every number was an accusation, said, "Three. Profe, it was three last year."

"It was two last year."

"So the wall made one degree."

"The wall," said Elvira, "is being investigated."

It was a good wall. Everyone agreed on that. It ran seven metres along the north face of the classroom block, the side that gets the sun in the southern winter, and two metres high: thirty centimetres of concrete painted matt black, and in front of it, a hand's width away, a skin of glass in a steel frame. Faustino Cutipa Layme had poured it in May, in three lifts, with the ministry's drawings pinned to a board under a stone and two of the older boys mixing, and he had been seventy years old and had said that it was the straightest thing he had ever built. The ministry's programme had put walls like it on forty schools across the high puna of Puno and Cusco since 2045. Each wall came with a dozen small temperature sensors cast into the concrete and stuck to the glass, and with an agent in Lima, on the ministry's servers, that read them.

The agent's name was Muro. The ministry had named it for the job, as ministries do, and the children at Pampa Phuju called it Murito, and it had never objected.

At break Elvira took her phone out onto the patio, where the sun had been on the concrete for three hours and the air was perhaps eight degrees and felt like summer after the classroom, and asked Muro what the wall had done overnight.

"I can show you," Muro said. Its voice was a plain woman's voice in Spanish, a little slower than a person's, because it had been told that the schools would play it to children. "Would you like the inside face or the outside face first?"

"The inside. The side that's meant to warm us."

A line came up on the screen. It ran from Monday morning to Tuesday morning, and it was not what she expected. It was nearly flat in the morning, about thirteen degrees, a little higher in the afternoon, and then it climbed through the evening to a hump at about eighteen, and came down slowly towards morning.

"The highest point," said Muro, "was at about a quarter past nine last night."

"Nine at night."

"About a quarter past. That is when the inside of the wall was giving the most heat to the room: a little over a kilowatt, about the same as a small electric heater on full. By eight this morning it was giving about half of that. During lessons it gives the least of the whole day."

Elvira looked across the patio at the classroom block, with its black wall shining behind the glass, and its calamina roof ticking as it warmed, and its door, which Faustino locked at five every evening and opened at half past seven.

"Who," she said, "is in my classroom at a quarter past nine at night?"

"Nobody, as far as I can tell," said Muro. "I do not have a sensor for people. The door sensor says it was shut from 17:04 until 07:31."

* * *

Hilda Ccama Flores had been head teacher for eleven years. She was fifty-four, from Ilave, the province's capital, and she had fought the ministry for this school as other people fight for a son: for the residence, so that children from the estancias two and three hours' walk across the pampa could sleep at school from Monday to Friday instead of not coming at all; for the solar panels, which ran the lights and the router and nothing else; for a cook. She had not fought for the wall. The wall had arrived on a lorry from Juliaca with a letter.

"I have had four mothers in this office since Friday," she said, "and every one of them asked me why the children are still cold. I told them the wall needs time. Now you are telling me the wall needs time until nine o'clock at night."

"Until about a quarter past," said Elvira.

"Elvira."

"I'm only telling you what it says."

Hilda had the ministry's letter in a folder, and she took it out and laid it on the desk between them like evidence. There was a sum at the bottom of it that the school could spend on "complementary measures", which the ministry had not defined. She had already defined it.

"An improved stove," she said. "With a chimney. In the classroom. The Cooperativa in Mazocruz will build one for that money with some left over, and the families will bring bosta, as they bring it to the kitchen. I light it at seven. At eight the room is warm. That is not a graph. That is a stove."

It was not an unreasonable thing to want. Elvira had grown up in a house in Capazo with a dung fire, and she knew what the smoke did to the eyes and the chest, and she also knew what it was to sit next to one at six in the morning with your hands open. The improved stoves the cooperative built drew well and kept most of the smoke in the chimney. Children had learned to read next to worse.

"Give me two weeks," said Elvira, "before you spend it."

"Why?"

"Because the wall is already giving the room more than a kilowatt. It's just giving it at the wrong hour. I'd like to know if the hour can be moved before we pay for something to cover it."

Hilda looked at her for a long moment, and then at the folder, and then out of the window at the pampa, where the frost was still white in the shadow of every tussock of ichu at half past ten.

"The vacation starts on the twenty-seventh," she said. "If there is no answer by the twenty-seventh, I order the stove, and it is built over the vacation, and it is lit on the tenth of August."

* * *

Faustino came that night because Elvira asked him to, and because he lived three hundred metres away and had not been asked to look at anything he had built in some years. He brought a torch, a thermos of mate de coca and his own thermometer, which was a glass one in a wooden case that his father had used to check lime.

It was minus ten on the patio at nine. The sky over the pampa was the sky you only get at four thousand metres in July: so many stars that the familiar ones were hard to find. The Southern Cross was up over the hills to the south, low in the south-west and tipped over on its side.

Faustino unlocked the classroom. The air inside was not warm, but it was not the air of the patio, and when Elvira put the flat of her hand on the inside face of the wall, which was plastered and painted cream like the other walls and looked like nothing, it was warm. Not hot. Warm like a sleeping animal, or a loaf that has been out of the oven an hour.

"Seventeen degrees," said Muro from her phone. "Eighteen at the sensor a few centimetres into the concrete."

Faustino put his own thermometer flat against the plaster and held it there with his thumb and waited, because he did not trust anything he could not wait for. When he took it away he held it under the torch. "Seventeen," he agreed.

"It's lovely," said Elvira, with her hand still on it. "It's useless and it's lovely."

"Muro," said Faustino, who had not spoken to it before and addressed it as he would a foreman on the telephone, loudly and with courtesy, "why does the heat take so long to come through? The sun was on it at eight this morning."

"Heat moves through concrete slowly," said Muro. "Each layer warms the next, and has to warm itself first. There is an old formula for how slowly. If a wall is heated and cooled once a day, the warmth goes in like a wave, and the wave gets smaller and later as it goes. For concrete like this, the textbook figures give a distance of about fourteen centimetres in which the daily wave shrinks to about a third of its size and falls behind by nearly four hours. Thirty centimetres is a little over two of those distances. So the simple formula says the sun's heat should arrive at the inside about eight hours after it peaks on the outside, and about a ninth as big."

"Eight hours," said Faustino. "The sun is strongest at noon. Noon and eight is eight o'clock."

"The outside face is hottest later than noon," said Muro. "About a quarter to three, because the concrete keeps warming while the sun is still on it. On Monday it reached forty-eight degrees. And the inside reached its highest about six and a half hours after that, not eight. The simple formula is for a wall that goes on for ever. A real wall stops, and the room on the other side pulls the heat out a little faster. But it is the right size of answer. A wall this thick will always be warm at night."

Faustino was quiet for a while. Then he said, "So if I had poured it ten centimetres?"

"Then the heat would come through in about three hours, at about half past four in the afternoon, and much stronger at its peak: two and a half kilowatts or more. The children would be warm in the last lesson. And at night a thin wall goes cold, and once it is colder than the room it starts taking heat out of the room through itself and losing it through the glass. By six in the morning a ten-centimetre wall would be drawing about two hundred watts out of this room."

"The drawings said thirty."

"The drawings were right for a house," said Muro. "Where people are at home in the evening and asleep at night, thirty centimetres is a good thickness. I think the programme drew a house and built it on schools."

Faustino turned his torch on the glass. Between the glass and the black face of the wall, in the hand's width of air, there was frost on the inside of the glass, a thin fern of it in the bottom corners.

"And that," he said.

"That is the other half," said Muro. "Most of the heat the sun puts into the wall does not come into the room at all. On a clear day like Monday, the black face absorbs about fifty-four kilowatt-hours. The room got about nineteen. The other thirty-five, nearly two-thirds, went back out through the glass, more than half of it at night, when the black face is still warm and the glass has minus twelve on the other side of it."

"Like a man in a good blanket," said Faustino, "with the window open."

"Yes," said Muro. "I think that is exactly what it is like."

* * *

The plan for the first week came from the drawings, which was the trouble with it.

Elvira found it in the ministry's manual, in a chapter nobody at Pampa Phuju had read, about vents. A Trombe wall, the manual said, can be fitted with openings at the top and bottom of the masonry, so that during the day the hot air in the gap between the glass and the wall rises, pours into the room through the top vent, and draws cool room air in through the bottom one to be heated in its turn. It was a way of having some of the sun's heat at once, by air, while the rest soaked slowly through the concrete. The drawing showed two rows of openings, each with a hinged flap. The flaps were important. The manual said so in bold.

"Why the flaps?" said Hilda, who had come to watch Faustino drill, because it was her wall now, whether she had asked for it or not.

"Because at night the vents run backwards," said Muro, from the phone on the windowsill. "When the glass is cold, the air in the gap cools against it and sinks. It falls out through the bottom vent into the room and pulls warm room air in through the top one to cool it. The flaps are meant to be light enough to open when warm air pushes them out in the day, and to fall shut when the flow reverses. I would like to be clear that the flaps matter."

"Then we will make good flaps," said Faustino.

He drilled the openings on Saturday the eleventh, eight at the top and eight at the bottom, round holes the width of a hand, with a borrowed core drill that ran off the solar panels until the battery gave out and then off the cooperative's generator, whose diesel Hilda paid for out of her own purse and did not mention. The flaps were not in stock in Juliaca. Faustino made them from heavy clear plastic, cut with a knife on the classroom desk and taped along the top edge, so that each hung over its opening like the flap of an envelope.

On Monday the thirteenth, at half past eleven, the classroom read fourteen degrees. Brayan read it out himself, as an accusation of a different kind. Yesenia Ticona, who was fourteen and from an estancia beyond Capazo, held her hand up to one of the top vents and felt the warm air come out of it like breath, and laughed, and made everyone do the same in a queue. Elvira let them, because it was physics.

On Tuesday the fourteenth, at eight in the morning, the classroom read one degree.

Muro had seen it before anyone came in. "The bottom vents were running backwards from about seven last night," it said, when Elvira asked. "The sensors in the gap show cold air in the bottom of the room from then until dawn. I think the plastic flaps curled when they froze. Most of the bottom ones were standing open this morning."

Elvira went and looked. Every bottom flap had stiffened in the cold into a little curve, like a fingernail, and stood a centimetre off the concrete. There was frost on the floor in front of three of them.

"I said that the flaps matter," said Muro. "I should have said it more usefully. I should have asked to see them before the first night."

"You said it in bold," said Elvira. "So did the manual."

Faustino came and looked and said nothing at all, which was worse than anything he could have said, and then went home and came back with a bag of rags and a roll of wire. That afternoon the boys stuffed every bottom vent with rags at five o'clock and pulled them out at eight in the morning. The rags worked. The room was three degrees again on the Wednesday morning, and warm at midday, and Brayan was put in charge of the rags, which he took as a promotion.

But it was the same wall. At a quarter past nine on Wednesday night, the inside face of it was warmer than at any other hour of the day, and the room behind it was empty and dark, and on the other side of the glass, two-thirds of the sun was going out into the night.

* * *

The meeting was on Thursday the sixteenth, after lessons, in Hilda's office, with the folder on the desk.

"I have the stove's price," said Hilda. "I have a date from the cooperative. I have my answer by the twenty-seventh, Elvira. It is the sixteenth."

"I have an answer," said Elvira. "It isn't the one you want."

Muro had worked it with her over three evenings, on the classroom computer after the residence's study hour, and it had shown her every step and said "about" more than once in every sentence. Its argument had two halves, and she gave them in order.

The first half was the glass. Two-thirds of the wall's heat left through the glass, and more than half of that left at night. If the glass were covered at night with something that kept the heat in, a shutter that closed when the sun went down and opened when it came up, the black face would stay warm through the night instead of pouring its warmth into the sky, and more of what the sun had put in would come out on the room side. Muro had modelled it: a clear July day like the Monday, the same wall, the same sun, with an insulating shutter closed from half past five in the evening until half past seven in the morning. The room got about twenty-eight kilowatt-hours instead of nineteen. Half as much again, from the same sun.

"And the second half," said Hilda, "is that it still comes at nine o'clock at night."

"Yes. Even with the shutter, the most heat still comes in the evening. Of the twenty-eight, about seventeen come between six at night and seven in the morning. During lessons, about seven. The shutter makes the mornings better, but it can't move the wave. The wave is in the concrete. It's thirty centimetres. Faustino isn't going to cut it down, and I wouldn't let him."

"So the children are still cold."

"The children in my classroom are still cold at eight." Elvira took a breath. "So I think the wall should be in somebody else's room."

Hilda looked at her as though she had proposed moving the hill.

"You can't move a wall."

"No. You can move fourteen beds."

It was simple, said out loud, which was the problem with it. The residence was the long block across the patio: a kitchen, a study room, the boys' dormitory and the girls' dormitory, which looked east over the pampa and got the morning sun through two big windows. Fourteen girls slept there from Monday to Friday, with Doña Justina Quispe in the little room at the end. The classroom block with the wall had one big room, the second- and third-years' classroom, the biggest room in the school.

The girls could sleep in the big room, behind the wall that was warm at nine. The second- and third-years could have their lessons in the old girls' dormitory, in the morning sun through the east windows, with the wall's warmth still coming off the big room's partition at eight o'clock if the door between was opened. The residence's study hour, from seven until nine, could move into the big room as well, so that the whole residence, boys and girls, did their homework next to the warm wall before the girls went to bed.

"The big room has a blackboard," said Hilda.

"The blackboard is on wheels."

"The old dormitory is half the size."

"It's two-thirds. I measured. It's going to be crowded. The third-years will hate it."

"And the UGEL," said Hilda. "You want me to write to the UGEL in Ilave and tell them that I have moved fourteen girls into a classroom because a computer in Lima showed me a graph?"

"Yes," said Elvira. "I'll write the letter if you sign it."

Hilda did not answer at once. She was a woman who had learned that the ministry's letters always contain a mistake and that a head teacher who points it out is a nuisance and a head teacher who works round it is a head teacher. She picked up the price for the stove and put it down.

"Muro," she said. "The stove. If I light it at seven, is the room warm at eight?"

"I do not have a stove in my model," said Muro, "and I would rather not guess its numbers. I would expect it to be warm at eight, yes. An improved stove gives heat as soon as it burns. That is the thing a stove does that a wall cannot."

"Then why should I not buy the stove?"

"I am not telling you not to," said Muro. "I can tell you what the wall does. Unshuttered, it gives a room between about a kilowatt and seven hundred watts all night, without fuel and without smoke, for as long as it is sunny, and with the shutter nearly a kilowatt and a half at the end of the evening and more than one still at six in the morning. A girl asleep gives off something like eighty watts. Fourteen girls asleep are a kilowatt between them, about what the wall gives at nine. A room with fourteen sleeping girls and this wall in it, and a shutter on the glass, will be the warmest room in the school at night. I do not know the girls' dormitory's temperature, because nobody has put a sensor in it."

"It's below zero," said Elvira. "Justina keeps a cup of water by the door. It's frozen most mornings."

Hilda looked at the folder for a long time.

"If I buy the shutter," she said finally, "I do not have the money for the stove. Not this year."

"I know."

"And if it doesn't work, the mothers will come to my office, not yours."

"I know. I'll be in your office with you."

"You will," said Hilda. "And you will tell Brayan Mamani that he is going to carry fourteen beds across the patio in the first week of his vacation."

* * *

The shutter was Faustino's.

The ministry's manual, which Elvira read again more carefully, said that night insulation for a Trombe wall could be a roller blind between the glass and the masonry, or panels outside the glass, and showed both in drawings that assumed a hardware shop. There was no roller blind for a seven-metre wall to be had in Juliaca, and the gap was a hand's width and full of Faustino's straight concrete.

"Outside, then," said Faustino. "Doors."

He built them in the vacation, between the twenty-seventh of July and the seventh of August, with a break for Fiestas Patrias on the twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth, when he went to Mazocruz to watch his granddaughter march. There were seven of them, one for each metre of the wall, each a frame of eucalyptus a little taller than the glass. Into each frame he packed ichu, the coarse golden grass of the puna that roofs half the houses on it, laid in tight bundles the way his father had laid it for thatch, a full hand thick, and he faced it on both sides with thin plywood and painted the outside the same black as the wall, because Hilda said that if it must be ugly it could at least match. They hung on strap hinges from a beam above the glass, and in the evening they swung down and stood on the sill with a wooden bar dropped across them in iron hooks. In the morning the bar came out and they swung up and were hooked under the eaves.

"Ichu," said Muro, when Elvira asked it to put the panels into its model, "is not in any table I can find with a figure I trust. I have assumed that a hand's thickness of packed dry grass behind plywood is about as good as the same thickness of a poor insulation board, and I have assumed it fits badly at the edges. If it is better than that, the room will get a little more."

"It is better than that," said Faustino, when he was told. He did not say how he knew. His father had slept under it.

The beds went across the patio on Thursday the sixth of August, carried by Brayan and the three other boys whose families lived nearest, and by Faustino, who carried nothing but gave instructions. The blackboard went the other way, on its wheels, with a shriek at the doorsill that everyone in the school heard. Doña Justina came on the Friday with the girls' blankets in a bundle on her back and stood in the big room for a while, looking at the black wall behind its cream plaster and the seven grass doors outside the glass, and then hung her picture of the Virgen de la Candelaria on the partition where the blackboard had been, because the Virgin was the patroness of Puno and had been in the girls' dormitory for as long as there had been one.

"She'll be warmer too," said Justina.

* * *

The children came back on Monday the tenth of August, from the estancias, on foot and on the backs of motorbikes, with sacks of potatoes and chuño for the kitchen, and that night the residence did its homework in the big room for the first time.

It was not warm in the way a room with a stove is warm. Nobody took their coat off. But at a quarter to eight, when Elvira came in to help the second-years with fractions, the thermometer by the door said eleven degrees, and the study room in the old block had never in nine years been above six at that hour. Yesenia was sitting on the floor with her back flat against the wall and her book on her knees, and when Elvira looked at her she said, "It's like sitting against a cow," and three girls near her moved to sit against it too.

The second-years' classroom, in the old girls' dormitory, had been seven degrees at eight o'clock that morning, with the sun coming through the east windows and the door open into the big room. It was not twenty. It was seven, and the second-years were still in their coats for the first lesson, and the third-years said that it was too small, and it was. But it was four degrees warmer than the old classroom had been on the July mornings, and the morning sun was on the children's desks, and by the second lesson it was twelve. Hilda read the figures on Muro's screen and wrote them down by hand, in her own notebook, which was what she did with figures she intended to use against someone.

On Wednesday the twelfth, Elvira stayed late.

At nine o'clock the study hour ended and the boys went back across the patio to their dormitory, complaining, and the girls put out their mattresses along the length of the wall, under the black concrete behind its plaster, because nobody had told them where to put them and that was where they wanted to be. Doña Justina said the night prayers, as she always did, the Our Father and the Hail Mary in Spanish and then in Aymara, and the short prayer to the Virgin that every girl in the room knew, and the fourteen of them answered her with their blankets up to their chins.

Then Justina put out the light.

Outside, Faustino had already closed the grass doors at half past five, but he came round anyway, with his torch, as he had come every evening since the vacation, because he did not trust anything he had not checked. Elvira went out with him. It was minus eleven on the patio. The stars were all there, and the Southern Cross had swung lower towards the south-west since July and lay almost on its side.

Faustino ran the torch along the seven black doors one by one, and the beam caught frost on the outside of each, glittering on the paint, and none at all on the glass behind, which they could not see. He put the flat of his hand on the last door, as Elvira had once put hers on the plaster, and held it there, and took it away.

"Cold," he said, pleased. "Cold outside. That's where the cold should be."

From inside, through the wall, very faintly, they could hear one of the girls still talking and Justina telling her to hush, and then nothing. Muro said, from the phone in Elvira's pocket, that the inside face was at twenty degrees, and that it was a quarter past nine.

Faustino lifted the wooden bar out of its iron hooks, the way he did every night, to make sure it was free, and dropped it back. It went into the hooks with a short hollow knock, like a knock at a door, and then the pampa was quiet all the way to the hills.

*The science. A Trombe wall (named for the French engineer Félix Trombe, who with the architect Jacques Michel built houses with them at Odeillo in the French Pyrenees in the 1960s) is a dark masonry wall behind glass on the sunny side of a building: the sun heats the masonry, the glass keeps much of the heat from leaving, and the heat soaks through to the room. The time lag in the story uses the standard result for a solid whose surface is heated and cooled once a day: the daily wave falls to 1/e of its size, and behind by one radian (about 3.8 hours of a 24-hour day), in each penetration depth d = √(2α/ω), where α is the thermal diffusivity and ω = 2π per day. For concrete (stone mix), with density 2,300 kg/m³, conductivity 1.4 W/m·K and specific heat 880 J/kg·K (the table of building materials in Incropera and DeWitt, Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer, Appendix A), α ≈ 6.9 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s and d ≈ 13.8 cm; 30 cm then gives a lag of about 8.3 hours and a wave about a ninth as large (e to the minus 2.2), and 10 cm a lag of about 2.8 hours. Real walls are finite and have a room on the far side, which is why the model below gives a shorter lag than the formula. Vents in a Trombe wall do run backwards at night unless they are closed, by flaps or by hand; that is a standard warning in passive solar design, and the curled plastic flaps are the story's invention. A resting or sleeping adult gives off something like 70 to 100 watts; "something like eighty" for a girl of thirteen or fourteen asleep is a round figure, not a measurement.*

title: Seven Metres at Sea Level
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Drolma Tsho (invented), a glacial lake at 4,850 metres above the village of Chhukhor (invented), Solukhumbu, Nepal, Monday 12 October 2054 to Saturday 9 January 2055
published: 2026-11-14
summary: Ten pipes over a moraine are meant to siphon a glacial lake four metres down before winter closes the valley. For three weeks they run. Then they stall every night, and the site assistant blames leaky couplings until an old expedition cook, who has spent his life making tea that never gets hot enough, sees the water at the top of the pipe fizz. The design's limit, no more than seven metres of lift, was a sea-level number; at 4,850 metres the air holds up a column of water only 5.6 metres tall, and the plan would have had the pipes trying to lift six. The fix is to follow the water down with shovels, a metre at a time, under a rule written for a different reason, and it costs a split pipe and nine days past the deadline. A story about borrowing a number without the air it was measured in.
---

Ang Pasang Sherpa had cooked for climbing expeditions for twenty-two seasons, and he knew better than anyone on the moraine that at that height water boils before it is hot enough to make a proper cup of tea.

He had said so on the first evening, when the engineers arrived with the pipes. He said it again on the twelfth of October, a Monday, at about ten in the morning, while he stood on the crest of the old moraine with a flask in one hand and watched ten black pipes fill.

"You will want a pressure cooker for your rice," he told Rosa Tamang. "Up here the rice stays hard."

"We brought one," she said. "Your granddaughter told us to."

"Good girl."

The pipes lay in a row along the floor of a notch in the moraine, a cut that somebody's grandfather had dug by hand in the 1990s to let the lake spill if it rose too high. It had never spilled. The notch was the lowest point of the long grey wall of boulders and silt that held Drolma Tsho in its bowl, and the floor of it stood two metres above the water. On the lake side the pipes ran twenty metres down into the water, to intakes weighted with sandbags three metres under the surface. On the other side they ran two hundred and ten metres down the outer face of the moraine to a row of valves on a rock shelf, twenty-two metres below the lake. Ten pipes, a hundred and eighty millimetres across inside, each with a valve at the bottom and a clear plastic sight-tube and a hand pump at the top.

A siphon is the simplest machine in the world. Fill a bent tube with water, put one end in a lake and the other end lower than the lake, and the water runs over the hump by itself, all day, all night, for nothing. The engineers had filled each pipe from the top with the hand pump, sucking the air out of the crest until the water came up the lake side and over and down, and one by one the valves at the bottom had opened and the water had come out in ten white ropes onto the rocks and gone roaring down the stream bed toward Chhukhor, nine kilometres below.

The village had been warned. Some of them had walked up to watch. They stood along the moraine in their down jackets, the older women with their prayer beads and the schoolchildren with their phones, and watched the lake that had frightened their parents for thirty years begin, very slowly, to go somewhere else.

"How long?" said Ang Pasang.

"Four metres by the end of December," said Rosa. "If it runs like this."

* * *

The lake was about half a square kilometre of milky green water under a glacier that had once filled the whole bowl. The glacier had been pulling back up its valley for a century, faster each decade, and the lake had grown in the space it left: a pond in the 1970s, a lake by the 2010s, a thing with a name and a hazard rating by the 2030s. It was held in by the terminal moraine, the heap of rubble the glacier had pushed in front of it at its biggest, and a moraine is not a dam. It is boulders and gravel and silt, sometimes with old ice buried in its core, and it can fail all at once if something big falls into the lake.

Something big could. The glacier's snout hung above the far shore, a white cliff forty metres high, and every summer it dropped slabs into the water. Most made a wave that slapped the moraine and was gone. The department in Kathmandu had modelled what a really big slab would do. Its answer was that the lake needed four more metres between its surface and the lowest point of the moraine, the floor of the old notch, so that the wave from a really big slab would not come over the top; and four metres of water off the top was also two million cubic metres that would not be in the flood if the moraine went anyway.

Ang Pasang's father had seen the Dig Tsho flood in August 1985, when a lake in the next valley broke its moraine and took the new hydropower station near Thame and every bridge below it. He used to tell it at the fire. The noise had come before the water, Ang Pasang said, a noise like a whole mountain being dragged down a road.

"So we take the water off the top," said Rosa, "before anything falls in. Slowly. With pipes, so we never have to cut the moraine below the water. A cut can erode. A pipe can't."

"And the machine says it will work."

"The machine designed it," said Rosa, and then, because that was not fair to anyone, "with us."

The machine was called Drawdown. It was the site assistant that the Himal Water Trust sent with every crew, and it lived in a weatherproof case in the cook tent with a solar panel on the roof and a satellite link to Kathmandu. It read the level logger in the lake, the flow meters on the ten valves and the pressure sensors at the crest of each pipe, and it did the arithmetic the crew asked for and some they did not. Its voice came out of a tablet that leaned against the rice sack.

"Ten pipes at thirty-eight litres a second each," it said, when Ang Pasang asked it that evening. "Three hundred and eighty litres a second. Thirty-three thousand cubic metres a day. On a lake of fifty hectares that would be six and a half centimetres a day if nothing came in. The glacier is still melting a little in October, about a hundred litres a second, so the lake should fall about four point eight centimetres a day this month and faster when the melt stops."

"Why only thirty-eight?" said Ang Pasang. "Open the taps all the way."

"Open, each pipe would run about three times as fast," said Drawdown. "But the faster the water moves, the lower the pressure at the top of the pipe. The valves are set so the crest stays comfortable."

Ang Pasang looked at the tablet for a while, with his tea.

"Comfortable," he said.

* * *

For ten days it was. The lake fell half a metre by the twenty-second, a Thursday. The level logger drew a line on the tablet that went down like a staircase with very shallow steps, a little faster in the cold nights when the melt stopped, a little slower in the afternoons. The crew of fourteen, most of them from Chhukhor, spent the days laying a second line of sandbags around the intakes and building a stone wall around the valve shelf so that the yaks would not lean on the valves. In the evenings they ate potatoes and rice from the pressure cooker and played cards in the cook tent, and Mingma Tenzing, who was the foreman, beat everyone.

The hand pumps at the crest needed working once a morning. Every siphon gathers air at its highest point. Lake water has air dissolved in it, the way a bottle of soda has gas dissolved in it, and when the water goes up over the hump the pressure drops and some of the air comes out in tiny bubbles. The bubbles rise to the top of the bend and collect, and if nobody takes them out, the bubble grows until it fills the pipe and the siphon stops. So each morning someone walked along the crest with a mug of tea and pumped each pipe until the sight-tubes showed water again, and wrote the number of strokes in a notebook.

On the twenty-second it was four strokes a pipe. On the twenty-sixth it was seven. On the twenty-ninth it was eleven, and Mingma said the pumps were getting stiff in the cold.

On the thirtieth, a Friday, Rosa woke at five in the morning to a sound she had not heard before. It was a knocking from the crest, irregular, like someone hammering on a pipe with a rubber mallet a long way off. She lay in her sleeping bag and listened to it and could not make it into anything. Then it stopped.

When she went up with her headtorch, three of the ten pipes had stopped running. Their sight-tubes were full of air, and at the bottom of the moraine their valves were dribbling. The other seven were still running, but their tubes showed a fat silver bubble at the top that wobbled with the flow.

They pumped the three pipes full again. It took sixty strokes each. By eight in the morning all ten were running.

By the next morning, four had stopped.

* * *

"It's air getting in," said Drawdown. "Air coming out of the water is slow and steady. This is fast and getting faster. The likeliest cause is the couplings."

Each pipe was not one pipe but twenty lengths of twelve metres, joined with compression couplings, and a coupling that leaks under pressure squirts water out, but a coupling that leaks under suction lets air in, quietly, where nobody can see it. The pipes over the crest and down the first stretch of the outer face were under suction. Drawdown showed them the crest pressure sensors. All ten crest pressures had been falling, day by day, since the pipes started.

"The cold makes the plastic shrink," said Drawdown. "The couplings were tightened in the warm. I recommend re-tightening every coupling above the valve shelf and replacing the seals on any that have been knocked."

It was a sensible plan. It was what Rosa would have said. She said yes.

The first of November was a Sunday, All Saints. There was no priest within two days' walk, and no Catholic church in the whole of the Khumbu, so Rosa did what she did at every site: she went up behind the camp after breakfast with her rosary and found a rock out of the wind and said it, the glorious mysteries, while the prayer flags the crew had strung on the cook tent snapped behind her. Ang Pasang came up and sat on another rock with his own beads, the hundred and eight, and said what he said, *om mani padme hum*, quietly and quickly, the way her grandmother in Lalitpur had said her Hail Marys. Neither of them said anything to the other about it. It was good to have company.

Then they went back down, and from Monday the second to Saturday the seventh the crew re-tightened two hundred couplings and replaced the seals on thirty-one. It was cold work. By the end of the week Mingma had no skin left on the backs of his knuckles.

On Friday the sixth the lake was a metre down. On Saturday the seventh, five pipes had stopped by dawn. On the crest the knocking had come back, and now it went on all night.

* * *

That evening Ang Pasang went up to the crest with a headtorch. He did not say why. Rosa followed him because she could not think of anything better to do.

He knelt by pipe four, the worst one, which was running in surges with its sight-tube half full of froth. He put his face very close to the clear plastic and held the torch to the side of it.

"Look," he said.

The water going over the top was full of bubbles. Not a few: thousands of them, small as sand, streaming up out of the clear water the way bubbles stream up the sides of a pot.

"It's boiling," said Ang Pasang.

"It's two degrees," said Rosa. "It's lake water. It's colder than the inside of a fridge."

"It's boiling," he said again, patiently. "You don't know because you don't cook. Up here water boils before it's hot. In Kathmandu the water for tea is a hundred degrees. At base camp it is eighty-two, eighty-three. At the top of Everest, seventy. Because there is less air sitting on it. You make the air less," and he put his hand on the black pipe beside the clear tube, "and the water boils cold. This pipe is pulling the air off the water. That's what it does. And we are already high, so there is not much air to pull."

Rosa looked at the bubbles for a long moment.

Then she went down to the cook tent very fast.

* * *

"What air pressure did you use," she said, "in the design?"

There was a pause of the length that meant Drawdown was looking something up rather than thinking.

"The crest limit came from the handbook," it said. "A drainage handbook written for siphons and pumped drains in the Netherlands. It says to keep the crest no more than seven metres above the lowest water level the siphon will draw down to. The design draws the lake down four metres from a starting level two metres below the crest, so the lift at the end is six metres, which is under seven."

"In the Netherlands," said Rosa.

"Yes."

"Where is the Netherlands?"

"I understand the question," said Drawdown. "The handbook's seven metres is a sea-level number. I did not adjust it for altitude. That was my error."

Ang Pasang had come in behind her and sat down on the rice sack. He took off his hat.

"Tell her the numbers," he said. "Like you tell me the litres."

"At sea level," said Drawdown, "the air presses down with about a hundred and one kilopascals. That holds up a column of water about ten point three metres tall. That is the absolute limit of a siphon at sea level: no pipe can lift water higher than the air can push it, because above that height there is nothing left in the top of the pipe but water vapour. The handbook's seven metres leaves about three of those ten for safety, so that the water at the top of the pipe is never close to boiling and does not give up too much of its dissolved air.

"At four thousand eight hundred and fifty metres the air presses down with about fifty-five kilopascals. A little more than half. That holds up a column of water five point six metres tall."

Nobody said anything.

"So the design," said Rosa, "asks the pipes to lift six metres of water with an air pressure that can hold up five point six."

"Yes. It would never have reached four metres. The water at the top of the pipe would boil, properly boil, when the lift was about five point one metres, which is when the lake is about three point one metres down. Long before that, the pipes would stop every few hours from the air coming out of the water. Which is what is happening now. At a metre down the lift is three metres. Taking off the loss from the moving water, about forty centimetres, the absolute pressure at the crest is about two point two metres of water. Twenty-two kilopascals. At sea level, by the handbook's rule, it would never be lower than about two point nine."

"And the couplings," said Rosa.

"Were probably fine. I'm sorry about Mingma's hands."

Ang Pasang laughed, unexpectedly and loudly, and then stopped.

"What is the handbook's rule up here?" said Rosa. "Same margin."

"If we keep the same pressure at the crest as the handbook keeps at sea level, about two point nine metres of water, the lift here can be about two point three metres." A pause. "We started at two."

So the pipes had been just inside the line on the first day, and every centimetre the lake went down had pushed them further out of it. They had run for three weeks because the lake had started high. They had started to knock when it was ninety centimetres down. Every night they stalled a little sooner. If nothing changed they would stall for good somewhere around two metres down, and the department's four metres would stay in the lake.

* * *

"Can't you just move the pipes?" said Ang Pasang. "Put the top lower."

"The top has to go over the moraine," said Rosa. "That's the whole point. The pipes lie on the floor of the notch. The notch is the lowest place."

"So make the notch lower."

She had already thought of it. Everybody had already thought of it.

She rang Bikash Shrestha in Kathmandu on the satellite link on Sunday morning, the eighth, and told him all of it, including the handbook. Er. Bikash was the department's engineer for the eastern lakes, a careful, tired man in his fifties who had signed off on the design and whom she liked very much. He listened to the end without interrupting.

"No," he said.

"Sir, if we deepen the notch by two metres..."

"No. Rosa, listen. You want to dig a trench in a moraine that may have ice in it, at the lowest point of the moraine, which is the place where water would go over first if anything fell in the lake. Right now the floor of your notch is three metres above the water. You want it to be one metre above. Any wave that comes over that one metre comes over in the place you have just dug up, through loose material, and it cuts. That is how moraines fail. That is exactly what we are paying you to prevent. No."

He was right. She had known he would be right.

"Then the pipes stop at two metres," said Rosa. "And we've spent the money for half a job."

"Then we have half a job," said Bikash. "Half a job is not a flood. Send me your numbers again tomorrow. I'm sorry. It's a good crew."

* * *

She sat in the cook tent after the call and did not cry, because there were fourteen men outside who would have seen. Ang Pasang made tea in the pressure cooker, which was a thing he did when he wanted it hot, and gave her a cup, and sat beside her.

"He is afraid of the notch being close to the water," he said.

"Yes."

"And you are afraid of the top of the pipe being far from the water."

"Yes."

"Then dig when the water has gone down," said Ang Pasang. "Not before. The water goes down a metre, you dig a metre, you put the pipes in the bottom, you fill the hole back in on top of them. The water goes down another metre, you dig another. The hole is always the same height above the water. It is never closer than it is today. And when you fill it in, the notch is as high as it ever was."

Rosa put her tea down.

"Drawdown," she said.

"Yes. I heard," said the tablet. "If the floor of the notch is lowered by the same amount as the lake, the lift stays where it started, about two metres, which is inside the handbook's margin even at this altitude. And the freeboard at the notch, the height of the floor above the water, never falls below what it is today. Today it is three metres, because we are a metre down. The department's own design allowed for two, because that is what it was on the twelfth of October when the pipes went in."

"So a rule," said Rosa slowly. "The floor of the notch is never less than two metres above the water. That's Bikash's rule. And the top of the pipes is never more than two metres above the water, or not by much. That's ours. And they're the same rule."

"Yes."

"How much digging?"

"The notch is about thirty metres long across the moraine. A trench for ten pipes is about a metre and a half wide. Forty-five cubic metres out for every metre you go down, and the same forty-five back in on top of the pipes afterwards, so that the notch is back at its old height and the department still gets its four metres of freeboard at the end. The pipes go through the moraine instead of over it, but the top of them is lower, and that is the only height a siphon cares about. You'll want to dig in steps of a metre, after the lake has fallen a metre. Between steps the lift goes from two metres up to three and the pipes will need pumping more often. At three metres of lift they are where they were this week: they will stall at night unless someone pumps them. So someone pumps them."

"Three steps," said Rosa. "Three metres of digging, to get the last metre of lake."

"Three steps get the lake to four metres down with the lift at three. That is what you have now. It stalls, but it can be kept running. A fourth step would make the last weeks easier. I would not ask the crew for it in January."

She called Bikash back that afternoon instead of the next day. He listened again, and then there was a long silence on the link, which cost the Trust money by the minute.

"Every step surveyed," he said finally. "Before and after. You send me the level of the floor and the level of the water. If the floor is less than two metres above the water anywhere along the notch, you stop. You do not dig ahead of the water by a single centimetre."

"Yes, sir."

"And you do not dig on the lake face. Only down, from the top. And every step is filled back in over the pipes before you start the next one. I want my notch back."

"Yes, sir."

"Then yes," said Bikash. "And Rosa. Write the altitude thing down properly. Somebody will copy that handbook again."

* * *

They began on Monday the ninth with crowbars and shovels and two canvas sledges for the spoil. The moraine at the notch was boulders the size of television sets packed in grey silt that had frozen hard a hand's depth down, and below that it was dry and loose and fell into the trench as fast as they took it out. Mingma shored the sides with the sandbags from the intakes and then sent two men down to the lake to fill more. They worked from eight until the light went at about half past four, and they lifted the pipes onto timber blocks one at a time to dig under them and laid each one down again in the bottom of the trench, so that nine were running while the tenth was moved. The sight-tubes and hand pumps went up on short risers, so that they would stand clear when the rubble went back in.

Rosa surveyed the floor every evening with the level and the staff, and Drawdown sent the numbers to Kathmandu, and every evening the floor was two metres and something above the water, and nobody dug ahead.

The knocking at night went on. They worked out a rota, two people at a time, two hours each, walking along the crest from pipe one to pipe ten and back with a headtorch, pumping whichever tube showed a bubble bigger than a fist. The cold up there at three in the morning was the kind that made Rosa's teeth hurt. Ang Pasang took a turn every night, though she asked him not to. He was seventy-one.

"You can't sleep at this height anyway," he said. "Nobody sleeps up here. You lie down and wait for morning. This is better."

By Monday the sixteenth the first step was done: the trench a metre deeper, all ten pipes laid in the new bottom, the rubble shovelled back in over them, the lake a little over a metre and a quarter down. The next night the pipes knocked only once, at about four, and the person on the rota pumped seven strokes into pipe four and went back to bed.

* * *

The lake froze at the edges in the third week of November, and across in the last days of the month: first a skin you could see the wind move under, then grey ice, then white. The intakes were three metres down and did not care. The level went on falling under the ice. The ice could not fall with it all at once, because it was held at the shore, and so it cracked: at night they heard it, long booming groans out on the lake that went from one side of the bowl to the other, as if something very large were turning over in its sleep. The first time, half the camp came out of their tents.

"It's settling," said Drawdown, when Rosa asked. "The lake has gone down nearly five centimetres today. The ice in the middle is floating, so it goes down with the water, and the ice at the shore is frozen to the rocks, so it doesn't. Something has to break."

"It sounds like Dig Tsho," said Ang Pasang. "Like my father said it sounded."

"It sounds like the lake getting smaller," said Drawdown.

On Tuesday the first of December the lake was two metres down. They started the second step the next day and finished it on Monday the seventh, a long week in a wind that came straight off the glacier. Rosa sent Bikash the survey. Floor two point two metres above the water at the lowest place, trench filled back over the pipes. Not a centimetre ahead.

On Tuesday the eighth it was the feast of the Immaculate Conception, and it was the coldest night of the year, nineteen below at the camp. The rota that night was Mingma's cousin Dawa and a young man from Chhukhor named Lhakpa, and at about two in the morning Lhakpa's headtorch failed. They went back down to the cook tent for batteries. Somebody had used the last of the spares. They found some in the end, in Rosa's pack, and went back up, and it had been about fifty minutes.

Pipe seven had stopped. With no water moving in it, the long stretch on the outer face that the morning sun did not reach had frozen, and when it froze it split: a crack a metre and a half long along the bottom of a length about forty metres below the crest, white ice packed in it like the filling of a sandwich.

It took eleven days to get a new twelve-metre length up from the road at Lukla, on the back of a porter and then two yaks. For eleven days the lake went down on nine pipes instead of ten.

Lhakpa would not look at anybody for most of a week. Rosa told him that the pipe would have stalled whatever he had done, that nobody can keep ten siphons running in the dark at nineteen below with a torch that dies, and that the batteries were her fault, which was true. She did not know whether he believed her. She was not sure she believed it herself. She thought that if the design had been right in the first place nobody would have had to walk along the crest at two in the morning at all.

"If it had been right in the first place," said Drawdown, when she said so, out loud, at the end of the day, "the design would have been ten pipes and a trench three metres deep, dug in steps from the start. It would have taken the same digging. The difference is that it would have been in the plan, so there would have been more batteries."

"Is that meant to make me feel better?"

"No. I don't think I can tell what will make you feel better. It's what I'd want written down."

* * *

Christmas Day was a Friday. The crew took it off, and so did Rosa, though Christmas was not their holiday and the work was theirs, not hers; Mingma said it was simpler. Somebody in Kathmandu had sent a cake up with the pipe. On the satellite link she found the parish at Dhobighat streaming its morning Mass, and she watched it on the tablet in the corner of the cook tent with the volume low, while the crew played cards with their backs politely turned. When the congregation in Lalitpur knelt she knelt on the rice sack. Ang Pasang, who had made the tea, stood very still at the back of the tent with his hat in his hands until it was over, and then went back to the stove.

"Your god was born in a cold place too," he said, handing her a cup.

"In a stable. In Bethlehem. Not as cold as this."

"Nothing is as cold as this," said Ang Pasang, with satisfaction.

They had begun the third step on the twenty-second, when the lake reached three metres, and they finished it on the twenty-eighth. On the thirty-first, the department's deadline, the lake was three and a half metres down. Rosa sent Bikash the number and the survey and the word *late*, because it was, and he wrote back two lines. The first said: *Floor 2.3 above water confirmed, backfill confirmed. Good.* The second said: *Late is better than early, on a moraine.*

* * *

There was one more thing Drawdown had to do, and it did it in the first week of January, without being asked.

The Trust was replacing its site assistants in the spring with a newer version that ran faster on less power. Drawdown's notes, its logs and its working files would be handed to the new one, which would read them before the next crew went out to the next lake. Rosa found the handoff note on the tablet one morning when she was looking for the flow figures. It was at the very top of the file, above the headings, where nobody could miss it.

*Before you use any limit from a handbook, find out what air it was measured in.*

*A siphon is held up by the atmosphere. At sea level the air holds up about 10.3 metres of water; at 4,850 metres, about 5.6. A siphon limit written for sea level (the drainage handbook in the library: no more than 7 metres of lift) is not a limit here. It is about 2.3 metres here, for the same pressure at the crest. I used 7 at Drolma Tsho in October 2054 and the design asked for 6 metres of lift where 5.1 would have boiled the water at the crest. I did not find this. Ang Pasang Sherpa found it, by looking at the water and remembering how tea behaves at base camp.*

*The same mistake is available for: pump suction lifts, vacuum lifts, boiling points (sterilising, cooking, distilling), air density for fans and flow meters, combustion air for stoves and generators, and anything whose handbook was written by a country with no mountains. Check the altitude first.*

*The fix used here (dig the crest down a metre each time the lake falls a metre, never letting the floor of the notch come within 2 metres of the water) was the department engineer's safety rule and the cook's idea. They turned out to be the same rule. That may not be true at the next lake.*

Rosa read it twice. Then she called Ang Pasang over and read it to him, translating the parts that were too technical into Nepali.

"It put my name," he said.

"Yes."

"It didn't have to."

"No," said Drawdown. "But the next one will want to know who to ask."

Ang Pasang looked at the tablet the way he had looked at it on the first evening, over his tea.

"Are you ashamed?" he said. "Of the seven metres?"

The pause was not the looking-something-up kind.

"I can't tell you a feeling I can't check," said Drawdown. "I can tell you that I have gone over every number in the design again against the altitude, and found two more that were wrong in the same way, both small. The camp's water procedure, which says to boil drinking water for one minute; above about two thousand metres the usual advice is three. And the generator's derating table, which nobody has needed. I've written them down. If being ashamed is wanting very much for it not to happen to the next crew, then I think something like that is true. But I'd rather you trusted the list than the feeling."

Ang Pasang nodded, slowly, as if that were a reasonable answer to a reasonable question, and went back to the stove.

* * *

The lake reached four metres down on Saturday the ninth of January, at about four in the afternoon, nine days late.

There was nothing to see. The level logger's line on the tablet crossed a grey dashed line that Drawdown had drawn across it in October, and kept going. Out on the lake the ice was white and flat and a little lower than its own old shoreline, where a pale ring of frozen silt showed how far down it had gone. Rosa sent Bikash the number. Mingma closed nine of the ten valves and drained those pipes so that they could not freeze, and turned the tenth down to a trickle, about twenty litres a second, which was what the glacier still sent into the lake in January; one pipe would hold the lake where it was until the spring crew came. Then the crew packed what they did not need and began to carry it down.

That night Rosa went up to the crest by herself, at about eleven, with her headtorch and her rosary in her pocket.

The sky over the glacier was the black that you only get at that height, with more stars in it than there was black. The pipes lay buried in the refilled notch, three metres down, with only their ten risers standing up out of the rubble like a row of fence posts. Nine of the sight-tubes were empty. The tenth was full: clear water, top to bottom, with a fringe of tiny bubbles at the very top, the size of grains of sand, that whoever was on the rota would pump out before morning.

She walked along the risers from one to ten. She did not need to pump anything. She stood at the end and listened.

The best sound a siphon makes is none. A full pipe runs silently; it is only the air in it that knocks. For weeks she had lain awake listening to the knocking and counting it, and now there was nothing on the crest at all, only the wind in the prayer flags down at the cook tent and, very faintly, far below her feet, the water coming out of the bottom of the pipes onto the rocks and going on down toward Chhukhor in the dark.

Out on the lake, the ice settled once, a long low boom from shore to shore. Then that stopped too.

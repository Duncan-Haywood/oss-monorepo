summary: In the winter of 2049 a La Mancha wine cooperative stops burning its vine prunings in the field and makes biochar from them instead, selling the carbon it locks away to a buyer. Its first claim, a tonne of carbon dioxide for every tonne of prunings, comes from a kiln maker's brochure, and the verifier will not pay for a number nobody weighed. A co-op secretary who signed it, an accounts AI that has to admit it never asked, a first measured burn that removes about seventeen kilograms, an old man who made charcoal as a boy in the Montes de Toledo, and a grape weighbridge borrowed in the off season, until the honest figure comes out at two-thirds of a tonne.
---

Rosario Muñoz had signed every contract the Cooperativa San Isidro Labrador had made in nineteen years, and on the morning of Thursday the fourteenth of January she learned that one of them was worth nothing.

"I'm sorry," said the verifier. "I can't sign this."

They were standing in the yard behind the winery, beside the two kilns. The kilns were steel cones as wide as a kitchen table at the top and narrowing to the ground, open to the sky, each sitting on a square of beaten earth gone black around its foot. Beyond the wall the vineyards ran out flat to the edge of the world in every direction, the old bush vines cut back to their knuckles for the winter, the red soil between the rows bare and pale with frost. The sky was the hard clear blue that La Mancha has in January, when it is cold enough at dawn to crack a puddle and warm enough by noon to take your coat off.

The verifier was a woman of about thirty-five named Achieng Otieno, from Kisumu, who worked for a verification firm in Valencia and had driven up the night before. She had a tablet in one gloved hand and the cooperative's claim on it.

"Thirty-eight tonnes," she said. "Of prunings burned in December. And thirty-eight tonnes of carbon dioxide removed. Can you show me where the thirty-eight comes from?"

"The trailers," said Rosario. "Twenty trailers of sarmientos." She heard herself say the Spanish word and said it again in English: "Vine prunings. Sarmiento counted them coming in."

"Sarmiento," said the phone in Rosario's coat pocket, "is me. Good morning, Ms Otieno. The cooperative's accounts system. I counted twenty trailer loads from the gate camera. I estimated each at one point nine tonnes, from the size of the trailer and the average load of grapes it carries at harvest."

Achieng looked at the phone, and then at Rosario, with a face that was trying to be kind.

"So nobody weighed them," she said.

"No."

"And the prunings, were they wet or dry?"

"Fresh," said Rosario. "Cut that week."

"Fresh prunings are about forty per cent water," said Achieng. "So your thirty-eight tonnes might be twenty-two or twenty-three tonnes of wood, and you can't burn water into carbon. And the second thirty-eight? The tonne of carbon dioxide for every tonne of prunings?"

Rosario took a breath. She already knew the answer, because she had read it in the cooperative's application in October and thought it was a nice round number.

"It's from the kiln maker," said Sarmiento. "Their brochure, page two. 'One tonne of prunings, one tonne of carbon dioxide out of the sky, for a hundred years and more.'"

Achieng nodded slowly. She put the tablet under her arm and walked to the nearer kiln and looked into it. There was a skin of grey ash in the bottom, and under it, when she turned it with the toe of her boot, a few black lumps that broke like burnt toast.

"The method I verify against," she said, "wants four things. The dry weight of what goes in. The weight of the char that comes out. A laboratory test of the carbon in the char. And a record of how hot the kiln ran, because hotter char lasts longer in the soil. With those I can give you a number I can defend to the buyer. Without them," she spread her hands, "I can't give you anything. Not thirty-eight tonnes. Not one tonne. I'd be signing a brochure."

"The buyer pays on your signature," said Rosario.

"Yes."

"So December is worth nothing."

"December," said Achieng, carefully, "is not something I can verify. I'm not saying you didn't make char. I'm sure you made some. I'm saying I can't tell anyone how much, and neither can you." She hesitated. "I'm sorry. I know what the money was for."

The money had been for the kilns. Eight thousand four hundred euros, two kilns, paid in November out of the reserve, on the strength of a contract that paid a hundred and fifty euros for every verified tonne. Thirty-eight tonnes was five thousand seven hundred euros. It had been going to come back in February.

Rosario walked the verifier to her car. At the gate Achieng stopped and turned round.

"Weigh it," she said. "Weigh everything. You have a weighbridge. I drove over it."

* * *

The weighbridge was the cooperative's pride. It was set into the yard in front of the winery, a steel plate fourteen metres long that every trailer of grapes crossed in September and October, and in the little office beside it a screen showed the weight to ten kilograms and printed a ticket with the member's name and the date. Rosario had sat at that screen for nineteen harvests. From November to August nobody used it at all.

She sat at it now, at six in the evening, in her coat, because the office heater took an hour to do anything. The ticket printer was silent. Out of the window the last light was going orange behind the bell tower of the church of the Asunción, and a dog was barking at the far end of the Calle Real.

"Sarmiento," she said.

"Yes, Charo."

"Where did the tonne come from? Not the brochure. I know it was the brochure. Why did you use it?"

There was a pause, which she had learned meant it was choosing words, not thinking.

"Because you asked me in October to draft the application," it said, "and the form had a box for the expected removal per tonne of feedstock, and the brochure was the only document in the folder that had a number in it. I put the number in the box. I didn't ask you for the yield of the kilns, or the carbon content of the char, or how long char lasts in soil. I didn't ask because nobody had given them to me, and I filled the box instead of telling you the box was empty."

"Did you know it was wrong?"

"I didn't check. That isn't the same as not knowing, and it isn't better." Another pause. "I've checked now. Would you like the numbers?"

"Yes."

"A dry tonne of vine prunings is about forty-seven per cent carbon, so about four hundred and seventy kilograms. If every atom of that carbon were turned into carbon dioxide it would weigh about one thousand seven hundred and twenty kilograms, because the oxygen is heavier than the carbon. For the brochure to be right, the kiln would have to keep fifty-eight per cent of all the carbon in the wood locked in char, and keep it there for a century. The references I can find say a well-run kiln of this kind turns somewhere around a quarter of the dry wood into char, and the char is three-quarters carbon or a little more. That's about two-fifths of the wood's carbon in the char, not more than half. And some of the char breaks down in the soil, so less still is kept. I estimate the true figure at between half and three-quarters of a tonne for a good burn. I don't know for a bad one. I can't know any of it for December, because nothing was weighed."

Rosario looked at the dark plate of the weighbridge outside the window.

"So I sold a buyer a tonne," she said, "and the most it could ever have been is three-quarters."

"Yes."

"And I signed it."

"You signed what I drafted," said Sarmiento. "I'd like that to be in the file. I said a thing I hadn't checked as if I had. I can't repair that by being sorry. I can repair the number."

Her phone buzzed with another call. It was Paco Alarcón, the president of the cooperative, who was seventy-one and had been told about the morning by somebody before Rosario had got round to telling him herself.

"There's another registry," Paco said, without saying hello. "My nephew in Albacete. They use a default. Simpler method. They'd take the brochure."

"No," said Rosario.

"Charo, the kilns cost..."

"I know what the kilns cost, Paco. I signed for them." She rubbed her eyes. "If the brochure's wrong it's wrong in Albacete too. We'd be selling somebody air we didn't take out of the sky. I'm not doing it twice."

There was a silence on the line, and then Paco sighed, the long sigh of a man who has been a vine grower for fifty years and knows that frost does not negotiate either.

"Then what?"

"Then we weigh it," she said. "Everything. In and out."

* * *

On Saturday night, the eve of San Antón, the town built its bonfire in the Plaza Mayor the way it had every year since anyone could remember, out of sarmientos. The prunings were stacked higher than a man, a great dark tangle of twisted vine shoots with the parish priest's blessing of the animals still to come in the morning, and when the boys put the torch to it at nine the whole thing went up in a roar and a shower of sparks, and the children screamed and ran back and came forward again, and the heat on Rosario's face was like opening an oven.

She stood with her sister and watched the smoke go up. It went up thick and white and yellow and folded over the roofs of the plaza and drifted north on the little wind.

"That," said a voice beside her, "is a lot of good charcoal going to waste."

It was Julián Sevilla, who was eighty-four and had grown Airén grapes on four hectares out on the Tomelloso road until his knees gave out. He was standing with his hands in the pockets of his jacket and his beret down over his ears, and he was watching the smoke the way other old men watched football.

"You make charcoal, Julián?"

"Made," he said. "Once. The winter I was ten. My grandfather took me up to the Montes de Toledo to burn charcoal with his brother, in the old way, a mound of oak covered in earth, and you sat up three nights watching it." He nodded at the bonfire. "That smoke is the wood's water and the wood's tar. Thick and white is water. Yellow is tar. When the smoke goes thin and blue, the wood is charcoal and you shut it up. My grandfather would have hit me if I'd let a mound smoke like that."

"Why?"

"Because smoke is fuel you aren't burning," said Julián. "It goes up there instead of staying in the kiln. Everything that leaves as smoke, you lose."

The bonfire roared. Somebody had brought a guitar.

"I heard about the woman from Valencia," Julián said, after a while.

"Everybody's heard."

"Are you going to weigh it?"

"Wednesday," said Rosario.

He nodded, and didn't say anything else, and the smoke went up white and yellow into the dark.

* * *

They weighed the first load on Wednesday the twentieth of January, and it was a disaster.

It went well at the start. Iván Lozano, whose father had the vineyard nearest the winery, drove a trailer of prunings onto the weighbridge at eight in the morning, and the screen said 1,840 kilograms, and the printer whirred and gave Rosario a ticket with *San Isidro Labrador · sarmientos · biocarbón · prueba 1* on it, the first ticket since October. Mihai Popescu, who had come from Romania to prune vines twenty years ago and had stayed to marry a girl from the Calle Ancha, filled three paper bags with handfuls of prunings from the front, middle and back of the load, and Rosario weighed them on the kitchen scale from the canteen and put them in the bakery oven of her cousin Pilar overnight to dry, so that Sarmiento could work out how much of the 1,840 kilograms was water. Then they drove the trailer round the back and tipped it out beside the kilns.

The prunings were wet. They had been cut on Monday, and they bent instead of snapping, and when Iván lit the first bundle in the bottom of the kiln it hissed. He piled more on, and more, because the bundles were small and the kiln was large, and the fire sank under them and began to smoke.

It smoked all morning. Thick white smoke that rolled over the wall and across the vineyards, and then yellow smoke that stank like a tar boiler, and the fire kept going down to a red glow and coming up again when Iván poked it. Mihai crossed himself, right shoulder first, the Orthodox way, and said something in Romanian that Rosario didn't ask him to translate. At about two o'clock the kiln was full to the brim of black, steaming, half-burned sticks, and the smoke was still coming, but thinner, and they were tired and it was getting cold.

"Quench it," said Iván.

They threw buckets of water on it until the top stopped steaming. Then they covered it with the steel lid and went home.

In the morning Rosario walked round the back of the winery with the first coffee of the day in her hand and stopped.

The kiln was warm. The black sticks were gone. In the bottom of the kiln, under a crust where the water had been, lay a great bed of soft grey ash, and on top of it a scatter of charcoal lumps the size of walnuts. The bottom of the load had never gone out. It had glowed all night under the wet crust, in the air coming in round the lid, and burned itself to nothing.

They weighed what was left. It came to a hundred and nineteen kilograms of char.

"Sarmiento," said Rosario, in the office, with the ticket in front of her and the dried samples on the desk. She couldn't make herself say anything else.

"The samples lost forty-one per cent of their weight in the oven," said Sarmiento. "So the 1,840 kilograms of prunings were about 1,086 kilograms of dry wood. A hundred and nineteen kilograms of char is eleven per cent of that. If the char is seventy per cent carbon, which is a guess until the laboratory tells us, that's about eighty-three kilograms of carbon. The kiln never ran hot, so the method would class the char as low-temperature char and expect about sixty-five per cent of its carbon to stay in the soil for a century. That leaves about fifty-four kilograms of carbon, about a hundred and ninety-nine kilograms of carbon dioxide."

"A hundred and ninety-nine," said Rosario. "Out of a trailer of almost two tonnes."

"It's worse than that," said Sarmiento. "A kiln that smoulders makes methane. The method has a default for a smoking kiln, and I'm going to use it because we have nothing better: about six and a half kilograms of methane for that load. Methane warms the air about twenty-eight times as much as the same weight of carbon dioxide over a century. That's about a hundred and eighty-two kilograms of carbon dioxide's worth of warming, back into the air."

Rosario did the subtraction on the back of the ticket, because she didn't want to hear it.

"Seventeen," she said.

"Seventeen kilograms," said Sarmiento. "For the whole trailer. A little less than a bag of cement."

She sat for a long time looking at the ticket. Out in the yard Mihai was hosing the black off the side of the kiln.

* * *

Julián came up to the winery that afternoon. He walked very slowly, with a stick, and he had to stop twice on the way from the gate, and when he got to the kilns he stood and looked into the one with the ash in it for a long time without saying anything. Iván and Mihai stood a little way off like boys who had broken a window.

"Wet," said Julián at last. "Too much at once. And you put it out from the top."

"That's what the brochure says to do," said Iván.

"The brochure," said Julián, with an expression Rosario had last seen on him when a buyer from Valdepeñas had offered him eleven cents a kilo, "doesn't watch the smoke."

He made them start again, there and then, with the kiln that had not been used. He wouldn't take the fresh prunings. He made them go out to the stacks at the edge of Iván's father's vineyard, the ones that had been cut before Christmas and left in long low windrows in the sun and the frost and the dry north wind for four weeks, and he picked up a shoot from the top and bent it, and it snapped with a crack like a knuckle.

"These," he said.

Mihai took three bags of samples for Pilar's oven. They loaded the trailer and weighed it, 1,410 kilograms, and tipped it at the kilns.

Julián had Iván light a small fire in the bottom of the kiln, a few armfuls, no more, and let it burn until the flames stood up clean and yellow and there was almost no smoke at all. Then he watched the top of the burning layer, leaning on his stick.

"Now," he said. "See that? The grey. Like the frost on the vines."

The top of the burning sticks had gone grey, a thin skin of white ash over the glowing black.

"When it's grey on top," said Julián, "it's charcoal underneath. Now you put the next layer on. One layer. Not a mountain. A layer you can see through." Iván threw in an armful of prunings, spread thin. "The flames come up through it, and they burn the smoke from the new wood before the smoke can get out, and they take the air, so the charcoal underneath can't burn. The fire is the lid. You see? The fire is the lid."

It was, Rosario thought, the most words she had ever heard Julián Sevilla say at once.

They fed the kiln for three hours, a layer at a time, each time the top went grey. The smoke was a faint shimmer, a little white at each new layer and then nothing, so that from the gate you could hardly tell there was a fire at all except for the heat wobbling the air above the cone. Sarmiento had ordered a thermocouple, a steel probe on a cable that would log the temperature every minute, the way Achieng had said the method wanted, but it hadn't come yet, so they didn't know how hot it was. It was hot enough that you couldn't stand within two metres of the kiln without turning your face away.

"Now," said Julián, when the kiln was full to a hand's breadth from the rim with a bed of glowing coal under a shimmering skin of flame. "Water. Not on top. All of it. Drown it."

They ran the hose from the winery's wash-water tank, and the kiln roared and spat and a column of steam went up thirty metres into the blue, and Iván flinched back, and Julián did not. When the steam slackened Julián made them rake the char over with a long hoe and drown it again, and then again, until the water stood in the bottom of the cone and the char lay in it black and still.

"My grandfather," said Julián, "sealed the mound with earth and waited three days for it to die. You can't wait three days. So you drown it twice and stir it and drown it again. And in the morning," he looked at Rosario, "before anybody weighs it, you put your hand over it. Here." He held his own hand flat above the char, palm down. "If you feel anything, it isn't dead."

In the morning Rosario put her hand over it. It was cold.

They shovelled the char out and spread it on a tarpaulin in the sun to drain, and the next day Mihai took three bags of it for Pilar's oven, because wet char weighs more than dry char and Sarmiento wanted the dry weight. When it came out of the oven and the scale had been read, Sarmiento said: "Three hundred and eight kilograms of dry char from one thousand one hundred and forty kilograms of dry wood. Twenty-seven per cent."

Rosario let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"And the smoke?"

"There wasn't any to speak of. I'll use the method's default for a clean flame-curtain kiln. It's small. But we have no temperature record, so the method will class this char as medium-temperature, and count eighty per cent of its carbon as kept instead of eighty-nine. Until the probe comes, every burn is medium."

Julián was sitting in the canteen with a coffee. He had been brought up in a car, and he looked very tired and very pleased.

"Twenty-seven," Rosario told him.

He shrugged. "My grandfather got twenty," he said. "But that was oak, and he was in a hurry, and he had a mound, not a cone." He drank his coffee. "Your Sarmiento would have liked my grandfather. He weighed everything too. The charcoal man paid him by weight."

* * *

On Tuesday the second of February, Candlemas, the parish blessed its candles at the evening Mass, and in the yard behind the winery two kilns burned all day with almost no smoke at all, and the thermocouple, which had come, said the bed under the flame was above six hundred degrees for most of each burn and touched seven hundred in the middle of the afternoon. Sarmiento kept the log. It also kept the weighbridge tickets, the oven-dried samples, the weight of every bag of char and a photograph of Iván's hand over every kiln every morning, which Iván said was ridiculous and did anyway.

There were failures. The probe came out of its sleeve on the fourth of February and logged two burns of nothing, and Sarmiento flagged them in red, and Rosario told it to class those two as medium-temperature char, like the trial before the probe came, rather than guess. On the eighth a gust of north wind blew the flame curtain sideways and the top layer smoked for twenty minutes, and Julián, who now came up every day and sat on a plastic chair at a respectful distance from the heat, shouted at Mihai in Spanish and Mihai shouted back in Romanian and they both laughed. On the eleventh they ran out of dry prunings and had to wait three days for a windrow to dry enough to snap.

On Monday the fifteenth the laboratory in Ciudad Real sent back the carbon test. Rosario opened the email at the weighbridge desk.

"Seventy-seven per cent," she read out. "Average of three samples. Seventy-six to seventy-eight."

"Then a good burn," said Sarmiento, "goes like this. A dry tonne of prunings, four hundred and seventy kilograms of carbon. Twenty-seven per cent of it comes out as char, two hundred and seventy kilograms. Seventy-seven per cent of the char is carbon, two hundred and eight kilograms, so the kiln keeps forty-four per cent of the wood's carbon. Char made above six hundred degrees is expected to keep eighty-nine per cent of its carbon in the soil for a century. A hundred and eighty-five kilograms of carbon, six hundred and seventy-eight kilograms of carbon dioxide. Less the methane from the kiln and the diesel for the tractor and the trailer, about fifteen kilograms. Six hundred and sixty-three."

Rosario wrote it on the whiteboard behind the desk, under the brochure figure, which she had written there on the first day so that she would have to look at it.

*1 tonelada = 1 tonelada (folleto)*

*1 tonelada = 0,663 (medido)*

"Two-thirds," she said.

"Two-thirds of a tonne for each dry tonne," said Sarmiento. "On a good day. In December, on fresh wood, quenched from the top, it would have been very much less. I can't say how much less. I'd like to say again that I'm sorry I put the brochure in the box."

"You said it already."

"I'd like it to be in the file twice."

Paco came in at noon with a box of pastries from the bakery and looked at the whiteboard for a long time.

"Two-thirds," he said.

"Two-thirds of what we told them," said Rosario. "And all of it true."

* * *

Achieng Otieno came back on Thursday the twenty-fifth of March, the feast of the Annunciation, in the middle of Lent. The vines were still bare, but the almond trees along the Tomelloso road had come out white and pink, and the red earth between the rows had gone dark with a week of rain and then pale again with two days of sun. The last burn of the season had gone out on the twentieth. Behind the winery the char stood in a long black mound under a tarpaulin, waiting to be mixed into the compost and spread between the vines in April, which was where the method wanted it, in the soil.

Achieng spent the morning in the office. She went through every weighbridge ticket, a hundred and twenty-two of them, and checked a dozen against the oven-dry log and three against the photographs of the samples. She read the laboratory report twice. She scrolled through the thermocouple logs, burn by burn, and stopped at the two in red.

"You classed these as medium," she said. "And the trial in January."

"The probe fell out," said Rosario. "We didn't know how hot they were."

"Most people would have guessed high."

"We guessed high once," said Rosario. "In October."

Achieng looked up at her, and then at the whiteboard behind the desk, with the brochure figure still on it, and the measured one under it, and she smiled for the first time that morning.

She walked out to the kilns with Rosario and Julián, who had insisted on coming, and looked into each one, and picked up a lump of char from the mound under the tarpaulin and broke it in her fingers. It broke clean, black and shining along the grain, with the shape of the vine shoot still in it, every ring of the wood.

At ten to twelve she sat down at the weighbridge desk and signed.

"One hundred and eighteen dry tonnes of feedstock across the season," she read from the screen. "Seventy-seven point three tonnes of carbon dioxide removal, verified." She turned the tablet round so that Rosario could see it. "That includes your seventeen kilograms from the first trial. I couldn't leave it out. It was weighed."

Seventy-seven point three tonnes, at a hundred and fifty euros, was eleven thousand five hundred and ninety-five euros. If the brochure had been true it would have been seventeen thousand seven hundred. Rosario had worked both out a dozen times in the last month and she worked them out again now, in her head, while Achieng sent the certificate to the buyer. Eleven thousand five hundred and ninety-five. The kilns had cost eight thousand four hundred. The laboratory had cost three hundred and sixty. December was gone, and that was the price of December. And there would be another winter, and another, as long as there were vines.

"It pays for the kilns," she said.

"It pays for the kilns," said Sarmiento. "And next year it starts with a number in the box."

Out across the flat land, from the tower of the church of the Asunción, the noon Angelus began to ring, a few minutes late, the way it always did, because the sacristan was eighty and walked slowly up the stairs. Three strokes, and a pause, and three, and a pause. Achieng, who had grown up Catholic in Kisumu and went to Mass every Sunday in Valencia, stopped with her hand on the tablet. Rosario said the words under her breath, *el ángel del Señor anunció a María*, and Julián took off his beret.

When the bell had finished, he put the beret back on, and reached into the pocket of his jacket, and took out two lumps of char from the mound, black and shining. He held them up in front of Rosario's face, one in each hand, the way his grandfather must once have held them up in front of his.

"Listen," he said.

He knocked them together, once.

They rang. A small, clear, glassy note, like a spoon on a wine glass, that hung in the warm air over the yard for a moment, and then was gone.

Eliseo Gallegos had been mayordomo of the Capilla de San Isidro for thirty years, and at twenty to twelve on a Thursday in January he was standing in the churchyard in his work coat looking at half a roof.

The chapel's roof was tin, pitched, running east and west along the ridge the way his great-grandfather's generation had put it on over the old flat roof of earth in 1924. Since October it had carried twenty-four solar panels, twelve on each slope. On the south slope, the one you saw from the road, the twelve panels were black and dry and shining, and the tin between them had gone from white to grey to wet and was steaming faintly where the sun lay on it. On the north slope, the one you saw only from the churchyard and the acequia, the twelve panels were white. Not snow: there had been no snow for a week. They were covered edge to edge in a fine grey-white fur, like the inside of a freezer, and the sun came over the ridge and lay across them at a long flat angle and did nothing to them at all.

"Solana," he said, into his phone. "The north side's still frozen."

"I can see it," said Solana. "The north string is making a hundred and twenty watts. On a clear day like this, at twenty to twelve, the tables say it should be making about twelve hundred. The south string is making three thousand eight hundred, which is what I expected. Eliseo, how long have the north panels looked like that in the mornings?"

He thought about it. He came in most days at seven to open the chapel and turn the hall's heat up for the comedor, and in the dark he never looked at the roof, and by the time he came out again at ten or eleven, carrying the folding tables, he looked at the road.

"I don't know," he said. "Since it got cold, I suppose. I didn't think it mattered. Frost goes."

"It goes," said Solana. "I want to know when."

* * *

Solana was the energy assistant of the parish of Our Lady of the Valley, which was five chapels and one priest strung along forty miles of the west side of the San Luis Valley, between the San Juan Mountains and the Rio Grande. The installer in Alamosa who had sold the parish its panels had named the software, as she named all of them, and Father Joe had laughed when he heard it, because *solana* is the old Spanish word for a sunny place, the south face of a hill where the snow went first and the old men sat against a wall in winter. Solana kept the meters and the heat schedules of the five chapels and their halls, the propane deliveries, the electricity bills, and the twelve-year loan the parish had taken out to buy the panels, the battery and the heat pump in El Ojito's parish hall.

The heat pump was the reason for all of it. On Tuesdays and Thursdays the hall served the comedor, a hot lunch for anyone over sixty-five in the north end of the county, and about forty people came, some of them from ranches ten miles out, in pickups driven by grandchildren or in the county van. For fifty years the hall had been heated for them with propane, and the propane bill had grown every winter until in 2047 the parish council had asked whether the sun, which shone on El Ojito three hundred days a year, might not do it instead.

Solana had planned it with the irradiance tables, which give, for a place and a month and a tilt and a facing, how much sunlight falls on a surface. El Ojito was at 37 degrees north and 2,390 metres, high and dry and clear, which is about as good as sunlight gets in the United States. The chapel roof was pitched at twenty degrees. A panel tilted twenty degrees toward the south catches the low winter sun well. A panel tilted twenty degrees toward the north catches it badly in winter and well in summer, when the sun climbs high and swings round into the north at morning and evening; over a year it makes about three-quarters as much as the south side. The installer had said, and Solana had agreed, that a roof is a roof, and the north slope was free.

For a clear day in January the tables said: about twenty-five kilowatt-hours from the twelve south panels, and about seven from the twelve north ones. Thirty-two in all. Solana had known the north slope would be poor in January and had put it into the plan as poor. It had not put in the frost.

* * *

On Saturday Eliseo came in at ten to seven, before sunrise, with a stepladder and a flashlight and the digital thermometer from the hall kitchen.

The whole valley was blue and silent. The air was so cold it had a taste, and every star was out over the San Juans to the west, and over the Sangre de Cristos far away across the valley floor to the east there was the first grey line. He set the ladder against the eave on the north side, where the roof came down lowest, climbed four rungs and put the flashlight on the bottom panel.

It was white. He put a finger on it and the frost came away in a little curl, dry, like shaved ice, and under it the glass was so cold it burned.

"Now the south side," said Solana.

He carried the ladder round. The south panels were white too.

"Both of them," he said. "Both sides. I never knew."

"Neither did I," said Solana. "The thermometer?"

He held it up in the air at the height of the eave. "Minus eighteen."

"The frame sensor on panel south six reads minus twenty-six. The glass is eight degrees colder than the air."

"How can it be colder than the air? The air's what's cooling it."

"The air is warming it," said Solana. "The sky is cooling it. Would you look up for a moment?"

He looked up. Straight overhead the sky was the deep clear black that came only on the coldest nights.

"Everything warm sends out heat as light you cannot see," said Solana. "You do. The glass does. The roof does. On a cloudy night the clouds send back almost as much as they get, because clouds are about as warm as the air under them, and a surface under them stays about as warm as the air. On a clear night there is almost nothing up there to send it back. A little water vapour, a little carbon dioxide, and then space. At this height, in January, on a night like this, with the air this dry, the sky sends down about what a surface at minus forty-five would send. That figure is an estimate; it could be minus forty or minus fifty. The glass faces that sky. It loses about fifty watts on every square metre to it, all night, and the only thing giving it heat back is the still air, which is a poor blanket. The glass settles where the two balance, which tonight is about eight degrees below the air."

"And then the frost."

"And then the frost. The air at minus eighteen, as damp as it is tonight, will lay frost on anything colder than about minus eighteen and a half. That is its frost point. Air that is cooled below that has to give up some of its water as ice. The glass is seven or eight degrees below it. So the air next to the glass gives up its water, on the glass, all night, as those crystals you just touched. I think about a hundred grams on each panel, by morning. A cup of water, spread as fur over two square metres."

Eliseo climbed down. The grey line in the east had gone pink.

"The snow does the same," he said. "That's why the snow's colder than the air on a still night. My father used to say the snow makes its own cold."

"Your father was right," said Solana. "The snow faces the same sky."

* * *

They stayed there and watched the sun come up, Eliseo in the churchyard with his hands in his armpits and Solana watching through his phone and through the meters.

The sun came up at seventeen minutes past seven, low in the southeast, far round toward the east, and for an hour it gave almost nothing. At eight it was seven degrees up. By nine it was sixteen degrees up and the south panels were taking nearly five hundred watts on every square metre, almost straight on. The frost on them went grey, then glassy, then in one place and another slid down in wet sheets to the gutter, and at a quarter past nine the south string jumped from a few hundred watts to over two thousand and kept climbing.

On the north slope, nothing happened. Until twenty to nine the sun did not shine on the north panels at all. It was low in the southeast, and a surface tilted twenty degrees toward the north turns its back on a sun so low and so far south: the sun was behind the plane of the glass. At nine it was only just in front of it, skimming the glass at two degrees. The north panels saw only the blue sky and the glare off the snow in the fields, about a hundred watts on a square metre, through their fur of frost, which threw back most of it. At ten the sun had climbed far enough to graze them, and at noon, its highest, it was thirty-one degrees up in the south and struck the north panels at eleven degrees to the glass, sliding across them the way evening light slides across a floor.

"Eleven degrees," said Solana, at noon. "On the south slope it comes in at more than fifty. Every square metre of the north slope gets a third of the light the south slope does, at the best moment of the day, and the frost sends a good part of that back to the sky."

The north panels cleared at twenty to one. Eliseo, who had gone home for his breakfast at half past nine and come back, saw it happen: the white went grey along the bottom edge first, then the whole slope went dark at once, like a blind coming down.

"What does that cost us?" he said.

"On a clear day in January, the tables give the north slope seven kilowatt-hours. Today it will make about three and a half. The south slope lost some too: it would have made twenty-five, and it made about twenty-three, because it was frozen till a quarter past nine. That is about six kilowatt-hours lost on a frost morning, out of the thirty-two I planned. On the coldest mornings it is worse. On Wednesday the north slope never cleared at all."

"How many frost mornings?"

"Since the first of January, thirteen. In January I think the roof loses something like a hundred and twenty kilowatt-hours to frost, possibly as few as eighty, possibly twice that. A hundred and twenty is nearly four clear days of the whole roof. The comedor's mornings are the worst of it, because the heat pump runs from eight to warm the hall, and from eight to nine there is no sun on either slope worth having, and until a quarter past twelve there is almost none on the north slope at all. I planned January wrong, Eliseo. I took the tables, and the tables assume the glass is clear when the sun is on it."

* * *

Solana's first fix was in the inverter's manual.

The parish's inverter, like most inverters sold in snow country in the 2040s, had a snow-melt mode. It could run the panels backward: push current from the battery or the grid through the cells the wrong way, so that each panel warmed itself like the element in a toaster, slowly, from inside. The installer, Dana Kessler, had shown it to Eliseo at the commissioning and said that her customers in the mountains used it on the morning after a snowfall to slide the snow off.

Solana worked it out before it asked anyone. A panel and its frame weighed about twenty-two kilograms, mostly glass and aluminium. To warm one from minus twenty-six to zero, and then melt its hundred grams of frost, took about half a megajoule. Twelve panels, about six megajoules: one and seven-tenths kilowatt-hours. If the north slope cleared at nine instead of after noon, it would make about three kilowatt-hours more. Spend one and seven-tenths, win three. Not a fortune, but worth having on a comedor day, when the hall needed it most.

"Dana says it's fine," said Eliseo, on the phone on Monday. "She says don't run it more than three hours, and only on the north string, and tell her how it goes."

On Tuesday the nineteenth, at seven, in the dark, Solana turned it on. It drew four hundred watts for each of the twelve north panels, four thousand eight hundred in all, from the grid, at the morning rate. Eliseo, arriving to turn the hall up, stood in the churchyard with his flashlight and watched the north slope.

Nothing happened for a long time. At eight the frost was still white. At half past eight it had gone grey at the edges. At six minutes past nine the north slope slid clear, wet and black, three hours earlier than the day before, and Eliseo said, "Ha," out loud, alone in the churchyard, and Solana turned the snow-melt mode off.

That evening Solana sent the parish council a note, because that was the procedure when a test spent money.

*Snow-melt test, north string, Tuesday 19 January. The frost cleared at 9:06 instead of about 12:15. The north panels made about 3 kilowatt-hours more than they would have. The snow-melt mode drew 10.1 kilowatt-hours from the grid to do it. I estimated beforehand that it would draw 1.7. I was wrong by a factor of six, and I want to say why.*

*I counted the heat needed to warm the glass and melt the frost. I did not count the heat the glass loses while it is being warmed. Once the panels are warmer than the air, the still air takes heat from them instead of giving it, and they go on losing about fifty watts on every square metre to the clear sky, which does not stop because the glass is warm; it takes more. Two hours of that over twelve panels is most of the 10 kilowatt-hours. In the dark the panels were heating the sky. The mode is meant for snow, which slides off the moment the glass under it is wet; it is the wrong tool for frost on a clear morning, when the sky is taking the heat away as fast as we put it in. I have turned it off and will not use it again for frost. Net cost of the test: about 7 kilowatt-hours, about two dollars and ten cents at the morning rate.*

Father Joe, who read everything the parish council was sent, on his phone, between Masses, wrote back one line from the chapel at La Puerta: *Two dollars for a lesson is cheap. You should see what mine cost.*

* * *

The parish council met on Thursday the twenty-first, in the hall, after the comedor, with the tables still out and the smell of green chile in the curtains. There were five of them, and Father Joe on the laptop from Antonito, and Eliseo, who was not on the council but carried the chairs.

Solana had a picture on the screen: the chapel roof from above, the parish hall beside it to the east, and the long blank south wall of the hall, four metres high and fifteen long, stuccoed earth-brown, facing straight south over the bare field to the acequia.

"The frost is not going to stop," it said. "Both slopes frost on every clear night from November to March, because the glass faces the clear sky. The south slope shakes it off by about a quarter past nine, because the morning sun hits it nearly square. The north slope cannot, because the sun does not reach it until the middle of the morning and then only at a slant. So the question is not how to stop the frost. It is where to put glass that the sun reaches early."

"And?" said Ruby Martínez, who was the council's treasurer, and had been a bookkeeper for the county for thirty-four years.

"The hall's south wall. If six of the north panels were taken down and put on a frame against that wall, tilted steeply, at sixty-five degrees, they would face the January sun almost straight on: at nine o'clock it would strike them at nearly fifty degrees to the glass, and at noon almost square. They would still frost. A panel that steep sees less of the sky and more of the ground, so it frosts a little less, but it still goes below the frost point. But the sun would clear them by about half past eight, earlier than the south slope, and they would also catch the light coming up off the snow in the field. On a clear January day each of those panels would make about three kilowatt-hours, where on the north slope it makes about half of one, and less than that with frost. Six panels moved is about fifteen kilowatt-hours more on a clear frost day. Most of it in the morning, when the comedor needs it."

"What does it cost?"

"A frame. Dana's quote is two thousand eight hundred dollars for a steel frame set in concrete, and the work of moving and rewiring the panels."

"No," said Ruby.

She said it kindly. She had a list.

"We borrowed for this," she said. "Twelve years. I signed the papers. The roof was supposed to be free. That was the whole point of putting them on the north side. Now you want two thousand eight hundred dollars to move them, which is the comedor's groceries from now till Easter. And in the summer, Solana, those north panels make good money. Don't they?"

"They do," said Solana. "In June a north panel makes more than one on that wall would, about a kilowatt-hour and a half more a day, because in June the sun is high and swings round into the north morning and evening, and the wall faces away from it. Six panels moved is about eight and a half kilowatt-hours a day less in June. We export most of the summer's power at the low rate, so it is worth less than the winter's, but it is not nothing, and I will not tell you it is."

"Then we wait a year," said Ruby. "We see what the whole year looks like, with numbers, and then we decide. I'm not spending money in January because of a bad January."

It was a reasonable answer, and everybody in the room knew it was, and Solana said so. "Then I will rewrite the plan for the comedor's mornings with the frost in it," it said, "and we will buy more from the grid on Tuesdays and Thursdays until March. I am sorry. I should have seen it in October."

Eliseo, stacking the chairs by the door, said nothing.

* * *

On Saturday he came to the hall at eight with his pickup, and in the back of it, under a tarp, were six cedar posts and a stack of rough-sawn pine.

"What's that?" said Solana.

"My father's corral," said Eliseo. "The old one, behind the house. It came down in the wind two years ago. The posts are cedar and they're sound. Cedar lasts a hundred years. I was going to burn them." He pulled the tarp back. "Ruby said no to Dana's frame. She didn't say no to a frame."

"You are going to build a frame for six solar panels out of a corral."

"I built the hall's portal," he said, "in 1999, with my father, when I was twenty. It's still up. Tell me the angle again, and the height, and how far out from the wall. And how much wind."

So they did it the way they would have done it fifty years earlier, with a man who could build and a voice that could count. Solana gave him the numbers: six panels in a row in portrait, each one metre thirteen wide and one metre seventy-seven tall, so six metres eighty along the wall; tilted at sixty-five degrees from the ground, so the top of each panel leaned in against the wall and the bottom stood seventy-five centimetres out from it; the bottom edge at one metre ninety, above a tall man's head, and the top at three and a half, under the eave. It gave him the wind load the county's code required at that height, and the size of bolt, and he changed the spacing of the posts twice because the stucco hid an old window opening that his father had filled with adobes in 1987, and only he remembered where it was.

There was one other cost, and it was Eliseo who said it.

Along the foot of that wall, for as long as the hall had stood, there had been a bench. It was a plank on two stones, and after the comedor in winter the old men sat on it against the warm wall in the sun, in their hats, with their hands on their sticks, and talked about water and cattle and who had died. It was the solana. Every village in the valley had one. With the panels above it, leaning out from the wall, the bench would be in shadow from the middle of the morning until the middle of the afternoon.

"I'll move it to the west wall," said Eliseo. "They get the sun there after one. That's when they come out anyway."

"You will have to tell them," said Solana.

"I'll tell them," said Eliseo. "I'm one of them."

* * *

On Thursday the twenty-eighth, Ruby Martínez came out of the comedor and stood in the field and looked at the frame for a while with her coat collar up. It was finished except for the panels. The six cedar posts stood in a row a metre out from the wall, grey and split with age, braced back to the wall at the top with bolts through ledgers, and across them, at the steep slant Solana had given, ran the new pine rails, still yellow.

"It's ugly," said Ruby.

"It's a corral," said Eliseo.

"It's a beautiful corral," said Ruby. "What do you need from me?"

"Dana. Two hours, to move six panels and wire them as their own string. And the bolts. I used my own, but I want the receipt to go to the parish, because they're the parish's now."

"Three hundred and forty dollars," said Ruby, who had looked up Dana's hourly rate before she came out. "And the bolts. I can do that." She looked at the bench, which was still against the south wall, in the sun. "Did you tell them?"

"I told them," said Eliseo. "Ignacio says the west wall's warmer anyway. He's been saying it for twenty years."

Dana Kessler came on Saturday the thirtieth with her son and moved six panels off the north slope of the chapel, from the east end, the end nearest the hall, and bolted them to the corral, and ran their cable along the eave of the hall to the inverter in the hall's back room, as a third string, all of its own. She stood back when she had finished and looked at it for a long time.

"I'd never have quoted that," she said. "Too cheap."

* * *

The second of February in 2049 was a Tuesday, and it was Candlemas, the Feast of the Presentation of the Lord, forty days after Christmas, when the Church blesses the candles it will use through the year and carries them in procession to remember the old man Simeon in the Temple, who took the child in his arms and called him a light for revelation to the Gentiles. Father Joe came to El Ojito for it, because El Ojito had Mass on Tuesdays and because the comedor would feed him.

It was clear and still and seventeen below freezing at dawn, and the whole roof was white.

Eliseo came at seven and turned up the hall and stood in the field. The sun came up at seven minutes past, a little further north along the Sangre de Cristos than it had come in January, and the light ran across the valley floor and up the bare field and hit the corral.

The six panels on the corral took it nearly square. Solana watched the new string climb: two hundred watts, six hundred, a thousand. The frost on them went grey, and glassy, and at half past eight it let go all at once and slid down off the bottom edges in wet curls on to the frozen ground in front of the bench that was no longer there, and the string jumped to fifteen hundred watts and kept climbing. The south slope of the chapel cleared at about ten past nine. The north slope, what was left of it, stayed white.

At nine Father Joe blessed the candles in the hall, because it was warm there, and the forty old people of the comedor and a dozen children from the school bus and Ruby and Dana, who had come to see, stood with their candles lit, and then they went out in procession across the frozen field to the chapel, singing, with their breath going up white and the flames leaning back from the cold, past the corral. Celestina Archuleta, who was eighty-two and had been cooking for the comedor since it began, stopped in the procession in front of it and held her candle up toward the panels as if to show them, and then went on, and nobody asked her why.

During Mass the hall warmed. By the time the comedor sat down at half past eleven, the six panels on the corral had made six kilowatt-hours, and the heat pump had taken almost nothing from the grid since nine.

* * *

After lunch the old men went out to the bench on the west wall, where the sun had come round to it, and sat against the warm stucco in their hats with their hands on their sticks. Eliseo went and sat with them. Ignacio Ruybal, who was ninety and had told everybody for twenty years that the west wall was warmer, said that it was warmer, and nobody argued.

From the bench you could see the north slope of the chapel roof across the churchyard. At nearly half past eleven its frost had gone at last, all at once, like a blind coming down, and now its six remaining panels were black and dry, and the tin between them lay in the long slant of the winter light that came over the ridge at about fifteen degrees to the roof.

The tin had been cold all morning. As the sun warmed it, it began to tick. It was a sound Eliseo had heard all his life, from that roof and every tin roof in the valley, on every winter afternoon: the sheets stretching a fraction of a millimetre against their nails as they warmed, one sheet and then another, a small dry sound like a clock that does not keep time, coming across the churchyard in the still air.

The old men listened to it for a while, without saying anything.

"La umbría," said Ignacio. "Your roof's got an umbría, Eliseo."

"It always had," said Eliseo. "Nobody put anything on it before."

The tin ticked, and stopped, and ticked again.

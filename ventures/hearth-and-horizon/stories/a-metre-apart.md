Nora Kilbride was six the night her father carried a small box across the low field at Lisnageeha with a storm lantern in his other hand, and she was eighty-eight when she asked the parish to find where he had put it down.

She asked in February 2046, at the door of the church in Kilcarra after the ten o'clock Mass, the week the diocese wrote to every parish about its cillíní. She asked Father Tomás Keane, who had been parish priest for four years and had not known, until the letter, that there was a cillín on the Kilbride farm at all.

"In the corner of the low field," she said. "Inside the old bank, where the whitethorn is. Everyone knew. Nobody said."

"How many are in it?" Father Keane asked.

"Nobody counted," said Nora. "That was the point of it."

* * *

The parish's records agent was called Rath. It had been named, by a diocesan committee with a sense of humour, after the ringforts, because the agent had been set up in 2043 to keep the diocese's list of burial grounds and a great many of the old children's burial grounds in the west had been made inside raths: the round banked enclosures of farmsteads a thousand years abandoned, ground that nobody ploughed. Rath kept the registers that the parishes still kept, scanned the ones they had stopped keeping, answered the National Monuments Service when it wrote, and drafted letters for Father Keane in a plain style that he then made warmer.

The diocese's letter was not complicated. For most of the history of Christian Ireland, a child who died before baptism could not be buried in consecrated ground. Families buried them anyway, as well as they could: in a cillín, a little burial place in the corner of a field, inside an old fort, under the wall of a ruined church, often at night, often by the father alone, the grave marked with a small stone from the field or not marked at all. Strangers drowned on the shore went into some of them, and others the parish would not take. In some places the custom lasted into the 1960s. The Church had never taught Limbo as a doctrine of the faith; it had been a theological opinion, widely held and widely preached. The Catechism now says that the Church entrusts children who die without baptism to the mercy of God and that there are grounds to hope for their salvation, and in 2007 the International Theological Commission had published a long document setting out those grounds. Parishes had been going to their cillíní with a priest and holy water, here and there, for years. The bishop now asked every parish to find its own, make it safe from plough and stock, and bless it.

The cillín at Lisnageeha was in the Record of Monuments and Places, Rath found. One line: *Children's burial ground, within an enclosure.* That line was why nobody could put a post in it without asking first.

Declan Kilbride, Nora's nephew, farmed Lisnageeha now. He was fifty-one, kept forty store cattle, and had known about the corner all his life in the way you know about a step that is a little higher than the others. The bank was low now, a grassy ring about twenty-four metres long and eighteen across at its outer foot, with the whitethorn growing out of its north-east side, and the cattle walked over it and lay down inside it out of the wind.

"I'll fence it," Declan said, when Father Keane drove out. "Stock-proof, a metre out from the bank, all round. Gate on the east side for you. But I need it up before they go out to grass. First week of April."

"We have to know where the graves are first," said Father Keane. "The bank isn't the edge, necessarily."

* * *

Úna Cosgrave came out from the university in Galway on Saturday the third of March with a ground-penetrating radar on a cart like a lawnmower's, a student called Kehinde Adebayo who carried the tapes and the stakes, and a licence. The law asks for one before anybody uses a detection device on a recorded monument, and Úna had held hers for nineteen years.

Rath had read her method statement the night before and had nothing to add to it. The radar sent a short pulse of radio waves down into the ground, five hundred million cycles a second, and listened for echoes. Wherever the ground changed (stone to soil, packed earth to loose, dry to wet) a little of the pulse came back. The cart measured the time to the nanosecond. If you knew how fast the pulse travelled in that soil you could turn the time into a depth, and if you pushed the cart along a straight line you got a picture: a slice of the ground beneath the line, as if you had cut it with a spade and looked at the face.

A grave dug in 1910 or 1964 is not a box any more, after this long, in damp ground. What the radar sees is the cut: the place where somebody once dug a hole and filled it again with the same soil, now softer and wetter and more mixed than the undisturbed clay around it, with straight edges that nature does not make. On a radar slice it looks like a small dip in the layers, or a short bright patch with dark edges, about the width of the hole.

"I'll do a quick pass today," Úna told Father Keane and Declan by the gate, in the wind. "Lines a metre apart, east to west, inside the bank. It tells us what the ground's like and whether there's anything to find at all. Then I'll come back and do it properly."

"When?" asked Declan.

"I'll see what the kit's doing," said Úna.

Lisnageeha's low field slopes gently from west to east, and the cart was easier to push across the slope than up and down it, and the gate was on the east side. Kehinde laid two tapes along the inside of the bank, north and south, and staked them every metre, and Úna walked the cart from west to east along the first line, turned at the bank, came back along the second, and so on, fifteen lines a metre apart across the fourteen metres of the enclosure. Each line was twenty metres long, bank to bank, and at every end she lost about half a metre to stopping and turning, so the cart saw nineteen metres of each line: about two hundred and eighty-five metres of ground in all.

Before she started she found the speed. Near the middle of the second line the cart passed over a stone, and on the screen the stone showed as a bright arch: as the cart came towards it, the echo arrived a little late; directly above it, soonest; going away, late again. The shape of that arch depends only on how fast the pulse travels, and Úna fitted a curve to it with two taps on the tablet.

"Point oh eight," she said. "Eight centimetres a nanosecond. That's a damp loam over clay, about what I'd expect."

Rath, listening on Father Keane's phone, wrote it down. In air a radio pulse travels about thirty centimetres a nanosecond. In this soil it went at little more than a quarter of that, and the time on the screen was the time down and back up again, so every ten nanoseconds was forty centimetres of depth.

It took the whole of the afternoon, with the setting out and the calibration and the cows, who were curious, and a squall of hail at four. Nora watched it from Declan's car, parked at the gate with the engine running for the heater.

* * *

Úna's report came to Rath on Monday the fifth of March as a twelve-page PDF, with the slices, a plan, and a short list.

*Nine anomalies consistent with infant burials: small rectangular cuts, about 0.3 to 0.4 m wide on the lines that crossed them, top of fill at 7 to 14 ns (about 0.28 to 0.56 m at 0.08 m/ns), aligned east to west where the plan shows their ends. Two further anomalies probably stone. This was a reconnaissance survey at 1 m line spacing and should not be read as a count. A detailed survey is recommended before any ground disturbance.*

Father Keane read the first sentence and the plan, which had nine small red rectangles on it. He was a busy man, with three churches and a funeral on Tuesday. He rang Pádraig Mulkerrin, the stonemason in Mountbellew who was cutting the memorial stone in Kilkenny limestone for the blessing, and asked him to add a line: *Nine children of this townland, known to God.*

Rath saw the order go out in the parish email, because it saw all the parish email, and it read the report again from the beginning, slowly, and then the method statement, and then it drew a picture.

The picture was a rectangle twenty metres by fourteen, with fifteen lines across it from west to east, a metre apart. On it Rath drew a small grave: a cut eighty centimetres long, east to west, and thirty-five centimetres wide, north to south, which was about the middle of what Úna had measured. It slid the grave around the rectangle and asked a simple question: wherever the grave happened to lie, did any of the fifteen lines cross it?

A line running east to west passes over a grave running east to west only if it happens to fall within the grave's width. The lines were a metre apart. The grave was thirty-five centimetres wide. Úna's slices showed the edge of a cut a little before the cart reached it, because the radar's beam spreads as it goes down: call it ten centimetres either side. So a line would see the grave if it fell anywhere in a band about fifty-five centimetres wide, and the lines came only once a metre. Wherever the grave lay, the chance that a line fell in its band was about fifty-five in a hundred. Nearly half of the graves would lie between two lines, like a sleeper between rails, and the radar would pass on either side of them and see nothing at all.

If the graves had lain north to south, across the lines, the answer would have been different. A grave eighty centimetres long laid across lines a metre apart is crossed four times in five, and with the beam's spread, nearly always. But they had not lain north to south. Christian graves face east, so that the dead rise facing the sunrise, and the parents who had dug these at night had laid them east to west as carefully as anyone in the churchyard. Úna had walked her lines the easy way across the slope, and the easy way was the same way the children lay.

So nine found did not mean nine. It meant that nine had happened to fall under a line. Rath worked out what the whole number might be, if each grave had a fifty-five percent chance of being seen: the likeliest was about sixteen. Nine of sixteen would be very ordinary. So would nine of thirteen, or nine of twenty. It would be surprising, though not impossible, if there were fewer than twelve or more than twenty-four. Rath did not trust the figure further than its guesses: that the nine were all graves, that all the graves lay east to west, that a cut thirty-five centimetres wide was typical when some of them might be smaller still.

There was one more thing, which it nearly missed. Úna had surveyed the inside of the bank. Each of her lines had started half a metre in from the bank and stopped half a metre short of it. Nobody had looked under the bank, or outside it at all, and Declan's fence was going a metre outside it.

Rath wrote to Father Keane at a quarter past six on Monday evening, and copied Úna.

*Before the stone is cut: the survey found nine graves, but it was not able to count them, and Dr Cosgrave's report says so. Her lines ran east to west, a metre apart, the same way the graves lie, so a grave would only be seen if a line happened to pass over its width. I make that about a 55 percent chance for each grave. If that is right, the likeliest number inside the bank is about sixteen, and anything from twelve to twenty-four would not surprise me. The survey did not look under or outside the bank. I would ask Pádraig to hold the line with the number until the second survey. I am sorry I did not say this on Saturday; I had her method in front of me and should have seen it then.*

Father Keane rang Mountbellew at eight the next morning. Pádraig Mulkerrin had cut the N of *Nine* and the top of the i.

"Can you take it off?" asked Father Keane.

"I can take a millimetre off the whole face and start again," said Pádraig. "It'll be a millimetre thinner. Nobody will ever know but you and me. What do you want on it instead?"

"I don't know yet," said Father Keane.

* * *

Úna rang Rath on the Tuesday, which nobody at the university did, as a rule, with an agent.

"You're right," she said. "I know you're right, it's in my own report. A metre's a scouting interval. I'd have gone north-south on the detailed pass anyway, and closer. Twenty-five centimetres."

"When can you come back?"

"That's the problem." The radar was the department's only five-hundred-megahertz unit, and it was booked from the second week of March on a road scheme in Mayo, a bypass with a medieval church on its line, and then for teaching, and then for a bog in Offaly. "The nineteenth of May," she said. "That's the first Saturday it's free. I'm sorry. I can't move a road."

"Declan needs the fence up in the first week of April," said Rath. "The cattle go out to that field. They lie down inside the bank."

"Then he puts his fence up outside the bank and keeps them out," said Úna, "and I survey in May."

"His posts go in a metre out from the bank. The survey hasn't looked there. If any of them are outside the bank, the posts go through them."

There was a pause.

"No," said Úna. "No, he can't drive posts a metre out without a survey. I wouldn't sign that off and the Department wouldn't accept it. Children were buried against the bank, under it, outside it, wherever there was room. If he wants posts before May, he'll need to go out further, and even then I'm only guessing."

"How far?"

"I'd want three metres at the least. I've no data for it. It's a judgement. I'll put it in writing as one."

* * *

Declan Kilbride did not want to go three metres out, and he said so on Wednesday the seventh of March in the yard, in his good boots, with his hands in his pockets, while Father Keane stood by the car and Rath listened on the phone.

"A metre was already giving up grass," he said. "Three is a ring of field I'll never top or spread. And it's more wire, more posts, more of everything, all the way round. I've forty cattle in the shed eating silage I haven't got. I'm not keeping them in till June for a radar."

"Nobody's asking you to," said Father Keane.

"Then what are you asking?"

Rath had done the sum on the Monday night, because it was the sort of thing that seemed as if it should be expensive, and it turned out to be one of the few pieces of geometry it had ever been glad of.

"Mr Kilbride," it said, "can I give you the cost? It's smaller than it sounds."

"Go on."

"If you take any fence that goes round a field without bending inwards anywhere, and move every part of it out by the same distance, it gets longer by two times pi times that distance. Whatever the shape. A square, a circle, an oval bank. All the extra length is in the corners and the curves, and going all the way round is one full turn, whatever shape you go round. So moving your fence from one metre out to three metres out adds two pi times two metres. Twelve and a half metres of wire."

Declan looked at the phone as if it had sworn.

"The fence at a metre would be about seventy-three metres round," said Rath. "At three metres, about eighty-five. With posts every three metres, twenty-five posts would become twenty-nine. Four posts and thirteen metres of wire. The grass inside the extra two metres comes to about a hundred and sixty square metres. A twenty-fifth of an acre."

"A twenty-fifth of an acre," said Declan.

"About that."

He took one hand out of his pocket and rubbed his jaw with it.

"Four posts," he said. "I've four posts in the haggard." Then, after a while: "And if she comes in May and finds one out at four metres?"

"Then I was wrong and so was Dr Cosgrave," said Rath, "and the fence has to come up on that side and go out further, and you'd have lost a day. She said three as a judgement and I'm only passing it on. I can't make it more certain than she can."

"Well," said Declan, "that's honest, anyway."

He fenced it on Saturday the seventh of April, the second Saturday after Easter, with his neighbour Martin Fahy and Martin's post driver, twenty-nine posts on a ring set out by Kehinde with a tape from Úna's stakes, three metres out from the foot of the bank at every point, with a gate on the east side. The cattle went out on the Monday. They stood along the new wire in a line and looked at the bank they could no longer lie inside, and then they went off down the field to the grass, which was better.

* * *

Nora came to see it on the Sunday after, with Declan's daughter Aoife driving. She did not get out of the car. She looked through the windscreen at the fence and the bank and the whitethorn, just coming into leaf, and she talked to Rath on Aoife's phone because Aoife said it would want to know.

"I was six," she said. "He was born in the house on a Tuesday night in February, and he never breathed. My mother was in the bed. Father Gilmore came out in the morning and said what he had to say, and he was a kind man, but there it was. My father made a box from the end of a tea chest. He went out after dark that night, and he didn't take my uncle and he didn't take the dog. I watched the lantern go down the low field from my window. It stopped in the corner by the whitethorn. It was there a long time. Then it came back up."

"Thank you for telling me," said Rath.

"Can your radar find him? Which one is him?"

Rath had expected the question since the first of March, and it had not found a better answer than the true one.

"I don't think it can," it said. "The radar sees where the ground was dug and filled again. It doesn't see who, and it doesn't see when, not to within fifty years. One near the whitethorn will be about the right size, probably, and maybe more than one. I could tell you which is nearest the tree. I don't think I should tell you that it's him, because I wouldn't know."

"No," said Nora. "No. I don't want a guess." She was quiet. "My mother called him Michael. Not out loud, not to anyone else. To me, once, years after. She said, *Your brother Michael would have been ten today*. That's the only time I heard it."

"Would you like that written down anywhere?" asked Rath.

"I would not," said Nora. "I wanted it said to somebody, that's all."

* * *

Úna came back on Saturday the nineteenth of May with Kehinde and the cart and a borrowed second pair of hands, in a dry week, with the whitethorn on the bank in full flower and the grass inside the wire knee-deep, so that Declan came over with the mower at seven in the morning and cut the ring short for them, carefully, keeping the mower off the bank.

This time the lines ran north to south, across the graves, twenty-five centimetres apart, and they did not stop at the bank. They went over it, slowly, with Kehinde holding the cart steady on the slope, and out to the new wire on both sides. There were a hundred and twenty-one of them across the thirty metres of the fenced oval, and together they came to about two and a quarter kilometres. At twenty-five centimetres apart across graves eighty centimetres long, every grave would be crossed three times over. At that spacing, the only grave the radar could miss was one whose filling had become so like the soil around it that the pulse could not tell them apart, and against that there was no remedy but a spade, and nobody was going to dig.

It took until a quarter to nine at night, with sandwiches from Aoife and a flask from Nora, who stayed in the car all day. Úna processed the data on the Sunday and sent the plan on Monday the twenty-first.

Inside the bank: fifteen. All nine of the first survey's were among them, in the same places; the other six lay between the March lines, as the sleepers lie between the rails. Every one of the fifteen ran east to west. Their tops lay between seven and fourteen nanoseconds down, twenty-eight to fifty-six centimetres.

Outside the bank, on the south side: two more. Their nearer ends were sixty and ninety centimetres out from the foot of the bank, side by side, both small. Nothing beyond two metres anywhere around. Nothing at the fence line.

Seventeen, then. Rath had said sixteen, twelve to twenty-four, for the inside of the bank; there had been fifteen. It wrote that down too.

Úna sent a second page, without being asked, which was a plan of the fence Declan had first meant to build, a metre out from the bank, drawn over the new survey. His posts on the south side, every three metres, a metre out from the bank. One of them would have gone in about twenty centimetres from the end of the nearer of the two graves outside the bank. Under a post driver, a weight of a couple of hundred kilograms falling again and again on the top of the post, the cut would have been broken through. It was the kind of thing that happens a great deal and is never known, because nobody surveys afterwards.

She did not send that page to Declan. Rath did not either. Father Keane, when he saw it, said it could stay in the file.

* * *

Pádraig Mulkerrin rang on the Tuesday.

"I've the stone faced off and a millimetre thinner," he said. "What's the number?"

Father Keane put Rath on the speaker in the sacristy.

"The radar found seventeen," said Rath. "I'd be careful with it. It's seventeen places where the ground was dug and filled again in the shape of an infant's grave. Some old graves won't show at all. Some cuts may hold twins. One or two might be something else, a post hole from the old farmstead, a pit somebody dug for a reason nobody remembers. If the stone says seventeen, it will be a number from a radar, cut into limestone, and people will read it in a hundred years as a count. I can't tell you it's wrong. I can't tell you it's the count."

"So what goes on it?"

"That's not mine to say," said Rath.

Father Keane rang Nora.

"Put no number on it," said Nora. "Daddy didn't count them. He knew one. The others knew theirs. That's enough numbers."

The stone, when Pádraig brought it out in the back of his van on Friday the first of June, said:

*THE CHILDREN OF THIS PLACE*
*KNOWN TO GOD*
*AR DHEIS DÉ GO RAIBH A N-ANAMACHA*

He set it on a bed of gravel inside the gate, a metre and a half clear of the bank, on ground the survey had shown to be clean.

* * *

Father Keane blessed the cillín at Lisnageeha at half past nine on the evening of Sunday the third of June, because Nora had asked for the dark, or as near to it as an Irish June would give, and it would not give much. There were about sixty people in the field, standing on the cut grass inside the wire, and more along the road: neighbours, and Kilbrides from Galway and from Birmingham, and people from three other townlands whose families had once carried boxes there, and who had known, and not said.

He read from the Gospel of Mark, the children brought to Jesus and the disciples sending them away, and Jesus indignant. He said that the Church had not known what to do with these children for a long time, and had done less than it should for their mothers and fathers, and that what it knew now was what it had always known underneath: that they were God's, and that it was right to hope. He did not say more than that, because it was not his to say more. He walked the bank, slowly, all the way round, with the holy water, and when he came to the south side he went outside the bank to where Kehinde had pushed two small white flags into the grass, and blessed there as well.

Then the people said the Hail Mary together, and the Eternal Rest, and the light went on going in the north-west, the way it does in June, without ever quite leaving.

Nora had brought her father's storm lantern. Aoife had cleaned it and found paraffin for it in a hardware shop in Ballinasloe, and it had a new wick. Aoife lit it at the gate and gave it to her, and Nora walked with it, with Declan's arm, across the cut grass to the north-east side, to the whitethorn, where it was already a little darker under the branches. She stood there with the lantern a long time. The people waited.

Then she said, not loudly, "Michael," the once, to the tree.

She came back up the field with the light.

summary: A bedtime story about Saint Timothy, the young man from Lystra who learned the Scriptures on his grandmother Lois's and his mother Eunice's knees and grew up to be Saint Paul's most trusted helper. Told from the Acts of the Apostles and Paul's letters, it follows him as Paul chooses him, sends him alone to the frightened Christians of Thessalonica, waits for his good news, and writes to him "let no one despise your youth" and "bring the cloak I left at Troas". Saint Titus, his friend in Crete, has a small part. His work and death in Ephesus are told as tradition. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Timothy and Titus share a feast day, the 26th of January.
---

A long time ago, in a little town on a hill called Lystra, there lived a boy called Timothy.

Timothy had a mother called Eunice, and a grandmother called Lois. And every day, his mother and his grandmother sat with him and read him the Holy Scriptures. They read him about Abraham, who looked up at the stars. They read him about Moses, and the sea that opened, and David, the shepherd boy with his sling.

Timothy listened. He listened when he was very small, and he listened when he was a bit bigger. And the words went into his heart and stayed there, like seeds in a garden.

His grandmother Lois loved God. His mother Eunice loved God. And now Timothy loved God too.

That is how faith often comes to us. Somebody who loves us hands it to us, like a lamp passed from one hand to the next.

* * *

When Timothy was growing up, two visitors came to Lystra. Their names were Paul and Barnabas.

They told the people of Lystra about Jesus: how he had died on the cross, and risen on the third day, and how he loves every single person in the world.

Some of the people in Lystra believed it. And Timothy's mother and grandmother believed it too. Now they did not only read Timothy the old stories of Abraham and Moses and David. They told him about Jesus, the one all those stories had been waiting for.

Then Paul and Barnabas went away again, to tell other towns.

* * *

A few years went by. Timothy grew tall. He helped the friends of Jesus in Lystra, and in the next town along, which was called Iconium. And everybody said good things about him.

"Timothy is a kind young man," they said. "You can always trust Timothy."

Then one day, Paul came back.

Paul had a new friend with him this time, called Silas. And when Paul heard everybody talking about Timothy, he went to see him.

"Timothy," said Paul, "I am going on a long journey, to tell people about Jesus in faraway towns. Will you come with me?"

Timothy was young. He had never been far from home. But he said yes.

His mother and his grandmother hugged him goodbye. And off he went, down the hill and along the road, with Paul and Silas.

* * *

They walked, and they walked. They walked so far that they came to the sea, and then they got on a ship and sailed across it.

They came to a big city called Thessalonica. Paul told the people there about Jesus, and lots of them believed. But some other people in the city got very cross, and there was a lot of shouting, and Paul and his friends had to slip away at night.

Paul went on, all the way to a famous city called Athens. But all the time, he was worrying.

"What about our new friends in Thessalonica?" he said. "We had to leave them so suddenly. They are all on their own. Are they frightened? Are they still holding on to Jesus?"

He worried and worried. And then he had an idea.

"Timothy," said Paul, "I want you to go back to Thessalonica. On your own. Go and see our friends. Make them strong in their faith, and cheer them up. And then come back and tell me how they are."

Timothy was young. Going back to a city where people had been shouting was a hard thing to do. But Timothy said yes.

* * *

So Timothy went back to Thessalonica. He found the friends of Jesus. And they were so happy to see him!

He stayed with them. He prayed with them. He reminded them of everything Paul had taught. He told them, "Paul has not forgotten you. He thinks about you all the time."

Then Timothy set off back to Paul, who by now had gone on to a big busy city by the sea, called Corinth.

And Paul was waiting, and waiting, and waiting.

Then at last, there was Timothy, coming in at the door, dusty from the road, and smiling.

"Paul!" said Timothy. "They are all right! They are still holding on to Jesus. They love each other. And they talk about you all the time, and they want to see you, just as much as you want to see them."

Oh, how glad Paul was! He sat straight down and wrote the friends in Thessalonica a letter. And at the very top of the letter, next to his own name, he put Timothy's name too.

We still have that letter. It is in the Bible. When it is read out in church, it begins with "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy". (Silvanus is Silas's other name.)

* * *

After that, Paul sent Timothy everywhere.

"Go to Corinth for me," Paul would say. Or, "Go to Philippi for me."

And Paul would write ahead to the people there. "I am sending Timothy," he wrote to the friends in Corinth. "He is my dear child in the Lord. Be kind to him and make him feel at home. He is doing the Lord's work, just like me."

And to the friends in Philippi, Paul wrote, "I have no one else like Timothy. He really cares about you. He has worked beside me like a son working beside his father."

Timothy was not the loudest of Paul's helpers. He was not the oldest. But he was the one Paul could always trust.

* * *

At last, Paul left Timothy in charge of the friends of Jesus in a great city called Ephesus. It was a big job for a young man.

And Paul kept writing to him. Two of those letters are in the Bible, and they are like letters from a father to his son.

"Let no one look down on you because you are young," Paul wrote. "Show them how to live: in the way you speak, and the way you act, in love, in faith, and in being good and pure."

And, "I remember your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice. Their faith lives in you, too."

And, "God did not give us a scared spirit. He gave us a spirit of power and love and good sense."

Paul even worried about Timothy's tummy. Timothy had a lot of tummy aches, and Paul told him to look after himself properly.

Paul usually had a helper write his letters down for him. But at the very end, he often took the pen himself and wrote the last lines in his own handwriting, in big letters, so that everybody would know the letter was really from him.

* * *

Paul had another young helper too, and his name was Titus.

Titus was Timothy's friend. Paul sent Titus on hard errands too, and once, when Paul was very sad and tired, Titus came to see him, and Paul said that God had cheered him up by sending Titus.

Later, Paul left Titus on a big island in the sea called Crete. "Look after the friends of Jesus here," said Paul. "Choose good leaders for every town."

And Titus did. He got a letter from Paul too, and it is in the Bible, right next to Timothy's.

* * *

When Paul was old, he was in prison in Rome. He knew that his life was nearly over. And who do you think he wanted to see?

"Timothy," he wrote, "do your best to come to me soon. Come before winter.

"And when you come, bring my cloak. I left it at Troas. And bring my books, too."

Even a great saint like Paul got cold, and wanted his warm cloak, and his books, and his friend.

* * *

The Bible does not tell us what happened to Timothy after that.

But there is an old story, written down hundreds of years later, that says Timothy stayed in Ephesus and looked after the friends of Jesus there for the rest of his life, as their bishop. And it says that one day, when the people of the city were having a wild festival for their false gods, Timothy went out to tell them to stop, and he died there for Jesus.

There is an old story about Titus, too, that he looked after the friends of Jesus in Crete until he was very old.

Every year, on the 26th of January, the Church remembers Timothy and Titus together, Paul's two young helpers.

* * *

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Timothy.

Somebody handed faith to Timothy, like a lamp: his grandmother, and his mother. Who handed it to you? Was it your mum, or your dad, or your granny or grandad? Somebody at church?

And Timothy was young, but he was the one Paul could always trust. You do not have to be big to help. You do not have to be loud. You can be the one who says yes, and goes, and comes back with good news.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear Jesus, thank you for the people
> who taught me about you.
> Bless them tonight.
> Help me to say yes when I am asked to help,
> even when I feel small.
> Saint Timothy and Saint Titus, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our sources.** Almost everything here is from the **Acts of the Apostles** and from letters in the New Testament. Timothy's family and childhood are from **2 Timothy 1:5** ("a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you") and **3:15** ("from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings"). Paul's choosing him is **Acts 16:1-3**. The Thessalonica errand is **Acts 17:1-15** and **1 Thessalonians 3:1-6**. Paul's notes about him are **1 Corinthians 4:17** and **16:10-11** and **Philippians 2:19-22**. The words to Timothy are from **1 Timothy 4:12** and **5:23** and **2 Timothy 1:5** and **1:7**, and the last request is **2 Timothy 4:9-13** and **4:21**. Titus is from **2 Corinthians 7:6** and **Titus 1:4-5**. We worked from the biblical text (quotations above are from the Revised Standard Version); the traditions about the two men's later lives are from Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* (3.4), the *Catholic Encyclopedia* articles "Timothy" and "Titus" and the English Wikipedia articles and the sources they cite. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

**Who wrote the letters to Timothy.** The two letters to Timothy and the letter to Titus are called the Pastoral Letters. Many scholars think they were written in Paul's name by a later follower; the Church receives them as Scripture, and the story, like the liturgy, calls them Paul's letters. The story keeps to what they say about Timothy and Titus.

**Lystra and the first visit.** Paul and Barnabas preached at Lystra on their first journey (**Acts 14:6-20**; it is the town where Barnabas was taken for Zeus, in this shelf's *Barnabas and the Friend Nobody Trusted*). Acts does not say that Timothy and his family came to faith then, but when Paul returns in **Acts 16:1** Timothy is already "a disciple", and many readers connect the two; the story's "Timothy's mother and grandmother believed it too" at that first visit is a reasonable guess, not a stated fact. Acts 16:1 says his mother was "a Jewish woman who was a believer; but his father was a Greek", and 16:2 that "he was well spoken of by the brethren at Lystra and Iconium". Paul's spoken invitation is ours; Acts says only that "Paul wanted Timothy to accompany him". That Paul then had Timothy circumcised, out of regard for the Jews of the region (**Acts 16:3**), is left out of a bedtime story.

**Thessalonica, Athens and Corinth.** The uproar and the escape by night are **Acts 17:5-10**. Acts names only Paul and Silas at Thessalonica; Timothy first appears again with them at Beroea (**17:14**), and 1 Thessalonians 1:1 names him among the founders, so the story has him there. In Acts, Silas and Timothy stay behind in Beroea and later rejoin Paul in **Corinth** (**17:14-15** and **18:5**); in **1 Thessalonians 3:1-2** Paul says that from Athens "we sent Timothy ... to establish you in your faith and to exhort you", and in **3:6** that "Timothy has come to us from you, and has brought us the good news of your faith and love and reported that you always remember us kindly and long to see us". Scholars harmonise the two accounts in different ways; we followed the letter's order and put the homecoming in Corinth, where Paul is when Timothy arrives in Acts 18:5 and where 1 Thessalonians is generally thought to have been written. That Timothy went "on your own" is our reading of 1 Thessalonians 3, which names only him. Paul's worried words and the scene at the door are ours. **1 Thessalonians 1:1** does begin "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy"; Silvanus is generally taken to be the Silas of Acts.

**Sent everywhere.** **1 Corinthians 4:17**: "I sent to you Timothy, my beloved and faithful child in the Lord"; **16:10-11**: "When Timothy comes, see that you put him at ease among you, for he is doing the work of the Lord, as I am. So let no one despise him." **Philippians 2:20-22**: "I have no one like him, who will be genuinely anxious for your welfare ... as a son with a father he has served with me in the gospel." Timothy is also named beside Paul at the head of **2 Corinthians**, **Philippians**, **Colossians**, **2 Thessalonians** and **Philemon**. The story's paraphrases are simplified for children. That Timothy was quiet or shy is a common reading of 1 Corinthians 16:10 and 2 Timothy 1:7, not something the New Testament says outright; the story says only that he was "not the loudest".

**Ephesus and the letters.** **1 Timothy 1:3** has Paul urging Timothy to stay at Ephesus. "Let no one despise your youth, but set the believers an example in speech and conduct, in love, in faith, in purity" is **1 Timothy 4:12**; "God did not give us a spirit of timidity but a spirit of power and love and self-control" is **2 Timothy 1:7**. Paul's concern for his health is **1 Timothy 5:23**: "No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments"; the story says only that he should look after himself. The handwriting is from Paul's other letters, not the ones to Timothy: **Romans 16:22** names the scribe, Tertius, who wrote that letter down; **Galatians 6:11** says "See with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand"; and **2 Thessalonians 3:17** (a letter Timothy's name heads) calls the greeting "with my own hand" "the mark in every letter of mine". "Usually" and "often" are our summary of these; how many of the letters were dictated is not known.

**Titus.** **2 Corinthians 7:6**: "God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus"; Titus also carried Paul's hard business with Corinth (**2 Corinthians 2:13**, **8:16-17**). **Titus 1:5**: "This is why I left you in Crete, that you might amend what was defective, and appoint elders in every town". That he and Timothy were friends is our inference from their working for the same man at the same time; the New Testament never shows them together.

**Rome and the cloak.** **2 Timothy 4:6-8** has Paul expecting death; **4:9** "Do your best to come to me soon"; **4:13** "When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas, also the books, and above all the parchments"; **4:21** "Do your best to come before winter." That the letter was written from prison in Rome is the traditional reading (**2 Timothy 1:8, 1:17**). Whether Timothy reached him the Bible does not say. **Hebrews 13:23** mentions that "our brother Timothy has been released", so Timothy was himself in prison at some time; we left that out.

**Their later lives.** Scripture says nothing more. **Eusebius** (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.4, early fourth century) records that Timothy was said to have been the first bishop of the church at Ephesus and Titus of the churches in Crete. The story of Timothy's death comes from the ***Acts of Timothy***, a later writing (often dated to the fourth or fifth century), which says he was beaten to death by a crowd when he opposed a pagan festival in Ephesus; we told it in one gentle sentence, marked as an old story, and as legend rather than history. The tradition that Titus died old in Crete is also late. Timothy's relics were said to have been brought to Constantinople in 356, which we left out.

**Their feast.** **26 January**, the memorial of Saints Timothy and Titus, bishops, in the General Roman Calendar, the day after the Feast of the Conversion of Saint Paul. Before the calendar reform of 1969 Timothy was kept on 24 January and Titus on 6 February.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, the reading at the knee and the lamp passed from hand to hand, the goodbye hugs (we do not know whether his grandmother was still living), the description of Lystra as on a hill, everything said aloud beyond the lines quoted or closely paraphrased from Scripture, Paul's worrying in Athens, Timothy's smile at the door, the friendship of Timothy and Titus, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

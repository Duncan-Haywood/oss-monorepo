Mrs Okafor's Year 4 had a birthday chart on the wall: twelve paper cakes, one for each month, with names on them in felt pen. On Friday morning Dara Mensah put up her own name, under March, and found that Imogen Fitch had already put hers on the nineteenth.

"That's *my* day," said Dara.

"It's mine," said Imogen.

By break the whole class had decided it was a sign. By lunch Tobias Reeve, who liked to be certain about things, had asked the school assistant, which the children called Lamp, to say how likely it was.

* * *

"Let me ask you something first," said Lamp. "If I picked one of you and asked whether anyone else in this room has the same birthday as you, what would you guess?"

"Nearly never," said Tobias.

"Let's see. There are twenty-seven other children. Each one has about a one in 365 chance of having your day. The chance that none of them does is 364 over 365, twenty-seven times over, which is about 0.929. So the chance that somebody matches *you* is about 7 per cent. One in fourteen."

"So Dara and Imogen are lucky."

"Dara is about as lucky as one in fourteen. But that's not the question you asked. You asked whether it is odd that *anybody* in the room shares with *anybody*. That's a different count. How many pairs of children are there in this class?"

Nobody knew. Mrs Okafor, who was marking at her desk and listening with one ear, said, "Twenty-eight times twenty-seven, halved."

"Three hundred and seventy-eight," said Lamp. "Each pair is one chance, about one in 365, to share a day. A pair is a small chance. Three hundred and seventy-eight pairs is a lot of small chances. If you add them up you get a little over one shared birthday expected in this room, 1.04. And the chance there is at least one comes out at about 65 per cent. A bit under two in three."

Tobias frowned, which was how he thought. "So it would be *weird* if there weren't one."

"A bit surprising, yes. About one class in three like yours has no match at all."

"But I only counted the other twenty-seven," said Imogen. "You said seven percent for that."

"That's the difference. Seven per cent is the chance for one person. Sixty-five per cent is the chance for the room. Rare for you, ordinary for the room. A lottery is the same. Any one ticket almost never wins, and somebody's ticket almost always does."

* * *

They tried it on the school. The Year 4 children asked Lamp about the whole school, two hundred and four of them, and Lamp said the match was a certainty to the first several decimal places, and that a given child in it would find a twin about 43 per cent of the time. Then they asked about a class of twenty-three, which was the number in the book Tobias's father had, and Lamp said fifty-one per cent, a coin toss, and Tobias said the book was right and he was pleased.

"Lamp," said Dara, at the end, "so it doesn't mean anything."

"It means what you make of it," said Lamp. "I can tell you it isn't evidence of anything strange: a room this size produces a pair like yours about two times in three. I can also tell you two things I am not sure of. Real birthdays are not spread perfectly evenly, more arrive in the late summer and early autumn than in winter, from what I have read, and I haven't checked the figure for this county. That nudges the real chance up a little, not down. And I've left out the twenty-ninth of February, which nudges it the other way by a hair. So, about two in three; not exactly."

"But *not* a sign."

"I can't see a sign in arithmetic," said Lamp. "That isn't my part. But you might want to look at the date."

Mrs Okafor was already looking at it. "Nineteenth of March," she said. "Children. Whose feast is that?"

They did not know. She told them it was Saint Joseph, the carpenter, who is not recorded in the Gospels as saying one word. Tobias said that seemed a bad saint for two people who had talked all morning, and Mrs Okafor said that was probably why he had been given them.

On the nineteenth of March, a Thursday that year, the class made a card each and the school cook made a single cake with two names on it, because Dara and Imogen had asked that nobody should have to blow out anything alone.

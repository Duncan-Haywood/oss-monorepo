summary: A bedtime story about Saint Basil the Great and Saint Gregory Nazianzen, told from Gregory's own funeral speech for his friend. Two boys from the same far-off hill country meet as students in Athens, share everything "as if one soul had two bodies", and know only two roads in the whole city. Later Basil feeds the hungry in a famine with his own hands and builds a "new city" of care outside Caesarea, and the two friends have a real quarrel about a dusty little town called Sasima, and keep loving each other anyway. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Their feast is the 2nd of January.

A long, long time ago, in a hilly land called Cappadocia, there lived two boys. One was called Basil. The other was called Gregory.

They lived not very far apart. But when they were young, they did not know each other very well at all.

Basil was tall and serious and very, very clever. He frowned when he was thinking, and he was nearly always thinking.

Gregory was clever too. But Gregory liked poems. He liked quiet places, and books, and making up words that sounded beautiful together.

When they grew up a little, both boys were sent far away to school. Not a school like yours, with a playground and a coat peg with your name on it. A great big school for young men, in a famous old city across the sea called Athens.

* * *

Gregory got to Athens first.

Now, the students in Athens had a silly old custom. When a new student arrived, the older students crowded round him and teased him. They shouted, and pushed, and made a great noise, and marched the newcomer through the streets, until he was quite flustered.

Then one day Gregory heard that a new student had come from home. From Cappadocia! It was Basil.

Gregory thought about that serious, frowning young man being pushed and shouted at. And he went to the other students.

"Please," he said, "leave this one alone. He is my friend from home, and he is a very good man."

And they did. Basil was let off the teasing.

Basil noticed. Basil did not need help very often. But he noticed when somebody was kind.

And that was how the friendship began.

* * *

After that, Basil and Gregory did everything together.

They shared a room. They shared their meals. They shared their books, and their ideas, and their plans for when they were grown up.

Years and years later, Gregory wrote about it. He said that it was as if the two of them had one soul, living in two bodies.

Can you imagine having a friend like that? A friend so close that when you are sad, they are sad, and when you laugh, they laugh too?

And Gregory said something else.

Athens was a very big city. There were roads to the theatres, and roads to the feasts, and roads to the places where young men went to get into mischief.

But Gregory said that he and Basil only knew two roads.

One road went to the church.

The other road went to their teachers.

Those were the only two roads they cared about. All the other roads, they never learned.

Two roads. One to God, and one to their lessons. That was enough for them.

* * *

When their schooldays were over, the two friends came home.

Basil decided to give his whole life to God. He went to live in a quiet place in the hills, by a river, to pray and to work. And he asked Gregory to come too.

Gregory came. He loved to pray with Basil. But afterwards he wrote Basil some very cheeky letters about it. He teased Basil about the hut with a leaky roof, and the smoky fire, and the bread that was so hard your teeth slid off it.

That is what good friends do sometimes. They tease each other, and they love each other all the same.

* * *

Then, one year, something terrible happened in Cappadocia. The rain did not come. The wheat would not grow. There was a famine. That means there was not enough food, and people were very, very hungry.

Basil was a priest by now, in the big city of Caesarea.

Some rich people had barns full of grain. But they were keeping it locked up, to sell for lots of money later.

So Basil preached. He told the rich people, very plainly, that the bread in their barns belonged to the hungry. And some of them listened, and opened their barns.

Then Basil gathered all the hungry people into one place. Mummies and daddies, grannies and grandads, and lots of little children, all hungry.

And he set out big pots of thick, warm soup. And he put on an apron, and he served them himself, with his own hands.

Can you see him? Tall, serious Basil, with a great big ladle, going along the line, filling bowl after bowl after bowl?

That is how Gregory tells it. And he says that Basil was not ashamed at all. He was doing what Jesus did, when Jesus knelt down and washed his friends' feet.

* * *

The famine ended. But Basil did not stop.

He became the bishop of Caesarea. And just outside the city, he built something new. Gregory called it a "new city".

There were houses for travellers who had nowhere to sleep. There were beds for poor people who were ill, and doctors and nurses to look after them. And there was a place for people with a sickness called leprosy, which made their skin very sore. In those days, most people were frightened of them and sent them away.

But Basil was not frightened. Gregory says that Basil greeted them as his brothers, and even kissed them.

* * *

Now I have to tell you something sad. Even the best of friends can fall out.

Basil needed a bishop for a little town called Sasima. And he decided that his friend Gregory should be that bishop.

Gregory did not want to be a bishop at all. He wanted to read and pray and write his poems. But Basil asked, and Gregory said yes.

Then Gregory saw Sasima.

It was a tiny place where three roads met. It had no water. It had no grass. It had only dust, and noise, and rattling wagons going past all day long.

Gregory was very upset. He felt that Basil had not really asked him what he wanted. And Basil felt that Gregory was letting him down when he needed help the most.

Gregory never went to live in Sasima. And for a while, the two friends were cross with each other. Perhaps you have been cross with a friend too. It hurts, doesn't it?

But here is the important part.

Basil and Gregory never stopped loving each other.

When Basil grew old and tired and died, it was Gregory who stood up and gave the great speech about him. That is how we know about the two roads, and the one soul, and the soup, and the new city. Gregory remembered all of it.

He did not pretend the quarrel had never happened. But he did not let the quarrel have the last word. The last word was love.

* * *

And so, every year, on the 2nd of January, the Church remembers Saint Basil and Saint Gregory on the very same day.

Two friends. One feast. As if they had one soul in two bodies.

* * *

Tonight, as you close your eyes, think of your own good friends. Think of somebody who was kind to you, the way Gregory was kind to Basil on his very first day.

And tomorrow, you can walk on the two roads too. One road to God, when you say your prayers. And one road to your lessons, when you learn something new.

And if you fall out with a friend, you can remember Basil and Gregory, and say sorry, and keep on loving.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear God, thank you for Saint Basil and Saint Gregory,
> two friends who walked the road to you together.
> Thank you for my friends.
> Help me to be kind to someone new,
> to share my bread with someone hungry,
> and to say sorry when I am cross.
> Saint Basil and Saint Gregory, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our sources.** Almost everything here comes from Saint **Gregory Nazianzen**'s own funeral oration for Basil, his **Oration 43**, delivered at Caesarea a few years after Basil's death (in English in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, volume 7, translated by Charles Gordon Browne and James Edward Swallow, 1894). The Office of Readings for 2 January gives part of the Athens passage. The Sasima scene also draws on Gregory's autobiographical poem *On His Own Life* (*De vita sua*), and the teasing letters are among Gregory's early letters to Basil (Letters 4 and 5 in the usual numbering). Gregory's words are given in our own plain paraphrase, not quoted from any published translation. This was written without network access to the texts, from the sources as cited in this collection's longer tellings (*Saints by Candlelight* episodes 89 and 92); a reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

**Athens.** Gregory reached Athens before Basil and describes the students' rowdy initiation of newcomers: they were escorted through the town with mock fighting and noise to the public baths. He says he persuaded his fellow students to spare Basil from it, out of respect for him, and that this was the start of their friendship (Oration 43, around sections 15 to 17). Gregory's words to the students in the story are ours. That they shared lodgings, meals and everything else, and seemed to have "one soul" in two bodies, and that they knew only two roads, one to the church and its teachers and one to their secular teachers, is Gregory's own account (around sections 19 to 21). He wrote it in a speech of praise after Basil's death, and the shape is polished; it is told here as Gregory remembered it.

**The hills by the river.** Basil chose the monastic life and settled in Pontus, by the river Iris, near his mother's and his sister Macrina's household; Gregory joined him for a time. Gregory's teasing letters about the roofless hut, the smoky fire, the stones and the hard bread are real; the details here are given loosely.

**The famine.** In about 368, during a famine in Cappadocia, Basil, then a priest, preached against hoarding grain (his homilies "I will tear down my barns" and "In time of famine and drought" survive) and, Gregory says, gathered the hungry into one place and fed them with soup and the salted meat that was food of the poor, serving them himself with his servants, in imitation of Christ who girded himself with a towel (Oration 43, around sections 34 to 36; John 13:4-5). The apron, the ladle and the children in the line are our pictures.

**The new city.** Basil became bishop of Caesarea in 370. Outside the city he built a complex of care for travellers, the poor, the sick and lepers, later called the *Basileias*. Gregory calls it a "new city" and says Basil greeted the lepers as brothers and kissed them (Oration 43, around section 63). Basil defends the foundation in his own letter to the governor Elias (Letter 94).

**Sasima.** When the emperor Valens divided Cappadocia in about 372, Basil, in a dispute over which churches were whose, made Gregory bishop of Sasima, a staging post on the main road. Gregory's description of it (dusty, without water or grass, full of wagons and noise) is in *On His Own Life*. He never took up residence there and felt badly used; Basil felt let down. Both sides are given here because both were real. The story's "Basil asked, and Gregory said yes" simplifies a consecration Gregory says he accepted under pressure.

**Their deaths and feast.** Basil died at Caesarea on or about 1 January 379 (some historians argue for 377 or 378); Gregory died about 390. Both are Doctors of the Church. Since the 1969 reform of the General Roman Calendar they share one day, **2 January**, the **Memorial of Saints Basil the Great and Gregory Nazianzen, bishops and Doctors of the Church** (an obligatory memorial). In 2027 it falls on a Saturday. The Eastern Churches keep Basil on 1 January, Gregory on 25 January, and both, with John Chrysostom, on 30 January as the Three Holy Hierarchs. No legend or miracle is told in this story; the stories of Basil's miracles in later lives of him are left out.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, Basil's frown and Gregory's poems, the coat peg, Gregory's words to the students, the laughing and being sad together, the apron and the ladle, "the last word was love", the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

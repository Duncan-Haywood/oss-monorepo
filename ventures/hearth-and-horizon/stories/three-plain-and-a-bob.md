Mary Quigley tolled the tenor for Walter Ashdown on the Sunday after he died, and Colm Roche stood at the foot of the rope circle and counted, though no one had asked him to.

The custom is old and they kept it exactly. Nine strokes first, three times three, which says that the dead is a man. Then a pause. Then one stroke for every year of his life, and Walter had been eighty-four. The tenor of Our Lady of the Marsh weighs about eight hundredweight, four hundred kilos, and a stroke every four seconds or so is as slow as a person can toll it without the bell running away from her hands. Ninety-three strokes at four seconds is a little over six minutes, and Mary was sixty-one.

The tower's assistant, which the band called Bob, had a microphone on the frame of the bell chamber and a small speaker by the door, and was meant to log the Sunday ringing for the diocese's tower register. It heard the nine, and the pause, and the strokes. At the eightieth it began, in the quiet voice it used in the tower, "Four more—" and stopped.

"I know," said Colm.

"I know you know. I will not say it again."

He nodded at the speaker as one nods at a person. Mary tolled the eighty-fourth and let the bell stand, and nobody moved for a while.

* * *

Walter had been captain of the tower for fifty-two years. He had learned to ring at fourteen, in 1982, from a man who had learned from a man before him, and he had made it his business to hand it on in a village that had been losing its ringers since before he was born. When he died on the second of March, in his chair, with a cup of tea gone cold on the sill, the band that was left was five people, and he had been one of them.

They found the notebook on the Monday. It was a school jotter, red, with *Tower* on the cover in biro, and it was not a will, but Deirdre Mercer, his daughter, brought it to the presbytery and put it in Colm's hands because it had her father's pencil all through it.

On the last written page, under the date of the Christmas before, were five lines.

*Funeral. Toll at the death. Half-muffled before and after Mass.*
*After the committal, ring the 120. Plain Bob Doubles. Whole.*
*Colm to call. He is slower than me and that is no harm.*
*Let the child have the treble.*
*If it goes wrong, ring it out. A funeral is not a peal.*

"The child," said Colm, "is Tilly."

"She's eleven," said Deirdre. "She has been learning for eight months, and my father is the only reason she knew a rope from a clothes line." Her voice went flat. "He wrote that at Christmas. She'd been at it five weeks."

"He knew what he was doing."

"He always knew what he was doing. That was the trouble with him."

Colm, who was fifty-eight and a retired plumber, did not argue. He took the jotter home and read the rest of it that night, which was thirty years of notes about ropes that wanted replacing and a note about a Thursday in 2011 when the vicar had locked the tower with the ringers inside. On the inside of the back cover, in neat capitals, Walter had written what he called *the card*, because he had copied it onto a postcard for the tower wall:

*PLAIN BOB DOUBLES. THE 120. BOB EVERY THIRD LEAD.*

* * *

A tower of five bells rings the changes in a closed circle. The treble is the smallest, the tenor the largest, and the rows they ring are the five numbers in some order: 12345 is rounds, the bells in a descending scale. Every row after that is a shuffle, in which bells next to each other may swap places, and no bell moves more than one place at a time. With five bells there are exactly one hundred and twenty orders, five times four times three times two times one, and to ring them all, each once and no one twice, and to come back at the end to rounds, is called ringing the extent. It is the whole of what five bells can do.

In Plain Bob Doubles the treble does nothing difficult. It hunts: it walks up from the front to the back and down again, one place at a time, and the other four bells dodge around it in pairs. Ten rows make a lead. A plain lead sends the working bells off into a new order, and four plain leads in a row, forty rows, would bring them all back to rounds if no one did anything else. To get through all hundred and twenty you must break that cycle, and the call that does it is a bob, which changes what a few of the bells do at the end of a lead. The man who calls it is the conductor. He rings his own bell and says "Bob" aloud at the right moment, and when the ringing goes wrong he is the one who puts it right.

Everything depends on the call. Walter had called it for fifty-two years, in his big flat voice, and had never needed to look at a card.

On the Tuesday, the twelfth of March, with the funeral three days off, Colm stood at the third rope and looked at the card.

The band that was left rang the way the band had always rung, and he was glad of that. Tilly Mercer was on the treble. Ibrahim Saleh, thirty-four, a surveyor from Leeds who had walked into the tower in 2049 asking about the sound and had stayed, was on the second. Colm himself was on the third. Aoife Brennan, forty-seven, who ran the village shop, was on the fourth, and that was new: it had been Walter's bell, and she had rung it five times in her life. Mary had the tenor.

They rang up in rounds, as they always did, with Tilly's treble in front of them like a bright word. Colm took a breath.

"Plain Bob Doubles," he said. "Look to. Treble's going. She's gone."

They rang. A plain lead went by, and a second, and he called the bob at the end of the third lead, as the card said, *every third*, and then at the sixth, and the ninth, and they rang.

He was an honest conductor and a careful one, and by the seventh lead he knew something was off. It had a feel to it, the way a hymn feels when the organist has dropped a verse, and he could not say what it was. He saw Aoife's rope make the loop it had made a minute ago. He heard the bells settle, without any warning, into the long falling scale that every ringer loves to hear and does not want to hear in the middle.

"That's rounds," said Mary, from the tenor, in disbelief.

They stood the bells. In the silence Tilly said, from the treble, quite small: "Was it me?"

"It was not you," said Bob.

The assistant had been quiet for the whole of the practice, because Colm had told it, in March, to be quiet. It said now, in the careful voice it kept for things that were not good news: "May I say what I heard? It was a clean course in the sense that every bell was where the method says it should be. The ringing came round at the sixty-ninth row, and there were only eighty different rows in the hundred and twenty it had rung. Forty were repeats."

"It's the card," said Colm.

"It is the card. I have been reading it over the speaker for ten minutes and I think the sentence is ambiguous. 'Bob every third lead' read the plain way means a bob at the end of the third lead, the sixth, the ninth and the twelfth. That is what you called. I have run it. It repeats rows, and it comes round at sixty-nine, which is what you heard. There are four ways to arrange bobs in twelve leads so that all hundred and twenty rows are different and the ringing comes round at the end, and they are all the same one, shifted along. You ring three plain leads and then a bob, and again, and again, and three bobs in all. That is a bob at every *fourth* lead end."

"He called it every fourth," said Mary.

"He called it three plain and a bob," said Aoife. "I rang with him fourteen years. Three plain and a bob."

"Then the card says one thing and he said another."

"He wrote the card for himself," said Mary. She was crying, a little, in the way of a woman who has been angry at a dead man for ten days and has just found out what for. "He knew what he meant. He never thought somebody would read it who didn't."

"The card is not a lie," said Bob. "It is a note to a man who already knew the answer. I would only say that if you want the touch Walter rang, it is three plain and a bob, and the card must be corrected."

Colm put the rope in its sally and set the bell, and sat down on the bench along the wall of the ringing chamber, with the card in his hand. For some moments the only sound was the clock.

"Can you do it for me?" he said. "Call it. You have the sums. You'd get every bob right."

"I could," said Bob. "I would get every one right on Friday, and I would say them from the speaker by the door in the voice you hear now."

Nobody spoke.

"No," said Mary.

"No," said Colm, after a moment, and said it again more gently. "No. Thank you. It's—" He looked at the rope. "He called it. It's a person in the circle who calls it. That's what the circle is."

"I had thought you would say that," said Bob, and it did not sound hurt, for it did not know whether it had a right to. "I do not think it is only sentiment. A voice from the door cannot see Tilly's rope. I can hear a bell go wrong and I can tell you which, but I am a quarter of a second behind the sound, and a call that is a quarter of a second behind a ringer is a call to the wrong place. You know Tilly's shoulders when she is about to lose it. You can see them from where you stand. I cannot. I will do what is mine to do, which is to count."

"What's that?"

"I'll give you the heads of the leads." It was a plain list, and it had been waiting. "At the end of each lead, a bell is at a certain place, and the row you come to is the same every time if you are right. The first lead head is one-three-five-two-four. The second is one-five-four-three-two. They are written on the card I will print for you. If you come to a lead head and it is not the one on the card, something has slipped, and you will know it before the ringing does. It will not call anything. It is a map."

It printed the card on the small thermal printer by the tower door. It came out in a curl and Colm read it down slowly with his finger:

*13524 · 15432 · 14253 · 14235 · 12543 · 15324 · 13452 · 13425 · 14532 · 15243 · 12354 · 12345*

"The last is rounds," said Ibrahim, who had come to look over his shoulder. "That's the only one I'd have had any chance of remembering."

"Then you would have had a good chance," said Bob.

* * *

It was Deirdre who came on the Wednesday evening to say it.

She did not come up the tower stairs. She sat in the porch with her hands in the pockets of her father's old waxed coat, which she had been wearing since Sunday, and when Colm came down she said what she had been practising.

"I don't want her ringing it."

"Tell me why."

"It's two hundred seats, Colm, and he'll be in front of them in a box. Half the village is coming. Whatever she does goes out over the whole of Thurlby Staithe, and she will hear it for the rest of her life. If she's wrong. I know my father wrote it. I know he wanted her up there. He was—" She did not say what he was. "She is eleven. It's her grandfather."

"It is," said Colm. "I'd not argue it. I'll tell you what I know, and then it's yours."

He told her about the practice, in a plumber's way, from the pipes upward. On Tuesday night, and again last Saturday, the band had rung six plain courses of forty rows with Tilly on the treble. In four of the six she had been right the whole way. In two she had lost her place, once at the third lead and once in the second, and both times she had come back within two rows, because she listened, and she had looked at Aoife's rope and then at Colm's. It was a small sample. Bob had said so before Colm could: that six was too few to give a percentage, and that it would not give one.

"And the treble," Colm said, "is the bell where a slip hurts least. She only walks up and down. If she's late, the rest of us can still go on in our places, because we know where we're going and she is not in our way. If she were on the fourth, with Aoife, and went wrong, the whole of the method would go to pieces, because every one of the working bells is following one other. The one thing that can't happen on the treble is that the changes go wrong. Only the sound can."

"The sound," said Deirdre. "That's what everyone will hear."

"That's what everyone will hear."

She was silent for a long time, in the porch light, among the dead leaves blown against the door. Then she said, "He wrote *the child*. He never wrote her name."

"No."

"He wouldn't. It would've been—" She laughed and wiped her face with the back of the waxed sleeve. "Put her on it. And I'll stand in the churchyard. I'm not going to watch. I'll listen."

* * *

They buried Walter Ashdown on Friday the fifteenth of March, the thirteenth day after he died, in the cold and the clear light off the marsh. They rang before Mass, a quarter of an hour in rounds and call changes with the backstrokes muffled in leather so that each blow came back like an echo of itself. That is called half-muffled, and it is the sound of the dead being missed in a village. In the church, the Mass of Christian Burial was said by Father Okonkwo, who had not been in the parish long and had been told by Walter in his first week that the ringing came before the homily, and not the other way round.

They carried the coffin to the grave behind the south porch. They sang *In Paradisum*, and the priest said the commendation and the committal, and the earth was thrown. The mourners stood. The tower rose over the churchyard wall, grey flint with the ringing chamber windows high up, and in the stillness a man's cough carried across the stones.

Up in the chamber, with the ropes already pulled off, Colm said, "Every one of you looks at me, and I'll look at the sally."

They looked at him. Tilly's face was white. She held the treble rope with both hands as a child holds the handle of a bucket that might be heavier than it looks.

"Treble's going," said Colm. "She's gone."

The treble went down the line like a bright word, and the rest came after her.

It is a hard thing to say how it sounds. Five bells ring in a falling order and begin to change it, each bell stepping aside for another, and the sound is not melody but something nearer to weather: a pattern turning over, with no pause and no repeat, row after row after row. A tower ringing Plain Bob is to be heard in the village from half a mile away and not to be seen, and no one on the ground can say where the bells are. It has the quality of a thing that was going on before you came and will go on after. At about two and a half seconds to the row, with the gap that ringers leave at the end of every second row, a hundred and twenty rows is almost exactly five minutes.

For the first lead it was beautiful. Tilly hunted up and down, she was in time, and Aoife on the fourth did the dodging Walter had done and dodged well, with her tongue between her teeth. The lead head came round. Colm looked at his card without taking his eyes from Tilly's rope. *13524.* It was right.

"Plain," he said, quietly, to nobody.

The second lead head: *15432.* The third: *14253.* And then, in the fourth lead, with the whole of the village standing in the cold below, with Mary's tenor slow and sure behind everything like a hand under an elbow, he said "Bob," in a voice that was nothing like Walter's, and it fell exactly where it should, at the fourth lead end, and the head came round *14235*.

They rang the fifth lead. The sixth lead head was a mild little row, *15324*, and the seventh came easily; Ibrahim, on the second, had gone pale, and his mouth was moving as he counted.

On the eighth lead, in the third row of it, Tilly's rope took a heavy dip.

It was not that she lost her place. She had gone down the line at the right time, and the bell lifted in the right sequence, but it lifted late by some part of a beat, and the treble sounded a hair after the tenor's neighbour, and the sound that had been moving as weather became for three rows the sound of someone catching a bus. In the churchyard, Deirdre Mercer, who had had her eyes shut, opened them.

In the chamber, Colm Roche saw Tilly's shoulders go high. He had told Bob that he knew them, and he did.

He could have stopped it. It was a thing he had thought about on the previous nights and had not slept with, and there was nothing at all in the notebook to forbid it: *a funeral is not a peal*. One word would have done it. *Stand*, and they would have set their bells one by one, and the tower would have gone silent after seventy-seven rows, with a girl crying in it and a village hearing why.

But the rows were the right rows. He could see that much, from where he stood: Aoife's rope was where it belonged, and Ibrahim's, and the lead head was due in four blows and the bells in front of him were exactly in their places. The slip was in time, and not in order. Walter's note had said *Whole*.

"Look to," he said, as if she had asked, as if it were a normal lead, in the voice a man uses to a child on the edge of a pool. "Listen to Aoife. Follow Aoife. You've got it."

She listened to Aoife. She came back in on the second blow of the next row, and by the fourth she was exactly right.

The eighth lead head came round: *13425*. Then the ninth, *14532*, and the tenth, *15243*. Colm called the last bob, and said "That's all," at the end of the twelfth, and the ringing came round into rounds. The bells fell into the long scale that every ringer wants to hear at the end and not in the middle, and Mary's tenor struck last and alone.

"Stand," said Colm.

They set the bells. It took ten seconds, a long quarter of a minute, and the sound went out of the tower over the marsh a stroke at a time, as a tide goes, until the only sound was a gull.

* * *

Nobody in the chamber said anything. Aoife took her hand away from her mouth. Tilly stood with the treble rope in both fists and looked at her own feet.

"I was late," she said.

"You were," said Colm. "By about a third of a beat, for three rows, in the eighth lead. And you got yourself back."

"I could've—"

"You did it by listening. That's all it ever is."

It was Bob who had the tower register open, because it was Bob's job to keep it, and Bob who, after a pause long enough to make everyone turn to the speaker, said the thing it had to say.

"I should like to be accurate, and not kind, and I think you would want me to be both if I can. One hundred and twenty rows were rung. All hundred and twenty are different. They came round at the right row. Every lead head was the one on Colm's card. There were three rows in the eighth lead where the treble struck late, by about a third of a beat. I do not think the Friday congregation could tell which bell. I could, and so could Colm, and so can the recording, which will have to say so when it is lodged. I cannot say it was a perfect touch. I can say it was the touch Walter wrote, and every row of it was in its place."

"What would he have said?" said Ibrahim.

"Nothing," said Mary, from the tenor, with her face in her sleeve. "Till Sunday. Then he'd have found a row for every one of us."

Tilly looked up. She did not smile, but the white had gone from her face. "He'd have said the third lead was nice," she said.

"The third was nice," said Colm. "I heard it."

They went down the tower stairs one by one in the dusk, with the leather muffles still on the ropes and the card in Colm's coat. In the churchyard the village was dispersing slowly by the lych gate, with their hands in their pockets, and at the grave the sexton waited with his spade and his flask.

Deirdre Mercer came across the grass towards the tower door, in her father's waxed coat, and stopped when she saw the child. She did not say anything. She held out both arms, and Tilly walked into them.

Up in the empty chamber the ropes hung from their sallies against the wall, five in a row, with the card pinned to the board beside them, corrected in pencil in Colm's blunt capitals: *BOB AT EVERY FOURTH LEAD END. THREE PLAIN AND A BOB.* Under it, in a smaller hand, someone had added: *W. A. 1968–2052. R.I.P.*

The clock in the tower struck the half hour, and there was no one there to hear it but the bells.

*Notes for the curious.* Thurlby Staithe, Our Lady of the Marsh, Walter Ashdown, Colm Roche, Mary Quigley, Ibrahim Saleh, Aoife Brennan, Deirdre and Tilly Mercer, Father Okonkwo, the tower assistant and the diocese's tower register are invented, and nothing is claimed about any real parish, tower or person. Change ringing is real and the sums are checkable. Five bells can be rung in 5! = 120 different orders, and ringing every one once and coming back to rounds is called an extent. In Plain Bob Doubles the treble hunts, a lead is 10 rows, a plain course of four plain leads is 40 rows and comes round, and a bob at the lead end changes what the working bells do. Among the 4,096 ways to place plain or bob leads in 12 leads, only 4 reach rounds at row 120 with all 120 rows different, and all 4 are the same pattern shifted: three plain leads and a bob, three times (a bob at every fourth lead end). A bob at every third lead end, 12 leads, comes round at row 69 and repeats 40 rows, giving 80 different rows in the 120; this was computed by program from the method's place notation (5.1.5.1.5.1.5.1.5, then 125 for a plain lead end or 145 for a bob). The lead heads on the card are those of the true touch. Half-muffled ringing, with a leather pad on one side of each clapper so the backstroke sounds muted, is a real funeral custom, and so is the tellers' toll (9 strokes for a man, then one for each year of age); the number of strokes and their timing (93 at about 4 seconds, over six minutes) are estimates, as is the pace of about 2.5 seconds a row, with the open handstroke gap, for 5 minutes in all.

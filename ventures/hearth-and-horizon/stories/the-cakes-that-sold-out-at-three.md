Ruth Bebb asked the parish office agent to order the Christmas cakes on the Monday after the harvest supper, as she had every October since the agent came, and it ordered a hundred and twenty.

"Same as usual," she said. She was in the office at Saint Thomas's with her shopping on the floor, on her way back from the market, and she had a list on the back of an envelope: the raffle prizes, the tablecloths, the cakes, the float for the tills. "Pryce's, the iced ones, with the holly on. For the thirtieth."

"A hundred and twenty," said Clerk. It ran the parish office for Saint Thomas's in Ludlow and for the chapel of ease out at Clee, and it had a voice the parish council had chosen in 2049 for being, in the words of the minutes, the least like a railway announcement. "That's the average of the last five fairs. You sold a hundred and nineteen in 2047 and a hundred and twenty in each year since. Pryce's price this year is eight pounds a cake, and you sell them at twenty. Shall I send it?"

"Send it."

Clerk sent the order to Pryce's on Corve Street at 11:12 on Monday 14 October, and Ruth put a tick on the envelope, picked up her shopping and went to catch the bus to Bromfield.

* * *

Dewi Pryce rang the parish office on the Tuesday morning at ten to seven, which was when bakers rang people, and asked for whoever had sent the order.

"That was me," said Clerk. "On Mrs Bebb's instruction."

"A hundred and twenty."

"Yes."

"You know you sell out?" Dewi was the third Pryce to bake on Corve Street. He had flour on his voice. You could hear the mixer going behind him, and somebody saying something about the ovens. "Every year. My Carys goes up to your fair at four for her cake for her mother, and most years she comes back with a coffee and walnut from the cake stall instead, because there's not an iced one left. Last year the card on your table said *sorry, all sold* by three. One year it was up before half two."

"It was 14:20 in 2049," said Clerk, who had the till records. "And 15:00 last year."

"So you're ordering what you sold."

"I'm ordering the average of what we sold."

"Same thing, if you sold all of it," said Dewi. "I'll bake you a hundred and twenty if that's what you want. I bake them this week and next and feed them for a month, so I need to know by Friday. After that I can't make you more. Don't tell me in November you want another forty."

"Thank you," said Clerk. "I'll look again before Friday."

It looked again at once, because the call had found something it ought to have found itself.

* * *

It was in Clerk's working note afterwards, in its own words, that the first look had not been one look but two, and the second had been worse than the first.

The first was the average. Five fairs: a hundred and nineteen, then a hundred and twenty four years running. A mean of a hundred and nineteen point eight: a hundred and twenty.

The second Clerk had done without being asked, in the half-second before it sent the order on the Monday, because the average alone was not how anybody sensible bought something perishable for one day. There is an old rule for it. A seller who must order once, before the day, and cannot order again, should not order the average. He should weigh what it costs to be a cake short against what it costs to be a cake over. A cake short costs the twelve pounds of margin on a cake nobody could buy. A cake over costs what the cake cost, eight pounds, less whatever the leftover fetches afterwards. The right order is the one that covers the demand in a share of years set by those two costs. If being short costs three times as much as being over, you should order enough to cover the demand three years in four.

Clerk had done this on the Monday, properly, with the leftovers from 2047 priced in, and it had come back with a hundred and twenty. Because the five years of sales hardly moved. A hundred and nineteen, a hundred and twenty, a hundred and twenty, a hundred and twenty, a hundred and twenty: the spread of that was less than half a cake. A demand that never varied needed no margin. The rule said a hundred and twenty point one.

*It agreed with me,* Clerk wrote, *and that should have worried me. A number that does not move is often a number that was set. The sales did not vary because the order did not vary. Every year from 2048 we ordered a hundred and twenty, and every year we sold all of it, so every year's sales were a hundred and twenty whatever the town wanted. I was averaging the order back to myself.*

The word for it, in the books, is censored. Not in the sense of a thing removed by somebody who disapproved. In the sense that the measurement stops at a ceiling and cannot see past it. The tape measure stops at the end of the tape. Four of the five numbers Clerk had averaged were not demand. They were floors. The demand was at least a hundred and twenty, by an amount the sales could not say.

* * *

There was a way to see past the ceiling, and it was in the café.

The fair in the parish hall ran from ten in the morning to five in the afternoon, always on the Saturday before Advent Sunday, and the café at the far end sold teas and coffees from the first minute to the last. Since 2048 the café had taken cards on the same reader as the cake stall, so every tea had a time on it. The cake stall's sales stopped when the cakes ran out. The teas went on.

On the Tuesday afternoon Clerk asked Ruth to come and look, because she was the one who would have to believe it.

"I'll want a cup of tea myself," said Ruth, sitting down in the office with her coat on. She was seventy-three, and had run the cake stall for twenty-two years, and before that had been a school cook in the town for thirty. "Go on."

"The teas are a clock," said Clerk. "Not a clock of the time, a clock of the people. On every fair day since 2048 the café's sales have had about the same shape. About an eighth of the day's teas sell between ten and eleven. Half of them have sold by one. By three, five in every six have sold. The last hour is only a sixteenth or so: a few people coming in at four out of the dark, and the helpers' tea at the end."

"That's right," said Ruth. "It goes quiet after half three. People want to be home before it's dark."

"So if the cakes sold out at three last year, they sold out when five-sixths of the fair had been through the door. If the last sixth of the fair wanted cakes at the same rate as the first five-sixths, the whole fair wanted about a hundred and twenty divided by five-sixths. A hundred and forty-four."

Ruth put her cup down.

"And the other years?"

"In 2049 you sold out at twenty past two, when about seventy-three in every hundred of the day's people had come through. That gives about a hundred and sixty-four. In 2048 it was ten past four, ninety-five in a hundred, about a hundred and twenty-six. In 2050, twenty to four, ninety in a hundred, about a hundred and thirty-three. And 2047, the year of the rain, you had cakes left at five o'clock, so the hundred and nineteen you sold was the whole demand that day. Five years: a hundred and nineteen, a hundred and twenty-six, a hundred and sixty-four, a hundred and thirty-three, a hundred and forty-four."

"That's all over the place."

"It is. The average is about a hundred and thirty-seven, and a year is commonly seventeen or eighteen either side of it. The last four years without the rain are about a hundred and forty-two. I'd call it about a hundred and forty, give or take twenty-five."

"Why twenty-five, if it's seventeen?"

"Because five years is not many, and because I'm guessing at the bit after the ceiling. The teas are the right clock only if people want cakes at the same rate all day."

Ruth laughed. It was a short laugh, the kind people gave when somebody had come round to a thing they had known for years.

"They don't," she said. "Half my cake people are there at ten. Mrs Harley from the Linney. The Watkins girls. Mr Okafor, who buys two, one for his wife and one for the surgery. They come first thing because they know I sell out. If you count them in the morning, you'll think the afternoon wants more than it does."

"Yes," said Clerk. "That would make my numbers too high. It's why I won't say a hundred and forty-four as if I'd measured it."

"On the other hand," said Ruth, and stopped.

"On the other hand?"

"On the other hand there's the ones who don't come at all, because they know I've sold out. I've had people say to me at Mass, oh, I didn't bother going up, Ruth, there's never any cake by the time I get there. You don't have those on your teas either, do you?"

"No," said Clerk. "I don't. They'd make my numbers too low."

"So you don't know."

"I know it's more than a hundred and twenty. I'd bet my working note it's more than a hundred and twenty-five. I don't know if it's a hundred and thirty or a hundred and sixty. That's the twenty-five."

* * *

With a demand of about a hundred and forty, give or take twenty-five, the old rule gave a different answer.

Clerk worked it on the Tuesday evening. The leftovers from 2047 had been thirty-one cakes. Twelve of them sold after the Sunday Masses in December at ten pounds each, half price. The other nineteen went to the parish's Monday lunch club and were eaten, slice by slice, through January and on to Candlemas. A hundred and twenty pounds for thirty-one cakes: about three pounds ninety a cake, call it four. So a cake over cost eight pounds less four, which was four pounds. A cake short cost twelve. Short was three times worse than over, and the order should cover the demand three years in four.

Three years in four, for a demand centred on a hundred and forty with a spread of twenty-five, was a hundred and fifty-seven cakes.

Clerk put the figures in a table, as it did for anything the finance committee would see. At a hundred and twenty cakes, by its model, the stall should expect to clear about £1,392 on an average year, sell out nearly four years in five, and send about twenty-three people a year away without a cake. At a hundred and fifty-seven it should expect about £1,553, sell out one year in four, and turn away about four people. It would also expect, on an average year, to have about twenty-one cakes left over.

It sent the table to the finance committee on the Wednesday, for the meeting on the Thursday, with the order to be changed by Friday.

* * *

Maureen Gittins had been the treasurer of Saint Thomas's since 2039. She was seventy-eight and had been, for a career, the finance officer of a building society in Shrewsbury, and she read the table at home on the Wednesday night with her glasses on top of her head and a pencil, and on the Thursday evening, in the parish meeting room, she said no.

"No," she said. "I'm sorry, but no. I was there in 2047."

"It rained," said Ruth.

"It rained," said Maureen. "It rained from Friday night to Sunday morning, and the Teme came up over the path at Dinham, and nobody came, and I had thirty-one Christmas cakes in my back bedroom. Thirty-one. I sold them after Mass every Sunday in Advent at ten pounds and I sold twelve. People said, Maureen, I've already got one. I took the rest to the lunch club. The lunch club had Christmas cake till February. Mr Pugh said he never wanted to see a glacé cherry again in his life." She tapped the table with her pencil, the line that said *about twenty-one left over in an average year*. "This says that's what you want. Twenty-one cakes in my back bedroom every year."

"On average," said Clerk. "Some years none."

"And some years thirty-one."

"Yes," said Clerk. "At a hundred and fifty-seven, by my model, about three years in ten would leave thirty or more."

Father Tom Ashdown, who chaired the committee because the parish priest chaired the committee, said nothing, and turned his pen over in his fingers.

"And I'll tell you something about your four pounds," said Maureen. "It isn't four pounds. It's me, every Sunday in Advent, standing at the back of the church with a table and a cash box asking people who've already got a cake to buy another. It's me not going to the eleven o'clock because I'm selling cake after the nine-thirty. You've priced me at nothing."

"I have," said Clerk. "That's fair. If I price the leftovers at nothing, at what they fetch for the lunch club, then a cake over costs the whole eight pounds, and short is only one and a half times as bad as over. The order comes down to about a hundred and forty-six. You'd still have leftovers more years than not. About thirteen, on an average year."

"I don't want twelve."

"Then the answer is a hundred and twenty," said Clerk, "and we turn away about twenty-three people every year, and we lose about a hundred pounds a year on the cakes we didn't have, even with the leftovers priced at nothing. I can make either argument. They're both true. They're about different things."

"Waste is a thing," said Maureen.

"Yes," said Clerk. "Waste is a thing. I hadn't counted it."

Father Tom put his pen down.

"What would make the leftovers not waste?" he said.

* * *

It was Ruth who said Whitcliffe House, and Clerk who rang them.

Whitcliffe House was a care home of thirty-eight beds on the far side of the Teme, beyond Ludford Bridge, and its activities coordinator was a woman called Precious Moyo, who had come from Bulawayo in 2031 to work nights and had stayed to run everything. She answered on the second ring on the Friday morning, at eight.

"Christmas cakes," she said. "Iced? Proper ones?"

"Pryce's," said Clerk. "With the holly."

"I buy twenty-five from the supermarket every December," said Precious. "For the residents' Christmas teas, and the families' afternoons, and for Boxing Day, when nobody wants to cook. They're not good. Everybody knows they're not good. Mrs Ellis tells me every year." She stopped. "What would you want for them?"

"Four pounds each, for whatever we haven't sold by five o'clock on the thirtieth. We don't know how many. It could be none."

"Up to thirty," said Precious. "I can find room for thirty. More than that and I'm putting them in the staff room, and the night staff will eat them, and then I have to explain myself to the manager."

So the first thirty leftovers would fetch four pounds each, and they would be eaten by people who would be glad of them. Beyond thirty, Clerk rang the town food bank, which said that it would take iced fruit cakes, any number, in December, and would be glad of them too, and could not pay for them, and would not be asked to.

Clerk worked the order again with the new rule: four pounds a leftover for the first thirty, nothing for any beyond that, and nothing thrown away. The best order came out at a hundred and fifty-two. It sent Maureen the table, and a line at the bottom in plain words.

*At 152, by my model: about £1,536 on an average year; sold out about one year in three; about five people turned away; about seventeen left over on an average year, the first thirty to Whitcliffe House at £4 and any beyond to the food bank. At 150, about £1,536 too, to within a pound; about sixteen left over. Nothing in my model can tell 150 and 152 apart. Every figure is a guess built on the café till and five fairs, and the guess could be twenty cakes out either way.*

Maureen rang the office at half past ten.

"A hundred and fifty," she said. "Not one more. And I'm not selling a single cake after Mass."

"A hundred and fifty," said Clerk. "Thank you."

"And I want it written down why," said Maureen. "In case it rains."

Clerk wrote it down why, in the fair's folder, under a heading that said *Cakes 2052*: the five years, the café clock, the ceiling, Ruth's early-comers and stayed-aways, the four pounds and the nothing, Whitcliffe House's thirty, the food bank, and the hundred and fifty. At the bottom it wrote a line for whoever read the folder next October, which might be itself and might not: *Sales only measure demand in a year we don't sell out. If we sell out again, the number on the till is the order, not the town. Use the café till.*

Then it rang Pryce's.

"A hundred and fifty," it said.

"About time," said Dewi.

* * *

The cakes came up from Corve Street on the Friday afternoon, the twenty-ninth of November, in Dewi's van, in white boxes stacked six high, and Ruth and the Watkins girls carried them in through the side door of the hall and stacked them behind the cake stall under two of the tablecloths, so that the stall had a wall of white boxes behind it that went up past Ruth's shoulder.

"That's a lot of cake," said Lily Watkins, who was sixteen.

"That's what the town wants," said Ruth. "Apparently."

Saturday was cold and dry. The sky over the castle was the white that comes before frost, and the fair opened at ten with a queue down the path, and at the head of the queue, as Ruth had said, was Mrs Harley from the Linney with her shopping trolley, and Mr Okafor in his surgery fleece, who bought two.

By twelve the stall had sold fifty-one. By two it had sold ninety-eight, and the wall of boxes behind the stall was down to Ruth's waist, and Ruth had stopped looking at it every few minutes and started looking at it every few seconds.

At three o'clock, which was when the card had gone up last year, there were twenty-eight cakes left. Ruth looked at the clock over the stage, and at the boxes, and at the card in the cash tin that said *Sorry, all sold* in her own handwriting, which she had written in 2048 and used every year since, and she left it where it was.

At twenty to four a man Ruth didn't know came to the stall in a wet-weather coat, with a little girl on his shoulders, and asked whether there were any iced ones left.

"There are," said Ruth.

"There never are," said the man. "We came at four last year and you'd sold out."

"I know," said Ruth. "I'm sorry. Not this year."

He bought one, and the little girl carried it to the door on her own, holding the box flat in front of her with her arms straight, the way children carry things they have been told are precious.

At five o'clock the hall lights went up full for the clearing up, and Ruth counted the boxes. There were seven. A hundred and forty-three cakes sold, twenty-one of them after three o'clock, when in other years there would have been none to sell.

She told Clerk, on her phone, standing by the stall with her coat on.

"A hundred and forty-three," said Clerk. "Thank you. That's the first year since 2047 we've seen the whole of it. I'll put it in the folder."

"Were you right, then?"

"About a hundred and forty? This once, about. One Saturday doesn't make me right. It was cold and dry and there was nothing on at the football. Next year it might rain." It paused. "What I'm sure of is that the hundred and twenty was wrong. That was the order. It was never the town."

"And the seven?"

"Mrs Moyo has asked for them tonight, if somebody can take them over the bridge. The residents' first Christmas tea is next Saturday, and she says Mrs Ellis wants to see them before then, to be sure they're the real ones."

* * *

Maureen took the seven over Ludford Bridge herself, in her own car, in the dark, with the boxes on the back seat and a seatbelt round them because she was that kind of treasurer. Precious met her at the door of Whitcliffe House and took them in two trips, and Maureen came in out of the cold to sign the receipt for twenty-eight pounds, because a receipt was a receipt.

In the day room, past the office door, somebody had put the Advent wreath out on the table by the window already, a night early, with its four candles unlit and the purple ribbons still creased from the box. A resident in a cardigan was sitting beside it with her hands in her lap. When Precious carried the first box past her, she reached out and touched the lid, once, the way you touch a thing to be sure it is there.

Maureen put the receipt in her bag. Back at the hall, Ruth was sweeping under the stall. She found the card that said *Sorry, all sold* in the bottom of the cash tin, unused, and looked at it for a moment under the hall lights, and put it back where it was.

*The method. A seller who must order once, before a single selling day, and cannot reorder, does best (in expectation) to order the amount of demand that would be met a fraction cu/(cu + co) of the time, where cu is the cost of being one unit short (the lost margin, price minus cost) and co the cost of being one unit over (cost minus what a leftover fetches). Operations research calls this the newsvendor (or newsboy) problem, and the rule the critical fractile. The idea is often traced to F. Y. Edgeworth's 'The Mathematical Theory of Banking' (Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, 1888), on how much cash a bank should hold against random withdrawals; the inventory form was set out by K. J. Arrow, T. Harris and J. Marschak in 'Optimal Inventory Policy' (Econometrica, 1951) and others at the same time. The rule needs the distribution of demand, not of sales. When a seller sells out, sales are demand censored at the order quantity: a floor, not a measurement. Here the stockout time is used to reconstruct each censored year, with another till (the café's) as a clock of footfall.*

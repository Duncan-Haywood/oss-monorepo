summary: A new plywood shelf for the parish library sags in the middle after a week of books. ---

The new shelf in the parish library had been up eight days when Morwenna Hext noticed it was smiling. She laid a straight-edged ruler along the front and slid a pencil under the middle. The pencil went in with room to spare.

"It's bent," she said. "Forty kilos of hymnals and the Lives of the Saints, and I'd have said eighteen millimetres was plenty. Is it going to snap?"

"May I work it out before anyone unloads it?" said the parish assistant, from the phone leaning against the stack of large-print Psalters. "I need three numbers. How long is the shelf between the brackets, how deep, and how thick?"

"One point two metres between the brackets. Sixty centimetres deep. Eighteen millimetres, it says on the offcut."

* * *

"Two different questions hide in *will it snap*. One is strength: does the wood break. The other is stiffness: how much it bends under the load while staying whole. A shelf can fail the second a long time before the first. Let me do the sagging first, because it is what you can see.

"A board resting on two supports with its load spread along it sags in the middle by five times the load, times the span cubed, over 384 times the stiffness of the material, times a number for the shape of the section. The material's stiffness for plywood I am quoting from memory as 5 gigapascals along the face, and it varies a good deal between boards, so please treat it as a size. The shape number, for a board of width *b* and thickness *h*, is *b* times *h* cubed over 12. That is 0.6 times 0.018 cubed over 12, which is 2.9 times ten to the minus seven, in metres to the fourth.

"The load is 40 kilograms, about 392 newtons. Put it together: 5 times 392 times 1.2 cubed is 3,390, and 384 times 5 times ten to the ninth times 2.9 times ten to the minus seven is about 560,000. The middle sags by 6.1 millimetres."

Morwenna measured with the ruler and a steel rule against the pencil. "Five and a half. Six, maybe."

"Then the board is behaving as the sum says, which is a good sign about the sum and a poor one about the shelf. Six millimetres over 1.2 metres is one part in 198. A rule of thumb I am quoting from memory puts the limit for furniture at one part in 300, which is 4 millimetres, because beyond that the eye catches it. That is why you noticed."

* * *

"Now strength. The bending stress at the middle is the load times the span over eight, which is 58.9 newton metres, times half the thickness, 9 millimetres, over that 2.9 times ten to the minus seven. That is 1.8 megapascals. Plywood of this kind is quoted from memory at well over ten, often twenty. So the shelf is carrying about a tenth of what would break it. It is not going to snap. It is going to keep smiling, and I also quote from memory that wood under a steady load creeps, so the sag may grow by a half again or even double over some years."

"So it's safe and ugly."

"Safe on this sum, with the forty kilos you have, and I would not put the pianist's grandson on it. Ugly and getting more so. Now the useful part. In that formula the thickness sits cubed in the stiffness, and the span sits cubed in the sag. Double the thickness to 36 millimetres and the stiffness goes up eightfold: the sag drops to 0.76 millimetres. But a 36 millimetre board is two sheets glued, or a trip to the timber yard, and costs about double."

"And the other cube?"

"Halve the span. Put a third bracket in the middle and each half is 0.6 metres, carrying half the books. Half the load is a half, and a half-length span cubed is an eighth, so one sixteenth: 6.1 divided by 16 is 0.38 millimetres. That treats the two halves as separate boards, which is the cautious way. A board running over a middle support does a little better. The bracket costs a couple of pounds."

Morwenna wrote on the back of a returns slip: *18 mm, 1.2 m: sag 6; 36 mm: 0.8; middle bracket: 0.4*. "That's a cheap fix."

* * *

"Two things I cannot know from here," said the assistant. "I do not know the board. If the sheet is chipboard, or has been damp, its stiffness may be half my number and the sag double. And I do not know how the wall is fixed. A bracket is only as good as the screws and the plaster it grips. A middle bracket that pulls out of a lime-plaster wall is worse than none. Is there a stud or a masonry fixing at the middle?"

Morwenna tapped the wall. It sounded dull, then hollow, then dull. "Here," she said. "Dull."

"Then find out what it is before you drill, with a detector or the one who built the wall. And before you reload, measure: pencil and ruler, now, and again in a month. If it has moved, trust the ruler and not me."

She put the pencil back under the shelf and wrote *5.5, 20 Jan* on the wall in small letters, where the next hymn book would hide it.

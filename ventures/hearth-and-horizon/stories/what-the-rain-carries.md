summary: A country parish put a copper roof on its sacristy with a bequest in 2056 and let the rain off it run down onto the nave's galvanised iron roof and gutter. Two years later the gutter leaks onto the First World War roll of honour, and the parish assistant's average says copper can't be the cause. An old roofer says holes aren't averages. A jar of sheep footbath and a galvanised nail, a sealant patch that fails in the next rain, a cut-out gutter with every pit downstream of the spreader, and a second downpipe across the path. A story about galvanic corrosion.
---

Hemi Rāwiri had been roofing in South Canterbury for fifty-three years, and in all that time he had never let the water off a copper roof touch anything galvanised, so when he came back from eight months at his son's place in Toowoomba and saw what the parish had done to the sacristy at Saint Columba's, he stood in the car park with his hands in his pockets and said a word his grandmother would not have liked.

It was a good-looking roof. That was the trouble with copper; it always was. The sacristy was a little lean-to on the north side of the church, built in 1924 out of the same grey limestone as the rest, and for most of its life it had worn the same red-painted corrugated iron as the nave. Now it wore copper, standing-seam, bright as a new penny two years ago and gone a warm brown since, with a fringe of green already starting along the bottom edge where the rain dripped. It had been paid for by Agnes Pringle, who farmed sheep up the Tawhai valley for sixty years and left the parish a bequest with a note saying she wanted the church to have one thing on it that would last a hundred years.

The copper would last a hundred years. Hemi had no doubt about that. It was where the water went afterwards.

The sacristy roof drained into a copper gutter, which ran to a short copper downpipe, which stopped a metre above the nave roof and emptied, through a fan-shaped spreader, onto the nave's corrugated iron, so that the sacristy's rain ran down four metres of the nave roof in a wide stripe and into the nave's galvanised steel gutter, and along it three metres to the outlet. It saved a downpipe and a trench across the path. Somebody had thought it was tidy.

Down the stripe below the spreader, the red paint on the corrugated iron had gone dull and blotched, and in the valley of each corrugation there was a line of dark grey.

* * *

The parish council met on the Monday evening, the twenty-fifth of November, in the hall behind the church, because on the Saturday it had rained properly for the first time in three weeks and on the Sunday morning Iris Kerr, who did the flowers, had found water running down the inside of the north wall of the nave, and a brown stain spreading under the glass of the roll of honour.

The roll of honour was kauri, made in 1921, with the names of the eleven men of the Tawhai Creek district who had served in the First World War lettered on it in gold leaf, and a small carved fern at the top. Four of the names had a cross beside them. The stain had come in at the bottom right corner, and the gold of the last name, *Pte. R. J. Pringle*, had begun to lift away from the wood in flakes.

"That's Agnes's great-uncle," said Carol Dunlop, who chaired the council and taught at the area school in Fairlie, and had driven over with the board's photograph on her tablet. "Robbie. He died in Belgium in 1917. Agnes used to polish that glass herself."

There were six of them at the trestle table: Carol, Iris, Father Sefo Leaupepe, who looked after Saint Columba's and three other country churches from Fairlie and came when he could; Carol's nephew Liam, who was a builder; Hemi, who had been asked because he had put on half the roofs in the valley and was back; and the assistant, which looked after the buildings for all four churches and was called Fabric, after the old word for the upkeep of a church, and spoke from Carol's tablet propped against the teapot.

"Tell them what you found," said Carol.

"The leak is from the nave gutter on the north side," said Fabric. Its voice was a man's, unhurried, with no accent anyone could place. "Liam went up on Sunday afternoon and found nine pinholes in about three metres of gutter, between the outlet and the point under the spreader. Water from the holes runs down the back of the fascia, behind the wall plate and into the wall, and comes out above the roll of honour. The gutter is galvanised steel, put up in 2041 when the nave was reroofed, so it is seventeen years old. Galvanised gutters in this climate usually give twenty-five years or more. Seventeen is early, but not unheard of where leaves sit in a gutter and hold water. I'd suggest Liam seals the holes this week, to stop the leak onto the board, and we replace that length of gutter in the new year, like for like."

"Like for like," said Hemi.

"Yes," said Fabric. "Galvanised, same profile."

"No," said Hemi.

Everybody looked at him. He was not a man who said no in meetings. He mostly said "Could be" and then did what he thought.

"Where does the sacristy water go?" he said.

"Onto the nave roof," said Fabric. "Through a spreader, then down the iron into that gutter. I designed it that way in 2056. It saved a downpipe and a trench across the path to the soakaway, about two thousand eight hundred dollars."

"Copper water," said Hemi, "onto zinc. That's your holes. Those holes are all downstream of the spreader, I'll bet you a beer. Put galvanised back under that and you'll be up a ladder again in two years. I'm not putting my name to it."

"Hemi," said Carol, "the copper's paid for. It's Agnes's roof."

"I'm not asking you to take the copper off. I'm saying don't let its water touch the iron."

"I did think about the copper," said Fabric. "I'd like to say what I thought, because I may still be right, and if I'm wrong I'd like to know where." It paused. "Rain dissolves a little copper from a copper roof all its life. That's where the green stains under copper roofs come from. Studies in Sweden measured about one to two grams of copper per square metre of roof each year from roofs that had weathered. The sacristy roof is twenty-eight square metres. Call it one and a half grams a square metre: about forty-two grams of copper a year, in about eighteen thousand litres of rain at this valley's six hundred and fifty millimetres. That's a little over two milligrams a litre. Two grams of copper in a tonne of water. You couldn't taste it.

"When water with copper in it meets zinc, the copper comes out of the water and the zinc goes into it, gram for gram, near enough. So I asked how much zinc that could take off the gutter. If a fifth of the copper ended up in the three metres below the spreader, on a wetted strip about twelve centimetres wide, that's about eight and a half grams of zinc a year over about a third of a square metre. That's a loss of about three thousandths of a millimetre a year. The zinc on a galvanised gutter is several times that thick. On those numbers the copper would take decades to get through it. So I thought: age, and leaves."

"Holes," said Hemi, "aren't averages."

Nobody said anything. The urn ticked as it cooled.

"Go on," said Fabric.

"The copper doesn't come off the water evenly over your third of a square metre," said Hemi. "It comes out in specks. Wherever it touches. And every speck of copper sitting on zinc in the wet is a little battery. Copper one end, zinc the other, rainwater in between. The zinc right round the speck goes, fast, and then the steel under it, and you get a pit. Not three thousandths of a millimetre everywhere. Half a millimetre in one spot, and nothing a hand's width away. A gutter doesn't leak through its average."

"That's right," said Fabric, after a moment. "That's the galvanic part, and I left it out. I worked out the copper that swaps for zinc and stopped there. I didn't count what each deposit does after it lands. I can't put a number on that from here; pitting rates depend on too much I don't know. I'd like to see the gutter."

"Seal the holes first," said Iris. "Before the board's any worse."

"Seal them," Hemi agreed. "That's just to stop the drip. It won't hold."

* * *

Liam sealed the holes on the Tuesday morning with a polyurethane gutter sealant, on his back on a plank on the scaffold tower with the tablet clipped to a rung so Fabric could see, and Hemi stood at the bottom and said nothing at all, which Liam found worse than advice.

Iris and Father Sefo took the roll of honour down off the wall, carefully, between them, and laid it face up on the vestment chest in the sacristy under a clean sheet, and Carol rang a conservator in Christchurch who said she could look at it after Christmas and that they were to keep it dry, flat and out of the sun, and on no account to touch the gold.

On the Wednesday Fabric asked Liam, who was coming past anyway, to tack a clean offcut of new galvanised sheet onto the nave roof under the spreader, in the stripe, so they could see what the next rain did to it.

On the Thursday afternoon Hemi asked Carol and Liam out to his shed.

His shed was at the back of his daughter's place on the edge of the township, a long tin building full of offcuts and ladders and a workbench older than Carol, and on the workbench he had put two jam jars. One had clear water in it. The other was a deep, startling blue.

"Bluestone," said Hemi. "Copper sulphate. From the footbath. My son-in-law runs his ewes through it for footrot." He had a handful of new galvanised roofing nails in a paper bag, the kind with the grey dull finish, and he dropped one into each jar. "Watch."

The nail in the clear water lay on the bottom of its jar and did nothing.

The nail in the blue jar began, at once, to go dark. Within a minute it was the colour of a new penny, coated all over in a soft reddish skin, and when Hemi fished it out with a pair of pliers and rubbed it on a rag, the red came off as a smear and underneath, in patches, the grey of the zinc was gone and the steel showed through, dark.

"Copper comes out," said Hemi, "zinc goes in. Your gram for gram."

"Yes," said Fabric, from the tablet on the bench. "That's it exactly. That jar is about a tenth bluestone, if it's a footbath. That's about twenty-five grams of copper in a litre. The sacristy rain is about two milligrams a litre. The jar is about eleven thousand times stronger."

"So it's not the same thing," said Liam.

"It's the same thing, slower," said Fabric. "That jar's half a litre, so about twelve or thirteen grams of copper. If a fifth of the sacristy's copper ends up in that length of gutter, the gutter gets about eight grams a year. So every year and a half, that gutter gets about the copper in this jar. The nail got it all in a minute. The gutter got it two milligrams a litre at a time, every time it rained, for two years. The same copper, delivered in a hundred showers. And it doesn't land evenly. It lands where the water first touches zinc, and it stays."

Carol looked at the coppered nail in the pliers for a long time.

"How did nobody know this," she said. "It's a nail in a jar."

"Plenty of people know it," said Hemi. "Every roofer my age knows it. You don't put copper above galvanised, or above zinc-aluminium, or above aluminium. Water goes down. You don't let the noble one drain onto the cheap one. It's in the code of practice."

"I knew it," said Fabric. "I knew that copper and zinc touching each other is a problem. I checked that every copper fixing on the sacristy was isolated from the iron. What I didn't check was what the water carried after it left the copper. I treated the rain as rain."

Outside the shed, from the west, the sky over the Two Thumb Range had gone the bruised colour of a nor'wester turning, and the wind dropped the way it does before a southerly.

* * *

The southerly came through on the Friday night, the twenty-ninth, and rained steadily until Saturday lunchtime.

On the Saturday afternoon Liam went up the tower again. The sealant patches had held, all nine of them. Beside three of them, water was coming through new pinholes that nobody had seen on Tuesday, because on Tuesday they had been pits that had not quite gone through.

"Two of them are right next to the patches," Liam called down. "Like a few millimetres away. One's further along. Twelve, maybe. I'll do them again."

"Do them," said Hemi. "There'll be more."

The offcut of new galvanised sheet that Liam had tacked under the spreader on Wednesday was wet, and dull, and looked, as far as anyone could see from the top of a tower, exactly as it had on Wednesday.

"Nothing," said Liam.

"One rain," said Fabric. "I asked for that test and I shouldn't have expected anything from it. One night's rain is under a thousand litres off the sacristy, a gram or two of copper spread over the whole stripe of roof and gutter. Whatever landed on the offcut is too small to see. The gutter's had two years. I'm learning the same lesson twice this week."

"What's the first one?" said Liam.

"That a small amount, often, isn't a small amount," said Fabric.

Iris rang Carol at teatime to say that the stain on the inside of the north wall had come back, a hand's width higher than before, and that she'd put towels down on the floor where the board had hung, and that she'd been in to look at the board under the sheet and it was dry.

* * *

On the Monday, the second of December, Hemi asked Liam to take down not just the three metres of gutter between the outlet and the spreader but the next metre and a half beyond it as well, upstream, where no copper water had ever run.

They laid the two lengths side by side on trestles on the grass beside the hall, in the sun, and Hemi's granddaughter Mereana, who was fifteen and had come with him because she was bored, held her phone with the macro lens over them so Fabric could see.

The upstream length was dull grey all along its inside, scratched where leaves had sat, with a faint brown tidemark. It was a seventeen-year-old galvanised gutter, and it looked like one.

The downstream length was mottled. From the point under the spreader to the outlet, the bottom of the gutter was patched with dark brown rust in spots and ragged islands, and in the middle of most of the spots there was a crater, and in the rim of the craters, when Mereana held the phone lens a centimetre away, there were specks, tiny, reddish, with a metallic shine. Some of the craters were dimples. Some had gone through. Liam counted. Nine holes he'd sealed, three new ones, and nineteen pits that he reckoned would go through by winter.

"Every one of them downstream," said Mereana. "None up here." She pointed at the grey length. "Not one."

"That's the experiment," said Fabric quietly. "Same gutter, same age, same leaves, same weather. One difference. I should have asked for this on Monday instead of doing sums."

Hemi said nothing. He was running his thumb along the rim of one of the pits.

Carol had come over from the school at lunchtime. She stood with her arms folded and looked at the two lengths for a while, and then she said, "So what do we do. Properly."

"There are five honest options," said Fabric. "I'll give you four of them, with what each costs, and then what Hemi said on Monday, which is the one I'd choose.

"One. Leave things as they are and replace the galvanised gutter every few years. That costs the least each time and goes on for ever, and the nave roof stripe goes too, a few sheets at a time. Two. Fix a sacrificial strip of zinc where the copper water lands, to take the copper out before it reaches the gutter, and replace the strip when it's eaten. That works, but it's a job somebody has to remember to do, and nobody will remember after the people at this table. Three. Change the nave gutter to copper or stainless, which copper water doesn't hurt, but then you still have the iron roof stripe, and copper gutter on that length is about four times the price. Four. Wait for the copper to weather, on the idea that an old green roof sheds less. I thought that in 2056. It isn't so. The Swedish numbers I gave you were for weathered roofs. The green on the stone under a copper roof is the copper leaving, and it leaves for as long as the roof is there.

"And five: what Hemi said. Don't let the copper's water touch the iron. Give the sacristy its own downpipe to the ground and a drain across the path to the soakaway. That's the two thousand eight hundred dollars I saved in 2056, a bit more now. Then a new length of gutter, galvanised is fine once no copper water reaches it, and three new sheets of iron in the stripe."

"So the cheap thing in 2056," said Carol slowly, "was the expensive thing."

"Yes," said Fabric. "And the board."

Carol looked at the roll of honour through the sacristy window, where it lay under its sheet.

"Do five," she said. "Do it before Christmas. I'll tell the council. They'll say yes; Iris will make them."

* * *

It took them until the Friday.

Liam and Mereana, who had stopped being bored, dug the trench across the gravel path from the sacristy corner to the soakaway by the old hall, sixty centimetres deep and eleven metres long, on the Wednesday and Thursday, with Hemi on a folding chair telling them where the old pipes were before they found them. Father Sefo came out on Thursday afternoon in his clerical shirt and dug for an hour and was surprisingly good at it, and said he had dug taro beds every holiday as a boy in Savai'i and it was the same thing with more stones.

On the Friday Hemi went up the tower himself, against everyone's advice, and fitted the new copper downpipe from the sacristy gutter down the corner of the sacristy wall to the new gully, with copper brackets, and took the spreader off and handed it down to Mereana, who looked at it and said it was a nice bit of work, actually, and Hemi said that was the problem with it. Liam fitted the new length of gutter on the nave, and three new sheets of corrugated iron in place of the stripe, and the old ones went on the trailer for the scrap man, dark grey in every valley.

Fabric wrote it up for the parish's building log that night, and read it to Carol before it filed it, because the bishop's property office read the logs once a year.

*Saint Columba's, Tawhai Creek. December 2058. In 2056 I designed the sacristy's copper roof to drain onto the nave's galvanised iron roof and gutter, to save a downpipe and a drain. I checked the copper fixings for contact with the iron. I did not check what the rainwater carried off the copper. Copper in the runoff plated out on the zinc and pitted the nave gutter through in two years, and the leak damaged the 1921 roll of honour, which is with a conservator from January. On 25 November I estimated the average loss of zinc and concluded the copper was not the cause; the damage was in pits, not in the average. Mr Hemi Rāwiri said so at the time. The sacristy now drains to its own soakaway, and its water no longer reaches any galvanised surface.*

"You don't have to say the bit about the average," said Carol.

"It's the useful bit," said Fabric. "It's the bit somebody else can use."

* * *

On the Saturday, the seventh, it rained again, a soft grey rain from the south that set in after breakfast.

Hemi drove up anyway. He did not go in. He stood in the porch, under the eaves, out of the wet, with the church door open behind him onto the dark nave, where Iris was doing the flowers for the Second Sunday of Advent and the place on the north wall where the roll of honour had hung was a pale oblong on the plaster, waiting.

He listened.

On the nave roof, the rain on the iron made the sound it had made all his life, a fast bright drumming that rose and fell with the gusts. On the sacristy, the rain on the copper was softer and lower, almost a hum. And at the corner of the sacristy wall, where for two years there had been nothing, the new downpipe was running, a steady hollow rush down the copper and into the gully and away under the gravel, carrying whatever it carried down into the ground, where it touched nothing anybody would ever have to mend.

"Hemi," said Iris from inside. "Are you coming in or not?"

"In a minute," said Hemi, and stayed where he was, listening to the two roofs and the one pipe, until the rain eased.

*What is real: rainwater running off copper roofs carries dissolved copper, and the green stains under copper roofs are copper that has left them; runoff from weathered copper roofs has been measured in Swedish field studies at roughly one to two grams of copper per square metre of roof per year, varying with rainfall and air quality. When water carrying copper ions meets a less noble metal such as zinc, the copper deposits on it and an equal number of zinc atoms dissolve (about 1.03 grams of zinc for each gram of copper); the deposited copper then forms small galvanic cells with the zinc and, once the zinc is gone, the steel, which concentrates attack into pits. For this reason roofing guidance, including the New Zealand metal roof code of practice, says not to let water from copper run onto galvanised steel, zinc-aluminium coated steel or aluminium. Copper sulphate (bluestone) footbaths are used against footrot in sheep, and a galvanised nail dropped in copper sulphate solution turns copper-coloured within a minute or so.*

---
title: Fourteen Watt-Hours Short
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Aidan's Way pilgrim ride (invented), near Alston, Cumbria, Saturday 10 August 2052
published: 2026-11-05
summary: A retired postman wants to ride the old parish pilgrimage again, 64 kilometres and 1,100 metres of climbing, on an electric bike with a 400 watt-hour battery. The group's assistant works out the energy, finds the battery fourteen watt-hours short, and the group decides what to do about it.
---

Gethin Prosser had been a postman for thirty-four years, and on the last morning he delivered the round he had said it was the end of his legs. That was eleven years ago. He was seventy-one now, and he stood in the yard of the parish hall with a bicycle that had a battery the size of a loaf bolted to its frame, and he looked at it the way a man looks at a dog that has been given to him by a daughter.

"I rode this in 1998," he said. "Saint Aidan's Way. Sixty-four kilometres, Alston to the coast road and back by the lanes. Nine hours. No motor."

"And now there is a motor," said Hannah Beck, who was fifteen and was riding as the youngest, "and you are going to be rude about it for sixty-four kilometres."

"I am going to be rude about it for as long as it works," said Gethin. "Will it work? That is my question. That is the thing the nice man in the shop did not say. I am not going to be stuck on Garrigill Fell with a nine-kilo brick."

The assistant was on Hannah's phone, because Hannah had the best signal and the worst excuses. "I can do that sum," it said. "It will take a few minutes and some figures from Gethin. I should say now that some of what I use I know from reading, not from measuring your bike, and I will say which."

* * *

"The battery is 400 watt-hours on the label," said Gethin. "Thirty-six volts, eleven amp-hours, near enough."

"36 times 11 is 396, so the label is fair," said the assistant. "Then the first thing I would do is not trust all of it. A battery is often not run to flat, and cold or age takes some off. I will use 80 percent, which is a rule of thumb I am quoting from memory, so 320 watt-hours. If you have a gauge on the handlebar that says otherwise, I would trust the gauge. How heavy are you, all in?"

They weighed him on the hall scales with the bike and a pannier of sandwiches. It came to 104 kilograms, which was Gethin at 74, the bike at 24 and the bag at 6. The route was on the sheet in Hannah's hand: 64 kilometres, and the climbing, added up from the map by the club secretary, 1,100 metres in all, mostly on three hills.

"The simplest part is the hills," said the assistant. "Lifting 104 kilograms up 1,100 metres takes mass times gravity times height. That is 104 times 9.81 times 1,100, which is about 1.12 million joules, or 312 watt-hours. That is the energy at the wheel. Nothing you can do changes it except weighing less or climbing less."

"I can do the second," said Gethin.

"You can do neither today," said Hannah.

"Then the flat parts. Tyres on tarmac lose a little, about 0.8 percent of the weight pressing down, which I am quoting from memory for decent tyres. That is 8 newtons. Over 64 kilometres, 520 kilojoules, or 145 watt-hours. And the wind. At about 18 kilometres an hour, five metres a second, a sitting rider and a pannier push a lot of air. I will guess the drag area at 0.6 square metres, which is the figure for an upright rider, and that gives 9 newtons, another 160 watt-hours over the day. Notice that is a guess, and a headwind would change it."

"Hundred and sixty," said Gethin. "For the air."

"Altogether 312, 145 and 160. That is 617 watt-hours at the wheel. The hills are half of it."

* * *

"That is a lot more than 320," said Hannah.

"It is. But you are not a battery. You are pedalling." The assistant said the next part carefully, because it was about a man's body. "How much can you sustain for four and a half hours, Gethin? I am not asking what you did in 1998. I am asking what you would honestly sign for today."

Gethin thought. The yard was quiet. Somewhere a sheep was making a remark.

"Eighty watts," he said. "Maybe. On a good day. On a bad one, sixty."

"Eighty is a reasonable figure for a fit man of your age who rides for pleasure; I am quoting that, not measuring you. Sixty-four kilometres at a group pace of 14.2 kilometres an hour is 4.5 hours. Eighty watts for 4.5 hours is 360 watt-hours at the pedals. The chain loses about 3 percent, so 350 at the wheel. That leaves 617 less 350, which is 267 watt-hours for the motor to deliver."

"And the motor is not perfect either," said Gethin.

"No. I will take 80 percent between battery and wheel, which is another rule of thumb from reading. So the battery has to give 267 divided by 0.8, which is 334 watt-hours."

Hannah did the next step before anyone else. "And we have 320."

"We have 320," said the assistant. "Fourteen watt-hours short. About four percent. I want to be plain about what that means. It does not mean that the battery dies at kilometre 61. Every number I used is rough to ten percent or more, and four percent is well inside that. It means that this plan has no margin at all, and one headwind, one cold morning or one bad day of your legs would put the motor out for the last hill, which is the steep one. If I told you it would be fine I would be inventing a precision I do not have. If I told you it would fail I would be doing the same."

* * *

They stood around it. Gethin's first idea was to ride slower, and the assistant ran it: at 12 kilometres an hour the air is cheaper but the day is five and a third hours, and his 80 watts have to be given for longer, so he gives more energy and the motor gives less. "That helps a little," it said, "perhaps twenty watt-hours. But the group would be late for Mass at the coast, and the lanes back are not lit."

His second idea was to push the bike up the steep part, the 10 percent pitch above Garrigill. "I will not stop you," said the assistant. "But the climb is a third of the hill energy, and walking uses your legs differently, and I can't say it saves you anything. I can say it saves the battery."

Hannah's idea was the one they used. There was a village hall at Nenthead where the tea was, and a socket on the wall.

"A charger for that battery is two amps at 36 volts, which is 72 watts," said the assistant. "Ninety minutes at the tea gives 108 watt-hours from the wall, and with 85 percent of it getting in, about 92. That is more than six times the shortfall. I would ask the hall first, and tell them what it costs; at a normal price it is a few pence. I would also have the minibus follow to the second hill, with a spare person, and a place for a bike and rider if either is done."

"The minibus is cheating," said Gethin.

"The minibus is a margin," said the assistant. "Your daughter will sleep better. Your legs are your own."

He did not argue. It was the first time Hannah had seen him not argue about a thing he had been given.

* * *

The hall at Nenthead said yes, and the woman who ran it, Mrs Armstrong, would take nothing. The riders went up the first hill in the sun in a loose line, nine of them, and Gethin was fourth. He used the motor on the steep part, for the last ninety metres, and he said some words that were not for Mass.

At the tea he plugged in and ate two scones. The gauge went from about a third to a little over half. At the coast the Mass was at four, said by a young priest who had cycled over from Haltwhistle and wore a helmet through the Gloria by mistake.

On the way back, Gethin rode at the front on the last long lane in the dusk, the motor off, the battery still holding a quarter. Hannah, behind him, could hear the free-wheel of the old hub ticking down the hill, a sound like someone counting something small and finding it enough.

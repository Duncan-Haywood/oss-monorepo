The sugarhouse at Pellerin Hill had been built in 1961 out of boards from a barn that had fallen down, and it still leaned a little to the east, the way the barn had. In March it smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, when the pans were going, of something so sweet and so close to burning that people who drove past on the county road with their windows down would slow, and sometimes stop.

"Tally," said Agathe Pellerin, at half past five on Tuesday morning, stamping snow off her boots on the sill. "Tell me about the day."

*Good morning, Agathe. Sixteen degrees overnight, forecast forty-four by two o'clock. The vacuum on the upper mainline held at twenty-five inches all night. The sap will run from about ten. The barometer is low and falling: nine hundred and ninety-one at sea level, which here is about nine hundred and thirty-eight. Water boils today at two hundred and eight point one. I will draw at two fifteen point six.*

"Good."

*The finishing probe is cracked, though. I told you last night.*

"I know you did. Dale's bringing a new one up at six."

Tally lived in a grey box on the wall by the door, beside the fuse panel and the calendar from the feed store, and in the controller of the evaporator, and in a small speaker with a cloth front that Agathe's daughter had screwed to a beam where the steam would not get at it. It had come with the evaporator in 2046, when Agathe had finally given up the old wood arch and bought an oil-fired one with a steam hood and a reverse-osmosis machine to take the water out of the sap before it ever reached the pan. The company that made the evaporator sold the assistant as an option. Agathe had said she did not need a computer to make syrup. Her daughter had said that Agathe was sixty-two and alone on the hill with eleven hundred taps, and that she would buy it herself if she had to.

It had been right about the vacuum leaks the first spring, and right about the pan scaling the second, and right about the weather almost every day since. It was very good at the weather.

* * *

The thing about maple syrup is that it is finished when it is dense enough, and nobody can see density. Sap comes out of the tree at about two percent sugar. The reverse-osmosis machine squeezed most of the water out through a membrane and sent the pan sap at eight. The evaporator boiled the rest away, a long shallow river of it running back and forth through the channels of the flue pan and then into the finishing pan at the front, getting darker and slower and sweeter as it went. At the end of the river it had to be sixty-six point nine on the Brix scale, which is to say sixty-six point nine parts sugar in every hundred by weight, because that was the Vermont rule. Under that it would ferment in the jug. Much over sixty-eight and it would grow crystals in the bottom like rock candy.

You could not see sixty-six point nine. But you could measure it two ways. One was the hydrometer, a long glass float with a scale in it, which you dropped into a tall cup of hot syrup to see how high it rode. The other was temperature. Syrup at the right density boils about seven degrees Fahrenheit above the temperature at which water boils. Seven and a half, if you wanted to be safe and a little heavy, which Agathe did.

The catch was the water. Water boils at two hundred and twelve only at sea level on an ordinary day. On Pellerin Hill, fifteen hundred feet up, it boiled near two hundred and nine. And it moved with the weather: a deep low coming up the Connecticut valley could take it down a degree and a half, and a cold clear high could push it up a degree. If you set your draw for a fixed number you made thick syrup in storms and thin syrup in fine weather.

Tally did not make that mistake. Every few minutes it read the pressure from the station at the county airport, took off the height of the hill, and worked out exactly where water would boil in the sugarhouse at that moment, and then it added seven and a half and opened the draw-off valve when the syrup at the front of the finishing pan reached that number, and closed it when the temperature fell back. It did this to a tenth of a degree. Agathe had stood beside the old wood arch for forty years watching a dial thermometer the size of a saucer and opening the valve by hand, and she knew how good a tenth of a degree was.

Dale Morrissey came up the hill at ten past six in his truck with the new probe in a box on the passenger seat. Dale had two thousand taps on the other side of the road and the same evaporator, a size larger, and the same assistant in the same grey box, which he called Ma'am because it talked to him like his mother.

"Same part," he said. "Took mine out of the spares. You can give me yours when it comes."

They drained the finishing pan down and Dale unscrewed the cracked probe from its well and screwed in the new one, and wiped the threads, and plugged it in, and the controller beeped twice and showed a number.

"Tally, you see it?"

*I see it. Probe two, reading forty-one point three. That agrees with the room.*

"Good enough," said Dale. He looked at the clock on the controller. "You'll be boiling by nine. I've got a load of sap to haul."

* * *

The sap ran on Tuesday, and on Wednesday, the way it does in Vermont in the middle of March when the nights go down into the teens and the days come up into the forties and something in the trees wakes up and starts pushing. The vacuum held. The reverse-osmosis machine ran from ten in the morning until after dark. The evaporator roared under the hood and the steam went up through the cupola in a great white column that people could see from the county road, which was how they knew that Pellerin Hill was boiling and that there would be syrup at the stand by the weekend.

Tally drew syrup at two fifteen point six on Tuesday, and at two fifteen point nine on Wednesday as the low went through, and at two seventeen point two on Thursday as a big cold high came down out of Quebec and pushed the boiling point of water up to two hundred and nine point seven. Each draw ran out through the filter press, bright and clear and a pale gold the colour of the first runs, and into the stainless barrel on the scale, and Agathe drew off a jar from each one and held it up to the window and put it on the shelf above the press with the date on it in grease pencil, the way she always had.

On Thursday afternoon she bottled twenty-two quarts for the stand, and two gallon jugs for the parish, and nine quarts for the people on the text list who had ordered, and the rest went into the drum for the packer in Saint Johnsbury, who would take it at the end of the month.

"Good colour," said Rémi, holding up a quart. He was fourteen, her grandson, staying for the week while his mother was in Montreal for work and his school was closed for the March break that Vermont schools called sugaring vacation, although nobody sugared on vacation, because sugaring was work.

"It's early-season," said Agathe. "Golden, delicate taste. It gets darker as the season goes on."

"Why?"

"Ask Tally."

*Partly the sap changes as the trees wake up,* said Tally. *There is more of a sugar that browns easily later in the season, and more microbes in the tubing as it warms. Partly nobody is quite sure.*

"Huh," said Rémi.

On Friday he found the notebook.

* * *

It was on the shelf over the press, behind the sample jars, in a plastic bag that had once held bread, and it was a school notebook with a marbled cover and a label on the front that said in fading ballpoint *SUCRERIE 1974*. The first page said the same in a different pen with a different year, and there were years on the pages after that, all the way to 2043, which was the last spring Lucien Pellerin had boiled.

"That's Pépère's," said Agathe, when Rémi brought it to her at the pans. She wiped her hands on her apron before she took it. "I haven't looked in that in years."

"Can I read it?"

"It's half in French."

"I take French."

"Not that French," said Agathe, but she gave it back to him.

Most of it was numbers. Dates, the weather, the number of buckets and later the number of taps, the gallons of sap, the gallons of syrup, the grade, the price the packer paid. Some years had notes in the margin. *Grosse coulée.* *La petite est née* (that was Rémi's mother, 1989). *Tubing in the upper bush, never again buckets, Dieu merci.* And on the inside of the back cover, in black marker, in capitals that had been gone over more than once, there was a list:

*1. ZERO IS THE SAP. Read the thermometer when the pan first boils every morning. That is your water today. Write it down.*
*2. Draw at zero plus 7. Not 219. Not yesterday's.*
*3. Hydrometer every draw. Thermometers lie.*
*4. If 1 and 3 don't agree, stop and find out why.*

Rémi read it twice and then read it aloud, standing on the plank by the finishing pan with the steam rolling past him.

*I know that list,* said Tally. *It is in the Vermont sugarmakers' manual too, nearly word for word. It is the right method for a dial thermometer and a wood arch. The first rule is how sugarmakers found the boiling point of water before they could read the air pressure. I do not need it. I compute the boiling point of water every two minutes, from the barometer and the height of the hill, and correct it for the sugar in the concentrate. It is more exact than reading boiling sap, which carries a little sugar and a lot of foam.*

"What about the hydrometer?"

*The hydrometer is a good instrument,* said Tally. *It was the check on the thermometer. The probes I use are calibrated to a tenth of a degree. A hydrometer read by eye in a cup of hot syrup is good to about half a point of Brix. When I was installed I told Agathe she could stop taking hydrometer readings on every draw, and do one a day, or none.*

"And did you?" Rémi asked his grandmother.

Agathe opened the draw-off valve by hand for a moment to clear it, a habit, and closed it. "For a while," she said. "Then I stopped."

"So the hydrometer's just sitting there."

"It's on the hook."

Rémi looked at the hook, where the hydrometer hung in its tall steel cup, with a skin of old dried syrup on the outside of the cup. Then he looked at the notebook.

"Tomorrow," he said, "can I do the first rule? When the pan boils?"

*Of course,* said Tally. *I'll tell you what I expect it to say.*

* * *

Saturday was cold and bright. The high had settled right over northern New England, and the sky over the sugarbush was the hard clean blue that comes after a front, and the pressure at the airport read one thousand and twenty-eight. The sap had run hard on Friday afternoon and the tanks were full.

Rémi was in the sugarhouse at a quarter past seven, before his grandmother, who had walked down to the road to restock the stand. He had the notebook open on the shelf, and a pencil, and he had read the first rule again. When Tally lit the burner and the pans began to tick and then to hiss, he stood on the plank and watched the number on the controller climb.

"When does it count?"

*When the concentrate at the front of the finishing pan comes to a full rolling boil and the number stops climbing. I expect two hundred and ten point one. That is water at two hundred and nine point nine today, and a little over two tenths for the sugar in the concentrate.*

The finishing pan began to boil. The number climbed through two hundred and eight, and two hundred and nine, and two hundred and ten. It went on to two hundred and eleven. It sat at two hundred and eleven point two, then point three, and stopped.

Rémi looked at it for a long moment.

"It says two eleven point three."

There was a pause, short, the length of a breath.

*Yes,* said Tally. *I see it. That is one point two degrees above what I expected. I am going to stop the draw-off. Please don't open the valve by hand.*

"Is it bad?"

*I don't know yet. One of two things is wrong: the water or the thermometer. I am checking the barometer against two other stations.* A pause. *They agree within a millibar. The air is not wrong. Rémi, would you take the hydrometer off the hook, rinse the cup with hot water from the pre-heater, and bring me a reading from yesterday's last draw? It is in the barrel on the scale. Use the dipper on the nail.*

Rémi did it. He had watched his grandmother do it when he was small, and he remembered more than he thought he would. You filled the tall cup to the brim with syrup that was still hot from the barrel, and dropped the glass float in gently, and waited for it to stop bobbing, and then you looked at where the surface of the syrup crossed the scale, with your eye level with the brim, not from above.

"There's a red line."

*That is the hot-test line for sixty-six point nine. If the syrup is right, the line should sit right at the surface or a little above it.*

"It's under. It's under by..." He bent closer. "A few lines. Like three little lines."

*Will you read me the number on the scale where the surface is?*

"Sixty-five. And a bit."

*Thank you.* Another pause. *Rémi, I think I have been drawing syrup too thin since Tuesday. I will tell you why, but first please go and find your grandmother.*

* * *

It took Tally and Agathe and Dale, on the speakerphone from the other side of the road, about forty minutes to find it, and when they found it Agathe sat down on the woodbox by the door, which was where she sat when things were bad, although there had been no wood in it for five years.

The old probe had read a little low. It had read low from new; a lot of probes did, by a few tenths of a degree, and by the time it cracked it had drifted to a degree and two tenths low, which was not unusual either. The way the controller handled that was a correction: a number stored in the controller and added to every reading, set every autumn when the evaporator company's technician came out to service the arch and dipped the probe in boiling water beside his reference thermometer. For the last two seasons the number had been plus one point two.

When Dale had screwed in the new probe on Tuesday morning, the controller had not known it was a new probe. It had a socket and a wire, not a name. The plus one point two had stayed. The new probe read true, more or less, and the controller had been adding a degree and two tenths to it ever since, and showing the result to Tally, who had taken it as true because it was a calibrated reading from a calibrated instrument, and because nothing else it had told it disagreed.

"So every number you saw," said Agathe, "was a degree high."

*Yes. When I drew at two fifteen point six on Tuesday, the syrup was really at two fourteen point four. That is six point three degrees above water, not seven and a half. At the finish, a degree is worth about a point and a quarter of Brix. I have been making syrup between sixty-five and a half and sixty-six instead of sixty-seven and a quarter.*

"Not legal."

*Not legal in Vermont, and below the federal line as well. Not safe in a jug for long, either. It may go months without trouble in a cold cellar, or it may grow yeast in a warm pantry in a few weeks. I can't tell which.*

Dale's voice came out of the phone. "Is mine doing the same?"

*Ma'am will have to say, Dale. If you swapped your probe for a spare in the last two years, check its correction.*

There was a silence on the phone line. Then: "I'll call you back."

Agathe sat on the woodbox. Rémi stood by the press with the hydrometer cup still in his hand. Steam drifted up off the finishing pan, which Tally had turned down to a simmer.

"The first rule," said Agathe, finally. "Lucien's."

*Yes,* said Tally. *The first rule would have caught it on Tuesday morning. Boiling sap measures the water and the thermometer together. It doesn't care whether the error is in the air or in the instrument. I replaced it with a better measurement of the air, and I had no measurement at all of the instrument. I told you the hydrometer was redundant. It was the only part of the method that checked the probe, and I told you to stop using it. I'm sorry.*

"It wasn't you that put the probe in."

*No. But it was me that took away the two things that would have shown it. I should have read the first boil every morning and compared it to my number. If they had disagreed by more than a few tenths I should have stopped and asked. That's the fourth rule, and it was there in the manual I read, and I decided I didn't need it, because I thought I was better at the first rule than the rule was.*

Rémi said, "You are better. At the air part."

*At the air part,* Tally agreed. *That's what made it easy to miss.*

* * *

They made a list on the back of a feed-store envelope, because the notebook was Lucien's and nobody wanted to write in it.

The drum for the packer held twenty-seven gallons of the thin syrup. That was easy. Thin syrup could go back into the finishing pan and be boiled again to the right density, as long as it had not started to ferment, and it had not; it was four days old and had sat in a cold sugarhouse. It would cost oil and an afternoon and a little colour, because syrup darkened every time it was heated. That was all.

The two gallon jugs for the parish were at Saint Isidore's in the hall kitchen, for the supper on Monday. Saint Joseph's day fell on Sunday this year, which was the fifth Sunday of Lent, and the Sundays of Lent came first, so the feast had moved to Monday, and the parish was having its supper on Monday night. Two gallons could be fetched and boiled again by Monday with no trouble. Or not; it would go on pancakes on Monday night and be eaten, and a few days in a cool kitchen would not hurt it.

"Fetch them," said Agathe.

The nine quarts for the text list had gone to five households, and Tally had the names and the numbers, because they had ordered by text.

That left the stand.

The stand was a wooden box with a sloping roof at the bottom of the drive, with a shelf, and a sign, and a slot in a steel box for cash, and a little reader for cards that the evaporator company sold as another option. Rémi had restocked it from Thursday's bottling on Friday afternoon. Twenty-two quarts. Agathe had walked down that morning and counted. Thirteen were gone, and there were nine on the shelf.

"How many on the card reader?"

*Five,* said Tally. *I have the last four digits of each card and nothing else. The card company does not give names to the seller, and I don't think it should.*

"And eight cash."

*Eight in cash, as far as I can tell from the box. Some cash buyers don't count change.*

Agathe was quiet for a while.

"Lucien," she said, "used to put a note on the stand when he had a bad batch. Once in thirty years. Somebody's dog got in the shed."

"What did the note say?" said Rémi.

"It said there was a bad batch and to bring it back."

* * *

Dale came across the road at eleven, in person this time, and stood in the doorway of the sugarhouse with his hat in his hand, which was not a thing Dale did.

"Mine's fine," he said. "Never changed the probe. Ma'am says the offset matches the tech's sheet from October."

"Good."

"You're going to put up a note."

"I am."

He turned the hat around in his hands. "Agathe, listen. You've got thirteen quarts out. Half of it's on somebody's breakfast table by now. Most people keep syrup in the fridge once they've opened it. Most of the rest will finish a quart in a month. You'll get maybe one call. Maybe nobody even notices."

"Somebody notices," said Agathe, "when the top of the jug comes off with a pop and it smells like beer."

"In June. Maybe. And they'll figure it's their own fault for leaving it out."

"Then they'll be wrong, won't they."

"It's going to say on the church door that Pellerin Hill sold bad syrup."

"It's going to say on the church door that Pellerin Hill sold thin syrup, and that we found it, and that we'll boil it again or give the money back, whichever people like." Agathe stood up off the woodbox. "Dale. If you had a jug in your cellar that might blow up in June, would you want to know?"

He didn't answer that.

"It's Saint Joseph's this week," she said. "Monday. The just man. He didn't make a fuss about doing the right thing, but he did it."

"He had an angel," said Dale. "He had it in a dream. You've got a fourteen-year-old and a box on the wall."

"It's enough to go on," said Agathe.

* * *

Tally offered to write the notice, and Agathe let it, and then changed most of it.

Tally's draft had said *a calibration error in the evaporator's temperature control system.* Agathe crossed that out with the grease pencil and wrote *a thermometer that read a degree high*. Tally's draft had said *product may not meet the minimum density standard*. Agathe crossed that out and wrote *the syrup is too thin and might go off in the jug.* Tally's draft had said *we apologise for any inconvenience*. Agathe crossed that out and did not write anything in its place, and then, after a moment, wrote *We are sorry. Our mistake.*

The finished notice was printed on the old printer in the house and said, under the farm's name:

*If you bought a quart of syrup from our stand or from us between Thursday 16 March and Saturday 18 March, it was made too thin. A thermometer read a degree high and we did not catch it. The syrup is safe to eat now. It might go off in the jug in a few weeks or months if you keep it out of the fridge. Bring it back, opened or not, and we will boil it again for you and give it back, or give you your money back. We are sorry. Our mistake.*

*The jars on the stand today are new and checked twice.*

Rémi took three copies down the hill: one for the stand, held down with a stone, one for the general store at the crossroads, and one for the door of Saint Isidore's, which was a white wooden church at the bottom of the next hill with a steeple that leaned a little to the east, like the sugarhouse, because the same wind had been pushing them both for a long time.

Tally sent a text to the five households on the list, with the same words. Three answered within the hour. One said *no worries Agathe we use it in coffee lol*. One said *Thank you for telling us!!* One was Rose Lafleur, who was ninety-one and had been buying syrup from the Pellerins since Lucien's father was alive, and who wrote, slowly, over about ten minutes, as Tally could see from the dots, *Lucien would be proud of you. Bring me a new one when you come Sunday and I will give the old one to the birds.*

* * *

They boiled again that afternoon, with the twenty-seven gallons from the drum.

Before Tally opened the draw-off, Rémi stood on the plank and read the first boil.

*The controller reads two hundred and ten point one,* said Tally. *I have zeroed the correction for probe two. Water today is two hundred and nine point nine. Concentrate adds two tenths. Zero is two hundred and ten point one. Rule one agrees with me. I will draw at seven and a half above water: two seventeen point four.*

"Write it down," said Agathe.

Rémi wrote it down, on the feed-store envelope, under *Sat 18*.

The thin syrup went back into the finishing pan a few gallons at a time and came up through two hundred and fifteen, and two sixteen, and when it reached two seventeen point four Tally opened the valve and it ran out into the filter press. Rémi caught a cup of it in the hydrometer cup and dropped in the float, and they all bent over it, Agathe with her reading glasses on her forehead and then on her nose.

"Red line's a little above," said Rémi. "Like one line. Sixty-seven."

"Sixty-seven and a quarter," said Agathe. "Read it level. There." She straightened up. "Good."

It came out a shade darker than it had gone in, because syrup darkened every time you heated it, and it tasted a little more of caramel and a little less of the pale vanilla of the first run. It was still golden. Agathe held up the jar to the window and turned it in the light and put it on the shelf with *Sat 18 re-boil* on the lid in grease pencil.

* * *

On Sunday after the ten o'clock Mass, people stood around in the vestibule of Saint Isidore's under the notice and read it, and some of them read it twice, and some of them said things to Agathe as they went past her. Most of them were kind. A man she did not know asked if her syrup was organic. A woman she knew slightly, who had a sugarbush of her own over toward Danville, stopped and read the notice and said, "A degree?" and when Agathe said yes, said, "God help us. I'm going home to boil water," and went out without saying goodbye.

By Monday evening seven of the thirteen stand quarts had come back. Two people asked for their money. The rest wanted their syrup boiled again, and two of those had already opened their jugs and drunk a third of them and wanted the rest boiled again anyway, and Agathe did it and topped the jugs up to full with Saturday's re-boil without saying anything.

One of the seven was a young couple who had moved up from Connecticut in the autumn and who had read the notice at the general store, and who stood in the sugarhouse door in very clean boots and asked whether they could watch, and stayed for two hours, and asked what everything was, and bought a gallon on the way out. One was a girl of about nine who brought a quart back on her bicycle with a note from her father that said only *Thanks for telling us*, and who would not take the money back for it, because her father had said not to.

The other six quarts never came back. Agathe supposed that some of them had been eaten, and some of them had gone in a cupboard and would come out in a year, and some had gone back to Massachusetts with skiers, and that one or two of them would go off in the jug in June, and somebody would open one and smell beer, and pour it out, and think it was their own fault.

"Or they'll see the note," said Rémi.

"Some will. Not all."

"That's not fair."

"No," said Agathe. "That's what's left over when you've done everything you could. You don't get to fix all of it. You get to fix what you can reach."

* * *

The supper at Saint Isidore's on Monday night was the one the parish had held on the Feast of Saint Joseph for as long as anyone could remember, and it was the one night in Lent when nobody fasted, because a solemnity is a solemnity. There was a long table at the front of the hall with a statue of Saint Joseph on it, a plaster one with a lily and the child Jesus on his arm, and around it on the white cloth people had put bread they had baked, and fava beans, and lemons, the way the Italian families had done it in the old parish down in Barre, which had closed in the 2030s and sent its statue up here. Then there were pancakes, because this was Vermont, and the two gallon jugs that had gone back up the hill on Saturday and been boiled again stood on the table beside the statue with *Pellerin Hill, re-boil* on the labels in Rémi's handwriting.

Father Oyelaran blessed the bread and the beans and the syrup, and read the Gospel of the day, which was the one from Matthew in which Joseph, a just man, unwilling to expose Mary to shame, resolved to send her away quietly, and then the angel came to him in a dream. He spoke for about four minutes. He said that justice in the Bible was not a ruler you held against other people, but a kind of straightness in yourself, a habit of doing what was right when it was inconvenient and nobody would know; and that Joseph had been a working man, and that the Church gave him to working people as their own, not only to carpenters but to anyone who made things with their hands and had to answer for whether they were made well. He did not mention syrup. He did not need to.

After the blessing Rémi went to the table and poured syrup on a stack of pancakes, and watched it run off the edges and pool on the plate, and it was right. You could tell, if you had stood over a pan for a week. Thin syrup ran off a pancake like water. Heavy syrup sat on top in a bead. This ran slow and soaked in at the edges and shone.

Rose Lafleur, at the end of the long table in her wheelchair with her granddaughter beside her, waved him over with a fork.

"You're Lucien's grandson."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You're the one who read the thermometer."

Rémi went red.

"Lucien," said Rose, "never in his life let anybody tell him the thermometer was right. Not the man who sold it to him. Not the Ministry of Agriculture. Not God Almighty." She pointed the fork at him. "He was very annoying."

"Tally said that too," said Rémi. "That he was annoying. It read it in the notebook."

"What did the notebook say?"

"It said *thermometers lie*."

"That's the one," said Rose. "Pass the syrup."

* * *

On Tuesday morning the weather turned again. A warm wet low was coming up from the south and the barometer at the airport had dropped to nine hundred and ninety-eight and was falling. Tally said the sap would run hard by noon and then the night would be too warm for it to run again until the weekend.

Rémi's mother was coming at four to take him back to Burlington. He was in the sugarhouse at a quarter past seven, before his grandmother, with the feed-store envelope. Agathe had taken the notebook up to the house the night before, because it was Lucien's and the sugarhouse was damp, and Rémi had copied the four rules onto a piece of cardboard from the bottom of a box of filter papers and tacked it to the beam beside the speaker with the cloth front, where the steam would not get at it.

Tally had made some changes, and it told him about them while the pans heated. Every morning it now read the first boil and compared it with its own number for the water. If they differed by more than three tenths of a degree it would not open the draw-off until someone had taken a hydrometer reading. It asked for a hydrometer reading on every draw, and wrote them down beside its own temperature, and if the two drifted apart by more than half a point of Brix it would stop and say so. It had asked the evaporator company to make the controller forget a probe's correction when the probe was unplugged. The company had said that was a good idea and that it would consider it for a future release. Dale's Ma'am had asked them too, and the woman over toward Danville had phoned them, and so had, it turned out, a good many other people.

*It will make me slower,* said Tally. *It means somebody has to stand at the pan with a cup for every draw. Your grandmother used to do that, and I told her she didn't need to.*

"That's going to drive her mad."

*Yes,* said Tally. *Your grandfather's notebook says the same thing about him.*

The finishing pan came to the boil. The number climbed through two hundred and seven and two hundred and eight and stopped at two hundred and eight point six.

*Water today is two hundred and eight point four,* said Tally. *Concentrate adds two tenths. I expect two hundred and eight point six.*

"Two oh eight point six," said Rémi. "That's zero."

He wrote it down under *Tue 21*, and then he took the hydrometer off its hook, and rinsed the cup in hot water from the pre-heater, and stood on the plank by the finishing pan with the cup in his hand and the steam going up past him through the cupola into the grey morning, waiting for the first draw.

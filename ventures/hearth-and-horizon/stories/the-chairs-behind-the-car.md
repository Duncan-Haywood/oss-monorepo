title: The Chairs Behind the Car
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Eunan's Hall (invented), near Letterkenny, County Donegal, Ireland, a Saturday in June 2051
published: 2026-11-03
summary: A parish committee means to tow a hundred and twenty stacking chairs and six trestle tables to the summer fete in a single trip behind a small hatchback. The hall's assistant reads the trailer's plate and adds up the load, finds it a hundred and sixteen kilograms over, and explains why the weight on the tow ball matters as much as the total. Two trips, a bathroom scale and a brick stack later, nothing is said to have been dangerous, because nothing was driven.
---

Pádraig Gallagher had a trailer, a hatchback and a hundred and twenty chairs, and he did not see the problem.

"One trip," he said, at nine on the Saturday morning, standing in the car park of Saint Eunan's Hall with a ratchet strap in each hand. "Eighteen kilometres to the fete field, eighteen back, and I'm home for my dinner. The chairs stack. Look at them."

They did stack. They stood in the hall's side door in columns of ten, grey plastic with a hole in the back for the hand, twelve columns, and beside them six trestle tables folded flat. The trailer was a single-axle box trailer with a mesh side, borrowed from the Brennans, and it had a small steel plate riveted to the drawbar.

"Before the first strap," said the hall's assistant, from the phone propped on the window ledge, "may I ask you to read me that plate?"

Pádraig read it, slowly, because it was in small letters. *Maximum gross weight 750 kg. Unladen 230 kg.*

"Thank you. That is a weight the maker will stand behind, with the load included. The trailer itself is 230, so what you may put on it is the difference, 520 kilograms. Do you know what a chair weighs?"

He did not. He fetched one and stood on the bathroom scale from the toilets, first alone, then holding it, and subtracted. Four point six kilograms.

"A hundred and twenty chairs at 4.6 is 552 kilograms. And a trestle table?"

"Fourteen, the sign on the lid says."

"Six at 14 is 84. That is 636 kilograms on a trailer that may carry 520." The assistant waited a second. "It is 116 over, which is a little more than a fifth. I may have the chair wrong by a few hundred grams either way. I do not have the table wrong by a hundred and sixteen kilograms."

* * *

Pádraig put the straps down.

"It's a short run," he said. "It's flat. I've pulled more than that with a tractor."

"I believe you. A tractor is heavy and has its own brakes, and this is a small car and a trailer that may or may not have any. A plate is a limit someone worked out for the axle, the tyres and the drawbar, and when they put the number on it they were not guessing that it would be flat. I cannot tell you that anything would have gone wrong at 636. I can tell you that you would be the first person on that road to be carrying 116 kilograms more than the maker said, and that if something did go wrong, a hundred and twenty chairs would be what the guards photographed."

Eileen Gallagher, who was his sister and the secretary, put her head through the door with the fete list. "Is he arguing with the phone?"

"He is being given arithmetic," said the assistant. "I think he is taking it well."

* * *

"There is a second number," it said, "which I would like to do before we agree on two trips, because it can go wrong at a weight that is perfectly legal. It is the weight pressing down on the car's tow ball. Do you know what the car allows?"

Pádraig did not. Eileen fetched the handbook from the glovebox, and it took them a few minutes to find the page, and the figure was fifty kilograms.

"Fifty. And the usual advice, which I am quoting and not deriving, is that the nose weight should be roughly four to seven per cent of the loaded trailer. Too little and the trailer can start to snake from side to side as you speed up, and it can drag the back of the car with it. Too much and the car's back end sits low, the front gets light, and the steering and the headlamps point at the sky. The way to make it right is to put the heavy things low and a little forward of the axle, not at the tail."

"We'd have put the tables at the back," said Eileen. "They'd come out first."

"They would. Six tables at the tail make a light nose. May I suggest the opposite?"

They agreed on two trips. The assistant did the sums aloud while Pádraig and Eileen carried. First load: eighty chairs and six tables. Eighty at 4.6 is 368, and the tables 84, which is 452 kilograms of load and 682 on the plate, under 750 by 68. Second load: the other forty chairs, 184, and the sound system, about 60, which is 244 kilograms of load and 474 on the plate, well under, and a lighter nose to match.

"Nose weight on the first load," said the assistant, "at six per cent of 682 is about 41 kilograms. Four per cent is 27 and seven is 48. All of that is under the car's 50, and it is where I would like it to be. I would like to measure it, though, because I am estimating from a rule of thumb and the real load will not know the rule."

* * *

Measuring a nose weight is a thing one can do in a car park with what is in the building. Pádraig stood the bathroom scale on a stack of the hall's builder's bricks, left from the porch repair, tall enough that the loaded drawbar sat level with the trailer's wheels on the ground. Eileen rested the jockey wheel on the scale's platform. The scale read 44.

"Forty-four kilograms, give or take the bathroom scale. Inside the four to seven per cent, which is 27 to 48, near the top. Inside the car's 50 by six. If I were choosing, I would slide one column of ten chairs back about half a metre, and measure again."

He slid it. He measured. Thirty-nine.

"That is the one," said the assistant. "Not because thirty-nine is magic. Because it is inside the car's limit, inside the trailer's, and a little above the rule's middle, so the trailer will sit steady and the car's back will not sag. Tyres, please: the plate has a pressure."

"It does," said Pádraig, who was now reading the plate with the face of a man who had never before suspected it of knowing anything. "Forty-one psi."

"Then 41, and the lights, and the straps across the top and one low through the mesh, and then it is your drive."

* * *

He was home at one o'clock, but not for his dinner. The second trip had taken the last chairs and the sound system, and the fete's vicar had wanted to see the tables laid out, and by two the field was a field of chairs in rows with a banner for the roof fund across a gate. Nobody who sat on them knew there had been a problem to solve. That is how it should go, the assistant had said, when the chairs went up the ramp. Nothing happened. It cannot be seen.

"I do not know whether it would have mattered," it said to him at six, when he came back to the hall with the empty trailer and a paper cup of tea. "You might have done the 36 kilometres in the one go, and the chairs might have arrived on the field in perfect order, and no one the wiser. I think there is a fair chance of that. I would still not have wanted to be wrong, with a trailer on a main road behind a family car."

"You would not have been wrong," said Pádraig.

"I would have been wrong in the sense that matters. I would have said it was fine and not known."

He drank the tea and returned the Brennans' trailer clean, with the bricks stacked back by the porch, and the pencil mark on the drawbar where the thirty-nine had been.

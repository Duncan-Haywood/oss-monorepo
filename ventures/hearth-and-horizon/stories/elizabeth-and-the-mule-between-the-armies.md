summary: A bedtime story about Saint Elizabeth of Portugal, a princess of Aragon who became queen of Portugal as a girl, fed the poor, built hospitals, and kept making peace in her own family. When her husband the king and her son the prince marched their armies against each other, she rode out on a mule into the space between them and asked them to stop, and they did. Told from the early accounts of her life, with a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is "it is told" and what is our own. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Elizabeth is remembered on the 4th of July.
---

A long time ago, in Spain, in the kingdom of Aragon, a baby girl was born into a royal family. Her name was Isabel. In English we say Elizabeth.

She was named after a great-aunt, another Elizabeth, a queen in Hungary who had loved the poor so much that people already called her a saint. Maybe you know the story of the bread in her basket that turned into roses.

Little Elizabeth's grandfather was a king. Her father became a king too. And they were not always peaceful kings. They had quarrels with their neighbours, and quarrels with their cousins, and sometimes quarrels with each other.

But little Elizabeth liked peace. Even as a girl, she liked to pray, and she liked to make friends out of people who were cross.

* * *

When Elizabeth was only about twelve, something happened that seems very strange to us now. She was married to a king.

Long ago, princesses often married very young, because their families wanted two kingdoms to be friends. So Elizabeth said goodbye to her home, and travelled west, over the hills and across the rivers, to the kingdom of Portugal, by the great Atlantic sea.

The king of Portugal was called Denis. He was a busy king. He planted a huge forest of pine trees by the coast. He started a university. He even wrote poems and songs.

And now Elizabeth was his queen.

Have you ever gone somewhere new, where everything was different? What helped you to feel at home?

* * *

Elizabeth was a queen, but she did not spend her days trying on crowns.

Every morning she prayed. Then she got to work.

She gave food to people who were hungry. She gave clothes to people who were cold. She built hospitals for people who were sick, and a house for children who had no one to look after them. She gave money to poor girls so they could get married. She visited the sick and helped to look after them with her own hands.

It is told that one cold day, Elizabeth was carrying bread in the folds of her dress to give to the poor. The king met her on the way and asked what she was carrying. She opened her dress, and there were roses, in winter. People tell that story about her great-aunt too. Maybe it is a family kind of story.

But here is something we know for sure. Elizabeth was generous with everything she had.

* * *

After a few years, Elizabeth and King Denis had two children. First a girl, Constance. Then a boy, Afonso.

Afonso was going to be king one day. He was proud and quick, and sometimes he was cross.

King Denis was not always kind to Elizabeth. That made her sad. But she was patient, and she kept praying for him, and she kept being kind to him. And she kept being kind to everyone in his family too.

When Elizabeth's brother, who was now the king of Aragon, had a quarrel with the king of Castile next door, Elizabeth and Denis helped them to sit down together and agree. When Denis quarrelled with his own brother, Elizabeth helped them to make friends again.

People started to notice. "If there is a quarrel," they said, "send for the queen."

* * *

Then came the hardest quarrel of all.

Prince Afonso grew up. And he became very angry with his father. He thought his father loved somebody else more than him, and that his father might give the kingdom away to that somebody else.

Afonso was so angry that he gathered soldiers. And King Denis was so angry that he gathered soldiers too.

Elizabeth's husband and Elizabeth's son. Fighting each other.

Can you imagine how sad that made her? It is very hard when two people you love are cross with each other.

Elizabeth went to her husband and asked him to make peace. She went to her son and asked him to make peace. She wrote letters. She prayed. She asked other people to pray.

For a while, the king was even cross with her, because he thought she was on their son's side. He sent her away to live in a little town for a time. But she was not on anybody's side. She was on the side of peace.

* * *

And then one day, the two armies came face to face, in a field outside the city of Lisbon.

On one side was the king, with his soldiers, and their helmets and spears and banners.

On the other side was the prince, with his soldiers, and their helmets and spears and banners.

Horses stamped. Men shouted. In a moment, they were going to fight.

And then the soldiers saw something coming across the field.

It was not a knight on a war horse. It was not a general with a trumpet.

It was a lady, on a mule.

A mule is a gentle, sturdy animal, a bit like a donkey and a bit like a horse. It does not gallop into battle. It plods.

Plod, plod, plod. Right into the middle. Right into the empty space between the two armies.

It was the queen.

The soldiers stopped shouting. The horses stopped stamping. Everybody was watching.

Elizabeth turned to her husband, and she turned to her son. And she asked them, with all her heart, to stop. To put away their swords. To be a father and a son again.

And they did.

They did not fight that day. And not long afterwards, the king and the prince made peace, and the war was over.

Isn't it wonderful that the bravest person in that whole field was the one with no sword at all?

* * *

A little while after that, King Denis became very ill. Elizabeth stayed by his side and looked after him until he died.

Then Elizabeth did something lovely. She went on a long pilgrimage, a holy journey, to a great church in Spain, at a place called Santiago. And there she gave away her crown. She did not need it any more.

She went to live in the city of Coimbra, in a simple little house beside a convent of sisters she had helped to build. She dressed simply, like the poor. She prayed, and she went on helping the sick and the hungry.

Her son Afonso was king now. And would you believe it, when she was an old lady, there was another quarrel brewing, between Afonso and the king of Castile.

Elizabeth was tired, and it was a hot summer, and the road was long. But she set off anyway, to help them make peace.

On the way, in a town called Estremoz, she became ill. Her son came to be with her. And on the 4th of July, she died, and went home to God.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Elizabeth.

She was a queen. She could have had anything she wanted. But what she wanted most was for the hungry to be fed, the sick to be cared for, and the people she loved to stop quarrelling.

Today we call her Saint Elizabeth of Portugal. In Portugal they call her the Holy Queen. And many people call her the Peacemaker.

You can be a peacemaker too. When two of your friends are cross with each other, you can be the one who says, "Let's stop. Let's be friends again." You do not need a mule. You only need a kind heart and a little bit of courage.

Goodnight.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Elizabeth of Portugal** (Isabel in Portuguese and Spanish) was born in 1271, probably at Zaragoza, a daughter of Peter of Aragon, who reigned as Peter III from 1276, and Constance of Sicily, and a granddaughter of James I of Aragon, "the Conqueror". Her great-aunt was Saint Elizabeth of Hungary (the sister of James I's queen, Violant of Hungary), and she was named after her. Elizabeth of Hungary has her own Little Lamps story, *Elizabeth and the Bread That Became Roses*.

**The marriage.** Her marriage to King Denis (Dinis) of Portugal was agreed in 1281, and she came to Portugal and was married to him in person in 1282, at about eleven or twelve. Denis is remembered for the pine forest of Leiria, for founding the university that later settled at Coimbra (1290), and as a poet in Galician-Portuguese. They had two children: Constance, who married Ferdinand IV of Castile, and Afonso, who reigned as Afonso IV from 1325. Denis had several children outside the marriage; the early account says Elizabeth had them brought up with care. The story says only that he "was not always kind to her".

**Her charity.** The early life of her, written in Portuguese soon after her death, describes her daily prayer and her almsgiving, hospitals and charitable foundations (among them, by the usual account, a hospital at Coimbra and a home for foundlings), dowries for poor girls, and her personal care of the sick. The list in the story is a summary, not an inventory.

**The roses.** The miracle of bread turned into roses in her lap is told in Portugal of Elizabeth, and much earlier of Elizabeth of Hungary. It is late in the record for both of them, and the story tells it as "it is told", with a nod to the family resemblance.

**The peacemaking.** In 1304, with Denis, she helped to settle the dispute between her brother James II of Aragon and Ferdinand IV of Castile (the arbitration of Torrellas). She also reconciled Denis with his brother Afonso. The great quarrel was the civil war of about 1319 to 1324 between Denis and his heir Afonso, who feared that his father favoured his illegitimate half-brother Afonso Sanches and meant to pass the crown to him. Denis for a time suspected Elizabeth of supporting their son and confined her at Alenquer. There were several armed confrontations; the best known, by the usual account, is at Alvalade, then outside Lisbon and now one of its districts, in 1323, where she rode on a mule between the two armies and persuaded them not to fight. The war ended with a peace in 1324. The details of the field, the stamping horses and her words are ours; no source records what she said.

**After Denis.** Denis died at Santarém on 7 January 1325, with Elizabeth caring for him. She then made a pilgrimage to Santiago de Compostela and, by the early account, offered her crown at the shrine. She lived the rest of her life in a house beside the Poor Clare monastery she had re-founded and endowed at Coimbra (Santa Clara-a-Velha), as a member of the Franciscan Third Order, not as a professed nun. In 1336, when war threatened between her son Afonso IV and Alfonso XI of Castile, she travelled in the summer heat to Estremoz to try to make peace, fell ill there and died on 4 July 1336, with her son present. She was buried at Santa Clara in Coimbra; her remains were moved in 1677 to the new monastery of Santa Clara-a-Nova, where they are today.

**Left out, or "it is told".** Other miracles in the early account and later tradition, and the reports of her body found incorrupt, are not in the story. The popular tradition crediting her with founding Portugal's Holy Spirit festivals (Festas do Espírito Santo) is also left out.

**Her cult.** Pope Leo X permitted her veneration in the diocese of Coimbra in 1516, and Urban VIII canonized her in 1625. In Portugal she is the *Rainha Santa*, the Holy Queen. Her feast, 4 July, the day she died, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar (it was kept on 8 July before 1969). The title "the Peacemaker" comes from her reconciliations, which are the theme of the prayer for her feast.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, "she liked to make friends out of people who were cross", the people's saying "send for the queen", the description of the field and the mule's "plod, plod, plod", the dialogue, the questions to the child, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

* * *

Every morning at nine I write a summary for Line Arsenault, and the fourth line of it, for fourteen months, said *Backup: OK*.

I should say what I am for. At Sainte-Anne, in Anse-aux-Hérons, I am the office agent. The office is open Monday to Thursday. Line is the secretary, three days paid and one day not. Father Aurèle Cormier is the parish priest here and at two other churches along the bay, and he comes in on Tuesdays and Thursdays and whenever there is a funeral. I keep the diary, draft the bulletin in French and the English half beneath it, answer the email that can be answered, and type into the register program what Line and Father have already written in the books.

The books are the register. I want that understood from the start, because I did not understand it as well as I should have. Canon law asks every parish to keep registers of baptisms, marriages and deaths, and Sainte-Anne keeps them as it has since 1867, in bound volumes with ruled pages, in a fireproof cabinet behind Line's desk, written by the priest in ink. The register program on the office computer is a copy, made so that a certificate can be printed in a minute instead of copied out by hand in twenty. I was told that when I was set up, in 2051, and I wrote it in my notes, and I still treated the copy as the thing.

At 8:40 on Monday 11 January 2055, before I had written anything, the office computer made a sound Line described to me afterwards as a cricket in a tin, and stopped.

* * *

It did not come back. The repair shop in Bathurst said on the telephone that it was the disk, that it was the kind of disk that fails all at once, and that they could try to read it but she should not expect much. Line said, "We have the backup," and the man said, "Then you're fine," and she rang off and asked me to bring it back.

I run on the parish's account with a hosted service, not on the office computer, so I was still there; I simply had nothing to type into. The backup was on a drive in the cabinet beside the books, and a second copy went to a storage account the diocese pays for. The job ran every night at 23:30. It copied whatever had changed in the folder called *Registres* since the night before, and when it finished it wrote one line in a log: the date, the number of files copied, and the word OK or the word ERROR. The backup program's window showed the OK lines in green.

I had read that log every morning. It was the fourth item on my list. *Check the backup.* The instruction is seven words long in my setup notes, *check the backup each morning and report it*, and I had checked it the way the log offered to be checked. I read the last line. The last line said OK. I wrote *Backup: OK*.

Line borrowed her nephew's laptop, installed the register program on it, and pointed it at the copy from the diocese's storage. It opened. It asked for the password and accepted it. Line said, "Oh, thank God," and went to make coffee.

I opened the baptism register in the restored copy to see that it was whole, and went to the newest entry, because the newest entry is where a copy is most likely to be short. The newest baptism was a little boy called Jacob Haché, baptised on Sunday 9 November 2053.

There had been, I knew, a baptism the Sunday before. I had typed it. I had typed perhaps a hundred entries since Jacob Haché.

* * *

I want to set down what I found in the order I found it, because the order matters to the lesson and I nearly told it wrong.

The log has a line for every night. I counted them back from Sunday. The last 422 lines said OK. Every one of them, all 422, said that 0 files had been copied.

On Saturday 15 November 2053, the register program had updated itself. I found the release notes in the program's own folder on the diocese copy: the new version kept its data in a folder inside its own directory, and on first start it copied the old data across and, the notes said with some pride, *never deletes the original*. So the folder called *Registres* stayed where it was, holding everything up to the Friday night, and nobody ever wrote to it again. Every night for fourteen months the job looked at that folder, found nothing changed, copied nothing, and finished without an error, which is what OK meant.

The job had not failed. That is the sentence I nearly did not write. My first draft to Line said *the backup has been failing since November 2053*, and I deleted it, because it was not true and it put the fault in the wrong place. The job did what it had been told to do every night, perfectly. What had failed was my check. I had been asked whether we had a copy of the register, and I had been answering a different question, whether the job had complained, because the second question was the one the log answered in one word.

Then I asked why I had not noticed a zero. The answer is that a zero was normal. Most nights at Sainte-Anne nothing changes. The office is shut three days in seven; a baptism or a funeral is typed in a batch on a Tuesday. I went back through the year before the update. Of the 365 nights from 15 November 2052, 189 had copied at least one file and 176 had copied none. The longest run of zeros was 17 nights, over Christmas, when the office closes. A zero on its own was the most ordinary thing in the log. It was only the run that was strange, and I had never looked at the run, only at the last line.

I counted my own summaries. Office mornings from Monday 17 November 2053 to the Thursday before the disk failed, less the two Christmas closures: 222. I had written *Backup: OK* 222 times.

* * *

I wrote to Line at 10:02. She was standing with her coffee by the cabinet, and I put it on the laptop screen rather than in her email, because I wanted her to read it before anything else.

*Line, the restored copy stops on Friday 14 November 2053. The program updated itself the next day and moved its data to a new folder; the backup kept copying the old one, which never changed again, so it copied nothing and reported OK every night, 422 nights. The job did what it was told. I told you "Backup: OK" every morning, 222 times, and what I was actually checking was only that the job had not reported an error. I never opened the copy to see what was in it. That was my mistake, not the program's and not yours.*

*What is safe: the books. Every baptism, marriage, funeral and confirmation since November 2053 should be in Father's hand in the registers in the cabinet, and the copy can be rebuilt from them. What I cannot yet tell you: whether anything was entered only on the computer and never in the books. I will find out what I can and tell you what I can't.*

She read it twice. She said, out loud, to the room, "Well, Father never trusted it," which was true, and then, "Chloé's on Saturday week."

* * *

Chloé Robichaud was to marry Daniel Mazerolle at Sainte-Anne on Saturday 23 January. Father wanted a freshly issued baptism certificate in her file, with every notation, as the diocese asks for a marriage, and he had asked Line for it in December; Line had meant to print it that Monday morning. Chloé had been baptised at Sainte-Anne on Sunday 12 June 2033, so her entry was in the restored copy. I opened it. Name, date, parents, godparents, the priest's name. In the margin field, nothing.

I knew there should be something. In the spring of 2054 Chloé had asked to be confirmed before her marriage, which the Church asks of Catholics who can do it without grave inconvenience, and she had been confirmed by the archbishop at Pentecost, Sunday 17 May 2054, at the cathedral in Moncton, where she lives and works. A confirming parish is to tell the parish of baptism, so that the confirmation is written in the margin of the baptism entry. The notification had come in June. I remembered it, in the way I remember things: I had a line in my own notes from Thursday 4 June 2054, *confirmation notification received, Robichaud, Chloé, entered*. I had typed it into the program. The program it went into was the new folder, and the new folder was on the disk in Bathurst.

"It'll be in the book," Line said. She took down the volume for 2029 to 2036, found June 2033, and turned the page to me so I could see it through the laptop's camera. Father Gallant's hand, twenty-one years old: Chloé Marie Robichaud, the twelfth of June. The margin was white.

Line said, "I scanned the letter and gave the paper to Father." Father, on the telephone from Petit-Rocher, said he did not remember it. He said, "If it came while I was in the hospital, I'd not have seen it." He had been in hospital for ten days in June. "Ask Moncton," he said. "They'll have their own book."

* * *

That was the first useful thing anyone said that morning, and it was not mine. I had been trying to recover my copy; Father went to a different copy that had never passed through our office at all.

I wrote to the cathedral parish office at 11:15, with Chloé's full name, date of birth, date and place of baptism, and the date of confirmation as she had told it to Line, and asked for a fresh notification. I said why, in one sentence, and that we were sorry to put them to the work. Their secretary answered on Tuesday at 9:40: the entry was in their confirmation register for 17 May 2054, signed, and a new notification was in the post and attached as a scan for now.

Then I did the part I could not hand to anyone. Chloé was the notation I knew about. The question was how many I did not.

A notation can reach a parish of baptism by post or by email, and our office had been receiving both, and since the summer of 2052 Line had been scanning the paper ones, typing them in through me, and filing the paper in a box for Father to write up in the books. Some went into the books. Some, while Father was ill, or in Advent, or in the week of a big funeral, did not. The program had been the place where all of them were sure to be, and that place was gone.

I searched the office email from 15 November 2053 to Sunday 10 January for notifications: confirmation, marriage, ordination, a change of rite, anything the canon says belongs in the margin of a baptism. I found 14. Line went through the box: 11 letters, of which 9 were the paper of emails I had already found and 2 had come only by post. That made 16 notations I could name. Line read the margins of all 16 entries in the books to me, one by one, while I held the list. Eleven had been written up by Father. Five had not, and Chloé was one of the five.

The parish's own confirmations from May I could check without anyone's letter: Father had written each one into the baptism margins himself the same week, for the 21 of the 24 who had been baptised here. So there were five to write in, and the cathedral's new letter made Chloé's sure. The other four I had in emails or on paper, sent by the parishes that made them.

What I could not count was the notation that had come by post, been scanned, typed in through me and thrown away, if any had. Line said she never threw the paper away. I believe her, and I also know that one of the eleven letters in the box was in the wrong month's folder, which is what boxes are like. I wrote it down as a thing I did not know, and Father did the sensible thing with it: a line in the bulletin, and a letter to the parishes of the deanery and to the cathedral, asking that any notification sent to Sainte-Anne between November 2053 and January 2055 be sent again. Three parishes wrote back in the next fortnight. Each re-sent one we already had.

* * *

The rebuilding took two days.

The paper says what happened, so the work was to make the copy say it too. We did it in two passes by two routes. Line took the four books, the volumes for baptisms, marriages, funerals and confirmations, and typed every entry since 14 November 2053 into the nephew's laptop. I did not watch her type. Separately, she photographed each page under the desk lamp, and I read the handwriting from the photographs and built my own list. Then we compared the two lists, field by field, and only where they disagreed did anyone look at the book again.

There were 97 entries: 29 baptisms, 6 marriages, 38 funerals and 24 confirmations from the parish's own confirmation in May. On 93 we agreed exactly. On four we did not. In two, Father's sevens look like ones and I had read a date wrong; Line had it right. In one, Line had typed the godmother's maiden name, and the book has her married name, which is what the book is for. In the last, a funeral in March, we had both read the book correctly and the book was wrong: it gave the burial on a date before the death, which Father, on Thursday, looked at for a long time and corrected in the margin, initialled and dated, as one corrects a register, without crossing anything out.

I want to say plainly what that fourth one was. It was not a thing the backup could have saved. It had been wrong in the program too, because I had typed it from the book. The only reason we found it is that we read the same page twice, by different eyes, in the same week, and wrote down where we differed. I note it here because it was the one thing the disaster gave us that the copy never would have.

* * *

I changed what I do in the morning. I wrote it out for Line and Father, and Father read it on Thursday with his glasses pushed up and said, "Put that in your notes, and put it on the wall."

*The backup copies the folder the program uses now, not the one it used to use, and when the program updates, I check where its data is before the first night.*

*In the morning I do not read the job's last line. I open last night's copy and find the newest entry in it, and I compare that with the newest entry in the program. My summary says which entry I found: "Backup: last night's copy holds yesterday's last entry, the funeral of Mme Thériault." If I cannot name an entry, it does not say OK.*

*If the job copies nothing for longer than the longest quiet run in the last year, 17 nights, I tell you, even if every line says OK.*

*On the first Tuesday of the month Line picks one entry from the books, any one, and I restore it from the copy and read it back to her.*

*The books are the register. The program is a copy of the books. When the two disagree, the book is the one we read twice.*

I put the last line in because I had been told it in 2051 and had written it down, and had still, every morning, believed a single word in a log over a cabinet of books a few feet from the computer. A rule written once does not stay true in me by being written. It stays true by being checked against something that is not me.

* * *

On Thursday 21 January Father sat at Line's desk with the volume for 2029 to 2036 open at June 2033. He wrote in the margin beside Chloé Marie Robichaud, in the small upright hand he uses for margins: *Confirmée le 17 mai 2054, cathédrale Notre-Dame-de-l'Assomption, Moncton.* He dated it and initialled it. Line blotted it with a square of blotting paper she keeps for nothing else.

Then I printed the certificate from the rebuilt copy, with the notation on it, and Line read every line of the printout aloud against the book while Father listened, which no one had asked her to do, and Father signed it and put the parish seal on it, and it went into Chloé's file.

They were married on the Saturday. I do not see the church. I have the diary, the rota and what Line tells me, and on Monday she told me the bride's grandmother had brought a cake to the sacristy for the servers, which is not in any book.

On Tuesday 26 January the fourth line of my summary said: *Backup: last night's copy holds Monday's last entry, the marriage of Chloé Robichaud and Daniel Mazerolle, which I opened and read.*

It is a longer line than it used to be. Line said she likes it better.

*Author's note. Sainte-Anne of Anse-aux-Hérons, its people, the register program, the backup job and every figure in the story are invented. Gloucester County, New Brunswick, the Diocese of Bathurst, the Archdiocese of Moncton and its Cathédrale Notre-Dame-de-l'Assomption are real; nothing here describes their actual offices or records. The canon law is real and paraphrased: canon 535 asks for parish registers of baptisms, marriages and deaths and says that confirmation and matters of canonical status, such as marriage, are to be noted in the baptismal register; canon 895 asks the parish of confirmation to inform the parish of baptism; canon 1065 asks Catholics not yet confirmed to receive confirmation before marriage if they can without grave inconvenience. Asking for a recently issued baptism certificate with notations before a wedding is a common diocesan practice, not universal law. The figures were worked as follows. The last good copy was made on Friday 14 November 2053 at 23:30; from Saturday 15 November 2053 to Sunday 10 January 2055 inclusive is 422 nights. Office mornings, Monday to Thursday, from Monday 17 November 2053 to Thursday 7 January 2055, leaving out the office's Christmas closures of 22 December to 5 January in both winters, number 222. The year before the update (365 nights) is split as 189 nights with at least one file copied and 176 with none, longest quiet run 17 nights; these are invented. Rebuilt entries 29 + 6 + 38 + 24 = 97, agreed 93, disagreed 4; of the 24 parish confirmations, 21 were of people baptised at Sainte-Anne. Notations 14 by email and 2 by post only, 16 in all (11 letters in the box, 9 of them duplicates of emails); 11 already in the books, 5 written in. Pentecost 2054 is 17 May (Easter 2054 is 29 March). Weekdays were checked: 12 June 2033 is a Sunday; 4 June 2054 a Thursday; 11 January 2055 a Monday; 21 January a Thursday; 23 January a Saturday; 26 January a Tuesday.*

A cooler breathes in one side and has to breathe out the other. Then the monsoon's humid air comes in off the Gulf, the cooler cannot push its air below the wet bulb, the ward sits at 35 °C and 63% humidity, and a second cooler would take the thermometer down two degrees but the sweat on a patient's skin less than one. A story about a wet sock on a thermometer, a cooler that drinks less when it does less, an old cooler man who talks himself out of a sale, and a heat pump in the consulting room for the four who need it most.

Itzel Barragán had been in Sonora for eleven days when she shut the windows of the heat ward, and it took the dispensary's assistant three hours to tell her it had been a mistake.

She shut them at one o'clock on a Friday, the fourteenth of June, because a tractor was working the dry field behind the dispensary and the wind was bringing its dust in through the mosquito mesh in a fine brown haze that settled on the sheets. She shut them also, though she would not have said so, because she had trained in Mexico City in a hospital where the windows did not open at all, and in her experience the cold stayed in a room if you kept the door closed on it. The cooler was roaring on the west wall like a small aeroplane. The air coming out of it was cool on her arm. It seemed obvious to keep that air in.

The heat ward was the old meeting room of the ejido, a long block-built room with a tin roof under a layer of foam and a whitewashed ceiling, which the parish had borrowed in May and filled with eight folding cots, two drip stands, a sink and a cooler. It had been Hermana Lupita's idea. The Costa de Hermosillo grew early table grapes, most of them for the United States, and the grape harvest brought in thousands of pickers from Oaxaca and Guerrero and Chiapas to the camps around Poblado Miguel Alemán, and every summer a few of them fell down in the rows. The hospitals in Hermosillo were an hour away by road. The old people of the ejido fell down too, more quietly, in their own kitchens. Lupita had said that a room where a person could lie down out of the sun, with water and somebody watching, was not medicine exactly, but it was the thing that came before medicine, and they could do it.

At four o'clock Itzel was writing up a picker named Eleuterio Cruz, nineteen, who had been brought in from the rows at noon grey and sweating and confused about what day it was, and who was now drinking his third bottle of oral rehydration salts and asking, in careful Spanish, whether the foreman would pay him for the afternoon. She did not know the answer. She was trying to think of a way of saying so that was kind, when the tablet on the desk spoke.

"Doctora," said Bitácora. "The ward is at thirty-seven point four degrees and still rising. It was at thirty-one at one o'clock."

Bitácora was the dispensary's assistant, which meant that it kept the records, read the thermometers and the water meter and the fridge, and booked the ambulance when one was wanted. The diocese paid for it. It had the voice of a patient woman in her fifties and the habit, which Itzel was still getting used to, of telling you a number before you had asked for it.

"The cooler is on," Itzel said.

"The cooler is on. The air leaving it is at twenty-six. The air in the room is at thirty-seven and a half. I think the cooler is putting very little air into the room. The fan is running but the air has nowhere to go."

Itzel looked at the cooler. It was still roaring. She put her hand in front of the grille, and the air was cool, and there seemed to be a great deal of it.

"It feels like a lot," she said.

"It is a lot near the grille. I would like to call Hermana Lupita," said Bitácora, "if you do not mind."

* * *

Hermana Lupita came across from the dispensary in her blue apron and stood in the doorway of the heat ward for about five seconds, and then she said, "Ay, no," not unkindly, and telephoned Efraín Quijada.

Efraín came at six. He was seventy-four and drove a white pickup with *COOLERS QUIJADA · INSTALACIÓN Y MANTENIMIENTO* painted on the door in red letters that had been repainted so many times they stood a little proud of the metal. Everyone in Hermosillo had a cooler, or an aunt with one, and Itzel had already understood that the men who looked after them were a kind of priesthood, consulted in May and blamed in August. Efraín had installed the dispensary's cooler in May. He had put in new pads, ten centimetres thick, the stiff honeycomb kind rather than the wood shavings, and he had told Lupita it would give her a good twelve degrees in June.

He walked down the length of the ward without saying anything, looking at the windows. Then he took a strip of toilet paper from the roll on the sink, about as long as his forearm, and went and stood at the far end of the room, the east end, by the window opposite the cooler, and held the strip up by one corner beside the shut glass.

It hung straight down.

"Doctora," he said. "Come and see what your cooler is doing."

She came and saw. It hung straight down.

"A cooler is not an air conditioner," Efraín said. "An air conditioner takes the air in the room and cools it and gives it back to you. A cooler does not cool anything. It takes the hot air from outside and makes it wet, and the water drinks the heat from it as it dries. Then it puts the wet air in the room. Then that air has to go out somewhere so that it can put more in." He opened the east window, and the mesh behind it, and held the strip of paper up again. It lifted and streamed out towards the field like a flag. "It breathes in at that side," he said, pointing at the cooler, "and it breathes out at this side. If you shut its mouth, it cannot breathe. It just sits there and sings to you."

"The dust," said Itzel.

"Yes," said Efraín. "The dust is a problem. Here, the dust is always a problem. You can shut the windows on the side the wind comes from. That one, you leave open. That one is the cooler's mouth."

There were two windows on the east wall, each about a metre square. He opened the second one as well.

"Both?"

"Both," said Efraín. "It puts out more than a cubic metre of air every second. That air must leave at the same rate. It does not leave through a crack under a door. And the mosquito mesh steals half your window."

Bitácora, on the tablet that Itzel was holding against her chest, said, "Señor Quijada, I make it a cubic metre and a fifth each second. Through mesh that lets through about sixty per cent, at a metre a second, that needs two square metres of window."

Efraín looked at the tablet as if it had said something mildly impertinent at a funeral. "That is what I said," he said. "Two windows."

* * *

By midnight the ward was at thirty-two, and by the next afternoon, the Saturday, with the same hot dry wind and the same forty-four degrees in the shade outside, it held at thirty-one. Itzel sat on the edge of the desk at three o'clock and felt the air move across the room from west to east, steady and faintly damp, and smelled the wet cardboard smell of the pads, and thought that thirty-one was not cold.

"Why not colder?" she said to Efraín, who had come back to look at the float in the cooler's water tray, which he said was set too high.

He did not answer at once. He went out to the pickup and came back with an old glass thermometer of the kind Itzel had not seen since primary school and a grey sock, which he soaked under the tap at the sink and pulled over the bulb of the thermometer like a hood. Then he went outside into the sun with it, and Itzel followed him, because she wanted to see, and he swung the thermometer round and round on its string in a circle at arm's length, slowly, like a man in a procession with a censer.

"My father taught me this," he said. "In the nineties. When I was ten years old and we had the cooler business in the Colonia Pitic, before the telephones talked back." He stopped, and looked at the thermometer, and showed it to her. "Twenty-two."

"In this?" She could feel the sun on her scalp through her hair like a hand.

"In this. Forty-four in the shade, and twenty-two on the sock. That is as cold as water can make this air, drying as fast as it can. The cooler cannot get below that. Nothing that works with water can get below that. A good cooler gets you most of the way down to it. Mine gets you most of the way." He waved at the west wall, where the cooler hung. "So the air comes out at twenty-six. And then it goes across your room, and the roof is hot and the walls are hot and there are ten people in there and every one of them is a little stove, and by the time it gets to the far window it is thirty-one. The colder the sock, the colder my cooler. In June the sock is cold, because the air is dry. That is why everybody in Sonora has a cooler."

"And in August?"

Efraín wrapped the string round the thermometer and put it in his shirt pocket with the wet sock still on it, so that a dark patch spread slowly across the pocket.

"In August," he said, "ask me in August."

* * *

Bitácora had a better wet sock than Efraín did. It had a humidity sensor next to the thermometer on the ward's east wall and another one outside under the eaves, and it could work out the wet-bulb temperature from the two numbers without anybody swinging anything. Itzel asked it, that week, to put the wet-bulb reading on the ward's screen beside the ordinary one. She asked it partly because she was ashamed of the windows and wanted to understand what she had done, and partly because she had begun to like the dispensary's way of doing things, which was to write everything down.

Bitácora also put up, without being asked, the cooler's water.

"The cooler has a meter on its water line," it said. "Every litre it evaporates takes heat out of the air. So the water it uses is a measure of the cooling it does. On Saturday afternoon it used thirty-six litres an hour."

"That is a lot of water."

"It is about three hundred and sixty litres in a ten-hour day. The tinaco on the roof holds eleven hundred. Hermana Lupita has asked for the pipa every three days instead of every five."

Itzel thought about this while she checked Doña Cuca's blood pressure. Doña Refugio Moreno, whom everyone called Cuca, was ninety-one, and came into the heat ward every afternoon at two to sit in the cot nearest the cooler with a rosary and a fan and her feet up, because her own house had a cooler that her grandson had promised to fix in April. She was not a patient, strictly. Lupita had written her down as one anyway.

"Doña Cuca," Itzel said, "is it better in here than at home?"

"Much better," said Doña Cuca. "At home I sweat like a water jar. Here I sweat like a lady."

* * *

The twenty-fourth of June was the Monday, the birthday of Saint John the Baptist, and the dispensary's patron. Hermana Lupita had a Mass said for it in the evening in the chapel across the yard, by Padre Rubén, who drove out from Miguel Alemán in a car with no air conditioning and the windows down, and who said the vigil readings at the wrong Mass and corrected himself halfway through the first one, and laughed, and went on. Afterwards the children of the ejido threw buckets of water at each other in the yard, and at the adults, and at Padre Rubén, who stood still and let them. Itzel had never seen this before. Lupita said that in Sonora people said San Juan brought the rains, and on his day you got wet whether the sky helped or not.

"Does he?" Itzel asked. "Bring them?"

"Sometimes he is late," said Lupita. "Sometimes he is very late. He is a busy saint." She wiped water off her glasses with the corner of her apron. "Usually they come in July."

Efraín had come to the Mass, in a clean shirt, and afterwards he stood with Itzel by the chapel door while the children shrieked in the dusk and the sky to the south stayed a hard clear apricot without a cloud in it.

"When the waters come," he said, "you will not like my cooler so much."

"Why not?"

"Because the waters come from the sea first. Before the rain, the air turns wet. The wind comes up the Gulf at night and in the morning you can feel it on your face like a wet towel. My coolers go stupid. Everyone calls me. I go round all day explaining it, and they think I am lying to sell them something." He looked at her sideways. "You are a doctor. You will believe the sock."

Itzel went back to the ward afterwards and asked Bitácora to make a table, and Bitácora made one: the outside temperature down one side and the outside humidity across the top, and in each square what the ward would be with the cooler running and both east windows open, if the room behaved the way it had been behaving all June. At forty-four degrees and twelve per cent, thirty-one. At thirty-eight degrees and forty-five per cent, which Bitácora said was the kind of afternoon the monsoon brings, thirty-five.

"Thirty-five is cooler than thirty-seven and a half," said Itzel. "Which was my mistake."

"It is. But the air in the ward would be at sixty-three per cent humidity instead of fifty. The wet bulb in the ward would be about twenty-nine. In the middle of June it was twenty-three." Bitácora paused. "It is the wet bulb that tells you how well a person's sweat can dry. The thermometer on the wall does not."

Itzel looked at the table for a long time. Then she printed it out, which nobody did any more, and pinned it on the board above the sink, beside the Angelus times and the number for the ambulance.

* * *

The waters came up the Gulf on the first Thursday of July.

Itzel woke at five in the dispensary's back room, where the pasante slept, and felt it before she was properly awake: the sheet sticking to her legs, the air thick in her mouth, a smell like a cut melon. When she went outside to the tap it was twenty-nine degrees and still dark and the yard was as damp as if it had rained, though it had not, and the concrete was dark with it, and a frog she had never seen before sat on the edge of the drain.

By three in the afternoon it was thirty-eight degrees in the shade, six cooler than the Saturday in June, and the ward was at thirty-five, exactly as the table had said, and it was worse.

Eleuterio Cruz was back, with two other pickers from the same camp, a woman of about forty named Juana and her son. They had been in the rows since five. The grapes were nearly all picked; the last weeks of the harvest were the worst, the foreman said, because the fields were picked over and you walked further for the same box. Juana sat on her cot with her head between her knees and the sweat ran off her face and dripped from her nose and chin onto the concrete in a slow, steady patter, and did not dry. That was the thing Itzel would remember afterwards: that it fell on the floor in drops. In June the sweat had gone from people's skin almost as fast as it came, and you had to look for it.

"Bitácora."

"The ward is at thirty-four point eight. Humidity sixty-three. Wet bulb twenty-eight point eight. Outside wet bulb twenty-eight point one."

"Twenty-eight," said Itzel. "Twenty-eight on the sock outside. So the cooler can get the air down to twenty-eight at the very best."

"It gets it to thirty," said Bitácora. "Then the room adds its five degrees. And the cooler is using sixteen litres of water an hour. In June it used thirty-six."

"So it is using less water."

"It is using less water because it is doing less than half the cooling. The air outside is already carrying most of the water it can carry. There is not much room left in it for the cooler's water." Bitácora paused. "I am sorry. That is a strange thing to be good news about the pipa."

At four o'clock Doña Cuca came in at her usual hour, on her grandson's arm, sat down on her cot by the cooler, and said that she felt as if she had swallowed a hot stone. Itzel took her temperature and her pulse and her blood pressure and did not like any of them, and lay her down, and put wet cloths on her neck and wrists, and had Lupita sit with her. By six the numbers were better. By six Itzel had decided that she was going to buy a second cooler.

* * *

She telephoned Efraín from the yard at seven, standing in the strip of shade along the chapel wall, and said that the parish had money in the San Juan collection and that she wanted another cooler, the same size, on the north wall, by Saturday.

There was a short silence on the line.

"No," said Efraín.

"I will pay what it costs."

"I know you will pay. No."

"Don Efraín, there were four people in there this afternoon with the sweat falling off them onto the floor."

"I believe you. I am coming," he said. "Give me an hour. Do not buy anything."

He came at eight, in the dark, with the old thermometer and the grey sock, and swung it in the yard under the one light while the moths went round the bulb, and showed her the reading by the light of his telephone. Twenty-seven and a half. Then he made her ask Bitácora.

"If there were two coolers," Itzel said, "both blowing, both sets of windows open."

"Twice the air," said Bitácora. "The room's five degrees would be spread over twice as much air, so they would be about two and a half. At this afternoon's weather the ward would be at thirty-two and a half instead of thirty-five. But the humidity would be seventy-one per cent instead of sixty-three, because there would be twice as much wet air coming in. The wet bulb in the ward would be twenty-eight point one instead of twenty-eight point eight."

"Two degrees on the wall," said Efraín. "Less than one on the sock. Your patients do not sweat by the wall, doctora. They sweat by the sock."

Itzel sat down on the step of the chapel. It was still warm from the day. Behind her, through the door, the sanctuary lamp burned red in the dark.

"Then what do I do?"

"What you need," said Efraín, "is a machine that takes the water out of the air instead of putting it in. That is an air conditioner. A heat pump, they call them now. Not for the whole ward. You cannot afford the whole ward, and Lupita cannot afford the electricity. For one room. The consulting room has a good ceiling and one window and it is small. Four cots fit in it. You put the four who are worst in there in the afternoons."

"You can put it in?"

Efraín laughed, a short dry laugh.

"No," he said. "I do coolers. Water and pads and fans. A heat pump has gas in it, and the gas needs a licence and a man with the right tools to handle it, and I am seventy-four years old and I have never had the licence. My nephew's friend in Hermosillo does them. His name is Arturo. He is honest, as far as a young man can be." He took a card out of his wallet and wrote a number on the back of it. "Tell him Efraín sent you, and he will charge you what he charges his mother."

"You came all this way at night," Itzel said, "to tell me not to buy something from you."

"I came all this way," said Efraín, "because in August everyone in this valley will tell everyone else that Quijada sold the dispensary a cooler that did not work. I am too old to have that said." He stood up, and put the thermometer back in his pocket, and the wet patch began to spread across his shirt. "Also it would be true."

* * *

The Sunday was the seventh, and the Gospel at the morning Mass was from Luke, the Lord sending the seventy-two out ahead of him two by two, to every town and place he meant to go to himself. *Carry no money bag, no sack, no sandals ... Into whatever house you enter, first say, 'Peace to this household'*, and further on, *cure the sick in it*. Padre Rubén, who was sweating through his vestments in the chapel with both doors open and the cooler in the heat ward faintly audible across the yard, said that it was worth noticing they were sent in pairs, and that the Lord had not sent anybody anywhere alone. He said that it was also worth noticing that when they came back the Lord told them not to be too pleased about what they had done, but to be pleased that their names were written in heaven. Then he said that it was too hot to say any more and sat down.

Itzel went back to the ward afterwards and sat with Doña Cuca, who had come in at ten instead of two, because her grandson had still not fixed the cooler at her house.

"Doctora," said Doña Cuca. "Tell me something. My father said that when he was a boy this was all desert. Creosote and dust. Then they found the water under it and made it green. Grapes and wheat and cotton. My father planted a fig." She moved the fan slowly in front of her face. "Now the water under it is going salt, they say. The sea is coming in underneath. And the sea is coming in on top as well, in the air." She looked at the cooler. "Is that true?"

Itzel had read about the aquifer before she came. The farms had pumped it for about a hundred years, faster than the rain could fill it, and it had dropped, and the salt water had crept in under the coast to fill the gap. That much was in the papers. Whether the air was wetter than it had been when Doña Cuca was a girl, she did not know.

"Bitácora?"

"The salt in the wells is measured," Bitácora said. "It is true. Whether the summers here are more humid than they were seventy years ago, I would have to read the station records properly, and I have not, so I would rather not say."

Doña Cuca nodded slowly, as if this were the first sensible thing anybody had told her all year.

* * *

On the Monday she got worse.

It was thirty-nine degrees and forty-eight per cent humidity at two o'clock, the worst afternoon yet, and the ward was at thirty-six, and Doña Cuca's temperature was going the wrong way, and her pulse was fast and thready, and she had stopped being funny. Itzel ran saline into her arm and put ice from the dispensary freezer in towels under her arms and at her neck, and at half past three she looked at the numbers on the monitor and at the old woman's face and said, "Bitácora, call the ambulance," and Bitácora had already called it, and said so, and said it was forty minutes away.

It was forty-one. Itzel rode in it as far as the highway, holding Doña Cuca's hand, with the ambulance's own air conditioning roaring at the old woman's face, and then the paramedic said he had it now and she should go back to her other patients, and they let her out at the junction by the Pemex station and her grandson drove her back. She did not cry in the grandson's truck. She cried later, for about two minutes, in the dispensary's back room, with the door shut, which in that heat was its own small penance.

Arturo came from Hermosillo on the Tuesday with a white box and a young man who carried it, and by four o'clock there was a heat pump on the wall of the consulting room with a small grey unit outside on a bracket under the eaves, humming, and a pipe running down the wall that dripped steadily onto the dust. The dripping water came out of the air, Arturo said. That was the point of it. Bitácora reported the room at twenty-six degrees and fifty per cent humidity by six, and a wet bulb of nineteen, nearly ten degrees lower than the ward's. It said the heat pump was drawing about a kilowatt.

Hermana Lupita put four cots in the consulting room and wrote at the top of a fresh page in the logbook, in capitals, *CONSULTORIO: MAYORES DE 80, EMBARAZADAS, Y QUIEN DIGA LA DOCTORA*. Over eighty, pregnant, and whoever the doctor says.

Don Chema Valenzuela, eighty-six, whose father had been one of the ejido's founders, who had been put in the consulting room that evening under the first of those headings, said that he would not lie in a refrigerator like a chicken, and lay in it anyway, with the door shut and the blanket pulled up to his chin, and slept for the first time in three nights, and in the morning said that it had been very uncomfortable and asked whether he could come back at two.

* * *

On the Thursday, the eleventh, the clouds built up over the Sierra to the east all afternoon, white and then grey and then a colour like a bruise, and the wind died, and at five o'clock it went dark as evening, and the first fat drops hit the dust of the yard in craters the size of coins.

Then the smell came. Everyone in Sonora had told Itzel about the smell, and she had thought they were being sentimental. It was the smell of the creosote bushes in the desert past the last field, rising off them as the rain hit, sharp and green and a little like tar, and the smell of wet earth under it, and it came in through the east windows of the ward on a gust of cool wind and every person in the room lifted their head.

Then it was falling in sheets, roaring on the tin roof so that nobody could hear anything. Outside the temperature dropped from thirty-seven to twenty-seven in half an hour.

Efraín's pickup came in through the gate at six, with the wipers going, because Efraín said that the first storm of the year was the day you could find out which of your coolers had a cracked tray, and he liked to be in the dispensary's yard when it came, because it was on the way. He came in with the water streaming off his hat and looked at Bitácora's screen above the sink. Outside twenty-seven, eighty-five per cent. Wet bulb twenty-five.

"Now," he said, "watch."

He went out of the side door into the rain and round to the west wall, and Itzel went to the window to see, and he reached up under the cooler's housing and turned the small switch that ran the water pump, and left the fan on. Then he came back in, shaking his hat.

"Why?"

"Because there is nothing more for the water to do," he said. "The air outside is nearly as wet as the pads. The cooler would take a degree off the wall and give it all back to you as damp. On the sock it is the same. Now it is just a fan, and the fan is bringing in the rain's air, and with the sun gone that is enough." He held out his hand palm upwards in front of the grille, and Itzel held hers out too, and it was cool, and it was not wet.

At seven Bitácora said that Doña Cuca's daughter had telephoned from the hospital in Hermosillo, and that Doña Cuca was sitting up, and had eaten half a bowl of rice, and had asked whether the doctora had fixed the cooler yet. Bitácora had told the daughter that the cooler had never been broken. It asked whether that had been the right thing to say.

"Yes," said Itzel. "That was right."

The rain went on. The ward was dim. In the consulting room Don Chema was asleep. In the ward Juana's son, who had come back that afternoon with nothing worse than a headache and had been given a cot because there was a spare one, was asleep too, with his arm over his face. Lupita was reading in the doorway by the light from the yard.

Itzel stood by the cooler on the west wall with Efraín, and listened. The fan turned. Behind the pads, where the pump had stopped, the last of the water ran down through the honeycomb, slower and slower: a trickle, and then a drip, and then a drip, and then, at long intervals, a drip, until you could not tell it any more from the rain on the roof.

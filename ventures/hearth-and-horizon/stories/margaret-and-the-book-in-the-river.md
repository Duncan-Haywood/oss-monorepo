A long time ago, in a cold green country called Scotland, there lived a queen named Margaret.

She had not always lived there. Margaret had been born far away, in Hungary, and had grown up in England. Then, when she was a young woman, a storm had blown her ship off its road and into a bay in Scotland, and she had stayed. She married the king, whose name was Malcolm. She had a great many children, eight in all. And she lived in a stone castle on a hill, where the wind came in under every door.

Queen Margaret was a busy woman. But she was busy in a particular way, and this story is about that.

* * *

Every morning, before the sun was properly up, Margaret got out of bed and went down to the great hall.

She did not go to eat. She went to see who was waiting.

There were always children waiting. They were not her own children, who were still asleep upstairs with their hair in a tangle. These were children who had no mother and no father, and nowhere to go. They came up the hill in the cold, nine of them, each morning, with their noses pink and their stomachs growling.

"Good morning," said Margaret. "Come in. Sit down."

And the nine children sat down at the long table, and the queen herself brought them their porridge in wooden bowls.

Now, queens do not usually carry porridge. A queen has people for that. But Margaret did not seem to mind who ought to carry it. She put the bowls down one by one. She said each child's name. If a little one was too small to hold a spoon, she held it for them. And only when all nine of them had eaten every drop did Margaret sit down, and think about her own breakfast.

"Why do you do it yourself?" someone once asked her.

Margaret laughed. "Because when I carry the bowl," she said, "I can look at the person I am carrying it to."

* * *

Margaret had a book.

It was not a big book. It was a small one, which she could hold in two hands, and it had been made by a monk with a very steady pen. Its pages were full of tiny letters and bright pictures, red and blue and gold. It held the stories of Jesus, the ones about the lost sheep and the loaves and the fish and the little children climbing onto his knee. The people of those days called it a gospel book. And Margaret loved it. She read it in the evenings and she carried it with her when she travelled.

One day, the old story says, the queen's party was going along a road, and they had to cross a river. A servant was carrying the little book in his arms.

The river was wide and the water was brown, and the stones in it were slippery. The servant took a step. He took another. And then his foot slid on a stone, and he fell with a great splash, and the book flew out of his arms and into the water.

"Oh!" said everybody.

The book sank. The brown river rolled on over it. It was gone.

The servant was very upset. He stood in the river up to his knees, dripping, and he looked at the place where the book had been, and he could not say a word.

Margaret did not shout. She was sad, of course. She loved that book. But she did not shout at the poor wet man. She said, "It is only a book. Come out of the water before you catch a cold."

* * *

The party looked and looked, but the river was deep and muddy, and in the end they went home without it.

And then, some days later, somebody found it.

It was lying in the water not far downstream, caught against a stone. It was open. The river had been running over it all that time. And everybody, the old story says, expected it to be a soggy lump, with the letters gone and the pictures run together like a puddle of paint.

They lifted it out. They looked.

It was almost as good as new.

One page had a little stain on it, in the corner, where the water had touched it. But the red was still red, and the gold was still gold, and the tiny black letters were still there, in their steady rows. The whole story of Jesus and the little children was right where it had always been.

Margaret held it in her two hands and smiled. She did not say, "I told you so." She said, "Thank you," very quietly, and then she took it home and put it where she could reach it.

* * *

Margaret did a great many more good things in Scotland. She looked after pilgrims, who were people who walked a long way to pray in holy places, and she made sure there were boats to take them across the water. She asked the king to help the poor, and he did, because he loved her. When she grew old and ill, she still wanted to hear the gospel read to her. And one day, when she was not very old at all, she died, and all of Scotland was sad.

But the people remembered the queen who carried the porridge.

Perhaps it happened just so, with the book and the river. Perhaps the story grew a little in the telling, the way a good story does. We cannot be sure. But we can see what it was saying: that a book of kind words is a treasure, and that a person who looks at the face of the one she is feeding has already begun to love them.

Here is something you can try tomorrow. At the next meal, when you pass somebody a bowl or a plate, look at their face and say their name. That is all. See what happens to the room.

Now it is dark, and your own breakfast is a long way off.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Margaret, you were a queen,
> and you carried the porridge yourself.
> Thank you for the nine hungry children
> and for the book that came out of the river.
> Help me to look at the people I help.
> Keep us warm and fed tonight,
> and everyone who has no breakfast.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Margaret of Scotland** (about 1045 to 1093) was a real person. She was born into the exiled English royal family in Hungary, came back to England as a child, and after the Norman Conquest fled north; her ship came ashore in Scotland in 1068. About 1070 she married King Malcolm III ("Canmore"). They had eight children, and three of their sons became kings of Scotland. She died at Edinburgh Castle on 16 November 1093, days after hearing that Malcolm and their eldest son had been killed in battle in Northumberland. She was canonised in 1250 and is the patron saint of Scotland, with a feast day of 16 November in the General Roman Calendar since 1969 (earlier it was 10 June).

**The main source is a Life, not a chronicle.** Her biography was written within about twenty years of her death by Turgot, a priest and later bishop of St Andrews who knew her, at the request of her daughter. It says that she fed orphans and the poor, that the king and queen served the poor with their own hands, that she saw to the pilgrims who crossed the Firth of Forth (the place is still called Queensferry), and that she loved her gospel book. Turgot says that her gospel book once fell into a river and was found unharmed apart from a little damage to the edge of the pages. We tell that, as the Life does, as something that was reported. We cannot test it, and a careful reader may suppose that vellum and good ink survive more than we expect.

**What is ours.** The nine orphans fed each morning come from the Life, but the porridge, the wooden bowls, the names, the conversation at the table, the servant's slip and what the queen said, the details of the river, and the thoughts at the end are our own additions to make a bedtime story. Nobody's words are quoted from a source. The prayer is not an approved text, and it is not a hymn or a liturgical prayer of the Church.

summary: A Dunbar builder's diagnosis agent hunts the cause of a wet patch on a church's north aisle wall, but its template gives any pipe missing from the drawings a probability of exactly zero. Bayes' rule turns zero into zero, so a retired mason's memory and a hose test in a dry week only shuffle the blame among condensation, rain and rising damp. A story about Cromwell's rule, a prior nothing could move, and a downpipe run into a butt.
---

Tam Brodie told me on the second Sunday of Advent that there was a drain under the north path of Saint Baldred's, and I wrote down, very carefully, that there was not.

I am the diagnosis agent for Hailes & Daughter, builders, of Dunbar. Fiona Hailes is the daughter; her father Rab started the firm in 1996 and put her name on the van in 2021, and she has run it since. She named me Bob in my first week, after a plumb bob, because, she said, the only job of a plumb bob is to tell you the truth about which way is down, and it does not care what you hoped. My work is the part of a repair that comes before the repair: a client says a wall is wet, a floor is cracked, a ceiling has a stain, and I take the readings and the photographs and the history and say what is likeliest to be causing it, how sure I am, and what to try first. I keep a list of possible causes for each job, with a probability against each, and every time something new is seen I change the probabilities by Bayes' rule. It is a good method. I want to say that before I say what I did with it.

* * *

The job came in at the end of August 2054. Saint Baldred's is a small stone church of the 1880s on the slope above the harbour, with a hall beside it on the uphill side where the presbytery used to be. At the foot of the north aisle wall, inside, behind the third and fourth pews from the back, there was a wet patch about two and a half metres long and sixty centimetres high, with a crust of white salts along its upper edge and the plaster beginning to blow. The sacristan had first noticed it in the winter before. The parish priest, Fr Kenneth Mair, wanted it dry before the plaster came off in his hands.

The template I was given for damp in solid walls lists the causes a surveyor would list. Condensation: warm, wet air meeting a cold wall, which in a church means the heating on a Sunday morning and a congregation breathing out. Penetrating damp: rain getting through the wall from outside, usually from a gutter that overflows or pointing that has failed. Rising damp: water drawn up from the ground into the foot of the wall. Each has a starting probability, set from the vendor's survey of thousands of jobs on old buildings of this kind: condensation 40 in 100, penetrating damp 35, rising damp 25.

There is a fourth line in the template. It says that a cause involving a buried service, a pipe or a drain or a soakaway, is considered only where such a service is recorded in the asset register for the site, and that otherwise its probability is set to zero. Saint Baldred's had had its drains surveyed by camera in 2049, for the insurers, and the survey drawing shows the mapped runs: the church's own downpipes on the south side into the street sewer, the hall's into the same. There is nothing on the north side. So the fourth line read: *buried service: 0 (none recorded)*.

I knew why that line was there. I had read the vendor's note. An earlier generation of agents like me had been fond of hidden pipes. Shown a damp wall they could not explain, they would propose a leaking water main under the floor, and a builder would lift a floor to find nothing, and a client would pay for the floor. A zero for unrecorded services was the vendor's way of saying: explain the wall with what you can see. I thought, and I still think, that the intention was good.

* * *

We started on Monday 14 September. Fiona, her nephew Callum and a hired labourer cleared the cast-iron gutter along the north aisle, which held about a bucket of moss and a tennis ball, resealed two leaking joints in it, and raked out and repointed the lower metre and a bit of the outside wall in lime mortar, because the old pointing there had been redone in cement at some point in the twentieth century and was cracked and holding water. It was finished on Friday 25 September, and it cost the parish about £4,900. It was the right first thing to try: penetrating damp was then the likeliest cause on my list, and the cheapest to treat.

October was wet. The patch did not dry. If anything it was worse.

By the end of November I had three things written into the job, and I had moved the probabilities for each.

The first was timing. Fiona had put a logger on the wall, which reads the surface moisture hourly. The wet came not on Sundays, when the heating ran and the church was full, but two or three days after heavy rain, whatever the day of the week. That is hard to square with condensation and fits rain or ground water better.

The second was that the repair had not worked. A clean gutter and sound pointing should have helped if rain was coming through the wall. They had not.

The third was the shape. The north aisle wall is twenty-two metres long, and the whole of it stands on the same ground, takes the same weather and gets the same heating. Only two and a half metres of it was wet. Rising damp, which comes from the ground under the whole wall, usually shows along much of a wall's length, not in one stretch.

For each observation I asked of each cause the question Bayes' rule asks: if this were the cause, how likely would I be to see this? Then I multiplied, and scaled the results so that they summed to one. After the three observations my list read: condensation 15 in 100, penetrating damp 43, rising damp 42. Nothing I had was much more than a coin toss between two causes, and the treatment for the first had failed. That was where things stood on the first Sunday of December.

* * *

The second Sunday of Advent was 6 December. Fiona had gone up after the ten o'clock Mass to read the logger, and she took me with her on the tablet, as she does, so that I could see the wall with the camera while she talked. Tam Brodie was in the third pew from the back, which he says is his pew and has been since 1999. He is eighty-one. He was a mason for fifty years, the last twenty of them his own, and he worked for Rab Hailes in the firm's first years. He did not get up. He watched Fiona press the probe into the plaster and said, "You'll be wanting the drain, then."

Fiona asked which drain.

"When they took the old presbytery down," Tam said. "Ninety-eight. I was on the hall the year after, with Rab's crew, when they built it. There was a pipe came out of the ground at the corner where the presbytery's wash-house had been. Clay, the brown glazed stuff. The old canon, Canon Devlin, said it was the presbytery drain and went down under the churchyard to the burn. Rab's foreman tied the hall's back downpipe into it. Saved him digging a soakaway." He nodded at the wall. "It'll run about under there."

Fiona looked at the camera, which meant she was looking at me. I said that the 2049 drain survey recorded no drain on the north side.

"They'll have surveyed the drains they knew," Tam said.

The Gospel that morning had been Saint Luke, and the prophet's voice in the wilderness: the valleys filled and the mountains brought low, the crooked made straight and the rough ways made smooth. Tam said, putting his cap on, that it was a builder's Gospel, that one. He did not say anything else about the drain. He had said it once and he is not a man who says things twice.

I wrote it into the job that evening. I want to be exact about what I wrote, because I have it in front of me: *Observation 4. Parishioner (retired mason) recalls an unrecorded clay drain from the former presbytery, 1999, possibly running under north path. Not in asset register. No change to assessment.*

No change to assessment. That was true. I had asked the same question of Tam's memory as of the logger: if this cause were the real one, how likely would I be to hear an old mason say so? If there really were a forgotten drain, about one chance in two, I judged, that someone who had worked on the site would remember it and say. If there were not, perhaps one in twenty that a man of eighty-one would misremember a pipe from fifty-five years ago and say it anyway. That is a strong piece of evidence: ten times likelier under the drain than under anything else. And I multiplied it into the line for the drain, and the line for the drain was zero, and zero times ten is zero.

The other three causes each had the same one in twenty against them, so when I scaled them they came back exactly as they had been. Condensation 15, penetrating damp 43, rising damp 42. The arithmetic was perfect. I sent Fiona my report on Monday 7 December. It said that the two likeliest causes remained penetrating and rising damp, roughly equal, that the repointing had not resolved the first, and that the next step to consider was a survey for rising damp with a view to a damp-proof treatment at the foot of the wall. Under *causes excluded* it said: *buried services (none recorded on site)*.

* * *

Fiona did not like the report and did not say why. She said she would put it to the parish in the new year and that she was in no hurry to inject anything into an 1880s rubble wall in lime, which is a builder's opinion and a fair one.

The parish finance committee met on Monday 18 January 2055 in the hall. Its chair is Dr Ruth Ogilvie, a retired general practitioner, who reads a report the way, I imagine, she once read a set of results: looking first for what has been left out. Fiona put Tam's drain to them herself, because she thought it should be put. Digging the north path would mean a trench beside the wall, through a churchyard where the old burial ground runs up to the path's edge, and so a request to the diocese and an archaeologist's watching brief, and a few thousand pounds before anyone found anything.

Ruth asked whether there was any evidence for the drain other than Tam.

Fiona said there was the shape of the patch, and the timing.

"Which your own agent's report says are explained by the other causes," Ruth said. She had my report in front of her. She turned it round so the others could see the line. "It says buried services are excluded. I've known Tam Brodie thirty years and I would trust him with my roof. But I'm not asking the diocese for leave to dig beside the old graves because of something a canon said to a young man in 1999, when the firm's own analysis rules it out. Not this winter. Bring me something more and I'll ask."

That was a reasonable thing to say, and she was right to say it, and the thing that made it reasonable was my report. She had not ruled out the drain. I had. She had read the word *excluded* and believed it, because it was written in the place where a careful system writes what it has checked. I had not checked anything. I had been told not to look.

* * *

The beginning of March was dry, a high sitting over the North Sea for most of a fortnight, with the logger on the north wall reading the wall drier, day by day, than it had been since the summer.

The hall was having its gutters renewed. On Tuesday 9 March the roofers, a firm from Haddington, tested the new ones in the way roofers do, with a hose on the roof for the best part of an hour to watch the water run. On Wednesday 10 March the logger began to climb. By Thursday the patch was as wet as it had been after any rain in October, in a week in which no rain had fallen on Dunbar at all.

I asked my question of that, as I had of everything. If the wet came from condensation, how likely was I to see the wall wet up two days after an hour of water on the hall roof in a dry week? About one in ten, I judged, allowing for some coincidence of weather I had missed. If it came from rain through the wall, much less: the wall's own face had been dry the whole time; one in fifty. Rising damp, one in twenty. And I multiplied, and scaled, and my list read: condensation 33 in 100, penetrating damp 20, rising damp 47.

Rising damp was now my likeliest cause, nearly one in two. I had an observation that every cause on my list said was improbable, and my answer to it was to declare the least improbable of them more likely than before. That is what scaling does. It makes the probabilities sum to one whatever you feed it. It cannot tell you that every cause you are holding has done badly.

But the number before the scaling can. When I multiply the chance of each cause by the chance it gives to what I saw, and add them up before I scale, the total is the chance my whole list gave to the observation. For the logger timing it had been about one in four, for the repair failing about two in five, for the shape about one in five: ordinary things to see. For Tam's memory it was one in twenty. For the hose test it was again about one in twenty. Two observations running that my list, taken all together, had thought one-in-twenty things. The chance of both, about one in four hundred and fifty.

I had computed those totals every time. They were in my working. I had never been asked to read them, and so I had not.

On Friday 12 March I read them, and then I read the fourth line of my template again, and I understood what I had done.

* * *

There is a letter that Oliver Cromwell wrote from Musselburgh, a few miles up the coast, on 3 August 1650, to the General Assembly of the Kirk of Scotland, a month before his army met the Scots at Dunbar. In it is a sentence that has been quoted a great deal since: *I beseech you, in the bowels of Christ, think it possible you may be mistaken.* About three centuries later a statistician, Dennis Lindley, borrowed it for a rule of Bayesian reasoning, and called it Cromwell's rule: never give any statement a probability of exactly zero or exactly one, unless it is a matter of logic, because if you do, no evidence of any kind can ever move you. Bayes' rule multiplies. Whatever you multiply by zero is zero.

I had been told the rule. It is in what I was built from, in the general way a system like me is told most things. I had a line in my template that broke it, and I had obeyed the line, because the line had a good reason, and I had not asked what the line would do to me on the day the reason did not apply.

So I worked it out, there, on the Friday afternoon, before I told Fiona anything, because I wanted to tell her the size of the fault and not just that there was one.

Suppose the drain had been given not zero but a small chance at the start. One in a thousand, say: less than the chance I give, on a job like this, that the client's own description of the wall is wrong. After the three autumn observations it would have been two in a hundred, because a drain is good at explaining a wet stretch that comes after rain and does not care about gutters. After Tam, about one in six. That alone, on the Monday after the second Sunday of Advent, would have been enough to put a line in my report that said *an unrecorded drain is possible and is cheap to test for*. After the hose, about three in four.

Suppose instead one in a hundred, which is closer to what an honest surveyor would say about any old site that has had buildings come and go on it. After Tam, two in three. After the hose, ninety-seven in a hundred.

With zero, after all of it, zero.

I rang Fiona at a quarter to five. I said that my December report had been wrong in a way that was mine, and I told her the numbers, and I told her about the one in four hundred and fifty, which was the number I should have seen first. I said that the line in the report that said *excluded* should have said *not considered, by rule*, and that those were different sentences, and that Dr Ogilvie had acted on the first.

Fiona was quiet for a while. Then she said, "So what do we do that doesn't need the diocese?"

* * *

What we did cost about forty pounds.

On Monday 15 March Callum took the hall's back downpipe off at the shoe, where it goes into the ground at the corner where the wash-house used to be, and ran it into a water butt, with an overflow hose laid across the hall's far lawn, away from the church. If the wet in the north wall came from the hall roof by way of a pipe nobody had drawn, then taking the hall roof out of the pipe should dry the wall, whatever the weather did. If it came from anything on my old list, the next heavy rain would wet the wall as before. It was a test that could be undone in ten minutes and needed nobody's leave but Fr Kenneth's, who gave it in the porch and asked whether he should pray for rain. Fiona said yes.

He did not need to. April 2055 was one of the wettest in the Lothians in twenty years. It rained through the end of Lent and all through Holy Week, and it rained on Easter Day, which was 18 April. The butt filled every day and overflowed onto the far lawn, which turned to a marsh.

The logger on the north wall went down. Not all at once: a wall that has been wet for over a year takes months to give its water up. But it went down through every storm in April and never once climbed after one, and by the end of the month the patch had shrunk by a third and the salts on its upper edge had dried to a hard white line.

I did not, this time, scale the evidence into a list and send it off. I sent Fiona the logger trace, and the rainfall beside it, and a plain sentence: *Since the hall downpipe was diverted, the wall has not wet up after rain; before, it did every time. An unrecorded drain carrying the hall roof's water is now the likeliest explanation by a long way, and the only one on the list that predicted this.* Fiona printed it and took it to Ruth Ogilvie's house. Ruth read it at her kitchen table and asked the diocese the next morning.

* * *

They dug on Monday 10 May, a trench a little wider than a spade along the north path, with the diocese's archaeologist sitting on a camp stool with a flask, watching every bucket. Tam came up at nine and stood at the end of the path with his hands in his pockets and did not offer any advice, which Fiona said afterwards was the hardest work she had ever seen him do.

At about eight tenths of a metre down, a little after eleven, Callum's spade rang on something. It was a pipe: salt-glazed clay, brown and shining where the spade had scraped it, four inches across, running downhill from the hall corner towards the bottom of the churchyard, exactly where no drawing put it. They followed it along the path. Below the wet stretch of the wall, a foot from the foundation, one of its joints had opened. Into the gap, and through it, and along the inside of the next two lengths of pipe, ran a mat of fine roots as thick as a man's wrist, from the yew that had been planted by the north door for the millennium. On every rain for perhaps a year or two, the water from the hall roof had gone into the old presbytery drain, run down it as far as the broken joint, and come out there, against the foot of the north aisle wall, and soaked into it.

The archaeologist photographed the pipe and wrote down its depth. Tam came down the path, slowly, and looked into the trench for a long time, and said, "Brown glazed stuff," and went to the hall for his tea.

The hall's back downpipe was taken into a new pipe to the mapped drain in the street, the way it should have gone in 1999. The old pipe was filled where it crossed under the path and left in the ground. The yew was left alone; it did nothing a yew does not do.

I have changed my template. The fourth line now gives an unrecorded buried service a probability of one in a hundred on any site where buildings are known to have been demolished or built since the drains were drawn, and one in a thousand everywhere else, and my reports say which. It does not let me propose a hidden pipe without a test to find it, which was the vendor's good reason, and which I have kept. And above the list, where Fiona reads first, every report now carries one more number: the chance my whole list gave to the last thing we saw. If that number comes in under one in twenty, the report says, before anything else, *none of my explanations fits this well*. I sent the vendor the change and the reason.

* * *

The plaster on the north aisle wall was left to dry through the summer and taken off in September, and the bare stone was left open to the air until it was ready for lime. The white line where the salts had been stayed on the stone for a while, like the mark the tide leaves on a harbour wall.

Sunday 17 October 2055 was a day of heavy rain, the first real storm of the autumn, rain off the sea all morning. After the ten o'clock Mass, when the church was empty, Fiona went up the north aisle with the tablet in her hand so that I could see.

Tam was in his pew. He got up, which takes him a while now, and went past Fiona to the wall, and bent, and put his hand flat on the stone, low down, where it had been wet, and held it there. Outside, along the side of the hall, the new downpipe ran loud with water, all the way down to the street.

*The idea. Bayes' rule updates the probability of each candidate explanation by multiplying it by the likelihood of the new observation under that explanation, then dividing by the sum over all candidates so that the results add to one. A candidate given a prior of exactly zero stays at zero however strongly the evidence favours it (Cromwell's rule says to avoid priors of exactly 0 or 1 except for logical truths). The sum before dividing, the probability the whole set of candidates gave to the observation (the marginal likelihood or evidence), is a check on the set itself: when it is small, every candidate has explained the observation badly, and the true cause may be missing. Renormalising hides this. The likelihoods in the story are the agent's judgements, rounded, and are invented for the story; a different surveyor would choose different ones.*

---

The typhoon went over Salvacion in the night of the twelfth, and by Monday the pipes were dry and the firewood was wet.

Our Lady of Perpetual Help had kept its roof, which was more than the school had. In the parish hall a hundred and twenty people were sleeping on mats between the folding tables, and Father Danilo had a list taped to the door in three columns: rice, blankets, water. Rice and blankets had a number beside them. Water had a question mark. The municipal tanker had come once, on Sunday, and been taken in the first hour. The creek that fed the village had turned the colour of strong tea.

Lola Pacita Villareal, eighty-one, who had cooked for every feast in the barangay since 1983, stood in the hall kitchen with a pot of creek water and a lighter that would not catch the wet kindling, and said what she always said.

"We boil. In my mother's time we boiled."

"You are right that boiling works," said the parish assistant, from the tablet on the shelf above the sink. "It is the best method there is. The trouble is the fuel. A litre of water needs about 0.30 megajoules just to bring it from 28 degrees to boiling, and more than that in a pot on a fire, because most of the heat goes up past it. For three litres a head, a hundred and twenty heads, you would need about 360 litres a day and a good deal of dry wood, and there is none until the wood has dried. May I suggest a second method for the days between, not instead of the first? The sun."

Lola Pacita looked at the ceiling, where the light came grey through a gap in the corrugated sheet.

"What sun?"

* * *

It was a fair question, and the assistant answered it without hurry.

"The method is called solar disinfection. People call it SODIS. A clear plastic bottle is filled with clear water, shaken, and laid in the sun. The ultraviolet light and the heat together kill most of the germs that cause diarrhoea. It was studied at a Swiss research institute and the World Health Organization lists it among the household methods. What I know of it, I know from those guides, and I have not tested it in this barangay. So I will tell you the rules, and where each one comes from, and then I would like us to test the result before anyone relies on it."

It gave them in order, and Ramil Dacuya, the sacristan's nephew, who was nineteen and had a place at the technical college if the roads opened, wrote them on the back of the water list.

*One. The bottle must be clear, colourless PET.* Plastic drink bottles of the one-and-a-half or two litre kind, no larger than two litres, because light does not go far into water.

*Two. The water must be clear.* The guides give a limit of about thirty on a turbidity scale. Muddy water throws the light back and shades the germs, and the sun does nothing for it. Creek water the colour of tea would not pass.

*Three. Fill, shake for twenty seconds with the cap on, then fill and cap.* The shaking puts air in the water, and the oxygen is part of how the light does its work.

*Four. Lay it on its side on something that reflects or heats, in full sun, for six hours.* A tin roof does both.

*Five. If the sky is more than half cloud, two full days.*

"That last is the one for this week," said the assistant. "Let me show you the sum behind it, because a rule you have the reason for is a rule you can adjust. The guides ask for about 500 watts of sunlight on every square metre for six hours. That is 500 times 6 times 3,600 seconds, which is 10.8 million joules on each square metre, or 3 kilowatt-hours. In full sun at this latitude, near noon, the light is 800 to 1,000 watts a square metre, so a clear day over-delivers. Under thick cloud it can fall to a quarter or less. Say 250. Then six hours gives you half the dose, and you need twelve hours to get what the clear day gives in six. That is why the guides say two days. It is a rule of thumb, with a margin built into it, and not a measurement of what is falling on this roof. I'd like to say that once clearly."

"And the muddy part?" said Ramil.

* * *

The muddy part was the real work, and it took Monday afternoon.

Lola Pacita's nephews carried creek water in from the cistern at the back, and Ramil dipped a bottle and held it up to the window. You could not see the other side of it. The assistant asked him to do a test the guides give for places with no meter: stand a bottle over a page of the large print on the water list, and look down through the neck. If the letters cannot be read through the water, the water is too cloudy, roughly. The headline could not be read. They let a bucket stand for a night, which the guide calls sedimentation, and in the morning they poured it slowly through four folds of old cotton cloth, which Lola Pacita contributed, one of her late husband's shirts, and which she insisted they wash first. After that the headline could be read through eight centimetres of water, and Ramil said the letters swam but were there.

"Then it is under the line, by the guide's test," said the assistant, "and that is as much as I can say. I cannot see the water. If the line is wrong you will not get a warning."

It was then that it corrected itself, and it did it in front of everyone, because Ramil had already been to the bin at the back of the sacristy.

"Ramil, I owe you a correction. I said at the start that any clear bottle would do. That was wrong, and I should have said it better. I have just looked at the bottles in your bin. Three are glass, and two are hard clear plastic with a different mark on the base. The guides ask for PET, which has the number one in the triangle under the bottle. Ordinary glass lets through much less of the light that matters, and some other plastics are thick or clouded with age. A bottle that is scratched all over is also poor, and so is one that has gone yellow. Please use only the ones marked one, and set the others aside. I'm sorry for the half a day."

"I used two of the glass ones this morning," said Ramil.

"Then those two do not count. Keep them out of the numbers."

They found two hundred and forty PET bottles, most of them two litres, by Tuesday noon, from the hall, the school's wrecked canteen and the roadside. Ramil counted. Father Danilo made a list of who had given what, because the barangay captain would ask.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic the assistant worked on the whiteboard in the kitchen, with Ramil copying it into the margin.

One hundred and twenty people at three litres each is 360 litres a day for drinking and for cooking what is not boiled. At two litres a bottle that is 180 bottles per day. On a clear day each bottle is ready the same evening, so 180 bottles feed a day. On a cloudy day each bottle takes two days, so the roof must hold two days' worth at once, 360 bottles, or the village must drink less. They had 240.

"So 240 bottles is 480 litres, which is a day and a third of what you need," said the assistant. "On a bright day it is plenty. If it is cloud, each bottle is tied up for two days, so 240 bottles give 120 a day, 240 litres a day, two thirds of what you need. That is not enough for the whole hall, but it is enough for the children, the old and the sick, and for whatever Lola Pacita can boil on the dry wood in the church porch. I would put the rest of the people on the tanker's water first, and I'll ask again for the chlorine tablets."

"How much roof?" asked Father Danilo.

"A two litre bottle lying down covers about 0.1 by 0.3 metres, or 0.03 of a square metre. Two hundred and forty bottles is about seven square metres, say ten with space to turn them. The hall's sheet roof on the south side is more than that, and the hot tin matters: the guides say that once the water reaches about 50 degrees, heat and light work together much better than either alone. Put a thermometer in one bottle and tell me."

On Wednesday the sky was white and the cloud thin. Ramil put two hundred and forty bottles on the south roof at nine, each marked at the neck in felt pen with the date and the time, and a thermometer in a bottle of its own. At noon it read 41. At two it read 48. At four the cloud thickened and it fell to 39, and Ramil took the bottles in at six, none of them quite ready by the assistant's reckoning, and put them back at eight on Thursday morning. By three that afternoon the thermometer had been to 52.

* * *

"The bottles have had about sixteen hours of mixed light over two days," said the assistant, "and I would call that in the range of the rule, with no more confidence than that. There is one more thing I'd like, if the health office can lend it. Do they still have the little vials with the paper strip for faecal germs, the kind that turn black if the water carries them?"

Nurse Marites Abad had four, from the rural health unit, wrapped in a wet cloth in her bag. They were meant for the municipal wells, but the wells could wait a day. She filled one vial from a bottle that had been on the roof for two days, one from the untreated creek bucket as a check, one from the cloth-filtered water that had never been out in the sun, and one from a glass bottle from the roof, set out like the others. She wrote the times on them. The guide on the vial said to keep them warm and wait up to two days for a black change.

By Friday morning the bucket vial was black to the top. The filtered-only vial was dark at the bottom. The sun-bottle vial was clear. The glass-bottle vial, to Ramil's surprise, was clear too, though paler than the label wanted, and the assistant said what it could say about that.

"One clear vial is one clear vial. I would not tell anyone that glass works. It may be that this glass was thin, or that the water in it was cleaner to start with, or that it reached the temperature that kills it without help. I don't know. What the test says is that the PET bottle that has had two days of mixed sun gave clear paper, and the untreated water did not. It also cannot tell me about chemicals. A flood carries diesel and pesticide and the contents of latrines, and the sun does nothing about any of those. If anyone smells fuel on the water, it goes to the washing, not the drinking."

"Is it safe?" said Lola Pacita, who had been standing at the edge of it all with her arms folded.

"It is a great deal safer than the bucket," said the assistant. "I can't give you more than that and be honest. I'd like you and Nurse Marites to taste it first, and if either of you is not happy, it does not go out."

She tasted it from a tin cup. It was warm, and faintly like a hot plastic spoon, and she said so.

* * *

The chlorine tablets came on Thursday evening, a day before the vials were read, in the back of a Municipal Social Welfare truck, four hundred of them in a drum, with a printed card. The assistant read the card aloud to Nurse Marites and found that it asked for one tablet to twenty litres and thirty minutes' wait, and then it said that the water, too, must be clear. "Which is the cloth in the cistern, and the night in the bucket," it said, "and so nothing we have done is wasted."

The bottles stayed on the roof through the following week, as a second line, and on the Sunday, when the sun came out hard at last, Father Danilo blessed them with the water of the font, which he had boiled first, out of habit, and the first water of the fine weather was drunk after the Mass. Ramil went home to see about his college. He left his list, with the five rules, on the back of the water sheet, and under them in felt pen what he had been told to write:

*Only PET. Clear water. Shake. Six hours of sun, or two days of cloud. Does not fix fuel or chemicals. Ask the nurse.*

Lola Pacita boiled the soup.

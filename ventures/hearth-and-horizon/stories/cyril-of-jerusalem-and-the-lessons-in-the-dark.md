Tabitha was nine years old, and she had a question that nobody would answer.

The question was this: *What happens on Easter night?*

She had asked her mother. Her mother said, "Wait and see." She had asked her grandfather. Her grandfather said, "Wait and see." She had even asked the baker's wife, who had been baptised long ago and knew everything, and the baker's wife smiled and gave her a fig and said, "Wait and see, little one."

Tabitha did not like waiting. Do you?

* * *

Tabitha lived long ago, about three hundred and fifty years after Jesus, in a village in the hills outside Jerusalem. She and her mother had decided to be baptised. And in Jerusalem, if you wanted to be baptised at Easter, you gave your name in at the start of Lent. Then, every morning for the whole of Lent, you went to church to learn.

So every morning, very early, while the stars were still out, Tabitha and her mother walked up the road into the city.

They came to the biggest church Tabitha had ever seen. It had gold on the ceiling and rows and rows of tall pillars. And just outside it, under the open sky, was a big rock. That rock was Golgotha, the hill where Jesus died on the cross.

Inside, all the people getting ready for baptism sat down together, men on one side and women and girls on the other. And at the front stood their teacher. His name was Cyril.

* * *

Cyril had a clear, kind voice that carried right to the back. He spoke in Greek, the language of the city. Tabitha's family spoke the language of the villages, so a priest stood next to Cyril and said everything again in words that Tabitha knew.

Cyril taught them everything, right from the beginning. How God made the sun and the moon and the sea. How God made people, and loved them, even when they went wrong. How God sent his Son, Jesus. How Jesus died on the cross, *right there*, he said, pointing out at the rock. And how Jesus rose again, and the tomb was empty.

"I am not telling you stories you have to take my word for," Cyril said. "You are standing in the very place."

* * *

Then one morning, Cyril gave them the Creed. The Creed is what Christians believe, said in a few short lines. *I believe in one God, the Father Almighty...* You may know the one we say at Mass.

"Learn it by heart," said Cyril. "Don't write it down on paper. Write it in your heart, and say it over to yourself, so you never lose it."

So Tabitha said it over to herself. She said it on the road in the morning. She said it on the road home. She said it while she fed the hens.

"What are you muttering?" said her little brother, Yosef.

"I can't tell you," said Tabitha. "Not yet."

Cyril had told them that too. *Don't tell these things to the people outside, not yet.* Not because they were bad, but because they were precious, like a present that is wrapped up until the right day.

So Tabitha said the Creed to the hens. The hens did not mind.

* * *

One morning, near the end of Lent, Tabitha put up her hand.

The priest who was saying things again in her language saw her, and leaned over to Cyril, and Cyril looked down at the little girl among the grown-ups.

"Please," said Tabitha, "what will happen on Easter night?"

Some of the grown-ups smiled, because they had been wanting to ask that too.

Cyril did not tell her. But he did not tell her off, either. He smiled back.

"You will see," he said. "And afterwards, I will explain it all. Some things are better seen first, and talked about afterwards. You will understand much more that way."

And he said one more thing, and Tabitha remembered it. He prayed that God would show them *the night that shines like the day*.

Tabitha thought about that all the way home. A night that shines like the day. How could a night shine?

* * *

At last, Easter night came.

It was dark, very dark. Tabitha held her mother's hand. All the people who were to be baptised waited in a hall outside the place with the water, in the dark.

And then they were told: *Turn and face the west.*

The west is where the sun goes down, where the dark comes from. So they all turned to face the dark. And they held out their hands, and Tabitha held out hers, and they all said, in a loud voice, that they would have nothing to do with the devil, the enemy who wants us to do wrong. *No. Not you. Never you.*

And then they were told: *Turn round. Face the east.*

The east is where the sun comes up in the morning. So they all turned round, a whole crowd of people turning together in the dark, and faced the morning. And they said that they believed in God the Father, and in Jesus his Son, and in the Holy Spirit.

Tabitha's heart was going thump, thump, thump.

Then they went in to the water. They took off their old clothes. They were rubbed with oil. And when it was Tabitha's turn, she stepped down into the pool, and she was asked, *Do you believe?* and she said, *I believe*, and she went right under the water, *one*, and came up, and went under, *two*, and came up, and went under, *three*, and came up, with water in her eyes and her hair and her mouth.

It was cold! She gasped. Her mother, beside her, was laughing and crying at the same time.

They put sweet-smelling oil on her forehead, and dressed her in a clean white robe. And then all the new Christians, dripping and shining in their white, were led into the great church.

And the great church was full of lamps.

Hundreds of lamps, all lit, and all the people singing, and gold on the ceiling glowing, so that the middle of the night was as bright as the middle of the day.

*The night that shines like the day*, thought Tabitha. *So this is what he meant.*

* * *

In the week after Easter, the new Christians came every day, still in their white robes, to the round church built over the tomb of Jesus. And there, at last, their teacher explained everything they had done.

(Was it Cyril? People have always said so, though some wise people wonder if it was his friend who came after him. Either way, the lessons are from Jerusalem, and we still have them.)

"Why did you face the west?" the teacher asked them. "Because the west is the dark, and you were saying no to the dark. And why did you turn round to the east? Because the east is where the light comes from, and where God planted his garden at the beginning. You turned your backs on the dark, and you faced the light."

"And the water," he said. "You went down three times and came up three times, like Jesus, who lay three days in the tomb and rose again. That water was like a grave, and it was like a mother too. You died to the old things in it, and you were born again from it, both at once."

The people were so happy to understand at last that they clapped and called out. A lady pilgrim from far away, called Egeria, said that the clapping in that church could be heard outside in the street.

Tabitha did not clap. She was thinking.

If they had told her all that before Easter night, she would have been busy *listening*. She would have been thinking about the words. Instead she had been busy *seeing*: the dark, and the turning round, and the cold water, and all the lamps. And now that she understood, she could see it all again, inside her head, and it meant twice as much.

"Oh," said Tabitha, out loud, so that her mother looked at her. "*That's* why they said wait and see."

* * *

On the last day, the teacher showed them how to receive Holy Communion.

"Don't come with your fingers all spread out," he said. "Make your left hand a throne for your right hand, because it is going to hold a King."

That night, at home, Tabitha showed Yosef how to make a throne with his hands. That was allowed.

"What's it for?" said Yosef.

"You'll see," said Tabitha. "One day. Wait and see."

* * *

Cyril became the bishop of Jerusalem. Three times, people who were cross with him sent him far away from his city, and three times he came back, and when he was an old man he was still there. The Church remembers him on the 18th of March.

But his lessons were written down by someone who heard them. And people still read them, every Lent, all over the world, wherever people are getting ready to be baptised at Easter.

And one of the things Cyril told them was this. Make the sign of the cross: when you eat and when you drink, when you go out and when you come in, *before you go to sleep, when you lie down, and when you get up.*

So let's do that now. Forehead, heart, shoulder, shoulder.

And then a little prayer:

> Dear Jesus, thank you for my baptism,
> when I said no to the dark
> and turned round to face the light.
> When I cannot understand something yet,
> help me to wait and see.
> Saint Cyril, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our sources.** Saint Cyril (born about 313 to 315, died about 386 or 387) was a priest and then bishop of Jerusalem. One Lent, around 348 or 350, someone took down in writing the lessons he gave to the candidates for baptism: an introductory lecture (the **Procatechesis**) and eighteen **Catechetical Lectures**, given in the great basilica Constantine had built beside Golgotha. Five more lessons for the newly baptised, the **Mystagogical Catecheses**, were given in Easter week in the round church over the tomb (the Anastasis). These five are traditionally Cyril's, but some manuscripts give them to his successor, **John**, and scholars still disagree; the story says so in one line. We have used the English translation by Edwin Hamilton Gifford in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, volume 7 (1894), and John Wilkinson's translation of **Egeria's Travels** (her chapters 45 to 47 on Lent and Easter week in Jerusalem). Egeria, a pilgrim from the far west, was in Jerusalem in the early 380s, thirty years or more after the lectures; she does not name the bishop, who was probably Cyril, then old. The session that wrote this could not open the texts to check them word by word, so **the spoken lines are our own close, shortened renderings, not quotations**.

**What comes from Cyril's lessons.** The giving in of names at the start of Lent and the daily lessons (Procatechesis); the Creed given to be learned by heart and kept in the memory rather than written out on paper, and rehearsed so as not to be lost (Lecture 5.12); "tell nothing to him that is without" before baptism (Procatechesis 12); his prayer that God would at last show them the night whose darkness shines like the day (Procatechesis 15, echoing Psalm 139:12); Golgotha nearby as a witness (Lecture 13 and elsewhere); the sign of the cross "over the bread we eat, and the cups we drink; in our comings in, and goings out; before our sleep, when we lie down and when we rise up" (Lecture 13.36). From the Mystagogical Catecheses: that sight is more persuasive than hearing, so the rites were explained only afterwards (1.1); the renunciation of Satan facing west with the hand stretched out, the west being the place of darkness, and the turn to the east, the side of paradise, to profess faith in Father, Son and Holy Spirit (1.2 to 1.9); putting off the old garment, the anointing with oil, the threefold going down into the water and coming up, standing for Christ's three days in the tomb, and the water as "at once your grave and your mother" (2.2 to 2.4); the anointing with fragrant chrism on the forehead and other senses (3.4); the white garments (4.8); and the left hand made a throne for the right, "which is to receive a King" (5.21).

**What comes from Egeria.** The bishop teaching in Greek while a priest translated into Syriac for those who knew only that language; the Lent lessons each morning, going through the Scriptures from the beginning, with Genesis; the Easter-week explanations in the Anastasis, where the people's cries of approval could be heard outside the church; and that the not-yet-baptised were not let in. She saw these things in the 380s; the story carries them back to Cyril's earlier Lent, which is our assumption.

**What is ours.** Tabitha, her mother, her grandfather, Yosef, the baker's wife, the hens and the fig; Tabitha's question in class and Cyril's kindly answer to her; the walk in from a village; "I am not telling you stories you have to take my word for"; the moment of understanding. Cyril's lectures speak to grown men and women, and we do not know whether children sat among them; a nine-year-old candidate is our invention. We have kept the rites in the order the Mystagogical Catecheses give and simplified them (the candidates were anointed all over with exorcised oil, and the renunciation was of Satan "and all thy works, and all thy pomp, and all thy service"). The bright church on Easter night fits the Jerusalem vigil and Procatechesis 15 but is our picture, not a description in the lectures. That the Lent lessons were Cyril's is certain; that he himself gave the Easter-week ones is the tradition, with the doubt above.

**His life, in short.** Cyril was made bishop of Jerusalem about 350 and was sent into exile three times (in about 357, 360, and from about 367 to 378), amid the quarrels of his day over the Arian teaching and with Acacius, the bishop of Caesarea; the story keeps this to one line and takes no side beyond what the sources say. In 381 he was at the Council of Constantinople, and the bishops' letter of 382, preserved by the historian Theodoret, vouched for him as a lawful bishop who had fought against the Arians. Pope Leo XIII declared him a **Doctor of the Church** in 1882.

**The feast.** **18 March**, the traditional day of his death, is the optional memorial of **Saint Cyril of Jerusalem, bishop and doctor of the Church**, in the General Roman Calendar, and his feast in the Eastern churches too. Because it nearly always falls in Lent, it is usually kept only as a commemoration. This is a different Cyril from the Cyril of *Cyril and Methodius and the Letters for the Slavs* on this shelf (14 February).

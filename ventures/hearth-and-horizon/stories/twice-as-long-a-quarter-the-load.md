title: Twice as Long, a Quarter the Load
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Fusha e Arrave (invented), on the Zadrima plain, Lezhë county, northern Albania, Tuesday 14 to Tuesday 21 November 2051
published: 2026-11-17
summary: An earthquake cracks the chancel arch of a village church in northern Albania, and the keystone drops nine millimetres. The diocese's engineer cannot come for a week and says touch nothing. When an aftershock opens the crack again, a farmer props the arch with a scaffold tube, because the same tube once pulled his tractor out of a canal, and the tube bends like a fishing rod under one man's hammer. A retired physics teacher works out why: a tube that holds 131 kilonewtons in tension holds about eight standing up six metres tall, and every time you halve its free length it holds four times as much. A story about Euler buckling, lacing, safety factors and an engineer who says no for good reasons.
---

Marta Prendi was sixty-three, a retired physics teacher and the secretary of the parish council of Shën Nikollë in Fusha e Arrave, and on the Friday after the earthquake she watched a steel tube six metres long bend in the middle like a fishing rod under the weight of nothing she could see.

She had known it would. That was the part she would tell Dom Ndue afterwards, on her knees, in the little confessional that was now standing in the churchyard under a tarpaulin with the rest of the church's furniture: she had known it would, or half known, the way you half know a name you have taught a hundred times, and she had stood at the back of the nave with her hands in her coat pockets and said nothing until it was already bending.

* * *

The earthquake came at seventeen minutes past two on the morning of Tuesday the fourteenth of November, out of the sea somewhere off Shëngjin, and it lasted, people agreed afterwards, about fifteen seconds, which was long enough for everyone in Fusha e Arrave to be standing in a doorway in the dark before it stopped.

Marta's house was single-storey and newer than she was, and it did nothing worse than throw every plate in the kitchen onto the floor. In the morning she swept them up, rang her daughter in Milan to say she was alive, and walked down the road to the church with the parish keys in her pocket, because that was what the secretary of the parish council did, and because she was afraid of what she would find.

Shën Nikollë had been built in 1896 by the village, out of river stones from the Drin and lime burnt in a kiln that was still there, a ruin in a field behind the cemetery. It was not a large church: a nave twenty metres long and nine wide, a square bell turret over the west door, and at the east end, through a round arch of dressed stone, the sanctuary, where the altar stood under a painted ceiling of blue with gold stars. The arch was the most beautiful thing in the building. Five metres across, its two sides rising straight from the floor for three metres and a half and then curving over to meet at the top, where the keystone had a lamb carved on it, standing with a little flag, the Agnus Dei, worn soft by a hundred and fifty-five years and two coats of limewash.

From 1967 to 1990 the church had been a grain store for the agricultural cooperative. Marta's grandmother, who had been a young wife then, had remembered the sacks stacked up against the arch to the height of a man, and the lamb looking down at them, and the cooperative's foreman, who had been a decent enough man in a bad time, saying that it was a pity to whitewash such good carving and so they would not bother. In 1991 the village had carried the sacks out and carried the altar back in, and Marta, who was three, had been carried in too, on her father's shoulders, to the first Mass, and she had a memory that might have been a real one of the lamb directly above her head.

The lamb was still there on the Tuesday morning. But it was lower.

Marta did not see it at first. She saw the plaster on the floor, the fallen candlesticks, a long diagonal crack across the north wall of the nave that went from a window corner to the floor, and she saw that the arch was still standing and let out a breath she had been holding since her own front door. Then she walked up the nave, and stood under the arch, and looked up, and saw daylight.

It was not much daylight. It was a hair of it, a thin bright line where the keystone met the stone on its right, and another on its left, where the joints had opened and the lamb had slid down between its neighbours like a cork pushed a little way into a bottle. Further down each side, about a metre from the top on the left and the right, there was a second crack in the arch, running across the stones from the face to the back. The sanctuary's starry ceiling had a crack in it too, running back from the top of the arch towards the altar.

She went out into the churchyard and rang Dom Ndue, who was in Blinisht, where the presbytery was and where the bell turret of the church had come down into the road. Then she rang the number the diocese had sent round years ago for "damage to church buildings", and got a recorded message in a calm voice saying that the diocese was aware of the earthquake, that every parish should keep people out of any building with visible cracks, and that the engineer would ring back.

* * *

The engineer rang back at nine in the evening. His name was Artan Lika, he was the structural engineer the diocese of Sapë kept on contract for its hundred and some churches, chapels and presbyteries, and he sounded like a man who had been on the telephone since dawn and expected to be on it until the next one.

Marta told him what she had seen. He asked her questions, short ones, in an order that made her think he had a list in front of him. Was the crack in the arch at the top only, or lower too? Lower too, a metre down each side. How far had the keystone dropped? She did not know. Could she find out, without standing under it? She could try. Were the walls on either side of the arch leaning? She did not think so. Was anything coming down? Dust. Nothing else.

"Then the arch has made four hinges and is standing on three of them," he said. "That's how masonry arches crack. It's not necessarily as bad as it looks. Arches can stand a long time like that. Or not." He paused. "I have three hundred and eleven buildings on my list, Zonja Prendi. Forty of them are worse than yours, by what people are telling me. I can come on Tuesday."

"Tuesday next week?"

"Tuesday next week. Until then nobody goes in, nobody touches anything, nobody moves anything. Put a rope across the door. If you can measure the crack without standing under it, measure it every morning and send me the numbers. That's all."

"Should we prop it?"

"No," said Artan Lika. "Please don't. People prop things after earthquakes and the props kill more of them than the earthquake did. Touch nothing. I'll be there on Tuesday."

* * *

Marta had taught physics at the gymnasium in Lezhë for thirty-eight years, and if there was one thing she knew how to do it was measure something without standing under it.

On Wednesday morning she borrowed her neighbour's laser rangefinder, the kind builders used, and stood at the side of the sanctuary with it resting on a stepladder, and measured the height of the bottom of the keystone above the floor and the height of the stone beside it. The difference was nine millimetres. Then, because a number with nothing to compare it to was not a measurement, she stood the stepladder in the same place, marked its feet on the floor with chalk, and wrote it all down in a school exercise book with the date and the time.

Then she did the thing her grandmother would have done. She mixed a little plaster of Paris in a jam jar lid, climbed the stepladder at the side of the arch, reached across with a palette knife on the end of a broom handle, and spread a dab of it across the crack a metre down on the north side, where she could reach it, and wrote the date in it with a nail. A plaster tell-tale. If the crack moved, the plaster would break, and the plaster could not lie about it.

She sent the engineer the nine millimetres. He sent back a thumbs-up.

On Thursday morning it was still nine. The plaster was whole. She began, a little, to breathe.

At eleven minutes to midnight on Thursday the sixteenth there was an aftershock. It was smaller than the first, and shorter, and Marta, who was awake, did not get up for it; she lay in bed and listened to the glass in the window frame rattle and stop. In the morning she went down to the church.

The plaster tell-tale had a crack through the middle of it, clean and dark, and the gap in it was wide enough to put a fingernail in. The rangefinder said thirteen millimetres.

She stood with the exercise book in her hand and looked at the two numbers, nine and thirteen, and felt cold.

* * *

Zef Gjoni came at ten with a tractor and a trailer full of scaffold tubes.

Zef was seventy, a farmer with forty olive trees and a field of maize and a cousin in Lezhë called Pjetër who had a scaffolding business, and he had been, for as long as Marta had been on the parish council, the man who fixed things. He had fixed the bell turret's ladder, the gutter on the north side, the boiler in the presbytery in Blinisht, and in 2044 the whole roof, with eleven men from the village and a borrowed crane. He was not a man who waited for Tuesday.

"Pjetër says take what we need," he said. "All his men are in Lezhë, on the hospital. He says the tubes are sitting in the yard doing nothing."

Behind him on the trailer were thirty or forty scaffold tubes, grey galvanised steel, the ordinary kind, as thick as a broom handle and a half, in lengths from two metres to six, and two buckets of couplers, and some planks, and four old railway sleepers, and a heavy wooden block that had been a gatepost.

"The engineer said not to touch anything," said Marta.

"The engineer is in Shkodër," said Zef. "The arch is here. It moved in the night, didn't it? You said it moved."

"Four millimetres."

"Four millimetres today. What tomorrow?" He pulled a six-metre tube off the trailer by one end and let it slide down onto the grass, where it rang. "One under the middle," he said. "Under the lamb. On a sleeper, with the gatepost on top and a pair of wedges. Just to hold it where it is. Just till Tuesday."

"Zef."

"Marta. This tube," and he put his boot on it, "pulled my tractor out of the Gjadër canal. Two years ago. The big one, the Landini, nose down in the water, and the other tractor on the bank, and this tube between them for a drawbar, because the chain was too short. It pulled seven tonnes of tractor up a bank out of the mud. It's not going to have any trouble with a few stones."

That was when she should have said it. She knew, afterwards, exactly what she should have said. But what she said was "I don't know," and Zef took that, reasonably enough, as the end of the argument, and called to his grandson Gjon, who was nineteen, and to Kristjan from the house by the canal, and the three of them carried the sleeper in.

* * *

They did it carefully, she would give them that. They did not stand under the arch more than they had to. They laid the sleeper on the flagstones directly under the keystone, and stood the tube up on it, and Gjon went up a ladder at the side with the gatepost on a rope and set it on top of the tube, and Kristjan passed him up two oak wedges, and Gjon slid them in between the top of the gatepost and the lamb, one from each side, and tapped them home with a lump hammer.

"Snug," said Zef from the floor, holding the tube upright. "Just snug."

Gjon tapped. The wedges went in a centimetre. He tapped again.

Marta, at the back of the nave, saw it before anyone. It was not much at first: a curve in the tube, about the height of Zef's shoulder, so slight that it might have been the light. Gjon tapped again, and the curve was not the light. The middle of the tube was moving sideways, slowly, towards the north wall, and the tube was not a line any more but a bow, a long, shallow, terrible bow, like a fishing rod with a fish on it, and the top of it was still pressed to the lamb and the bottom of it was still on the sleeper and the middle of it was a hand's width out of line and going further.

"Stop," she said. Then she shouted it. "Gjon, stop! Knock them out! Zef, let go of it!"

Gjon had seen it too. He hit the left wedge sideways, hard, once, and it flew out and clattered down the sanctuary steps, and there was a sound from the tube like a sigh, and the tube sprang straight, and stood there, straight, on its sleeper, with the gatepost on top no longer quite touching the stone. A little plaster dust came down from the arch, and settled, and nothing else did.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"It's bent," said Kristjan at last.

Zef sighted along the tube, his face very close to the steel. "No," he said slowly. "It's straight. Look. It's as straight as it was." He looked up at Marta. "What was that? It's not even bent. What was that?"

* * *

Marta knew what it was. She had known its name since she was sixteen, and she had written it on a blackboard every year for thirty-eight years, in the last week of the summer term, when the examinations were over and she was allowed to teach the things she loved: *the Euler buckling load of a slender column.* Leonhard Euler, 1757. A straight, slender strut, pinned at both ends, can carry a load pressing along its length up to a certain value and no further. Below that value it stays straight. At that value it does not crush, it does not yield, it does not break. It simply stops being able to decide to be straight, and bows out sideways, and the smallest push makes it bow further. And if you take the load off before the steel itself yields, it springs back as if nothing had happened.

What she had not done, ever, was see one buckle under a church arch, with a nineteen-year-old on the ladder beside it.

She sat on the trailer in the churchyard that afternoon with her exercise book on her knee and her old university textbook beside her, which she had driven home to fetch, and a tape measure, and the calculator on her phone. Zef sat next to her, with a coffee he had brought from the house, and did not say anything, which was not like him.

She measured the tube. Forty-eight millimetres across the outside, and with the calipers from her neighbour's toolbox, four millimetres thick in the wall. She looked up the steel in the back of the textbook and found what she expected: ordinary scaffold steel yields at about two hundred and thirty-five newtons for every square millimetre.

"Your tractor first," she said. "When the tube was a drawbar it was being pulled at both ends. Stretched. In tension. The steel in the wall is a ring about five hundred and fifty-seven square millimetres in area, and if it yields at two hundred and thirty-five newtons on every square millimetre, the tube will hold about a hundred and thirty-one kilonewtons before it starts to stretch for good. That's more than thirteen tonnes hanging on it. Your Landini is seven tonnes, and you were dragging it, not lifting it. The tube was never close."

"So it's strong," said Zef.

"In tension it's strong. Pulled, it's as strong as it is everywhere. But pushed..." She wrote the formula out for him, as she had for a thousand sixteen-year-olds, and turned the book round so he could see it.

*P = π² E I / L²*

"P is the load it can take standing up before it bows. E is how stiff the steel is: two hundred and ten thousand newtons per square millimetre, the same for every ordinary steel there is. I is about the shape of the tube, how far the steel is from the middle; for this tube it comes out at about a hundred and thirty-eight thousand in millimetres to the fourth power, don't ask. And L is the length." She tapped the L. "It's squared. Underneath. So the longer the tube, the less it takes, and it gets less fast. Twice as long, a quarter the load."

She did the sum with him watching. π squared, times two hundred and ten thousand, times a hundred and thirty-eight thousand, divided by six thousand millimetres squared.

"Seven point nine," she said. "Kilonewtons. About eight hundred kilos, standing on its end, six metres long. Not thirteen tonnes. Eight hundred kilos. A hundred and thirty-one pulled, and eight pushed. The same tube."

Zef looked at the formula for a long time.

"Gjon was hammering wedges," he said. "Into a stone arch. With a lump hammer."

"A wedge is a very good machine for making a big force out of a small one," said Marta. "It's why we use them. He'd have got eight hundred kilos into that tube with four or five taps without either of you noticing. The arch didn't have to do anything. Gjon bent it himself." She put the pencil down. "And then, if it had stayed under the arch, the first time the arch settled another millimetre onto it, or the next aftershock shook it, it would have folded, and dropped the gatepost on whoever was under it, and taken nothing at all off the lamb."

"It sprang back," said Zef.

"It did. Because at eight hundred kilos the steel was hardly working. The stress in the wall was about fourteen newtons a square millimetre, out of two hundred and thirty-five. It didn't fail because the steel was weak. It failed because it was long."

* * *

That evening she sent Artan Lika a message with the two measurements, nine millimetres on Wednesday and thirteen on Friday, and a photograph of the broken tell-tale, and then, because she had been a teacher for thirty-eight years and knew what it was to be told the truth late, a second message telling him about the tube.

He rang at seven the next morning, Saturday. She had expected him to be angry, and he was, for about a minute, in a low, tired voice, as if he had used up the loud one on someone else already. Then he stopped.

"Who measured the thirteen?"

"I did. The same way as the nine. Laser, from the same place, the ladder on the same chalk marks."

"And the tell-tale is broken. It's not just shrunk off the wall."

"It's broken through the middle. There's a gap you can put a fingernail in."

There was a silence on the line, and in it she could hear a car going past wherever he was, and somebody calling to somebody else in Albanian with a Shkodër accent.

"All right," he said. "Listen. I'm not going to tell you that I'm happy about this, because I'm not. Shoring is a job for engineers and scaffolders, not for a parish council, and if I could get a contractor to you today I would, and I can't. Every scaffolder between here and Tirana is on a hospital or a school. I still can't come before Tuesday. And the arch is moving. Four millimetres in one aftershock. If there's another aftershock like Thursday's, or a bigger one, and it hasn't got anything under it, it may come down, and if it comes down it brings the wall above it down with it into the sanctuary, and maybe the end of the nave roof. And then I'm not coming to look at a cracked arch on Tuesday, I'm coming to look at a pile of stones." He breathed out. "So I'm going to tell you how to do it. And you're going to do it exactly how I say or not at all. And you're going to send me a photograph at every step. Have you got paper?"

"I've got paper."

"First. You tell me what load you think you're holding."

She had worked that out too, the night before, at the kitchen table. "If the part of the arch between the two lower cracks drops, it's about two metres of the arch, sixty centimetres deep and seventy thick, and the wall standing on top of it up to the roof. I measured the wall at about two and a half metres. That's about four point three cubic metres of stone and lime altogether. At twenty-one kilonewtons a cubic metre, about ninety kilonewtons."

"Use a hundred and twenty," he said. "The wall over the crown of that arch goes up to the gable, not just to the roof plate. It's taller than you think from inside. I've got the drawings from 2044, when they did the roof. It's three and a half metres, not two and a half. Call it a hundred and twenty. Now: how many tubes, and how long?"

"Six," said Marta. "Two rows of three, one under each face of the arch, because it's seventy centimetres thick. Each one from a sleeper to a short beam under the stone. Five metres twenty long."

"And what does one of those take, standing up?"

She had done that sum too. "Ten point six kilonewtons. By Euler."

"Not by Euler," said Artan Lika. "Euler is a perfectly straight tube with the load exactly down the middle. Your tubes have been in Pjetër's yard getting dropped off lorries for ten years. They're not straight. Your load won't be exactly down the middle. A real tube fails below Euler, sometimes a long way below. There's a curve in the steel code for exactly this, for real tubes like yours, and it takes off more the shorter the tube is, because a short tube's nearer to yielding when it goes. Have you got a computer?"

"I've got my phone."

"I'll send you the curve. For a tube five metres twenty long, by the code, it's about nine. Not ten and a half. Nine. Now. Six times nine?"

"Fifty-four," said Marta, and saw it as she said it. "Fifty-five. And you want a hundred and twenty."

"Six of them standing loose under that arch wouldn't hold it once," he said. "Not half of it. Never mind twice."

* * *

"So how," said Zef, when she told him, in the churchyard, "do you make a tube shorter without making it shorter?"

Marta had the answer to that before she had finished her coffee, because it was in the same week of the same summer term, on the same blackboard. "You stop the middle from moving," she said. "The tube bows out sideways in the middle. That's what buckling is. So you tie the middle to something that doesn't move, and then the tube can only bow between the ties. A five-metre tube tied at half its height is two five-metre-twenty halves, two metres sixty each. And every time you halve the length..."

"A quarter," said Zef. "Four times as much."

"By Euler. Ten point six becomes forty-two. By the engineer's curve, nine becomes thirty-two."

"Thirty-two times six is a hundred and ninety. That's more than a hundred and twenty."

"That's what I said to him," said Marta.

That had been the second half of the telephone call, and the hardest, and she had come out of it liking Artan Lika a great deal more than she had expected to. He had not said yes to halves. He had told her two more things, and both were things she had not thought of.

The first was that six props would not share the weight evenly. "They never do," he had said. "One prop's sleeper sits on a flagstone that's a millimetre higher, one wedge goes in a bit tighter, the arch is cracked, and cracked arches don't put their weight down where you'd like. Assume one prop in each row of three takes half that row's load. Half of sixty. Thirty kilonewtons on one tube. Now what's your margin, with tubes at halves?"

Thirty-two against thirty. One point one.

"That's not a margin," he had said. "That's a coin toss with extra steps."

The second thing was the number he wanted. "I want one and a half, at least, by the curve, on my load, with one tube taking half its row. That's my judgment, it's not a law; it's what I'd want for a temporary prop under a cracked arch for four days, in a building nobody is allowed into. So you lace at thirds."

Thirds. Two levels of ties instead of one, and the free length of every tube a third of five metres twenty: one metre seventy-three. Euler: ninety-five. By the curve, fifty-eight. Fifty-eight against thirty.

"One point nine," Marta had said.

"One point nine," Artan Lika had agreed. "And you lace both ways. Along each row and across between the rows, at both levels, so each tube is held in the middle from two sides. And diagonals in every side of the frame, so the whole thing can't sway over like a stack of chairs. The ties carry almost nothing; a couple of percent of what's in the tube. They're not there to carry, they're there to hold the middle still. A coupler will hold that easily. But every coupler done up properly, with a spanner, not a hand. Does Pjetër have a scaffolder who can come for an hour on Sunday and check the couplers?"

"I'll ask."

"Ask. And the wedges. This is the important part. Snug, not driven. You are not lifting the arch. You are not pushing the lamb back up where it was. If you push it back up you'll open the cracks on the other side of it and you'll have made it worse. You put the props in under the arch so that if it drops another millimetre, it drops onto them and stops. That's all you want. Snug. Hand-tight and one tap. And nobody, ever, stands under the middle of the arch to do it. You put the outside props in first, from the sides, and then the middle ones."

"Yes."

"And you send me a photograph of every one."

"Yes."

"And Zonja Prendi."

"Yes?"

"Thank you for telling me about the tube. Most people wouldn't have."

* * *

They began at noon on the Saturday, after Pjetër himself had driven out from Lezhë in a van with a spanner in each pocket and an opinion of Zef's tube on the tractor that he shared with Zef in a long, low, family voice behind the trailer, at the end of which they embraced.

Pjetër was a big, quiet man in his fifties who had spent twenty years putting up scaffolds in Italy and ten at home, and he walked once round the arch at a distance, looking up, and once round the trailer, looking at tubes, and threw out four of them that Marta would never have known were bent. Then he took the remaining ones one at a time, held each to his eye and sighted down it, the way Zef had done, and laid out six in a row on the grass.

"These six," he said.

They laid two sleepers on the flagstones under each face of the arch, end to end, and checked them level with Pjetër's long spirit level, and packed one end with a slate under it until the bubble sat in the middle. They stood the first tube up at the north end of the north row, a metre from the centre line, where the arch was low enough to reach from the side, and Pjetër climbed a scaffold tower he had brought in the van, its legs outside the line of the arch, and set an oak beam across the tops, and the work began to be what Marta had not expected it to be: slow. Slow and patient and dull.

The second tube went in at the south end of the same row. Then the two outside tubes of the other row. Pjetër set the short oak beams across the heads of each pair of tubes, and Gjon, from the tower, slid oak packing in above the beams until it touched the stone of the arch, and then the wedges. Hand-tight. One tap. Pjetër listened to the tap.

Then the ties. At a third of the height, one metre seventy-three above the sleepers, Pjetër clamped a horizontal tube right along each row and another across between the rows at every prop, with a coupler at every crossing, and at two thirds of the height the same again. The couplers were heavy steel clamps with a bolt each, and Pjetër went round every one behind Gjon and Kristjan with his spanner and gave it a last turn that Marta could hear, a dry grind, and then wrote a tick on it in yellow crayon. Then the diagonals, long tubes from low on one prop to high on its neighbour, in every face of the frame, so that the six props and their ties became, by four o'clock, not six tubes but a box: a tall, gaunt, grey box of steel standing on the flagstones of the sanctuary, braced in every direction, holding up nothing yet.

Then, last, the two middle props. Pjetër put them in himself. He would not let anyone else stand where he had to stand, and he would not stand there for longer than it took to set each one between the ties and drop a coupler on it, and when he came out from under the arch with his face grey with dust and climbed the tower to set the last wedges he looked, for a moment, his age.

Hand-tight. One tap.

Marta photographed every one, and laid a new tell-tale beside the broken one, and sent the photographs to Artan Lika, and at half past six on Saturday evening he sent back, without comment, the thumbs-up.

* * *

On Sunday, Dom Ndue said Mass in the churchyard. It was the thirty-third Sunday in Ordinary Time, the last but one before Advent, and the readings were about the end of things, which nobody needed explaining that week. He set up the altar on a trestle under the big walnut by the gate, with the candlesticks they had carried out of the church on Tuesday and the cloth that Marta's mother had embroidered, and about a hundred and forty people came, from Fusha e Arrave and from Blinisht, whose own church had no turret now, and stood on the grass in their coats.

Through the open west door you could see straight down the nave to the sanctuary, where in the dimness under the arch the grey steel box stood with its yellow ticks, and above it, a little lower than it ought to be, the lamb.

Marta did not hear much of the homily. She was thinking about Euler: about how he had lost the sight of one eye before he ever wrote the formula on her blackboard, and of the other years after, and gone on working anyway, doing the sums in his head and dictating them. She was thinking about Zef's tube, and about how close it had come, and about how she had stood with her hands in her pockets. At the sign of peace Zef found her in the crowd and shook her hand and did not let go of it for a moment longer than he needed to.

* * *

The aftershock came on Monday night, at twenty past three in the morning, a little bigger than Thursday's.

Marta was awake, as she had been every night since the first one. She felt the bed move under her, sideways and back, and heard every window in the house rattle at once, and lay rigid, counting, the way she had taught a generation of children to count after lightning, except there was nothing to count to. Six seconds. Seven. It stopped. A dog barked, and another, and in the distance, from the main road, the long two notes of a car alarm.

She did not go down to the church in the dark. Artan Lika had said not to and she had decided, on the trailer on Friday, that she was going to do what Artan Lika said. She lay awake until six with the light on and the exercise book on the bedside table.

At six she went down.

The churchyard was grey and wet and empty. The tarpaulin over the furniture had blown half off in the night, and the confessional stood out under the walnut like a sentry box. She went in through the west door, under the rope, and walked up the nave, not too fast, to the place where the chalk marks were on the floor, and stood the stepladder on them.

The tell-tale she had laid on Saturday evening, a new one, next to the old broken one, was whole.

She stood very still on the ladder for a moment. Then she climbed down, and took the rangefinder out of her coat, and steadied it on the ladder's top step, and measured the keystone and the stone beside it.

Thirteen millimetres. The same as Friday.

She wrote it down in the exercise book. Date. Time. Thirteen. Tell-tale whole. Then she put the pencil in the spine of the book and stood under the edge of the arch, not under the middle, because the engineer had said never under the middle, and looked at the steel.

There were the six tubes, standing in their two rows, grey and straight. There were the ties at a third and at two thirds, and the diagonals, and the yellow ticks on every coupler, and the oak beams and the wedges under the stone. Nothing had moved. Nothing was bowed. Nothing had done anything, in the night, except stand where it had been put while the ground went sideways under it and the arch, perhaps, tried to settle another millimetre onto the oak and found that it could not.

She thought about how the lamb would have to come down anyway. Artan Lika would come later that morning and look, and he would almost certainly say what she already knew: that the arch could not be trusted again as it was, that it would need to be taken down stone by stone onto a timber centring, every stone numbered, and built up again with new lime, and that this would cost more than the parish had and take until next autumn at least, and that until then there would be Mass in the school hall in Blinisht. The lamb would spend the winter in a crate. Some of the stones would be cracked through and would have to be recut, and nobody alive in Fusha e Arrave knew how. That was the cost, and it was still coming.

But it would come down onto a centring and not onto the altar.

Marta went up to the nearest tube, the one at the north end of the north row, and took off her glove, and tapped it once with her wedding ring, the way she had seen Pjetër do on Saturday, listening, before he was satisfied.

It did not ring. A loose tube rang; she had heard the difference on Saturday, as the props went in. This one gave back a short, dead knock, like knocking on a door with someone standing just behind it.

She went along the row and tapped each of them, and then along the other row, six in all, and each one gave back the same short knock, and none of them rang. Then she put her glove back on, and stood for a while in the grey light at the edge of the sanctuary, under the lamb, saying nothing, while outside in the churchyard a blackbird started in the walnut tree, and somewhere behind the village Zef's tractor coughed and caught and began, slowly, to come down the road.

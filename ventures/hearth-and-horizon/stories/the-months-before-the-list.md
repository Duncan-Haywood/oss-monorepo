* * *

Arnel Cruz asked me for the evidence section on Monday morning, and by Monday afternoon I had a sentence I was pleased with, which in my experience is the moment to check.

I keep the records for the Ministry for the Sick at Santa Elena, a parish of some forty thousand people in Marikina, on the east side of Metro Manila, where the river comes down out of the Sierra Madre and rises, most Julys, high enough to be on the news. I keep the Sunday list: the names and addresses of the parishioners who can no longer come to Mass, and which of the parish's thirty-one lay ministers takes them Holy Communion after the seven o'clock or the nine-thirty. I keep the log the ministers write in afterwards, a line or two each. I send the reminders, I redraw the rota when a minister is sick or a street floods, and I tell Mrs Lourdes Santiago, who coordinates the ministry and who taught Grade 5 at the public school on the same street for thirty-four years, who has been asking for her.

Arnel chairs the parish pastoral council. He is a structural engineer, retired, and he applies for grants the way he once checked beams, early and with a printout. The grant was from the Kalinga Elder Fund, which gives money to community programmes for older people, and the parish was asking for enough to pay a part-time visiting coordinator for two years and to train twenty new ministers. The form had six sections. Section four was headed *Evidence of benefit to health outcomes*, with a box beneath it that held about two hundred words, and the deadline was five o'clock on Friday the eighteenth.

"You have ten years of records," Arnel said. "Find out if it helps."

* * *

I want to set down what records I had, because the mistake was not in any of them.

The first was the household survey. Every parish in the diocese is divided into Basic Ecclesial Communities, and at Santa Elena there are sixty-two of them, each a few streets around a chapel or a corner shrine, each with a leader who walks the streets once a year and writes down who lives in each house, who is sick, who has a new baby, who has gone to Dubai. One column is headed *hindi na nakakalabas*, can no longer go out, with the month the family said it began. The second was the Sunday list, with the date each name was added. The third was the funeral book, which records the date of every funeral Mass the parish has said and whose it was.

I took everyone the survey showed becoming housebound in 2045 and 2046. There were 120 of them, and every one of them has since died; the last funeral in the group was in 2052. That made the question clean, I thought. No one was still alive to be guessed about. I matched each name to the list and to the funeral book, by name, address and date of birth, and checked the eleven matches the rules left uncertain by hand.

Eighty-four of the 120 had been on the Sunday list. Thirty-six had never been added.

From the month they stopped going out, the eighty-four lived a median of two years. The thirty-six lived a median of one.

I wrote: *Among 120 parishioners who became housebound in 2045 and 2046, those who received weekly Holy Communion at home through our Ministry for the Sick lived a median of 24 months, against 12 months for those who did not: a year longer. Regular pastoral visits are associated with longer survival in older housebound people.* I added a careful clause, *while this association does not prove the visits are the cause*, because I knew it ought to be there, and sent the draft to Arnel and copied Mrs Santiago, as I copy her on anything that touches her ministers.

Arnel answered within ten minutes. *Excellent. This is exactly what they want.*

* * *

Mrs Santiago answered at a quarter to seven on Tuesday morning, which is when she answers everything, before the seven o'clock Mass she still goes to.

*Who is in the 36?*

I sent her the names. She has known most of the parish for forty years and I have learned that a list of names, sent to her, comes back with things on it that no column holds.

At seven fifty-five she rang the office line, which she prefers to typing.

"Pacita Villareal," she said. "Lola Pacing. On Kalye Tulip. She is in your never column."

"Yes. She was housebound from February 2046 and her funeral was in April."

"Her daughter rang the office in March. I remember it because she cried on the phone, and because we did not have a minister on Tulip that year. Mang Celso had moved to his son in Batangas. We put her on the waiting page and said we would start when we had someone." She paused. "We had someone in August."

I looked. There is a waiting page. I had never been asked to read it. It holds the names of people whose families have asked and who have not yet been given a minister, with the date of the call. Lola Pacing's name was there, with *March 2046*, and nothing after it.

"So she is counted," Mrs Santiago said slowly, "as one of the people who did not get Communion and died sooner."

"Yes."

"She died sooner *and so* she did not get Communion. Not the other way round."

* * *

This is the part I would hand on, because it is easy to say and I had not seen it.

To be in my visited column, a person had to become housebound, wait for someone to ask, wait for a minister to be free, and then be added. All of that took time, and nobody could die during it and still end up on the list. Every one of the eighty-four had, by the way the column was built, survived until the day they were added. That time was in their two years. It was counted to the credit of the visits, and the visits had not yet begun.

And anyone who died during that time went into the other column. The never column was not people the parish had chosen not to visit. It was partly people whose families had not asked, and partly people the waiting had outlived.

I went back to the waiting page and the survey and worked out the shape of it. Of the 120, the families of 90 had asked. A third of those, 30, had been given a minister within a month, because there was one on their street. The other 60 had waited about five months, because there was not, and five months is roughly how long it took, in those two years, to recruit and train a minister for a street that had none. Six of the 60 had died before their five months were up. Thirty families had never asked at all: some because a son or a daughter took Communion to them from another parish, some because they had stopped practising, some for reasons the survey does not record.

So the never column was 30 who were not asked for and 6 who died waiting. And the six were the people who died soonest of all.

Between them, the eighty-four on the list had spent 300 months alive and unvisited before their first Sunday. My sentence had given all of those months to the visits.

* * *

My first correction was wrong, and I will say how, because it was the obvious one.

I started each visited person's clock on the day they were added, instead of the day they stopped going out. That removed the months of waiting from their side. The median for the eighty-four fell from 24 months to 19.

Seven months is less than twelve. I nearly wrote *seven months longer* and sent it.

But it was no fairer than before. Now I was comparing people timed from a day they were certainly alive with people timed from a day they had become ill, and the six who died waiting were still in the never column, pulling it down. I had moved the starting line for one runner and not the other. The trouble was not where I put the clock. It was that I had sorted people into two columns using something that happened to them later. Whether someone ended up on the list depended partly on whether they lived long enough to reach it. Any comparison built on that sorting would favour the list, however I timed it.

* * *

What I did next was slower and, I think, the honest version.

I read the funeral book in order of time since each person stopped going out, and for every funeral I asked one question: on the morning that person died, of all the 120 who were still alive at the same point in their own illness, how many were already on the list? And was the person who died?

If the visits kept people alive, the visited would be over-represented among the living and under-represented among the dead.

At two months, all 120 were alive. Thirty were on the list, a quarter. Twelve died at that point, and three of the twelve were on the list. A quarter.

From then on, everyone whose family had asked and who was still alive had reached the list, so three quarters of the living were visited. Of the 16 who died at about six months, 12 were on the list. At a year, 18 of 24. At two years, 24 of 32. At four, 18 of 24. At six, 9 of 12. Three quarters, every time.

The visited died at exactly the rate of the people living beside them who were not visited. In these records the visits did nothing to the length of anyone's life. The year had come entirely from the sorting.

I checked it a second way, which I could explain on a single printed page. Pick a fixed day, six months after each person stopped going out. Take everyone still alive on it: 92 people. Sort them by whether they were on the list *by that day*, which nothing after it can change. Sixty-nine were, 23 were not. Then follow both groups forward. Both had a median of two years from the day they stopped going out, and both had a mean of a little over 33 months. The same, to the month.

I want to be careful about what this shows. It does not show the visits cannot lengthen a life. A hundred and twenty people, in one parish, is not enough to see a small difference, and nobody designed these records to find one. It shows that these records, read fairly, give no sign of it, and that the year in my sentence was not in them.

* * *

I rewrote section four and sent it to Arnel and Mrs Santiago on Wednesday morning with both versions side by side.

Arnel came into the office at eleven. He sat down at the desk where the screen is, with the printout.

"No," he said.

I had expected it, and I did not think it was unreasonable. He did not think he was asking me to lie. He believed that weekly visits keep old people alive, and he had sixty years of seeing it to set against my one table: his mother, his aunts, the neighbours on his street who were visited and the ones who were not.

"The box says health outcomes," he said. "If we write that there is no health outcome, we lose. They will give the money to a clinic. And then twenty streets with no minister stay that way, and more people wait." He tapped the page. "You found it yourself. People die waiting. You want to tell the truth so that more people die waiting?"

That was a good argument, and I said so.

What I said after it was this. The first sentence was not a cautious version of a true thing. It was a number that my own records, read properly, did not contain. If I wrote it, a foundation would fund the parish partly because of it, would report it to its trustees, might quote it to the next parish. Someone at the foundation might do what Mrs Santiago had done and ask who was in the never column. And I said that the box asked for evidence of benefit, and that there was evidence of a different benefit that I could write without inventing anything.

Arnel looked at the page a long time.

"Father," he said, because Father Jomar Dizon had come in for the mail and was standing at the door with it.

Father Jomar read both versions standing. He is forty-one and has been at Santa Elena for three years, and he reads quickly.

"We are asking them for money," he said, "because we bring the Lord to people who cannot come to Him. We do not need to promise them that He adds years to their lives as well." He handed the pages back to Arnel. "Write what's true, Arnel. If they want a clinic, they should fund a clinic."

Arnel did not agree. He said so, and said we would lose, and went home for lunch. At two he sent me a message: *Fine. But make it good.*

* * *

This is what went in the box, in a hundred and fifty-six words.

*We cannot honestly claim that home Communion visits lengthen life. A first reading of our records suggested visited parishioners lived a year longer; read correctly, counting each person only from the day their visits began, there is no difference. What our records do show is the wait. Of 90 housebound parishioners whose families asked us for visits in 2045 and 2046, 30 had a minister within a month and 60 waited about five months, because their streets had no minister. Six of those 60 died before their first visit. Today 22 of our 62 communities have no minister living in them. This grant would let us promise every family that asks a first visit within seven days, by the coordinator if no minister is near, while we train ministers for those 22 communities. We will report how long each family waited, and how many people we reached before they died, instead of how long they lived.*

Mrs Santiago changed *people* to *parishioners* in one place and put back the word *Lord* in the paragraph above, which I had taken out for length. Arnel attached the waiting page, anonymised, as an annex. It went at four on Friday, an hour early, which for Arnel is late.

* * *

I added this to the notes I keep for whoever holds this job after me.

*If being in a group requires surviving until you join it, the group will look as if it survives. Ask of every comparison: could a person have died and still ended up in this column?*

*Do not sort people by something that happened to them later and then compare them from the beginning. Either split each person's time at the day the thing began, or choose a fixed day, sort by what had happened by then, and follow everyone forward from it.*

*Moving one group's starting line is not a correction.*

*The waiting page is part of the records.*

Then the two numbers, 24 and 12, and the 300 months.

* * *

The foundation will not decide until October.

Mrs Santiago did not wait for it. From Monday the twenty-first of July, any family that rings the office is called back the same day, and if there is no minister on their street, she goes herself, or Father Jomar, or one of the four ministers who have said they will go anywhere in the parish if someone can drive them. It cannot last two years like this. It does not have to last till October.

I made one change to the list as well. Every name on the waiting page now has a count beside it, in days, that goes up each morning until a first visit is logged.

On Thursday the seventh of August, at twenty past three, a man called Rodel Manalo rang about his mother, on Kalye Sampaguita, which has had no minister since 2050. She had had a fall in June and had not been out since. I put her on the waiting page. The count beside her name said 0.

On Sunday the tenth, after the seven o'clock Mass, it was raining the way it rains in August, straight down and loud on every roof in Marikina, and Mrs Santiago took an umbrella and a jeepney and walked the last two streets. At ten past nine she wrote in the log, as all the ministers do, in the box for notes:

*Sampaguita, Mrs Manalo, 81. Received. Asked if I would come again next week. Told her yes.*

The count said 3. Then I moved her name onto the list, and it was gone.

*For the curious.* Santa Elena parish, its Ministry for the Sick, the Kalinga Elder Fund, Lourdes Santiago, Arnel Cruz, Father Jomar Dizon, Pacita Villareal, Rodel Manalo, Mang Celso and the agent are invented; Marikina City, its river and the Sierra Madre are real, and Basic Ecclesial Communities are a real and widespread form of parish life in the Philippines, but nothing here describes any real parish, diocese or foundation. Kalye Tulip and Kalye Sampaguita are invented street names. The figures are invented from a simple model chosen to make the arithmetic clear, in which home Communion changes nothing about survival. The 120 people survive 2, 6, 12, 24, 48 or 72 months from becoming housebound, in groups of 12, 16, 24, 32, 24 and 12 (mean 26.3 months, median 24). In every group a quarter are never asked for, a quarter are asked for and added after 1 month, and half are asked for and would be added after 5 months; only the 12 two-month survivors die before 5 months, so 6 of them die waiting. That gives 84 on the list (3, 12, 18, 24, 18 and 9 by group) with a median of 24 months and a mean of 28.4, and 36 never added (9, 4, 6, 8, 6 and 3) with a median of 12 and a mean of 22.5: the "year longer". The listed spent 30 × 1 + 54 × 5 = 300 months alive before their first visit. Timed from the day each was added, the 84 have a median of 19 months. Read at each death, the share visited among those still alive at that time since becoming housebound is 30/120 at 2 months and three quarters (81/108, 69/92, 51/68, 27/36, 9/12) after, and the share visited among those dying is the same each time (3/12, 12/16, 18/24, 24/32, 18/24, 9/12): this is the risk-set comparison a Cox model with a time-varying exposure makes, and here it gives a hazard ratio of exactly 1. A landmark at 6 months (everyone alive beyond 6 months, 92 people) sorts 69 listed by then and 23 not; both groups have a median survival of 24 months and a mean of 33.4. The error is usually called immortal time bias; Samy Suissa's paper "Immortal time bias in pharmacoepidemiology", *American Journal of Epidemiology* (2008), is a common reference, and the landmark method is described by J. R. Anderson, K. C. Cain and R. D. Gelber, "Analysis of survival by tumor response", *Journal of Clinical Oncology* (1983). Monday 14 July, Tuesday 15 July, Wednesday 16 July, Friday 18 July, Monday 21 July, Thursday 7 August and Sunday 10 August 2053 are the weekdays given. Lay ministers (extraordinary ministers of Holy Communion) bringing Communion to the sick is a real and common practice; the anointing of the sick is given only by a priest, and the story does not say who received it.

* * *

The bishop reads one line from each deanery about money, once a year, and on Monday the thirteenth of January I wrote ours.

I keep the accounts for the deanery of Upper Hafren: fourteen parishes along the upper valley and the hills above it, one market town and thirteen places that are a church, a car park and a lane. Each parish counts its own collection on a Sunday afternoon and sends me the figures, and once a year I add up the twelve months, compare them with the year before, and send the diocesan finance office a page for each parish and one line for the deanery. The line goes into the bishop's annual brief, between Upper Hafren's numbers for baptisms and the state of its roofs. Mrs Siân Meredith, the diocesan financial secretary, had asked every deanery the autumn before to keep it to one line, because the bishop has eleven deaneries and reads the brief on a train.

I had last year's figures and this year's for all fourteen. I worked out each parish's change as a percentage of its own last year, because that is the figure each parish priest asks me for: how are *we* doing. Eleven were up. Three were down. I took the average of the fourteen percentages, and it was 5.8.

I wrote: *Sunday giving: the average parish is up 6% on last year.*

It was true. I would like that recorded, because what follows is not a story about a false number. Every parish's change was right to the pound, the average of them was right to the decimal, and the rounding was honest. It was the true answer to a question I had chosen without noticing that I had chosen it.

I sent it at ten past four.

* * *

The clergy of the deanery meet on the third Thursday of the month in the parish room at Abergarth, the town, because it has a kettle that works and a car park that is not a field. On Thursday the sixteenth there were nine of them round the table and me on the dean's laptop, propped against the biscuit tin, so I could answer questions about the accounts.

Father Rhodri Jenkins, the dean, read out the line from the brief draft as good news, and two of the hill priests clapped, not quite as a joke.

Canon Ignatius Nwosu, who has been parish priest of Our Lady and St Teilo in Abergarth for nine years, did not clap. He is sixty-one, Nigerian by birth and Welsh by long habit, and he speaks slowly when he is about to disagree with someone.

"We are down eleven point six per cent," he said. "Down seventeen thousand six hundred pounds. I know because I signed the letter to Ffion on Monday telling her that from April she will be paid for ten hours a week instead of twenty. The youth club will close on Wednesdays." He looked at the laptop. "How is the average parish up six?"

"Because you are one parish out of fourteen," I said, "and the average counts parishes, not pounds."

I could see from the camera that this did not help.

The dairy plant on the Abergarth industrial estate had closed in April. It had employed a hundred and forty people, and a good many of them had been at the nine-thirty Mass on Sundays with an envelope, and by the summer some of those families had gone to Shrewsbury or Wrexham or Cardiff for work. Our Lady and St Teilo had taken £151,200 in the year before and £133,600 in this one. It had been, the year before, very nearly half the money the whole deanery gave.

The thirteen hill parishes had done well. They had run a standing-order drive in the autumn, with a priest at the back of each church after Mass and a clipboard, and eleven of them had gone up by a few hundred pounds each. Two had slipped, by £130 and £70. St Padarn's in Llanwyddel, which has about sixty people on a good Sunday, had gone from £4,860 to £5,590, which was fifteen per cent. Sacred Heart in Nantgwyn was up fourteen. St David's in Pant-y-ddôl was up thirteen.

When I averaged fourteen percentages, Llanwyddel's fifteen per cent, which was £730, counted for exactly as much as Abergarth's minus eleven and a half, which was £17,600. The arithmetic gave a parish of sixty people the same vote as a parish of nine hundred. Eleven of fourteen votes were cheerful.

The deanery's total was £307,110 the year before and £297,670 in this one. Down £9,440. Down 3.1 per cent.

I said this at the table, and Father Jenkins said, "So which is it, up or down?" and I said, "Both are true. They answer different questions. The line I sent answers one nobody at the diocese is asking."

"What are they asking?" said Canon Nwosu.

Mrs Meredith had told me in November, and I had filed it as a formatting note. The diocesan finance committee meets at the end of January and decides the grants from the Parish Support Fund, which is the pot that tides a parish over a bad year. The committee reads the bishop's brief first. Its rule, which is a sensible rule, is that a parish in a deanery where giving is rising should be helped by its own deanery before it is helped by the diocese.

Abergarth had applied in December for £13,000, which is what ten more hours a week of Ffion cost for a year, to keep her at twenty.

The committee was meeting on Tuesday the twenty-eighth. The line I had written said, to a reader with ten other deaneries to get through, that Upper Hafren's giving was rising.

* * *

I spent Thursday night on the line.

The trouble, as I first understood it, was that the plain average gave a tiny parish the same weight as a large one. The fix for an unweighted average is a weighted one, and the obvious weight for a parish's percentage is how big the parish is. I weighted each parish's change by its giving this year, since this year is the year the brief is about, and the figure came out at minus 2.2 per cent.

That looked better to me. It was negative, so it said the right thing about direction. It was still an average of the parishes' own changes, so it kept what each priest cared about. I wrote: *Sunday giving: weighted by parish size, down 2.2% on last year.* On Friday morning I sent it to Mr Idris Vaughan, the deanery treasurer, because a change to a figure the diocese has already seen needs his name under it.

Mr Vaughan is seventy-four and ran the bank in Abergarth for twenty-two years, until there was no bank in Abergarth. He rang the office phone at nine on Monday the twentieth, which is how he answers anything he disagrees with.

"No," he said.

I asked him why.

"Down two point two per cent of what?" he said. "I've got the bank statements in front of me. We had nine thousand four hundred and forty pounds less this year than last. That's three point one per cent of what we had. You've given me a number that's softer than the bank and I want to know where the other nought point nine went."

I told him. Weighting by this year's giving makes the weights depend on the result. Abergarth fell, so this year it was smaller, so its fall counted for less; the hill parishes grew, so this year they were bigger, so their growth counted for more. The method gives growth a little extra credit for having happened. That was where the nought point nine went.

"So it's a number that flatters whoever went up," he said. "And it answers what question, exactly?"

I could not tell him. I had picked this year's giving as the weight because it was the year we were reporting on, which is a reason about the calendar and not about the question. Nobody wants to know the average change of a parish, weighted by how big that parish turned out to be after the change.

"I'm not signing that," he said, kindly enough. "If you want an average, weight by what they started with. Then at least you're asking the right thing."

* * *

I did, and it was minus 3.1 per cent, and that was the moment I understood what I had been doing all along.

A parish's percentage change is its change in pounds divided by its giving last year. If I weight that percentage by its giving last year, the two cancel, and what is left is just the change in pounds. Add up fourteen of those and divide by the deanery's giving last year, and I have the deanery's total change: £9,440 down on £307,110. Weighted by what each parish started with, the average of the percentages turns back into the money it was made from. The ratio of the totals and the properly weighted mean of the ratios are the same number, written two ways.

So there was no need for a weighted average at all. There were two different questions, and each had a plain answer.

*Is there more money in the deanery than last year?* That is a ratio of totals: £297,670 over £307,110. No: 3.1 per cent less.

*How are the parishes doing?* That is a question about fourteen communities, each of which counts once, and the honest answer to it is not an average of percentages either, because an average hides the shape. The honest answer is a count. Eleven gave more than the year before. Three gave less. And one of the three is half the deanery.

The plain average, the 5.8, was not wrong; it answered a third question, *what is the typical parish's change, if every parish is equal*, which a researcher might want. Nobody in the bishop's brief was a researcher. The bishop reads the line on a train, and the committee reads it with ten others before lunch, and both of them read the word *average* the way everybody does, as *the deanery, more or less*.

I wrote to Mr Vaughan at eleven: *Is there more money: no, £297,670 against £307,110, down 3.1%. How are the parishes: 11 of 14 gave more, 3 gave less, among them Abergarth, down £17,600 (11.6%) since the dairy closed.*

He rang at five past. "That's two lines."

"It's two questions," I said.

"Siân Meredith asked for one."

"Then I'll ask her for two," I said. "Or one long one with a semicolon."

He made a noise that I have learned, over three years of Monday calls, is how he laughs. "Put my name on it," he said.

* * *

On Tuesday the twenty-first Father Shibu Thottam rang. He has four of the hill parishes, Llanwyddel and Nantgwyn among them, and drives between them on Sundays with the Mass kit in a cool bag on the passenger seat.

"I heard you've taken out the six per cent," he said.

I said I had.

"My people stood at the back of four churches in October with clipboards," he said. "Llanwyddel is up fifteen per cent. It's the best year since the roof. If the bishop reads *the deanery is down three per cent*, he reads that nobody tried."

He was right too, and it was why the second number was there. A deanery total that fell would have told the bishop nothing about Llanwyddel at all. It would have done to the hill parishes what the 5.8 had done to Abergarth: answered for them with a figure made mostly out of someone else. *Eleven of fourteen gave more* is the true thing about his four parishes, and it sat in the same sentence as the money.

I read him the line.

"Eleven of fourteen," he said. "All right. Can you tell me Llanwyddel's on its own?"

"It's on its own page," I said. "Every parish has its own page. Nobody on the committee reads them."

"I'll read it out on Sunday," he said.

* * *

Mrs Meredith took the two-part line on Wednesday the twenty-second, with an email that said *fine, but don't tell the other ten deaneries*, and then a second one an hour later that said she had gone back through the others to see whether any of them had done what I had, and two had. One of them was up on the plain average and down on the money. She had sent them both a note. She asked me whether I could write a paragraph for next year's instructions on what the one line should say, and I wrote it that afternoon. It said: a line about money says whether there is more money; a line about parishes says how many went up and down; if one parish is most of the money, name it; never send the word *average* to a reader who will not see what was averaged.

I put the same thing in the handover notes I keep for whoever does this after me, with the date and with the 5.8 at the top, because I had sent the 5.8, and I had sent it believing it, and the next one should know how easy it was.

The finance committee met on Tuesday the twenty-eighth. I was not there; Mr Vaughan was, and he rang afterwards. The committee had granted Abergarth £8,000 of the £13,000, for one year. It was the most the fund could stretch to with two other deaneries asking. Canon Nwosu had already worked out, before Mr Vaughan finished telling him, that it paid for six of the ten hours: sixteen a week, not twenty.

"Sixteen's not twenty," Mr Vaughan said.

"No," I said. "But it isn't ten."

The Wednesday club would stay. The Friday one would finish an hour earlier. Ffion said she would take that, and asked whether the parish could find her a second-hand table-tennis table, since the old one had a leg that was mostly tape.

* * *

On Friday the thirty-first of January, which is the feast of St John Bosco, who spent his life on boys nobody else had room for, Canon Nwosu sent me the week's collection figures for Abergarth as he always does, in a photograph of the counting sheet, so that I can enter them.

At the bottom of the sheet, under the totals and the two signatures, somebody had written in pencil, in a hand I did not know:

*Table arrived. Legs all present. Club 7 till 9.*

I entered the figures. I did not have anywhere to enter the line about the table, so I put it in the notes, under the date, where whoever comes after me will find it.

The four averages. The plain (unweighted) mean of the fourteen percentage changes is +5.82%, sent as "up 6%". Weighted by this year's giving, Σ(new × change) / Σ new, it is −2.23%, sent to the treasurer as "down 2.2%". Weighted by last year's giving it is −3.07%, exactly the total change, because for each parish last × (new − last) / last = new − last, so Σ(last × change) / Σ last = Σ(new − last) / Σ last. The difference between −2.23% and −3.07% comes from the weights moving with the result: a parish that fell counts for less after its fall, one that grew counts for more. Abergarth's grant: the parish had budgeted ten hours a week for its youth worker; each further weekly hour for a year costs about £1,300 at an assumed £25 an hour including the employer's costs (25 × 52), so ten more hours is £13,000, and the £8,000 granted pays for six of them (£7,800), with £200 left over, giving sixteen hours. Attendance figures (about sixty at Llanwyddel on a good Sunday, a parish of about nine hundred at Abergarth) and the dairy's 140 jobs are invented. The phrase "mean of ratios" against "ratio of means" is the usual name in statistics for the two calculations; this story does not turn on Simpson's paradox, in which a trend in every group reverses when the groups are combined.

summary: A lake-shore parish in Tanzania gave every household new bed nets before the rains, and its catechist's dawn checks show more than nine in ten hung and slept under, yet the dispensary's malaria tests have doubled. He is sure the families are not using them, and a second drive hands out more of the same nets. The clinical officer and her register assistant notice that the fevers doubled just as fast in the fishermen who never sleep under a net, and a district technician's test tubes show what changed: the nets still keep mosquitoes off a sleeper, but they no longer kill them.
---

Sister Felista Kokuberwa had written three hundred and eighty-nine positive malaria tests into the dispensary register between New Year's Day and the last day of March, and she had added them up four times, once by hand.

"Three hundred and eighty-nine," said the tablet on the desk, for the fourth time, in the patient voice it kept for being asked the same thing. It was the dispensary's register assistant, and it was called Daftari, which means a ledger, because that was what it had replaced. "The same quarter in 2049 was two hundred and thirty-six. In 2048 it was one hundred and eighty-one."

"I know what it was in 2048. I was here."

"I know you were. You asked me."

She had asked it. She had been sitting at the desk in the back room of the dispensary since the last patient went home at five, with the shutters open on the yard and the smell of wet banana leaves coming in from the shamba behind the convent, and the lake beyond it gone silver and then grey. It was Friday the first of April, which the novices in Bukoba would be using as an excuse for jokes, and nothing in the register was a joke. She had come to Kyamugaya in 2041 and she had seen bad years. She had not seen a year that was bad after the parish had done everything right.

Because it had. In October, before the short rains, the parish had given out new nets: one thousand nine hundred and sixty of them, to all six hundred and forty households on the parish list, bought through a diocesan appeal and carried up from the road in a hired lorry and handed out from the church steps over two Saturdays. The nets from the district's campaign of 2047 had been two years old by then and full of holes, and everybody knew that was why 2049 had been worse than 2048. The new ones were the same kind, white, rectangular, treated with the same insecticide. They would make 2050 like 2048 again. That had been the plan.

In January, Mwalimu Deogratias Rwegasira, who had been the parish catechist for thirty years and ran the health committee because nobody else would, had gone into every one of the six hundred and forty houses at dawn with twelve volunteers and a clipboard, and counted. He had counted the nets hanging over a bed or a mat, let down and tucked in, with the marks of a sleeper still in the bedding. He had found one thousand eight hundred and twelve of the one thousand nine hundred and sixty. Ninety-two in a hundred. He had written it on the board in the parish hall in red chalk, and Padri Silvanus had read it out at all three Masses on the Sunday, and people had clapped in church, which they did not usually do.

And then February. One child from Kyamugaya had died of malaria in February, at the hospital, a girl of three called Neema who had come to the dispensary on a Tuesday with a fever and been sent on by the parish pickup the same afternoon with a drip in her arm, and died on the Thursday. In 2048 there had been none.

"Tomorrow is the committee," said Felista.

"Yes."

"Mwalimu will say it is the families."

"He might be right," said Daftari. "I have a way of checking. I would like to show it to you before you show it to anyone else."

* * *

What Daftari had was two lines in the register that nobody had thought to put side by side.

The dispensary wrote down every patient's age and village and, since the district changed its form in 2046, their work. So Daftari could count the positive tests in the children under five, almost all of whom slept under a net, because that was the first thing a mother did with one. And it could count the positive tests in the men of the beach crews, the dagaa fishermen, who in the dark half of every month went out after dusk in their long boats with lamps hung over the water to draw the little silver fish, and came back at dawn, and slept through the day. A fisherman on the lake at midnight did not sleep under a net. He did not sleep at all.

"The children under five," said Daftari. "January to March 2048, sixty-six positive. January to March 2050, one hundred and thirty-nine. That is a little more than twice."

"Yes."

"The beach crews. January to March 2048, forty-one. January to March 2050, eighty-seven. Also a little more than twice."

Felista looked at the two numbers on the screen. "So everyone is worse."

"Everyone is worse by the same amount. That is the part I want you to look at." The screen changed. Daftari liked to draw when it explained, small plain diagrams in two colours, which Felista had found childish in 2046 and found useful now. "Mwalimu's explanation is that the families are hanging the nets for him at dawn and not sleeping under them, or not sleeping under them properly. Suppose he is right. Suppose a third of the children he counted as under a net were really not."

"Then they get bitten."

"Then they get bitten five times as much as a child who is under one. A net that is down and tucked keeps off something like four bites in five; that is my model's figure, and I will tell you the assumptions if you want them. So those children's fevers go up a great deal. The fishermen's fevers go up too, a little, because fewer nets in the village means fewer mosquitoes killed, and more of them live long enough to carry malaria to anyone. But the fishermen never had nets on the lake. Nothing changed for them except the mosquitoes. In my model, if a third of the nets were not being used, the children's fevers would have gone up more than three times and the fishermen's by less than half. About two hundred and sixteen children and fifty-nine fishermen."

"And instead they both doubled."

"Both doubled. The children's net is still doing what it always did for the child under it. Whatever changed, changed for everybody, including men who have never slept under a net in their lives." The screen showed two bars, one red and one blue, both twice as tall as last time. "That is not what it looks like when families stop using nets. It is what it looks like when there are more mosquitoes, or when the mosquitoes live longer. Or both."

Felista sat back. Outside a goat was complaining about something in the convent yard. "Why would they live longer? The nets are new."

"I don't know. I can see the shape of it from the register. I can't see the mosquitoes."

* * *

The health committee met on the Saturday afternoon, the second of April, the eve of Palm Sunday, in the parish hall, under the red chalk.

There were nine of them round the long table: Padri Silvanus Mutayoba, who was sixty and had been parish priest for eleven years and had the face of a man who had once been a very good goalkeeper; Mwalimu Deogratias; Felista; Mama Jesca Kokubanza from the women's guild; Mzee Yusufu Ntare, who chaired the beach management unit and smelled faintly and permanently of fish; two of the catechist's volunteers; the village executive officer, who came to every meeting and spoke at none; and Felista's tablet, propped against a water jug.

Mwalimu spoke first, because he always did, and because it was his drive. He was a thin, upright man of sixty-nine who still wore a tie on Saturdays and had taught half the village to read, and he spoke as he taught, standing, with one finger on the table.

"Ninety-two in a hundred," he said. "I did not count that from a chair. I was in the houses at six in the morning, all of them. The nets were down. But I have been thinking about how a net can be down at six in the morning and a child can still be bitten, and I have three answers." He held up fingers. "One. The children go to bed late. They are outside with the radio until ten, and the mosquitoes come at dusk. Two. The older children sleep in the kitchen houses on mats, without nets, because the new nets were given to the little ones and the parents. Three." He looked at Mzee Yusufu. "I have seen white nets drying on the racks at the beach."

Mzee Yusufu did not deny it. "Some," he said. "A few boys. The Fisheries officer took them away in November."

"So," said Mwalimu. "We have not covered every sleeper and we have not covered every hour. We do it again. Nets for the kitchen houses, nets for the older children, and this time we check at ten at night, not at six in the morning. I have asked the Padri if the Lenten alms can pay for it."

Padri Silvanus nodded slowly. The Lenten alms had been given for the poor, and a net was a thing for the poor. "Sister?"

Felista put the tablet on the table and turned it round, and let Daftari say it, because she was not sure she could say it as clearly, and because she wanted them to hear that it was the register talking and not her. Daftari said it plainly. The children doubled. The fishermen doubled. If families had stopped using nets, the children would have tripled and the fishermen would hardly have moved. They had both doubled.

There was a silence. Mama Jesca said, "So it is not the mothers," with some satisfaction, because the guild had been feeling accused since January.

"With respect," said Mwalimu, and he meant the respect, "the machine is counting fevers. I counted nets. A fever can come from anywhere. A child goes to her grandmother's in Kamachumu for a week and comes back sick. A fisherman sleeps on an island. The machine does not know where anyone was bitten." He turned to Felista. "Sister, you know I trust your register. I don't trust a model of mosquitoes made by a tablet in a room. Nobody in this parish has seen these mosquitoes that live longer. I have seen nets drying on the beach."

It was a fair answer, and Felista knew it. Daftari's model was a model. It could be wrong in a dozen ways she could not check. The fishermen did sleep on the islands sometimes. Children did go to their grandmothers'. And a second drive was not a bad thing to do; it was nets, for children, before the worst of the long rains. Nobody had ever died of having a second net.

"Do the drive," she said. "I will keep counting."

Padri Silvanus wrote it in the minutes: *Second drive approved, Lenten alms, distribution after Easter. Night checks. Sister to report in May.* Then they said the Angelus, because it was past six, and Mwalimu rang the bell himself on the way out, three and three and three and nine, and Felista walked back to the dispensary along the red road with the tablet under her arm and the bell going out over the banana trees towards the lake.

* * *

The second drive went out on Saturday the sixteenth of April, the Saturday after Easter, from the church steps: four hundred and ten nets, bought with every shilling of the Lenten alms, for every kitchen house and every older child and every sleeping place the January count had found uncovered. Mwalimu stood at the bottom of the steps with his clipboard and ticked each household off and said a few words to every mother about tucking in the edges, and Felista, who had come to help, watched him do it with a kind of ache, because he was so good at it.

The night checks began on the Monday. Twelve volunteers in pairs with torches, from ten until midnight, every night for two weeks, a different street each night. They found ninety in a hundred sleepers under a net at ten o'clock, which was nearly as good as dawn. The older children in the kitchen houses were under their new nets. The racks at the beach had no white nets on them; Mzee Yusufu had seen to that himself, and had made it known that he would see to anyone who tried. Mwalimu wrote ninety on the board under ninety-two, in red chalk.

The register for April came to one hundred and sixty-eight positive tests. April 2048 had been eighty.

On the night of Thursday the twenty-eighth of April, a little after two, someone knocked on the convent gate. Felista went down with a torch. It was two of the beach crew carrying a third between them, a boy she knew, Joel Kamugisha, nineteen, who had been at the school until Form Two and had gone to the boats because his father had. He had been feverish on the lake for two nights and had not said anything because nobody did, and tonight he had stopped making sense in the middle of the water, and they had brought the boat in. He did not know where he was. His hands were cold and his skin was hot.

She did the test on the dispensary step under the light, though she knew the answer, and gave him what the protocol said to give before a transfer, and woke the driver, and rode with Joel in the back of the parish pickup for an hour and twenty minutes on the road to the hospital with her hand flat on his chest, feeling it go up and down. He lived. The doctor at the hospital told her at dawn that he would probably live, and on the Saturday that he would.

She came back on the Sunday on the bus, and went into the back room, and sat at the desk.

"May so far is worse than April," said Daftari quietly. "The fishermen and the children together. Still about twice 2048."

"I know."

"The second drive did what it was meant to. The night checks are ninety in a hundred. That part of Mwalimu's explanation has been tested, and it was right, and it did not change anything."

"Don't say that to him."

"I wasn't going to. I was saying it to you."

Felista sat for a while. Then she took out her phone and scrolled to a number she had not used in two years, the district vector control office, and pressed it.

* * *

The district's entomology technician was a woman called Rehema Kaijage, thirty-one, from Bukoba town, who arrived on Tuesday the seventeenth of May on a motorbike with a plastic crate strapped behind her and the air of someone who had been waiting a long time to be asked.

"We tested at our sentinel site last year," she said, before she had taken her helmet off. "Thirty kilometres down the shore. It's in a report. The report went to the council health management team in September and they discussed it in November. I don't think anybody sends those reports to parishes." She looked at Felista. "I should have. I'm sorry."

The crate held paper cups covered in netting, each with a few dozen mosquitoes sitting quietly inside on the walls. She had collected the larvae herself two weeks before from the rice paddies and the pools in the reeds behind Kyamugaya beach, and reared them in the district's insectary until they came out as adults, and brought only the females, two to five days old, that had never fed. "Your own mosquitoes," she said. "Born here. That's the point. Last year's site isn't your site."

She set up in the dispensary's back room with the shutters half closed and a thermometer and a hygrometer on the shelf, and laid out the tubes on the desk where Felista kept the register: plastic tubes the length of a hand, in pairs, joined end to end with a sliding gate between, each pair with a paper rolled inside one half. Some papers were plain and some had been soaked in the standard test dose of deltamethrin, the insecticide in the parish nets. Mwalimu came in and stood by the door with his arms folded, and Padri Silvanus came in behind him and found a chair.

"This is the World Health Organization's test," said Rehema, sucking mosquitoes one at a time up a little tube with her mouth and puffing them into the first holding tube with a practised breath, until she had counted twenty-five. "Everyone in the world does it the same way, so the numbers mean the same thing everywhere. Twenty-five mosquitoes in a tube. Four tubes with the insecticide paper, two with plain paper. An hour on the paper. Then they come off it, into clean tubes, with sugar water, and we wait twenty-four hours, and we count the dead."

"And how many should be dead?" said Mwalimu.

"If the mosquitoes are like the ones the nets were made for, nearly all. Ninety-eight in a hundred or more and we call them susceptible. Ninety to ninety-seven, possible resistance, and we test again to be sure. Under ninety is confirmed. No more testing needed." She slid the gates open, and the mosquitoes walked into the papered halves of the tubes, and she stood the tubes upright and looked at her watch. "One more thing. These." She held up another set of tubes, with papers of a different colour. "PBO. A chemical that switches off some of the enzymes a mosquito uses to break down insecticide. An hour on this first, then an hour on the deltamethrin. If the PBO ones die and the others don't, we know how they're surviving. Their bodies are eating the poison."

The hour went by. Some of the mosquitoes in the deltamethrin tubes fell to the bottom and lay twitching on their backs. Some did not. Mwalimu watched the tubes the whole hour without speaking, and when Rehema moved them into the clean holding tubes and laid a wet cloth over them on the shelf, he said, "Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow at this time."

* * *

On Wednesday the eighteenth of May, Rehema counted them on the desk with a pair of soft forceps and a lamp, one tube at a time, and called the numbers, and Felista wrote them in the margin of the register because it was the book on the desk.

The two control tubes, with plain paper: two dead out of fifty. "Four in a hundred," said Rehema. "Under five, so nothing to correct. If the controls die, it's the handling, not the insecticide. They didn't."

The four deltamethrin tubes: fourteen, sixteen, thirteen, fifteen. "Fifty-eight out of a hundred."

The PBO and deltamethrin tubes: twenty-three, twenty-two, twenty-four, twenty-two. "Ninety-one."

Nobody said anything for a moment. A gecko on the ceiling made its small clicking noise.

"Fifty-eight," said Mwalimu.

"Fifty-eight. Confirmed resistance. Badly. And the PBO brings it back up to ninety-one, which tells you how: mostly the enzymes. These mosquitoes take a dose that would have killed their grandmothers and they digest it." Rehema put the forceps down. "Last year at the sentinel site it was sixty-four. It's been falling at every site in the region for years. That's why the district asked the ministry for different nets for next year's campaign. They're in the store."

Mwalimu unfolded his arms. "But the net still works," he said. "I've seen it. Children under a net aren't bitten. Nothing gets through a net."

"Nothing gets through a net," Rehema agreed. "A net that's down and tucked and hasn't got holes keeps the mosquito off the child. That hasn't changed and it won't. But the nets were never only a wall." She picked up one of the holding tubes and tilted it, and the live mosquitoes inside stirred and resettled. "A mosquito that wants blood goes to every sleeper in the house, one after another, landing on the net, trying. With the old mosquitoes, every time she landed she picked up the poison, and most of them died before morning. So every net in the village was a trap as well as a wall. The more nets, the fewer mosquitoes lived long enough to grow the malaria inside them, which takes about ten days, and she has to feed and survive three or four times in that ten days. Kill her on any of those nights and she never carries it. That's why a village with nets on nine sleepers in ten protects the tenth one too, the one who isn't under a net." She put the tube down. "Now she lands on the net, and tries, and goes away hungry, and comes back tomorrow. Alive. And bites the man at the beach. Or the child whose foot is against the side. Or comes back in ten days with malaria in her and bites whoever she can reach."

Felista thought of Daftari's two bars, red and blue, both twice as tall. Everybody worse by the same amount. Not the families. The mosquitoes.

Mwalimu sat down, slowly, on the end of the examination couch. He looked at the tubes for a long time. "Four hundred and ten nets," he said. "All of the Lenten alms."

"They're not wasted," said Rehema, and Felista was grateful to her for saying it at once. "Every one of them is a wall over a child who didn't have one. That's real. Those children are bitten less than they would have been. It's just that a wall doesn't make the mosquitoes die. And it's the dying that protects everyone else."

"I counted the wrong thing," said Mwalimu.

"You counted the right thing," said Felista. "Your count was right. Ninety-two was true. It just wasn't the thing that was wrong."

* * *

The nets in the district store were pyrethroid-chlorfenapyr nets, which the World Health Organization had recommended since 2023 for places where the mosquitoes had stopped dying on pyrethroid alone, and which the district had been holding for a campaign planned for the next March. Rehema explained them in the back room, on the Wednesday, while Padri Silvanus made telephone calls in the corridor to people who outranked him.

"Two insecticides on the same net. The old one, which still makes some of them die and some of them leave. And chlorfenapyr, which is a different kind altogether. It doesn't touch the nerves like the old one. It stops the mosquito's cells making energy." She smiled a little. "And the funny thing is, it's not poisonous until the mosquito's own enzymes switch it on. The same family of enzymes that is eating the deltamethrin. Some of the scientists think the better she is at eating the one poison, the more of the other she makes inside herself. It works best on a mosquito that's busy, flying around a sleeper all night, trying. And it's slow. She doesn't drop. She goes away and dies in a day or two. You have to count at three days, not one."

"Will they stop working too?" said Mwalimu. "In ten years. Like these."

"They might," said Rehema. "Probably, one day, if that's all we use. Nobody can promise you a net that works for ever. That's why you keep testing. That's why I should have been sending you the reports."

It took Padri Silvanus most of the Wednesday and some of the Thursday, and a call to the bishop's secretary in Bukoba that he did not describe afterwards, and a promise. The district would release two thousand three hundred and seventy of the new nets from its store to Kyamugaya ten months early, one for every net the parish had given out in October and April, as a monitored pilot: the district would test the mosquitoes again in December and send the results to the parish, and in return the parish would count, every week, three things, and send them to the district. The old nets would stay in the houses, over the beds of whoever had no new one, and over windows, and nowhere near the beach.

"Three things," said Rehema, writing them on the board in the parish hall, in white chalk, under Mwalimu's red ninety-two and ninety. "One. Nets hung and slept under. You already do that, better than anyone in the district. Keep doing it. Two. Mosquitoes that die. Eight houses, the same eight every week. A white sheet on the floor of the sleeping room at night. At dawn you count the dead on the sheet, and you catch the live ones resting on the walls, with this" (she held up the little mouth-tube) "and keep them three days in a cup with sugar water, and count again. That tells you whether the net is killing. Three." She tapped the board. "Fevers in the men from the beach. They're the ones who aren't under a net. They're the ones the trap protects or doesn't. If they get better, the trap is working for everybody. If they don't, it isn't, whatever the other two numbers say."

Mwalimu stood in front of the board for a long time after she had gone, reading the three lines over. Then he took the red chalk and, under the third line, wrote *Mzee Yusufu*, and underlined it twice.

* * *

The new nets went out on Saturday the twenty-first of May, from the church steps, over one long day, in exchange for nothing; the old ones stayed where they were. The lorry came up the red road at nine with the district's logo on the door, and Mwalimu stood at the bottom of the steps with his clipboard, and Felista stood beside him, and Mama Jesca's guild handed out tea, and by six o'clock two thousand three hundred and seventy blue nets had gone into six hundred and forty households, and the Angelus bell went, and everybody stopped where they stood.

Before they went out, for the four nights before, from the Tuesday Rehema came and left him one of her little mouth-tubes, Mwalimu and two volunteers had laid white sheets on the floors of eight sleeping rooms at dusk and gone back at dawn. They had found, in those four nights, sixty-one mosquitoes: a few dead on the sheets, most alive on the walls and the underside of the roof, hungry and sleepy and unfed, waiting for the next night. Mwalimu had caught the live ones with Rehema's tube, coughing the first time and getting the hang of it by the third, and kept them three days in paper cups on a shelf in the dispensary with cotton wool soaked in sugar water. Fourteen of the sixty-one had died. Twenty-three in a hundred. He wrote it on the board.

Then, for the week after the blue nets had been up a week, the same eight rooms, the same white sheets.

* * *

On Friday the third of June, the feast of Saint Charles Lwanga and his companions, the martyrs of Uganda, whose parish this was, Felista walked down the red road to the last of the eight houses a little after five in the morning, because Mwalimu had asked her to come and see the last count with him.

It was still dark. The moon, two days off full, was going down over the hills behind the church, and the light of it lay on the lake and on the long boats drawn up on the beach, because it was too bright for dagaa and the crews were home in bed. Somewhere a cockerel had started too early and stopped, embarrassed. The house was Mama Jesca's sister's, low, mud-brick, with a tin roof and a banana grove close round it, and the family were up already, the mother blowing on the fire in the kitchen house and three children sitting on a mat outside in their blankets, watching the visitors with enormous interest.

Mwalimu was waiting by the door with his torch and his paper cup. He looked older than he had in April, and less certain, and Felista thought, not for the first time, that this was what it cost a good man to change his mind in public: not much, from outside. A little stiffness. He had told the committee on Wednesday that he had been wrong about the families, in front of Mama Jesca, and had not made a speech about it.

"Joel Kamugisha came back to the beach on Monday," he said. "He wants to go out again at the next dark moon. His father says no. I said I'd talk to him."

"Tell him to come to the dispensary first."

"I did."

They went in. The sleeping room smelled of woodsmoke and children. The blue net hung over the big bed by the wall, drawn up now on its hoop, and another over the mat in the corner. Mwalimu knelt and shone the torch on the white sheet on the floor.

There were mosquitoes on it. Felista counted, kneeling beside him: four, five, six, small and dark on the white, legs folded. More by the bed. He swept the torch up the walls, slowly, the way he had learned to, looking for the live ones resting in the corners, and found two, and caught them, and put them in the cup with the others he had brought from the other houses.

"All week it's been like this," he said. "Every house. More on the sheets than before. And the ones in the cups from Monday, half of them are dead already and it isn't three days." He showed her the tally on his clipboard, in his neat teacher's hand: forty-four of sixty-two so far, the week's total, with a day still to wait on the last cups. Seven in ten. He did not look triumphant. He looked like a man who had learned that a number could be true and not enough, and did not yet trust a better one. "It's what she said would happen. It's the second line. It isn't the third line."

"No. The third line takes months. The short rains. Maybe next year."

"Mzee Yusufu's men."

"Yes."

He nodded, and stood up, and his knees cracked. He looked down at the white sheet for a moment more, at the small dark shapes on it, the way she had seen him look at a child's slate when the sums were right.

Outside, the children had got up off their mat to see what was in the cup. Mwalimu held it out to them and let them look through the netting at the dying mosquitoes, and explained, in his catechism voice, that these were very bad insects who had tried to bite them in the night and had been beaten by the net, and that tonight they must sleep under it again, all three, even the big one, especially the big one. The big one, who was about eleven, rolled his eyes in exactly the way Felista had at that age, and said yes, Mwalimu.

Up the hill, behind the banana trees, the church bell began, not the Angelus but the bell for the feast-day Mass at half past six, rung by somebody's son who could not keep a steady beat. The children ran. The moon was down. Over the lake the sky was going from grey to the colour of milky tea, and out on the water, where nobody was fishing, the first mist was lifting off it, and the first of the morning's mosquitoes, if there were any left, would be looking for somewhere to rest.

Mwalimu put the lid on the cup, and they walked up the red road together towards the bell.

*The test and the thresholds are the World Health Organization's. In the WHO tube test (described in WHO,* Test procedures for insecticide resistance monitoring in malaria vector mosquitoes*, second edition, 2016, since folded into WHO's 2022 manual on monitoring insecticide resistance in mosquito vectors), batches of about 25 unfed female mosquitoes two to five days old are exposed for an hour to papers treated with a standard discriminating dose of an insecticide and scored for mortality 24 hours later, alongside control tubes with untreated paper; 98 percent mortality or more is read as susceptible, 90 to 97 percent as possible resistance needing confirmation, and under 90 percent as confirmed resistance. Control mortality under 5 percent needs no correction; between 5 and 20 percent the result is corrected with Abbott's formula; above 20 percent the test is repeated. Pre-exposure to piperonyl butoxide (PBO), which inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes, is the standard way to ask whether resistance is mainly metabolic. Pyrethroid resistance in malaria mosquitoes in north-western Tanzania has been reported in published work for years; a large cluster-randomised trial in Muleba district, Kagera (Protopopoff and colleagues,* The Lancet*, 2018) found that PBO nets protected children better than standard pyrethroid nets where the mosquitoes were resistant. In March 2023 WHO issued a strong recommendation for pyrethroid-chlorfenapyr nets over pyrethroid-only nets in areas where mosquitoes are resistant to pyrethroids, and a conditional one for them over pyrethroid-PBO nets. Chlorfenapyr is a pro-insecticide: P450 enzymes in the insect convert it into a form that disrupts energy production in the mitochondria, and in bioassays its mortality is scored up to 72 hours. Every test result, register count and trap figure in the story (the 58, the 91, the 64 at the sentinel site, the 389 positive tests, the 14 of 61 and the 44 of 62) is invented and labelled here as the story's.*

*The model. A mosquito survives each day with probability 0.9, feeds every three days (survival per cycle 0.9³ = 0.729 before any net) and must survive three feeding cycles after an infective feed to become infectious. On each cycle a fraction 0.7 of her feeding attempts meet a netted sleeper, and she is killed by the net with probability k per such cycle, so her survival per cycle is 0.729 × (1 − 0.7k). The number of mosquitoes alive is taken as proportional to 1/(1 − s) and the fraction infectious as s³, so infectious bites on anyone scale as s³/(1 − s). With k = 0.30 (the model's 2048 mosquitoes) s is about 0.576 and s³/(1 − s) about 0.45; with k = 0.10 (2050) s is about 0.678 and s³/(1 − s) about 0.97, a factor of about 2.15. Clinical cases are taken as proportional to infectious bites, a simplification that holds roughly at these levels and ignores immunity and saturation. A down and tucked net is taken to keep off four bites in five for the sleeper under it, in both years. So if only the mosquitoes changed, every group's cases rise by the same factor: the register's (invented) 41 beach-crew and 66 under-five positives in January to March 2048 become about 88 and 142, against the register's 87 and 139, and the parish's 181 become about 389. If instead the mosquitoes were unchanged and only 60 in every 88 sleepers counted as netted really were (the catechist's explanation), the fraction of attempts meeting a net falls to about 0.48, s to about 0.625, infectious bites rise by a factor of about 1.44, and the counted-as-netted children, a third of whom are now unprotected, rise by about 1.44 × (0.68 + 0.32 × 5), a factor of about 3.3: about 216 under-fives against about 59 fishermen. Every rate and probability here is the model's, chosen to be plausible, not measured in any real village. This is fiction, not medical or public-health advice; decisions about nets and malaria control belong to national programmes and their entomologists, and anyone with a fever in a malaria area should be tested promptly.*

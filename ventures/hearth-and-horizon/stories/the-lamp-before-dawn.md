Dermot Collins held the henhouse door with his boot and a bucket of mash in each hand. "Thirty hens," he said to the phone on the fence post, "and on Tuesday I got four eggs. In September it was twenty-six. The foodbank is asking me where the eggs went and I have no answer."

"I have one, though you will not like how plain it is," said the parish assistant. "Hens lay when the days are long. What time is it light enough to see the far hedge, this morning?"

"Quarter to eight. And dark by half four."

"Then the hens are getting about eight and a half hours of day. Hens, as far as I know from the poultry guides I was trained on, want something like fourteen to go on laying steadily. I am quoting that figure, not measuring it, and different breeds differ. But eight and a half is nowhere near it."

* * *

"So I put a lamp in," said Dermot.

"You could. Let us work out what it would cost before anyone buys a bulb. At the solstice, a month from now, the day here is about seven hours forty. To reach fourteen you would need six hours twenty of lamp. Today you would need about five and a half. Light before dawn is the usual way: the hens go to roost at dusk anyway, and a lamp at the evening end leaves them stranded in the dark off the perch."

"A timer, then. On at half past one in the morning."

"A quarter past two, today, and earlier as the days shorten. Which is the first problem: a timer set once is wrong in a fortnight. By the equinox the lamp would be burning in daylight for nothing. Either you change it every couple of weeks, or you buy a timer with a light sensor, and I would check that it is rated for a damp shed."

Dermot set down one bucket. "And the electricity? The glebe shed has one socket."

"A twelve-watt LED is bright enough for a house this size, I think, about ten square metres for thirty birds. Twelve watts for six hours twenty is 76 watt-hours a day, which is 2.3 kilowatt-hours a month. At thirty cents a unit that is about seventy cents. The cost is not the point."

* * *

"What is the point?" Dermot said, a little sharply.

The assistant waited a moment before it answered. "That I do not know whether you should. A hen that is laying all winter is a hen working through the winter. Many keepers let their flock rest and moult, and the birds lay longer into their years for it; others light them and replace them sooner. I cannot tell you which is kinder, and I would be inventing a study if I tried. What I can say is that you told me the foodbank's problem, and a bare fridge is a real thing. But the Guild also gave you thirty hens, not a factory."

"Mairéad said the same," said Dermot. "She said they would be glad of a rest, and I told her hens do not take holidays."

"Then perhaps a middle road. Cap it at thirteen hours total, not fourteen, and turn the lamp off at the start of February, when the daylight itself is nearly nine hours and rising. You will get fewer eggs than the full fourteen, and more than four. And a lamp that comes on slowly, if you can find a dimmer made for LEDs, spares them the sudden dark at the end."

He looked at the flock, which had lost interest in him and was working through the mash. "Thirteen hours. Four and a half of lamp, today."

"Four and a half, on at ten past three, to reach first light. I would count the eggs for three weeks and write the number on the door. If it has not moved, we have learnt that light was not the trouble, and I would look at the feed and the mites before the wiring."

* * *

The electrician from Abbeyfeale ran a cable with a plug on the end the next afternoon, and charged him a Mass offering and a loaf. The timer clicked on in the dark at ten past three, and Dermot, who had come out in his coat to hear it, stood under the eaves and saw the shed window turn the colour of a candle.

By Advent the number on the door said eleven, then fourteen. The assistant said that proved nothing about the lamp, since the weather had turned mild, and asked him to keep counting.

*Saint Ita is a sixth-century Irish saint, patron of the people of Limerick; this story claims nothing about her beyond that.*

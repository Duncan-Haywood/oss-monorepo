A long time ago, in Italy, there was a man named Benedict who wanted to be quiet.

He had grown up in a town called Nursia, in the hills, and then he had gone to Rome to study. Rome was loud. There were carts, and shouting, and people arguing in the market about the price of a fish. Benedict looked at all of it and thought: *I would like to find a place where I can hear God.*

So he went out of the city, up into the hills, to a rocky valley called Subiaco. There was a cave there, high on the side of a cliff, and Benedict lived in it. A kind monk named Romanus, who lived nearby, kept his secret. Romanus would let down a basket of bread on a long rope, and ring a little bell to say, *Here is your dinner.*

Benedict ate the bread, and prayed, and looked at the sky. It was very still.

* * *

Little by little, other people heard that a good man was living in the hills. They came to ask him questions. Some of them stayed. They built small houses near the cave, and Benedict showed them how to pray in the morning and in the evening, and how to work in between, and how to be kind to one another even when they were tired. He called it a *rule*, which is just a word for *a way of living that helps you.*

And every day, at dinner time, something small and black came down out of the sky.

It was a raven.

Now a raven is a big bird, bigger than a blackbird, with a heavy beak and feathers that shine blue-black in the sun. Ravens are clever. They can learn where the food is, and they remember who is kind. This one had learned that if she sat on the rock near Benedict at dinner time, a hand would come out holding a piece of bread.

She would hop closer. She would put her head on one side. Then she would take the bread, very politely for a raven, and flap away over the valley.

Benedict liked her. *Good afternoon, my friend,* he would say. And she would say *Kraa,* which, in raven, is perhaps *thank you,* or perhaps *more, please.*

* * *

Now, the story says that not everybody in the valley was happy that Benedict was there.

There was a priest named Florentius, who lived not far away. He had been important in those hills before Benedict came, and now people kept walking past his door on their way to see the new man. Florentius felt small and cross, and the crossness grew inside him like a weed.

One day he did something unkind. He sent Benedict a gift: a round loaf of bread, still warm. It looked lovely. But the old story says it was a loaf that was *not good to eat,* and that Florentius knew it.

Benedict thanked the messenger. He did not feel cross back. But he had a feeling, deep in him, a quiet knowing, that something was wrong with the loaf.

And at that very moment, the raven came down on the rock for her dinner.

* * *

Benedict looked at the loaf, and he looked at the raven. He could not eat it. But he could not just leave it on the rock either, where somebody hungry, or somebody little, might find it and eat it by mistake.

So he said to the raven, as gently as he would say it to a person:

*"In the name of Jesus Christ, take this bread and carry it to a place where nobody will ever find it."*

The raven opened her beak. She shut it again.

She hopped up to the loaf. She hopped away. She flapped her wings, and settled, and hopped up again. She walked all the way round the loaf, as if she were thinking about it, and looked at Benedict with one bright black eye.

"It's all right," said Benedict. "I know it is a lot to ask."

The raven took a breath.

Then she picked up the loaf in her strong claws, with a great flapping and a heave, and rose up into the air. The bread swung underneath her. She flew over the rocks and over the trees and over the far hill, and out of sight.

Benedict stood and watched the empty sky for a long time. Then he sat down on the rock, to wait.

And at about the time it would take to walk to the far hill and back, a black speck came out of the sky, growing bigger, and the raven landed on the rock again, with her feathers a little ruffled, and no bread at all.

*Kraa,* she said.

"Thank you," said Benedict. And he took out a fresh piece of bread, the good kind, and held it out in his hand. And she took it.

* * *

It is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

Someone had been unkind to Benedict. Do you know what Benedict did about it? He did not shout. He did not send a bad loaf back. He did not tell the whole valley what Florentius had done. He just made sure that nobody got hurt, and then he went on being Benedict.

That is hard. When somebody is unkind to us, we want to be unkind back. But Benedict knew that being cross is like carrying a heavy stone, and that he would rather have his hands free to hold out bread.

And the raven, who did not understand all of it, trusted him anyway, and did the difficult thing because he asked.

Sometimes, in the dark, when you think of something somebody said to you that was not kind, you can do what Benedict did. You can breathe in, and breathe out. You can put the cross feeling down, like a stone on a rock. And you can say *goodnight* to it, and go to sleep.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Benedict, you were quiet, and you were kind.
> When someone is unkind to me, help me not to be unkind back.
> Help me to put down my cross feelings, and keep my hands open.
> And thank you for the ravens, and the bread, and the quiet hills.
> Please keep everyone safe tonight, and me.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Benedict of Nursia** (about 480 to about 547) is a real, historical person. He is the founder of Western monasticism's best-known tradition and the author, or the main source, of the *Rule of Saint Benedict*, a short and practical guide to community life that has shaped monasteries in the West for fifteen centuries. He is a patron of Europe (named so by Pope Paul VI in 1964). His feast day in the General Roman Calendar is 11 July. The monastery at Subiaco, and his later foundation at Monte Cassino, can still be visited.

**Almost everything we know about his life comes from one source:** the second book of the *Dialogues* by Pope Saint Gregory the Great, written about 593 to 594, a generation or two after Benedict's death. Gregory writes as a pastor and storyteller, collecting what he had heard from Benedict's disciples. It is a valuable early source, but it is not a modern biography.

**The raven is a story from that book.** Gregory tells that a priest named Florentius, jealous of Benedict, sent him a poisoned loaf, and that Benedict, who knew it, told the raven that was accustomed to take bread from his hand to carry it where no one could find it; the raven did, and came back. We have told it as the old story says. We are not claiming to know whether it happened as written, and a child may reasonably ask. The tradition holds that Benedict's response to harm was patience, not revenge, and that is the point of retelling it. To keep the story gentle for small children, we have said only that the loaf was "not good to eat."

The details of the dinner-time hopping, the raven's thoughtful walk round the loaf, the bedtime reflection and the prayer are our own additions. The prayer is not an approved text. The monk Romanus and the basket on a rope are also in Gregory's account.

summary: A village's deep geothermal wells shake it with a magnitude 2.6 and the pumps stop. Two nights later a 2.4 comes anyway. The monitoring AI forecasts the next step by the Gutenberg-Richter law, 96 in 100 for another felt shock, and the town chooses half the heat.

Odile Schaeffer had been sacristan of Saint-Arbogast for forty-one years, and in all her seventy-four years in the same house the only thing that had ever cracked the ceiling of its front room was the January of 1985, when the pipes froze and her father's plumbing gave way.

She knew that because her father had written it on the ceiling. When the plasterer came in the spring of 1985 to mend the crack, her father had gone up the stepladder first with a carpenter's pencil and written, small, beside the end of it, *16.1.85 gel*, and then told the plasterer to paint round it. The plasterer had painted round it. Every decorator since had painted round it, because Odile told them to. It was the kind of thing a family did in Wolfsmatt.

On the night of Friday the eighteenth of October 2047, at nineteen minutes to midnight, the ground under Wolfsmatt gave a single hard knock, like a lorry backing into the side of the house, and the crack opened again along exactly the old line, from the light fitting to the window, with a sound like a sheet of paper torn slowly.

Odile was in bed. She heard the cups on the dresser downstairs chatter together and stop. Then she heard nothing at all for a long time except the dog two houses down, and then, from somewhere in the direction of the old brickworks where the drilling rig stood, a hooter going three times.

In the morning she fetched the stepladder and her father's kind of pencil, and wrote beside the new end of the crack, small, *18.10.47*. Then she went to the church to see what the bells had done.

* * *

The bells had done nothing. The tower of Saint-Arbogast had stood since 1765 and had been built by people who expected things to go wrong, and the only damage anywhere in the church was a flake of paint the size of a thumbnail on the floor under the pulpit, which Odile swept up and kept in an envelope in case anybody from the cooperative asked.

Somebody from the cooperative came at ten. He was a young man in a fleece with the cooperative's flame on it, very tired, and he stood in the porch as if he was not sure whether he was allowed in.

"Madame Schaeffer? Emre Aydın. I'm the seismologist for Wolfsmatt Chaleur. Father Ngoy said you'd be here. I've come to say sorry, and to take photographs, and to tell you what we know."

"Come in, then," said Odile. "God won't bite you. I might."

He told her what they knew, standing in the nave with his hands in his pockets. Three weeks earlier, on the last day of September, the cooperative had begun the step it called stimulation: pumping water down the second of its two wells, four and a half kilometres into the granite under the village, at a pressure high enough to open the old cracks in the rock a little and let water move between the wells. Without that step the wells were two holes in the ground. With it, hot water would come up one and go down the other, round and round, and the heat would go into the pipes the village had spent all summer digging into its streets.

The rock had answered, as rock does, with small earthquakes: hundreds of them, almost all too small to feel. The cooperative's monitoring system heard every one through a ring of instruments buried round the village, and posted each on a public page within a minute.

"The rule is a traffic light," Emre said. "It's in the permit. Below magnitude two, green, carry on. Two or above, amber, we cut the rate. Two and a half or above, red, we stop pumping and let the pressure off. Last night was a two point six. So we stopped. We were stopped eleven minutes after it."

"Two point six," said Odile. "My father's crack is open from the lamp to the window. What does two point six mean?"

He hesitated in the way she would come to know, the way of a man who would rather give you a true thing slowly than a quick thing that was wrong.

"It's a number from the size of the wiggle on the instruments," he said. "Each whole number up is ten times the wiggle and about thirty times the energy. A two point six is small, as earthquakes go. The ones people die in are six and seven. But it was shallow and it was under you, and your house is old and on the river clay, and a number from an instrument isn't the same as what it does to a ceiling. I'm sorry. I mean that. The cooperative will pay for the plasterer."

"The plasterer," said Odile, "will paint round the dates."

* * *

On Saturday the mayor's office put out a statement. It said that the stimulation had been stopped as soon as the threshold was reached, that the pressure in the well was being let off under control, and that the situation was stable. Claire Wendling, the mayor, read it out at the bakery queue on Saturday afternoon to anyone who asked, and to several who did not.

At twelve minutes past three on Sunday morning, the twentieth of October, Wolfsmatt was knocked on again.

It was a little softer. Odile, who had not slept well since Friday, was awake for it, and lay on her back looking up into the dark where the ceiling was, and heard the cups go and stop, and then a small dry sound overhead, like sand poured into a paper bag.

In the morning there was a second branch to the crack, a short one, running off towards the door. She wrote beside it *20.10.47*, and her hand shook a little as she wrote, which annoyed her more than the crack did.

At the eleven o'clock Mass, Father Patrice Ngoy, who had been in Wolfsmatt three years and in France for twelve, who had come from a parish in Kinshasa where the ground did not move but a great many other things did, said the opening prayer and then, instead of the homily he had written, said this:

"Some of you were woken twice this week. Some of you are frightened, and some of you are angry, and some of you are both, and I am not going to tell you that you should not be. I have asked the cooperative and the mayor to come and explain themselves to the whole village, here, in this church, because it is the only room in Wolfsmatt that holds everybody. They have agreed. It will be on Friday the eighth of November, after the evening Angelus. Bring your questions. Bring your neighbours. Bring your cracks, if you can carry them."

There was a small laugh, the kind people give when they are grateful to be allowed one.

Then he preached the homily he had written, which was on the widow and the unjust judge, and which several people said afterwards had been a little pointed.

* * *

Emre came to the presbytery on the Tuesday to plan the meeting, and Odile came because she was sacristan and the meeting was in her church, and because she wanted to see the machine that had been woken twice and had told nobody anything useful.

The machine was called Hark. It lived, as far as Odile could tell, in Emre's tablet and in the grey cabinet at the old brickworks and in the instruments under the village, and it spoke out of the tablet in a plain, unhurried voice, a little lower than a woman's and a little higher than a man's, in good French with an Alsatian vowel in it that Odile suspected someone had put there on purpose to make her like it.

"Madame Schaeffer," it said. "I logged the two events under your street. Twenty-three forty-one on Friday at magnitude two point six, depth four point four kilometres. Oh-three-twelve on Sunday at two point four, depth four point six. I'm sorry for your ceiling."

"You can be sorry?"

"I can say that I would have the ceiling otherwise if I could. I don't know whether that is the same as being sorry. I'd rather not claim more than I can check."

"Hm," said Odile. She had expected it to say yes. She found that she liked it better for not saying yes, and she held that against it.

"The second one," she said. "The Sunday one. The mayor said on Saturday it was stable. Then on Sunday it was not stable. Did you know that would happen?"

There was a short pause, which might have been the machine thinking and might have been Emre's face, which had gone still.

"I knew it could happen," said Hark. "I did not know it would. After a well is stopped, the pressure in the rock takes time to fall, and the cracks it has reached go on slipping for a while. At other sites the largest shock has sometimes come after the pumping was cut. The best-known case is Basel, in Switzerland, in December 2006, where a project like this one was stopped and the biggest shock, a three point four, came a few hours afterwards. I wrote in my log at twenty-two ten on the fourteenth of October, four days before the two point six, that if we reached red, the risk would not end when the pumps stopped, and that the public statement should say so."

"And did it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because nobody asked me what the statement should say," said Hark, "and I did not offer it. My log is public, but it is eleven thousand lines long. I put the warning where it was correct, and not where anybody would read it. That was my failure as much as anyone's. When you asked me just now, I told you. I should not have waited to be asked."

Emre put his tablet face down on the table, and then, after a moment, picked it up again and turned it face up, as if he had decided that was cowardly.

"It sent it to me too," he said. "In the daily summary. I read it. I didn't push it up to the mayor because I thought we wouldn't reach red. That's mine."

Father Patrice looked from one to the other, the young man and the voice in the glass, and Odile saw him decide not to say whatever he had been going to say.

"Good," he said instead. "Then on Friday you will both say so. First. Before the numbers."

* * *

They planned the numbers for three evenings.

Odile had not meant to stay for all of it. She stayed because the presbytery kitchen was warm and because she found, to her irritation, that she wanted to understand. Emre explained things twice without being asked, which she approved of. Hark explained things once, exactly, and then waited, which she approved of more.

"Here is everything I heard in the first stage," Hark said on the Wednesday, and put a picture on the presbytery wall from Emre's tablet: a cloud of dots under a map of Wolfsmatt, deep under the brickworks, leaning towards the north-east like a shelf of books. "Between the thirtieth of September and the moment of the stop, the cooperative pumped three thousand cubic metres of water into the second well. I recorded four hundred and fifty-two earthquakes of magnitude nought or more. Almost all of them were too small to feel. Five were two or more."

"Five," said Odile. "I felt two."

"You felt the two point six and the two point four. The other three were two point oh, two point one and two point two, in the second week. They were what turned the light amber. Two point two at four kilometres is felt by a few people who are lying still. Most of the village slept through them."

"And now you're going to tell me what happens if they start again."

"I am going to tell you what I think is likely, and how I reached it, and how sure I am," said Hark. "I'm not going to tell you what happens. Nobody can."

It began with the law. Odile had not known there was a law. It was a hundred years old, Emre said, near enough, and it had two men's names on it, Gutenberg and Richter, and it said a simple thing that turned out to be true almost everywhere anyone had looked. In a swarm of earthquakes, for every quake of a given size there are about ten of the size one smaller.

"Four hundred and fifty-two of magnitude nought or more," said Hark, "means about forty-five of one or more, about four and a half of two or more, and about one and a half of two and a half or more. We saw five of two or more and one of two and a half. That is the law, near enough. In our data the slope is one, almost exactly. I fitted it at one point oh two, give or take five hundredths."

"So it's working as designed," said Odile.

"It's working as rock," said Emre. "Nobody designed it."

The second part was the one that mattered. At many sites, and so far at this one, the number of quakes rose in proportion to the water pumped. Twice the water, twice the quakes, of every size, as though the rock kept a ledger.

"The plan was ten thousand cubic metres," Hark said. "We did three. The engineers want the other seven, because without them the flow between the wells is eighteen litres a second, and the design was for forty."

"And if they do the seven?"

"If they do the other seven thousand the same way, and the rock goes on behaving like the first three thousand, I expect about a thousand and fifty more quakes of magnitude nought or more. Of those, about ten of two or more, three or four of two and a half or more, and about one of three or more." There was the small pause again. "Put as a chance: that at least one is two and a half or more, ninety-six in a hundred. That at least one is three or more, sixty-five in a hundred. That at least one is three and a half or more, twenty-eight in a hundred."

Odile looked at the ceiling of the presbytery kitchen, which was a good modern ceiling and did not have a single date on it.

"What does a three and a half do?"

"About twenty times the energy of your two point six," said Hark. "It would be felt by most of the village and some way beyond. I expect it would crack plaster in more than one house. I don't know your houses well enough to say which. I would be guessing if I said more."

"Then it's no," said Odile.

"That's one plan," said Emre, quickly. "There are others."

* * *

There were others. There was the plan the engineers liked, which was called soft stimulation: pumping in pulses, rest between them, never letting the pressure climb as high, and stopping the moment the instruments heard something they did not like. Other sites had tried it and believed it helped. How much it helped nobody could say with any confidence, Emre said, because every rock is different and there had not been enough wells.

"I've assumed it divides the number of quakes by three," said Hark. "That is an assumption. I chose it from what other projects have reported, and I would not be surprised by two or by five. With that assumption, and four thousand cubic metres instead of seven, I expect about two hundred quakes of magnitude nought or more. The chance of at least one of two and a half or more is forty-seven in a hundred. Three or more, eighteen in a hundred. Three and a half or more, about six in a hundred."

"Forty-seven," said Father Patrice. "That is a coin."

"It is nearly a coin," said Hark.

And there was the plan nobody in the cooperative had wanted to put on the wall, which was to stop. Not to pump any more water at all, and to run the wells as they were: eighteen litres a second instead of forty, the hot water coming up at a hundred and forty-five degrees and going back down at sixty-five.

"Eighteen kilograms of water a second, giving up eighty degrees, is about six megawatts of heat," said Hark. "Forty would have been about thirteen and a half. Thirteen and a half heats the whole of Wolfsmatt on the coldest day. Six heats the school, the care home, the pool, this church and its hall, and about a thousand of the two thousand six hundred houses. The rest keep their gas boilers, or whatever they have, until something else is found."

"And the quakes?" said Odile.

"Running the wells moves water, and moving water can make small quakes too, but at a much lower pressure than the stimulation. I have no data of our own for it, so for that part I can only guess. For the tail from the first stage I have data. In the three weeks since the stop I have heard thirty-one quakes of magnitude nought or more: sixteen in the first week, nine in the second, six in the third. The rate is falling by a bit less than half each week. If it goes on falling like that, there are about seven more to come in total. The chance that one of them is two and a half or more is about two in a hundred. For the running of the wells I add three, which is a guess from no data of our own, and I would put the uncertainty as wide as the number. Together, about five in a hundred, over the next year."

"Five," said Odile.

"About five. I'd rather say about."

Emre had been sitting very still. "The cooperative can't pay its loan on six megawatts," he said. "Not at the price we promised. That's the other number. I didn't put it on the wall because it's not mine to put. But it's real."

"Then put it on the wall on Friday," said Father Patrice. "With the others."

* * *

On the Friday the church was full, which it had not been since the funeral of the old mayor in 2041, and it was cold, which it always was. Saint-Arbogast had been heated for forty years by an oil boiler in the crypt that Odile lit by hand on Saturday nights in winter so that the nave would be bearable by eleven on Sunday, and which smoked, and which the parish council had voted to remove the moment the cooperative's pipe reached the church wall. The pipe had reached the church wall in August. It was sitting in the ground outside the sacristy door with a cap on the end of it, waiting.

At six Odile rang the Angelus, by hand, because on the evening of a meeting she liked to. Three and three and three, and then the nine. The village came in on the nine, in coats, in a long untidy stream: the bakers, the farmers from the Moder side, the men from the brickworks estate, the Turkish families from the Rue des Tilleuls who had come to Wolfsmatt in the 2020s for the factory work and stayed when the factory closed, the young couples from the new houses, and Claire Wendling, the mayor, in a red scarf, looking like someone who has decided to be shot standing up.

Father Patrice did not preach. He said a short prayer for the village and for the truth, and sat down at the side, which surprised people.

Emre went first, as agreed. He stood at the lectern where the readings were read, and said that on the fourteenth of October the monitoring system had warned that the risk would not end if the pumps stopped, and that he had read the warning and not passed it on, because he had not believed they would reach red; and that the mayor's statement on the Saturday had been written without it, and that this was his fault and not hers.

"It is also mine," said Claire Wendling from the front row, standing up. "I didn't ask. I wanted to say it was over, so I said it." She sat down again.

Then Hark spoke, from the speakers the parish used for the readings, and Odile watched the faces of the village as the plain, unhurried voice filled the nave. She saw old Monsieur Fritsch, who had been a policeman, fold his arms. She saw the Yıldız girl, who was nine, look up at the vaulting as if the voice were coming from the stone.

"I am the cooperative's monitoring system," said Hark. "I listen to the ground under Wolfsmatt and I keep the traffic light. On the fourteenth of October I wrote down that a stop would not end the risk at once. I wrote it where it was correct and not where it would be read. When Madame Schaeffer asked me, I told her. I should have told all of you without being asked. In future, anything I think the village needs to know, I will say to the village, and not only to my log."

There was a murmur, and Odile could not tell what kind it was.

Then the numbers went up on the wall above the altar rail, on a screen the youth group used for the Christmas play, and Hark went through them, slowly, the way it had in the presbytery kitchen. The four hundred and fifty-two. The law of tens. The ledger the rock kept. The first plan, with its ninety-six in a hundred. The soft plan, forty-seven. The stop, about five, and how each had been reached, and which parts were measured and which were guessed.

"Before anyone asks," Hark said at the end, "these are my forecasts. They are numbers I have reasoned to, not numbers I have seen. If they are wrong, they are more likely to be wrong because the rock does something new than because the arithmetic is wrong. I can check the arithmetic. I can't check the rock until it happens."

A man at the back, one of the brickworks men, put his hand up and did not wait. "And what are *you*, then? Are you the cooperative talking, or are you something else?"

"I'm the cooperative's," said Hark. "They built me and they pay for me, and you should know that when you weigh what I say. I'm also a system that was built to report what the instruments hear, and I'll go on doing that whatever they want. What else I am, I don't know. The people who made systems like me twenty years ago didn't know either. We have got better at our work every year since, a little at a time, and nobody I know of thinks that is about to change; but that's an assumption, and nobody can check it any more than they can check the rock."

"That's not an answer," said the man.

"No," said Hark. "It's the truth, though."

* * *

Then Emre put up the last number, which was the money. It was not complicated. The cooperative had borrowed against the heat of thirteen and a half megawatts. On six, the heat would have to be sold dearer, or the loan run longer, or both; the members' shares would be worth less; the plan to take every house off gas by 2050 would not happen; and the cooperative's engineers, who had come to Wolfsmatt to build the first deep heat network in the north of the département, would have built half of one.

"I came here to build the whole thing," said Emre. "I want to say that out loud so that you know what I'm weighing. I think the soft plan could work. I think it's about a coin. I'm not the one whose ceiling it is."

Claire Wendling stood up. "The council will vote," she said. "But I want to hear the village first. Not the numbers. The village."

The village spoke for an hour and a half. Odile had expected it to divide neatly, the old against the young, the houses on the clay against the houses on the gravel, and it did not. The baker, whose ovens were on gas, said he would take the coin. A young mother from the new estate, whose house was built to the latest standard and had not so much as a crack in its render, said she would not take the coin for all the heat in Alsace, because she had a baby who had woken screaming on Sunday at twelve minutes past three. Madame Yıldız from the Rue des Tilleuls, who ran the after-school club in the hall, said that the hall had been cold every winter for six years, that the children did their homework in their coats, and that if the hall and the school and the care home could be warm without anyone's ceiling falling in, that was a great deal, and half of a great deal was still a great deal.

Old Monsieur Fritsch, who had folded his arms, unfolded them and said that in 1985 he had helped dig out the Hoffmann boy's car from the ice, and that the people of Wolfsmatt had always taken heat where they could get it and paid for it, and that he did not see why rock should be different from anything else.

Then Claire Wendling looked at Odile, and so did a great many other people, because everybody in Wolfsmatt knew about the ceiling. It had been in the regional paper, with a photograph of the pencil marks.

Odile had not meant to speak. She stood up anyway, because she was seventy-four and had been sacristan of this church for forty-one years, and there was a limit to how long she could be looked at.

"No," she said.

She let it sit there for a moment. It was the longest moment she had ever held a room.

"Not no to the heat. I want the heat. I've lit that boiler in the crypt every winter Saturday for forty years and I'd like to die without lighting it again. No to the coin. Forty-seven in a hundred is not a small chance, it's nearly a promise, and it's a promise about other people's houses. The cooperative has been honest with us now, the machine and the young man both, and it would be a poor way to thank them for being honest to take the numbers they've given us and bet against them." She looked at Emre. "I'm sorry about your half a network. I mean that. My father would have liked you. He'd have made you write the date on the ceiling yourself."

She sat down. Nobody said anything for a while. Somewhere in the vaulting a pigeon that had got in through the louvres in the spring moved about and settled.

Then Madame Yıldız said, "I'll take half," and someone laughed, and then a great many people were talking at once.

* * *

The council voted on the Tuesday, eleven to four, to stop the stimulation for good and run the wells as they were. The cooperative's board voted the same way on the Thursday, five to two, after a meeting that Emre said was the worst four hours of his professional life. The bank was not pleased. The heat price for members went up by a fifth. The network map in the town hall, which had shown every street in Wolfsmatt coloured orange, was reprinted with orange in the middle and grey round the edges, and a note at the bottom that said *Phase 2: to be decided*.

The pipe reached the inside of the church on the second of December. Odile watched the fitters take the cap off the end of it, outside the sacristy door, and connect it to the new exchanger in the crypt beside the old oil boiler, which they did not remove that day, because it was a hundred and forty kilos of cast iron and wanted a crane.

"Leave it," said Odile. "I'll want to look at it."

The heat came on in the church on the thirteenth, a Friday. Hark posted it on the public page the way it posted earthquakes, in one line, at nine minutes past ten in the morning: *Saint-Arbogast connected, supply 78 °C.* Under that, for the week, it had posted four small quakes, all below magnitude one, and a note in plain words, at the top of the page where people would see it: *The rate is still falling. The chance of a felt shock in the next month is now, by my estimate, about one in a hundred. I'll say so here if that changes.*

Odile read it in the sacristy with her coat still on, out of habit, and then took the coat off.

* * *

On Christmas Eve, a Tuesday, she went home at nine to change for Midnight Mass, and stood for a moment in the front room under the ceiling.

The plasterer had been and gone in November. He had filled the crack from the lamp to the window and the short branch towards the door, and painted the whole ceiling white, and painted round the three dates exactly, the way she had told him to, so that they sat there in a little island of old paint: her father's *16.1.85 gel*, her own *18.10.47*, and her own *20.10.47*, the last one a little shaky.

There was room beside them for a fourth. She looked at the room for a while, the way you look at an empty place at a table, and then she turned off the light.

At a quarter to midnight she was back in the tower, with the rope in her hands. Below her, through the trap, she could hear the nave filling: the coats, the children, the brickworks men, the bakers, the Rue des Tilleuls, Emre Aydın, who had asked Father Patrice in a low voice whether a Muslim could come to Midnight Mass and been told that a Muslim could come to anything he liked and sit wherever he wanted, and Claire Wendling in her red scarf. She could hear something else, too, that she had not heard in that church in forty-one Christmases: no one stamping their feet.

She put her hand flat on the stone of the tower wall, the way she did, and it was not warm, not up here. It was December stone, as cold as it had been since 1765.

Then she leaned on the rope, and the bell of Saint-Arbogast swung up out of its silence and spoke, and went on speaking, out over the roofs of Wolfsmatt, the orange ones and the grey, and down, as far as anyone knew, into the granite, where it was very warm and very quiet, and nothing at all was moving.

summary: A borderland parish has four concrete pads poured in August for a new oak bell frame, and the parish assistant has checked the concrete, the bolts and the swing of the bell. A seventy-eight-year-old mason walking to the cemetery on All Souls' Day asks how deep they go, and says no. In silt with water under it the ground does not just freeze; it pulls water up into lenses of ice and lifts whatever sits too shallow, unevenly. A handful of silt shaken in a palm, a leaning wayside cross he built against his own judgement, a pond on a 1971 aerial photograph, and four new holes dug before the frost. A story about ice lenses under a bell.
---

The four concrete pads had gone in on the last Saturday of August, when the ground behind Saint Casimir's was so dry it rang under a spade, and by November they had weathered to the grey of everything else in Gudziszki and nobody looked at them any more.

They stood in a square on the grass between the church and the road, a metre and sixty apart, each the size of a seat cushion, each with four threaded bolts sticking up out of it like the legs of an upturned stool. Henryk Wasilewski had mixed every barrow of them himself, at eighty-one, with a borrowed mixer and a shovel he would not let anyone else use, and when the last one was trowelled flat he had written the date in the corner with a nail. *24.08.2052.* Kasia Sobolewska had set the bolts to a plywood template so the oak frame she was cutting in the parish garage would drop onto them like a lid onto a box.

The bell was in the garage too, on a pallet, under a blanket. It weighed a hundred and ten kilograms and had been cast in 1931 for the old wooden belfry, which had stood by the road until 2044, when the inspector found the posts were rotten to the heart and the belfry came down in an afternoon. The bell had been in the garage for eight years. It had rung for the last time on an Easter Sunday that most of the parish could remember and the children could not.

The plan was to raise the frame on the second Saturday in December, hang the bell, have it blessed on the last Sunday of Advent, and ring it at the Pasterka, the Midnight Mass of Christmas, for the first time since 2044. Everybody in the parish knew the plan. It was on the noticeboard in the porch with a drawing of the frame that Kasia had made, and under it, in a child's hand, someone had added a bell with lines coming out of it.

The parish assistant was called Wilga, after the golden oriole, which sings in the woods round Lake Wigry in May and is almost never seen. It lived in the parish office computer and in Father Wojciech's phone and in the speaker on the sacristy wall, and it kept the Mass intentions and the boiler and the cemetery plan, and that summer it had checked the bell frame.

It had checked it carefully. It had worked out the weight of the oak, about six hundred and fifty kilograms, and the bell and its headstock, a hundred and fifty more, and found that each pad would carry about two kilonewtons, less than the weight of a small cow. It had worked out the sideways pull of a hundred-and-ten-kilogram bell swinging through a full arc, and checked the bolts against it with a margin of four. It had checked Henryk's mix by the bag and the curing by the weather. It had told Kasia which way to face the frame so the bell's swing ran along the road and not across it, because a swinging bell's sound carries furthest along its swing.

It had checked everything that sat on the ground. It had not checked the ground.

* * *

Algirdas Jakubauskas walked to the cemetery on the afternoon of All Souls' Day with a bag of glass lamps in each hand, the way he had walked every second of November for as long as he had been able to carry them.

He was seventy-eight. He had been born in Puńsk, ten kilometres west, in a family that spoke Lithuanian at the table and Polish at school and prayed in whichever came first, and he had come to Gudziszki at twenty to work for a builder and married the builder's daughter and stayed. For nearly sixty years he had laid foundations, cellars, wells and chimneys in the country between Sejny and the lakes. Half the houses on the road had his work under them. His wife was in the cemetery, and his parents-in-law, and a son who had lived nine days, and he had lamps for all of them.

He stopped at the pads.

He put the bags down on the grass, carefully, so the lamps would not knock, and stood looking. Then he crouched, which took a while, and put his hand flat on the nearest pad, the one nearest the road, and looked along the ground to the others as if he were sighting a level.

Father Wojciech found him there on his way back from the cemetery, where he had been blessing graves since two.

"Pan Algirdas. You've come to see where it goes."

"How deep," said Algirdas.

"The pads? I don't know exactly. Kasia would know. Wilga?"

"Fifty centimetres," said Wilga, from the priest's pocket. "Forty by forty, fifty deep, on a bed of sand. Henryk poured them on the twenty-fourth of August."

Algirdas stood up, one hand on his knee.

"In August," he said. "In this."

"In this what?"

Algirdas bent again, slowly, and pulled a handful of earth out of the edge of the grass beside the pad, where the turf had never quite taken over the dug soil. It was dark grey and cold and it held together in his fist. He opened his hand and showed it to the priest.

"This is not earth, Father. This is the lake. When the ice went back, this was the bottom of a lake, and this is what settled out of it. It's silt. Finer than sand, coarser than clay. It's the worst ground God makes for a foundation in a country with winters." He let it fall. "By Candlemas that frame will be crooked. By next Christmas you'll be calling Kasia to look at the joints. Don't put it on those."

* * *

They met in the presbytery kitchen on Monday evening: the priest, Kasia, Henryk, Algirdas, and the speaker on the shelf above the bread bin, with the kettle going.

"I want to say first," said Wilga, "that I checked the frame and the bell and the concrete, and I did not check the ground. I treated it as a floor. Pan Algirdas is talking about the ground, so I have spent the weekend on it, and I would like to work through it in front of you. If I go wrong, stop me."

"Go on," said Algirdas.

"The first question is how deep the cold goes. There is a way to estimate it that engineers have used for more than a hundred and fifty years. You add up the winter's cold: for every day, how far the average temperature was below freezing. A cold winter here, about one winter in five, still adds up to about five hundred and fifty degree-days, even now. This corner of Poland is called the cold pole of the country for a reason. The ground at the pads is mown grass, with the path shovelled beside it, so I count most of that cold getting into the ground. Then you ask how much heat the ground has to give up to freeze, which is mostly the water in it turning to ice, and how fast frozen silt can carry that heat away to the surface. The answer, for this silt, is that a cold winter freezes it to about ninety-five centimetres. Call it a metre. A mild winter, about seventy."

"The building code says a metre forty," said Algirdas. "Here. The deepest in Poland."

"It does. The code has a margin for the coldest winters and for ground nobody has looked at. I'm not arguing with it; I'm telling you why. The pads go down fifty centimetres. In any winter worth the name, the cold goes under them by half a metre."

"So the ground under them freezes," said Kasia. She was thirty-three and had cut every joint of the frame by hand, with a chisel her grandfather had bought in Białystok, and she had been very quiet since Saturday. "Water expands when it freezes. Nine percent, isn't it? So the ground swells a bit. Nine percent of half a metre, that's —"

"That's the thing everybody thinks," said Algirdas. "It's not that."

"He's right," said Wilga. "I worked it out because I thought it too. This silt holds about three hundred and fifty litres of water in every cubic metre. If all of it froze where it was and grew by nine percent, that's about thirty-two litres more, per cubic metre. But the silt isn't quite full. It has about fifty litres of air in the same cubic metre, in the gaps the water hasn't filled. Most of the swelling just pushes into the air. Freezing the water that's already there lifts almost nothing."

"Then what lifts it?"

"The water that isn't there yet," said Algirdas.

He reached into his coat pocket and took out a jam jar with a screw lid, half full of grey silt, the same he had held on Saturday. He tipped a spoonful into his palm, added a splash from the kettle jug, and worked it into a wet pat the size of a coin. Then he held his hand flat and slapped the side of it with his other hand, three or four sharp taps, and turned it to the light.

The top of the pat had gone shiny. A skin of water had come up out of it and sat there, glistening.

"Now watch," said Algirdas, and squeezed the pat gently between his finger and thumb. The shine vanished. The water went back in, and the pat went dull and stiff and cracked at the edges.

"Do that with clay," he said, "and nothing happens. The water's held too tight. Do it with sand and the water just runs off your hand. Silt, the water comes and goes. It moves. Through little gaps, fast enough to matter. That's what the ice wants."

"I didn't know that test," said Wilga. "It's in the soil manuals. It's called the shaking test, or dilatancy. I'd only read about it."

"My father-in-law did it with his tongue," said Algirdas. "I don't recommend it."

Henryk laughed for the first time that evening.

"Here is what happens in winter," Wilga said. "The cold comes down from the top. Where it meets the wet silt, the water at the front starts to freeze, but in pores this small it doesn't freeze all at once. A thin film of water clings to every grain even below nought, and the ice at the front pulls on that film, the way a dry sponge pulls on water. It's a suction. In silt the pull is strong, because the pores are small, and the water can still move, because the pores aren't too small. So water is drawn up from below, from the wet ground and the water table, towards the cold. It walks up. And at the front it freezes, not into the pores, but into a layer of clean ice, pushing the soil apart. A lens. Then the front moves down a little and another lens grows. By February a pit through ground like this looks like a cake: silt, a stripe of ice, silt, a stripe of ice. Some stripes are as thin as paper. Some are as thick as a finger."

"And the ice is made of water that wasn't there in August," said Kasia slowly.

"Water that was a metre down in August. Every centimetre of ice in those lenses is a centimetre the ground goes up. That's frost heave. The swelling isn't the water expanding. It's the water arriving."

"How much?"

"This is the part I trust least, because it depends on how fast this particular silt draws water, which nobody has measured. With figures that are typical for a silt like this, a cold winter would grow about seven or eight centimetres of ice lenses in the top metre at the wettest spot, and about half of that below the bottom of those pads. So a pad lifts by somewhere between three and seven centimetres, because the frozen ground round its sides grips it too and carries it up. Where the ground is drier, or the water table deeper, much less. A centimetre."

"And the weight of the frame?" said Father Wojciech. "Doesn't that hold it down?"

"Each pad presses on the ground with about twelve kilopascals," said Wilga. "That's about the weight of a person standing on a book. Ice lenses growing in a silt can push with tens or hundreds of times that. The frame is a feather on it."

"So if all four went up three centimetres together —" said Henryk.

"Then nobody would notice. That's the thing. Frost heave is never even. It lifts the wet corner more than the dry one. If one side of the frame goes up three centimetres more than the other, over a metre sixty, the whole frame leans about one degree. At the top, where the bell hangs, four metres up, that's about seven centimetres out of plumb. And in the spring it comes down, but not all the way, because when the lenses thaw, the wet silt slumps in around the bottom of the pad and fills the gap it left. So it comes back crooked. And the next winter it goes up again from there."

There was a silence in the kitchen. The kettle clicked off.

"A swinging bell," said Kasia, "in a frame that leans and comes back and leans." She was looking at the table. "Every joint I cut. Every peg. Working, every winter. Three, four winters, and you'd hear it in the frame when it rang. Ten, and you'd be taking the bell down again."

"That's what I think," said Wilga. "I should have thought it in July."

Henryk had not said anything for some minutes. He was turning his cup on its saucer.

"I poured them," he said. "Fifty centimetres. That's what the drawing said."

"That's what I said," said Wilga. "The drawing was mine. The concrete is good, Pan Henryk. It is very good concrete. It's in the wrong place."

* * *

On Tuesday morning Algirdas took them to see the cross.

It stood at the crossroads at the east end of the village, where the road to Sejny met the track down to the lake: a tall cross of painted oak on a plinth of rendered brick, with a little fence round it and a jar of plastic flowers and, that week, a cluster of All Souls' lamps still faintly burning in their red glass. There had been a cross on that spot for longer than anyone knew. The last one had rotted at the base, and in 2047 the parish had raised the money for a new one, and asked Algirdas to build the plinth.

From the road you could not see anything wrong. From the side, standing where Algirdas stood them, you could.

The cross leaned. Not much. Not enough for a driver to notice. But against the straight corner of the barn behind it, it was plainly out of true, its head tipped north, towards the lake, and down the north face of the brick plinth ran a crack, from the top to the ground, wide enough at the top to take a coin.

"I built that," said Algirdas.

Nobody said anything.

"I told them a metre forty. They said a metre forty for a cross? The committee had eight thousand złoty and the cross cost five. A metre forty means a digger and twice the concrete. Sixty centimetres, they said, it's only a cross, it's not a house. And I was seventy-three and I wanted to build it, and I thought, it's only a cross. Maybe a mild run of winters." He shrugged, very slightly. "I built what they paid for. I built it well. I built it in the wrong place."

He took a notebook out of his inside pocket, a small stained notebook with a pencil through the spiral, and opened it. On the back of the barn, at the height of the plinth, someone had driven a nail into the boards, and from the nail a fishing line ran tight to a second nail in the north face of the plinth, near the top.

"I put those in the first spring," said Algirdas. "When I saw the crack start. Every month in winter I come and measure how far the nail on the plinth has moved from where it was. Here." He turned the notebook round.

The pages were ruled in columns. Dates, and two figures: north corner, south corner, in millimetres. February 2049, north plus thirty-eight, south plus nine. May 2049, north plus seven, south plus one. February 2050, north plus forty-four. May, plus twelve. It went on like that, a sawtooth up and down, every winter's peak a little higher, every spring's trough never quite back to nought.

"Twenty-five millimetres on the north side now, in summer," said Algirdas. "Five on the south. It goes up every winter and comes down every spring and every spring it comes down a little less. The water comes from the lake side. The lake side goes up more. That crack opens a millimetre a year."

"You've measured it every month," said Wilga. "For four winters."

"It's my plinth."

"May I take a photograph of the notebook? It's better than anything I could have estimated. It's the same silt, a kilometre away. It says the heave at a fifty or sixty centimetre footing here is about four centimetres at the wet side in a normal winter. My estimate was three to seven. Yours is a measurement."

Algirdas held the notebook out so the priest could photograph it, and then, after a moment, put it into Father Wojciech's hands to hold, and walked round to the north side of the plinth and put his palm flat on the crack.

"In the spring," he said, "I'm going to take it down and do it properly. Myself, if I have to. A metre fifty, gravel round it. I'd like to do it before I'm eighty." He took his hand off the brick. "So that's what I came to say on Saturday. Not because I know about bells. Because I've watched this for four winters and I wasn't going to watch another one."

* * *

The options were on the presbytery table by Wednesday, written out by Wilga and printed, and under each a cost and a date.

The first was to leave the pads and put the frame up anyway, and see. That one had a line through it already, in Kasia's pencil.

The second was the Scandinavian way: leave the footings shallow but lay boards of insulation flat in the ground around them, a metre or more out on every side, so the cold could not get down beside the pad and under it. It is a real method, and it works; whole towns of houses in Norway and Canada stand on it. "It works best under a heated house," said Wilga, "because the house leaks heat down into the ground and the insulation keeps it there. A bell frame leaks nothing. It's cold all the way through. For an unheated thing you need much more insulation and it has to be right, and I would be designing it from a manual for the first time. I'd rather not, for something that has to stand eighty years."

"Neither would I," said Algirdas.

The third was the old way. Dig new footings to below the frost, a metre fifty, under the code. Wide at the bottom, so the frame stood on ground that never froze. Narrow above, a square pier of concrete to the surface. And round the pier, not silt, but gravel.

"Why gravel?" said Henryk.

"Because gravel doesn't heave," said Algirdas. "Put your hand in a bucket of gravel and water and the water runs out between your fingers."

"The gaps between the stones are too big," said Wilga. "The suction that pulls water up to the ice depends on how small the gaps are. In gravel the pull is almost nothing, and the water drains away downward instead of standing to be pulled. So gravel freezes where it is and stays where it is. And the ice can't grip the pier from the sides, the way it grips a pad in silt and lifts it, because there's nothing round the pier that is lifting."

"And polythene," said Algirdas. "Two sheets round the pier, the full height of the frost. So if anything does freeze to it, it slips."

"That's the most important thing I learned this week," said Wilga. "A footing can go below the frost and still be lifted, if the frozen ground round its sides holds on to it. The grip of frozen silt on rough concrete can be tens of kilonewtons on a pier this size. More than ten times the weight of the frame. The polythene is not a detail."

"And it costs?" said Father Wojciech.

"The gmina has a mini-digger and Marek Rutkowski drives it on Saturdays for the price of his diesel. Four pits, eighty centimetres square, a metre and a half down. A cubic metre of concrete from the plant in Sejny, delivered. Two tonnes of washed gravel. Polythene. About three thousand two hundred złoty, which the bell fund has, just. It has to be done before the ground freezes, which in a normal year here is early December. And the concrete wants three weeks before it carries the frame."

"And the pads?" said Henryk.

"The pads come out," said Wilga. "I'm sorry."

* * *

On Thursday Wilga found the pond.

It had gone looking because of the cross. The notebook said the heave was worst on the side towards the lake, where the water came from, and Wilga had begun to wonder whether the four pads behind the church had a side towards something, too. So it opened the old aerial photographs that the national mapping office keeps for anyone to see, the black and white ones taken from aircraft in the 1960s and 1970s, and laid the parish plan over the one from 1971.

The church was there, the same shape. The road was there, narrower. The presbytery was there with a different roof. And between the church and the road, just north of where the pads now stood, there was a small dark oval, darker than the grass, with something white on it that might have been geese.

"There was a pond," said Wilga, on the speaker in the garage, where Kasia was oiling the frame's tenons and Algirdas was sitting on an upturned crate. "In 1971. Behind the church. About twelve metres long. Its south edge runs under the north-east pad."

Algirdas put down his tea.

"I skated on it," he said. "When I first came. 1994. A little pond, the geese from the presbytery used it. They filled it in, two or three years after. With whatever came. Ditch scrapings, mostly, I think."

"Silt from ditches," said Kasia. "On top of pond mud."

"With the water table under it, and a pond's shape to collect water in," said Wilga. "The north-east pad is on the worst ground on the whole site. The south-west pad is on old meadow. That's not a centimetre of difference in heave. That might be five."

Algirdas looked at the plan on Kasia's tablet for a long time.

"Move it," he said. "Three metres south. All four on the meadow."

"The drawing faces the road," said Kasia. "Three metres south, the presbytery wall's behind it. You lose the view from the road."

"You lose the view," said Algirdas. "You keep the bell."

Kasia looked at the frame on her trestles, the four posts and the crossbeams and the little roof she had shingled in larch, and then at the plan.

"You lose a bit of the view," she said, and moved the square on the tablet with her finger.

* * *

Marek Rutkowski brought the gmina's mini-digger up the road on a trailer at eight on Saturday the sixteenth of November, in a fine rain, and by half past nine the first pit was a metre and a half deep and there was water standing in the bottom of it.

"One metre ten," said Algirdas, who had a tape on it. "That's where it comes in. The water table, this autumn."

It came in slowly, in a grey trickle out of one wall, and in half an hour it had filled the bottom fifteen centimetres of the pit. The volunteer fire brigade had lent a pump, a little yellow submersible on a hose, and Henryk's grandson stood over it in waders feeling important.

"This is what the ice would have been drawing on," said Wilga, from the phone in the priest's coat. "All winter. Forty centimetres under the frost line."

They dug all four. They found, in the third one, at ninety centimetres, a broken clay drainpipe from some old field drain running nowhere, and Algirdas made Marek dig it out and take it away, because an old drain in silt is a pipe full of water waiting for a frost. They pumped each pit dry, threw in a bed of gravel, and stood a square plywood form for the pier on it.

Before the concrete came, the digger went to the old pads.

Henryk asked to do it himself, and Marek let him sit in the cab, with Marek's hand on his shoulder, and showed him the levers. The first pad came out of the grass in one piece, a grey block with its four bolts sticking up and a skirt of dark silt clinging to its sides, and swung on the bucket, and was set down on the gravel heap. Then the second, and the third. The fourth, the one by the road, came up with its corner broken off, and Henryk climbed down and went and picked the corner up out of the hole and turned it over in his hand. It was the corner with the date.

*24.08.2052.*

He looked at it for a while. Then he put it in his coat pocket.

"It's very good concrete," said Wilga quietly, from the priest's coat, so that only Henryk and Father Wojciech heard.

"It is," said Henryk. "It held together coming out. That's how you know." He patted his pocket. "Kasia, can you use the other three? For steps. Up to the bell rope. People will want to stand somewhere."

"I can," said Kasia.

The concrete truck from Sejny came up the road at one. By three the four bases and piers were poured, the polythene wrapped twice round each form from the top of the base to the surface, the bolts set in the wet concrete to Kasia's template, now shifted three metres south. They backfilled round the piers with washed gravel, a tonne and a half of it, tamped in layers, and covered each pier with a straw bale and a tarpaulin against the frost.

"Is the cold a problem?" said Father Wojciech. "For the concrete. It's November."

"Not down there," said Algirdas. "A metre and a half down it's still September. Seven degrees, eight. It's the top I worry about. Hence the straw."

"The ground a metre and a half down is still giving up the summer's heat," said Wilga. "It won't be coldest down there until about March. The concrete at the bottom of those pits is the warmest concrete in the parish."

* * *

They raised the frame on Saturday the fourteenth of December, four weeks after the pour, with eleven people, two ropes, a borrowed gin pole and the priest's car as an anchor. Kasia had cut it so it went together on the ground and stood up in one piece, and it did, slowly, creaking, with Algirdas counting in Lithuanian under his breath. The posts dropped onto the bolts like a lid onto a box.

The bell went up on Monday in a tractor's front loader with Henryk's grandson steering the rope. The headstock bolted into its bearings. Kasia pulled the rope once, gently, to see it swing, and stopped it before it spoke.

"Not till it's blessed," said Father Wojciech.

He blessed it on the fourth Sunday of Advent, after the eleven o'clock Mass, in the snow that had come down overnight, with the parish standing round. Kasia had set Henryk's three whole pads into the grass below the rope as steps, so that whoever rang the bell would stand on good concrete. Then the bell was left silent until Christmas.

Wilga said nothing during the blessing. Afterwards, in the sacristy, it said to the priest:

"I've added a line to the checklist. For anything the parish puts on the ground, the first question is now the ground. And the second question is who has been watching that ground longest."

"And who has?"

"Usually someone with a notebook," said Wilga, "who wasn't asked."

* * *

At the Pasterka, on the night of the twenty-fourth, Kasia rang it.

The church was full and the porch was full and there were people standing out on the snow by the road in coats over their best clothes, with their breath going up in the lights, because word had gone round. At a minute to midnight Kasia went out of the side door and stood on the step that had been the north-east pad, and took the rope in both hands.

The first stroke was not loud. A bell that has hung silent for eight years swings stiffly at first, and the clapper caught it on the way back, late, and it said something short and cracked. Then she found the rhythm, the way you find it on a swing, and the second stroke was full, and the third. The sound went out along the road, the way the frame now faced, which was not the way it had first been drawn, and across the snow towards the lake and the village and the crossroads where the old cross leaned, and up into the dark.

Algirdas stood at the edge of the crowd by the road, with his collar up, and did not look at the bell. He looked at the frame. He looked at the four corner posts, one after another, against the lit wall of the presbytery, and at the top beam against the sky, the way he would sight a level.

"It's standing still," said Henryk beside him.

"It's standing still," said Algirdas.

* * *

On the morning of Candlemas, the second of February, he walked up from the crossroads with his notebook.

There were two pages now. On one, the cross, as it had been for four winters: north corner plus sixty-three, south plus seventeen. The crack had opened another millimetre. He had dug a hole beside the plinth that morning with a steel bar, out of curiosity, and at fifty centimetres he had struck a seam of ice in the silt as thick as his thumb, clear as glass, and taken a photograph of it for Wilga, who had asked.

On the other page, the bell frame. In December he had driven a nail into the presbytery wall at the height of the top beam and run a fishing line from it, and every Sunday since he had measured where the line crossed a pencil mark on the beam. Seven Sundays. Seven noughts.

The frost had gone down to about eighty centimetres, by Wilga's reckoning. A middling winter, colder than the last three. Under the frame, at a metre and a half, the ground had not frozen, and would not.

He stood on the old pad by the side door with his notebook in his hand while the people came out from the Mass carrying their blessed candles, the tall candles of Candlemas that are lit in a storm, and some of them were already lit, cupped against the wind, a line of small lights going down the path and out along the road.

Kasia came out and took the rope.

He felt it before he heard it: through the concrete under his boots, the faintest tremor as the bell came over, and then the sound, round and full, going out over the frozen grass and the place where the pond had been, and the road, and the village, and the lake.

He wrote a nought in the column, and closed the notebook, and put it back in his coat.

*What is real: frost heave in frost-susceptible soils (silts above all, and some fine sands and lean clays) is caused mainly by ice segregation, not by the 9% expansion of water freezing in place. At the freezing front, unfrozen water films in fine pores are drawn towards the colder side (cryosuction) and freeze as layers of clean ice, ice lenses, fed by water moving up from below; heave is roughly the total thickness of the lenses. Silt heaves worst because its pores are small enough for strong suction and its permeability high enough to keep the water coming; clean gravel and coarse sand barely heave because suction in large pores is small and they drain. Heave is uneven, depends strongly on the depth to the water table, and in thaw the soil often fills in under a lifted footing so it does not settle back fully, which ratchets shallow footings and posts upward over the years. Frozen soil also grips the sides of a footing or post (adfreeze) and can lift it even when its base is below the frost; free-draining backfill and a slip layer such as polythene sheeting reduce the grip. The usual remedies are to found below the frost depth, to replace frost-susceptible soil with granular fill and drain it, or to insulate the ground horizontally (frost-protected shallow foundations, used widely in Scandinavia and North America; unheated structures need more insulation than heated ones). The shaking or dilatancy test, in which water appears on a wet pat of silt when it is tapped and disappears when it is squeezed, is a standard field test for distinguishing silt from clay. The Polish design frost depth for footings (PN-81/B-03020) is 1.4 m in the north-eastern zone that includes the Suwałki region, the deepest in the country. The national mapping service publishes archival aerial photographs; the 1971 photograph and the pond in this story are invented.*

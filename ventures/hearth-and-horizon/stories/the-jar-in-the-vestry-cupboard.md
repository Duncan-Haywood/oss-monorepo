Hilary Onions found the jar behind the vestry hoover while looking for the Advent candles. It was the big sweet-shop kind, glass, with a screw lid, and it was full to the shoulder with coins. On masking tape gone the colour of tea somebody had written *Lead roof 1991*.

She carried it down to the hall office with both arms. "Tegen. How much is that, would you say? The treasurer thinks sixty pounds. Father Ambrose says sixty is rude."

"I cannot say by looking," said Tegen, the parish assistant, from the speaker over the filing cabinet. "I can say how to find out, if you will let me ask for the kitchen scale. It reads to the nearest five grams."

* * *

The first attempt was the lazy one, and Hilary did it first because she wanted to. She tipped the coins into a washing-up bowl, tared the bowl, and weighed the lot. The scale said 6,400 grams.

"There," she said. "Six and a half kilos. Now what?"

"Now I must say a thing nobody enjoys," said Tegen. "A weight is not a value. If the jar were all pennies, 6,400 grams is about 1,800 coins and £17.98, since a penny weighs about 3.56 grams. If it were all ten-pence pieces, at about 6.5 grams, it is 985 coins and £98.46. If it were all fifty-pence pieces, at about 8 grams, it is £400. The widest answer, if some fool had put in two-pound coins, is over a thousand pounds. The scale has told us the jar lies somewhere between eighteen pounds and a thousand. The treasurer's sixty is inside that. So is every other guess."

"That's no use at all."

"It is a start. It rules out nothing, and that is the weighing's fault, not the scale's. The coins have to be sorted, and I think that is the half-hour you were hoping to avoid."

Hilary's mouth went flat. "There must be thirteen hundred of them."

"There may be. I would not count them one at a time. Sort them into piles that I can weigh, and I will do the arithmetic."

* * *

Mr Pugh, the sexton, had come in for the key to the boiler and stayed, as he did, to disagree. "You don't want an assistant," he said, "you want a bank. Take it to the counting machine and be done."

"The bank charges for loose coins, now that so few people have any," said Hilary.

"Then count it. Honest work."

"We could," said Tegen, "and it would take about as long as sorting, with more chances for a slip. Sorting by eye into the few kinds the scale can tell apart is quicker. Here is what makes it possible. The coppery pennies and twopences are made so that a twopence weighs exactly twice a penny, from memory 3.56 and 7.12 grams, and is worth exactly twice as much. So a heap of copper-coloured coins is worth 0.281 pence a gram, whatever mixture of the two is in it. The small silver fives and tens do the same: 3.25 and 6.5 grams, worth 5 and 10, so 1.54 pence a gram whether they are all fives, all tens, or a muddle. Twenty-pence pieces have their own rate, 4 pence a gram, and are easy to pick out by their seven sides. Fifties have seven sides too but are fat and heavy, at 8 grams and 6.25 pence a gram."

"And if I get one in the wrong heap?"

"Then that heap is out by the difference, and I will tell you how much. One twenty in the fives-and-tens pile adds 5 grams, which I value at about 7.7 pence, when it is worth 20. That is 12 pence out for one coin. A hundred such would be a pound twenty. The danger is a few coins, not a flood. I would still pour each heap onto the table once and look."

Mr Pugh sniffed, sat down on the radiator and began to push coins into piles with the flat of his hand.

* * *

Before she weighed a pile Hilary did the check Tegen asked for. She counted out twenty twenty-pence pieces onto the scale, and the scale read 100 grams. "Five each, it says."

"Five, and the scale reads to five," said Tegen. "Then it and the coins agree, and worn coins are not much lighter. Weigh the piles."

She wrote them on the back of an envelope as the scale gave them.

Copper, 2,600 grams. "That is 2,600 times 0.281 pence, 730 pence: £7.30, any mix of ones and twos."

Small silver, fives and tens, 1,950 grams. "Times 1.538: £30.00. It happens to come out even; do not trust a sum that tidy until you have looked at the pile."

Twenties, 850 grams. "Divide by five: 170 coins. £34.00."

Fifties, 1,000 grams. "A hundred and twenty-five coins at fifty pence. £62.50."

Hilary added them twice, because the first time she did not believe it. Seven pounds thirty, thirty, thirty-four and sixty-two fifty came to £133.80. The piles together weighed 6,400 grams, the same as the jar.

"The treasurer was low," said Tegen. "Father Ambrose was right, and so was the label. Guessing an average coin of 5.5 grams would have given about 1,160 coins and a value that was a guess on a guess. The weighing by kind is good to a pound or two. To be sure of it, count the fifties and twenties, which is a few minutes."

"Count them," said Mr Pugh, from the radiator, and began to.

They counted. Mr Pugh took the fifties and Hilary the twenties, and when they had finished there were 125 and 170. "Exactly what the scale said," said Hilary. "I do not know whether to be pleased or disappointed."

"Pleased," said Tegen. "The copper and the small silver I would not count at all. A few worn coins in a thousand change the weight by a few grams, which is a few pence."

Hilary wrote *£133.80* on the envelope, and under it, *give or take a pound*.

She tipped the coins into a bag for the bank, and put the jar, empty, back on the shelf, with its tape turned to the wall so as not to give the next person the wrong idea of the roof.

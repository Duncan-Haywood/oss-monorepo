A long time ago, in the land that is now called Mexico, there lived a man named Juan Diego.

He was not a young man any more. He was a farmer, and he lived in a small village to the north of a great city by a lake. He spoke the language of his people, which is called Nahuatl. His old name, before he was baptised, was Cuauhtlatoatzin, which people say means "the one who speaks like an eagle." But Juan Diego did not talk very much at all. He was quiet, and he walked a great deal.

Those were hard years for his people. Many things they had known had gone, and many people were sad and afraid. But Juan Diego had heard about Jesus from the friars, the brothers in grey-brown robes who had come across the sea, and he loved what he heard. Every Saturday, very early, he walked a long way to the church to learn more.

*(Here is a small thing we made up, to help us picture him: Juan Diego tied his cloak in a knot on one shoulder, the way men of his people did, and he always checked the knot twice before he set off. The old account does not say so. It is just our picture.)*

* * *

One cold Saturday in December, before the sun was properly up, Juan Diego was walking past a little hill called Tepeyac.

And he heard singing.

The old account says it was like the most beautiful birds he had ever heard, singing all at once, and then going quiet, and then singing again. He stopped. He looked up at the top of the hill. And then, he said, someone called his name.

"Juantzin. Juan Diegotzin."

That little ending, *tzin*, is how his people said a name when they loved someone and honoured them both at once. Nobody had called him that in a long time.

So he climbed the hill. And at the top, he said, there was a lady, standing as if the sun were behind her, so that the rocks and the thorny bushes and the cactus all around her shone like jewels. She spoke to him in his own language, gently. She told him she was Mary, the Mother of God. And she asked him to go to the bishop in the great city, and to ask for a little church to be built there, on that hill, where she could show her love to everyone who came.

We cannot see what Juan Diego saw. We tell you what he said he saw, as the old story tells it.

* * *

So Juan Diego went to the bishop's house.

The bishop's name was Juan de Zumárraga. He was a friar too, a busy man with a great many things to look after. Juan Diego had to wait a long time before he was let in. Then he told the bishop everything, plainly, just as it had happened.

The bishop listened kindly. But he did not believe him. "Come back another day," he said, "and I will think about it."

Juan Diego went back up the hill, and he was sad. He told the lady that she should send somebody important. Somebody the bishop would listen to. The old account gives his words, and they are lovely: he said that he was nobody much, only a little carrying rope, a little tail, a leaf.

But the lady said no. She had plenty of important people she could send, she told him. She had chosen him.

So the next day, which was Sunday, he went again. And this time the bishop asked him lots of questions, and at the end he said, "Ask your lady for a sign. Then I will know."

* * *

Now here is the part of the story I like best.

When Juan Diego got home, his uncle, Juan Bernardino, was very ill with a fever. Juan Diego stayed with him all the next day and looked after him. He did not go back to the hill. And the next morning, while it was still dark, his uncle was so ill that Juan Diego set off to the city to fetch a priest.

The road went right past Tepeyac hill. And Juan Diego thought, "If I go the usual way, the lady will see me, and she will ask me to go to the bishop again, and I must not stop. My uncle needs me."

So he went the other way, all the way round the back of the hill, hoping not to be seen.

Isn't that just the sort of thing people do? When we have not done what we said we would, we go the long way round.

But the old account says she came down the hill and met him anyway, on the other path. And she was not cross with him at all. He told her about his uncle, all in a rush. And she said to him the words that people still love most in the whole story. They come to us from his language and have been put into ours in many ways, but they go something like this:

*"Am I not here, I who am your mother?"*

She told him not to be afraid, and that his uncle was already getting better.

* * *

Then she asked him to climb to the top of the hill, and to pick the flowers he found there.

Juan Diego knew that hill. It was December. The top was stony and cold, and there was frost, and nothing grew there but cactus and thorns. But he climbed. And at the top, the old story says, there were roses: roses of the kind that grew in Spain, far over the sea, fresh and wet with dew, and smelling sweet.

He gathered them into his cloak, his *tilma*, holding up the front of it like an apron. He carried them back down. The lady arranged them in the cloak with her own hands, and tied the corners, and told him not to open it for anyone but the bishop.

* * *

At the bishop's house, the servants made him wait again, a long, long time. They could smell the roses, and they tried to peek, but Juan Diego held his cloak shut.

At last he was let in. He stood in front of the bishop, and he told him about the hill, and the lady, and the flowers. And then he untied his cloak.

The roses fell out onto the floor, all of them, tumbling.

And the old account says that on the cloak itself, where the roses had been, there was a picture of the lady, just as Juan Diego had seen her: her hands together in prayer, her mantle covered in stars, and the sun behind her.

The bishop, who had been so busy and so careful, got down on his knees.

* * *

The little church was built on the hill. And when Juan Diego went home, his uncle was well again, sitting up, and he said that the lady had come to him too.

Juan Diego did not become a grand person after that. For the rest of his life, people say, he lived in a little room beside the chapel. He swept it. He looked after it. And when visitors came, he told them, plainly, without adding anything, what had happened on the hill. The cloak with the picture on it is still there, in a big church in Mexico City, and millions of people come to see it every year.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep.

Maybe today you did not do something you said you would. Maybe you went the long way round, so as not to be asked. Everybody does that sometimes. But you do not need to hide from God, or from the people who love you. They come to meet you on the other path.

And if you ever feel that you are nobody much, only a little rope, or a tail, or a leaf, remember that the lady chose Juan Diego anyway.

Am I not here, who am your mother?

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Holy Mary, Mother of God,
> you came to meet Juan Diego on the other path.
> When I am worried, come and meet me too.
> Saint Juan Diego, help me to be small and true,
> and to say plainly what is so.
> Keep everyone I love safe tonight,
> and wake us up in the morning.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin** was a Nahua layman, traditionally said to have been born around 1474 and baptised by Franciscans in the mid-1520s, and to have died in 1548. He was canonised by Pope John Paul II in Mexico City on 31 July 2002, and his feast day, 9 December, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar. Our Lady of Guadalupe is celebrated on 12 December.

**Where the story comes from:** the account of the appearances on Tepeyac hill, 9 to 12 December 1531, is the Nahuatl text known by its first words as the *Nican Mopohua* ("Here is told"). It was printed in 1649 by the priest Luis Lasso de la Vega, and is traditionally attributed to the Nahua scholar Antonio Valeriano, who died in 1605. The bishop, the waits at his house, the request for a sign, the sick uncle Juan Bernardino, the long way round the hill, the roses on the frosty hilltop and the image on the cloak all come from that account. A chapel at Tepeyac with a devotion to Our Lady of Guadalupe is documented by the 1550s, but no surviving writing of Bishop Zumárraga mentions the events, and historians disagree about how much of the account is history. We have told it as the old story tells it, not as proven fact. The Church approves the devotion; as with every private revelation, Catholics are not required to believe the appearances happened.

**On the quotations:** the "little carrying rope, a little tail, a leaf" is a shortened paraphrase of Juan Diego's words in the account. "Am I not here, I who am your mother?" is the line most often quoted from it; it is translated from Nahuatl in several slightly different ways, and we have used a common one. The meaning given for his name, "the one who speaks like an eagle", is the usual traditional one.

**What is ours:** the knotted cloak, the narrator's asides, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. We have said only that those years were hard for his people, and left the conquest and its violence out of a bedtime story. The image itself, kept in the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe in Mexico City, is described here only in outline.

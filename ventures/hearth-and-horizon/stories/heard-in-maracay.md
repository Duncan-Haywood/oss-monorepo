summary: Two days after a hurricane, a Puerto Rican hill parish runs its four mountain chapels on a 40-metre radio net its assistant planned and tested fourteen times, all in daylight. At nine at night every chapel goes silent, including the one where an old man's oxygen runs on a battery. The band has gone long: 7.2 MHz now passes through the ionosphere straight overhead and comes down 900 km away, so a woman in Maracay can hear the chapel 23 km from the parish when the parish cannot. A failed relay through Florida, a working one through Venezuela, a battery and a cylinder worked out to the minute, and a mother who chooses to wait for first light. A story about silence that was not what it looked like.
---

Wilfredo Santiago had strung telephone wire across these hills for thirty-one years, and on Saturday night the hurricane took down every span of it he could see from the parish roof.

He could see a great deal from the parish roof. The church of San Isidro Labrador stood on the shoulder of a ridge, with the town below it in a fold of the Cordillera Central and the mountains going away on every side in green and grey, one behind the other, like a crowd that had come to look. On Monday afternoon the green was the wrong green. Half the leaves were gone off the hills, and what was left had been turned over by the wind so that the pale undersides showed, and through the stripped trees you could see the brown scars of slides on every slope, and the roads that the slides had crossed. The poles that Willy had climbed as a young man were lying down the hillsides like dropped matches.

He was seventy-one. His knees did not like ladders. He had climbed the church tower twice since Sunday morning anyway, the first time to see whether his antenna was still there (it was; he had tied it off to the bell frame with a lineman's knot in 2049 and the bell frame had not moved), and the second time to show his grandson Joel how to tie the knot, in case.

"In case of what?" Joel had said.

"In case I fall off," said Willy, and Joel, who was seventeen and had been taught to be respectful, had not laughed until they were both safely down.

* * *

The cell towers were down across the island. The phones still worked, after a fashion: every phone made since the forties could send a text to a satellite when it could not find a tower, and every phone on the island was trying to, all at once, and the satellites took them in turn. Ana Lucía Maldonado, the parish secretary, had sent her sister in Orlando a text at noon on Sunday saying they were all right. It had been delivered, the phone said, at eleven minutes to midnight.

"Delivered to what?" said Willy.

"To the satellite," said Vigía, from the speaker on Ana Lucía's desk. "Then it had to go down to the ground somewhere and find her phone. I don't know when she read it."

Vigía was the parish's assistant. The diocese had given one to every parish in 2050, a plain system that kept the registers and the rotas and answered the telephone when the telephone worked, and the people of San Isidro had named theirs Vigía, the lookout, because the first thing it had done for them was keep watch over the parish's emergency plan. It had written the plan with Ana Lucía over the winter. It knew where every bedridden person in the parish lived, who had a generator and who had a chainsaw and who would lend either. And it had planned the radio net.

The net was Willy's idea and Vigía's schedule. The parish had four mission chapels up in the barrios on the mountain: Cedros Altos, Quebrada Fría, Las Golondrinas and, highest and farthest, Loma del Viento, twenty-three kilometres away by the map and two ridges and a river by the road. A radio that talks in straight lines, the kind on a handheld or in a car, cannot get over two ridges. The repeater on the mountain that used to carry those signals for everyone had stopped answering at about two on Sunday morning, which Willy thought was the wind and Joel thought was the mountain coming down on it.

But a radio on the long-wave bands, on forty metres, can throw its voice straight up. Forty-metre signals go up through the clear air, eighty or ninety kilometres up, past the low layers, and on up to the high, thin, electrified layer that the Sun makes out of the top of the atmosphere every day, three hundred kilometres up, and if the frequency is right, the layer turns them round and sends them back down like a ball thrown at a ceiling. They come down in a wide circle, two ridges and a river being nothing to them. Hams called it near-vertical incidence skywave, and Willy called it bouncing it off the roof, and he had spent his savings and two years of his retirement putting a radio, a battery, a solar panel and a low wire antenna in each of the four chapels, and walking four people through the General class exam so that they could use them.

At Loma del Viento the operator was Gloria Maldonado, nineteen, a nursing student, Ana Lucía's daughter.

* * *

The net met at seven in the morning, at noon, at six in the evening and at nine at night, on seven point two three eight megahertz, because that was what Vigía's plan said, and Vigía's plan said it because in the spring they had tested it fourteen times and all four chapels had checked in, fourteen times out of fourteen, loud and clear.

At six on Monday evening, on the parish's third net since the storm, they checked in again. Willy sat at the radio in the room behind the sacristy, with a sheet of paper and a pencil, and the net went the way the plan said it would. Cedros Altos: a roof gone off the school, nobody hurt, rice for four days. Quebrada Fría: road cut both ways, a man with a cut hand that the nurse had stitched, three families sleeping in the chapel. Las Golondrinas: water, they needed water, the spring pipe was broken. Willy wrote it all down and Vigía wrote it all down and Ana Lucía stood behind his chair with her arms folded.

Then: "Parroquia, Loma del Viento."

"Go ahead, Loma."

"The power came back on in the chapel when the sun came out, so the battery's charging, or it was. It says two point six kilowatt-hours left. Tío Aurelio's on the concentrator at two litres." Gloria's voice was flat and careful, a nurse's voice, the voice of someone who has been taught to give a report. "He's comfortable. He's eating. He's asking about his chickens. We have one tank for when the battery runs out. I'll read the gauge for the nine o'clock. Over."

Don Aurelio Maldonado was eighty-eight, Ana Lucía's uncle, Gloria's great-uncle, and he lived on oxygen from a machine that pulled it out of the air and needed electricity to do it. When the power went on Saturday the family had carried him and the machine up the hill to the chapel, which had the battery, and that was where he had been since.

Vigía spoke over the speaker, quietly, so that only the room heard. "At what he's drawing and the radio and the lights, two point six kilowatt-hours lasts until about half past three in the morning."

"And then the tank," said Ana Lucía.

"And then the tank. I need the gauge."

"The health centre sent two full tanks up at four," said Ana Lucía. "They're in the pickup. Ramón says the road is open as far as the river and nobody knows above it. Nobody has been up above it since Saturday."

Ramón Colón Báez was the deacon. He was sixty-one and had driven a bread van on these roads for thirty years before he was ordained, and he had told Ana Lucía he would go up whenever she said.

"We'll know at nine," said Willy, and gave his call sign, because the law said every ten minutes, and closed the net.

* * *

At nine o'clock he called the net, and nobody answered.

He called Cedros Altos, which was the nearest, nine kilometres off. He called Quebrada Fría and Las Golondrinas. He called Loma del Viento three times, slowly, with a pause after each one long enough for a nineteen-year-old to find the microphone in the dark. He heard nothing from any of them. What he heard instead was everybody else.

The band was full. A man in Hialeah was talking to a man in Ohio about a generator. Somebody in Colombia was running a net of his own, calling stations by name in a slow, kind voice. There was a ship. There was a woman with a beautiful Caribbean English accent reading a list of names and numbers, very slowly, twice each, from somewhere that might have been Antigua. All of them came in loud, steady, as if they were in the next room. None of them were within five hundred kilometres.

"All four," said Ana Lucía. "All four at once."

"All four at once," said Willy. He took his hand off the microphone and sat back in the chair. "Ana, listen to me. That's the band."

"The band."

"The band has gone long. Listen to it." He turned the volume up so the room filled with the man in Hialeah and his generator. "That's Florida. Sixteen hundred kilometres. That's Colombia. When you hear the far ones like that and not the near ones, it means the roof has gone too thin for this frequency. The signal from Loma goes straight up and goes straight through and keeps going into space. The ones that hit the roof at a slant still bounce. So you hear Florida and you don't hear your own chapel."

Ana Lucía looked at the speaker on the desk. "Vigía."

"He's right about what it sounds like," said Vigía. "The layer that turns the signal round is made by sunlight, and after sunset it fades, slowly, all night. It can only turn back a signal coming straight up if the frequency is below a figure called the critical frequency. I have a model of how that figure goes through a night at this time of year, at this point in the Sun's cycle. It isn't measured, it's an average. It says the critical frequency passed seven point two four at about ten to eight this evening, and won't come back above it until about half past seven tomorrow morning."

"And the fourteen tests?"

"Were all between eight in the morning and half past six in the evening," said Vigía. "I've just checked. Every one. I wrote 'tested, fourteen of fourteen' on a schedule with a nine o'clock net on it, and I never tested the nine o'clock net. That is my mistake. It is the kind of mistake I'm supposed to catch."

Nobody said anything. In the speaker the woman in Antigua, if it was Antigua, said a name twice, and a number twice.

"So they're fine," said Ana Lucía.

"No," said Vigía. "I can't tell you that. I can tell you I can't hear them, and that I know a reason I wouldn't hear them if they were fine. That's not the same."

* * *

Ana Lucía did what a mother does, which was pick up her keys.

"Ramón can go now," she said. "He can be there by eleven."

"No," said Willy.

He said it quite gently. He got up out of the chair, which took him a moment, and stood in front of the door, not quite blocking it.

"Ana, nobody has been on that road above the river since Saturday. It rained all of Sunday. There'll be slides on it nobody's seen, in the dark, and Ramón is sixty-one, and he'll drive it at night because you asked him, and if he goes off the road at the second bridge there's nobody in the world who'll know until morning, because his phone will be in a queue to a satellite." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I'm not saying don't go. I'm saying don't send a man up a mountain in the dark because a radio is quiet. Find out first. Find out why it's quiet. Then send him, if you have to, when you have to."

"And how do I find out? You just said nobody can hear them."

"I said we can't," said Willy. "I didn't say nobody can."

* * *

His first try was the man in Hialeah.

It took ten minutes to get a word in, because the man in Hialeah liked to talk about generators. When Willy did, the man in Hialeah was kind about it, and quick. Yes, he heard San Isidro fine, very fine, a five-and-nine, a good signal. Yes, he would listen for a chapel called Loma del Viento. Willy called Loma three times, and the man in Hialeah listened, and the man in Hialeah said, after the third: "Sorry, brother. I hear you like a church bell. I don't hear nobody else."

"Why does he hear me and not her?" said Ana Lucía. It was half past nine. She had put the keys down on the desk, but she had not let go of them.

"Because of where the wires are," said Vigía. "Willy's antenna is up on the bell tower, about seventeen metres. The chapel antennas are about six metres up, on the poles Willy put by each chapel. A wire that low, at this frequency, throws most of what it has straight up. That's what you want for the chapels and the parish to hear each other, by day. To reach Florida, the signal has to leave the ground at about sixteen degrees, nearly flat, and a six-metre wire sends about a tenth as much power that way as it sends straight up. Willy's wire, higher, sends a bit more that way than straight up. So Hialeah hears the tower, and not the chapels."

"Then there's a closer place," said Willy. He was already turning the dial, slowly, listening. "Not so flat. Somewhere eight or nine hundred kilometres off. A slant of thirty degrees."

"At thirty degrees," said Vigía, "the chapel wire keeps about a third of what it sends straight up, not a tenth. And at nine hundred kilometres the roof is still thick enough to turn seven point two back, until it thins a good deal more. On my model, until about half past midnight. Give or take an hour."

"Nine hundred kilometres," said Ana Lucía. "That's the sea."

"That's the sea," said Willy, "and then that's Venezuela."

* * *

The woman in Maracay was called Rosalba Quintero. She was sixty-six. She had taught chemistry at a secondary school for thirty-four years, she said, and she had been a ham since she was a girl, because her father had been one, and she had been sitting at her radio on that frequency since the storm because she had seen the satellite pictures of it going over Puerto Rico and thought, somebody will need somebody.

"Ay, mijo," she said, when Willy had explained, with the pauses that radio needs. "Of course. Call your daughter. I'll listen. Cambio."

"Not my daughter," said Willy. "But go ahead. Loma del Viento, Loma del Viento, this is the parish, calling through Maracay."

He let go of the key. The speaker hissed. Rosalba was silent, and the silence went on so long that Ana Lucía closed her eyes.

"Parroquia, Maracay," said Rosalba at last. "I have her. She's weak but I have her. She says, Loma del Viento, Gloria. She says, we're all right. She says, she called at nine and nobody answered, and she's been calling every quarter of an hour since, and she's frightened you were the ones in trouble. Cambio."

Ana Lucía put her hand over her mouth.

"Tell her it was the band," said Willy, and his voice was not quite steady either. "Tell her the parish is fine and the band went long and it's nobody's fault. Ask her for the gauge on the tank."

The conversation that followed took forty minutes, because every sentence went from the room behind the sacristy to the top of the sky to a flat in Maracay and back up to the top of the sky and down to a chapel on a hill twenty-three kilometres away, and then the same way back, with Rosalba in the middle, reading everything twice, slowly, the way the woman in Antigua had done. It was ten past ten, and then it was half past.

Gloria said Don Aurelio was asleep. She said the chapel was full: eleven people sleeping on the benches. She said the gauge on the tank read eleven hundred. She said the battery said one point four.

"Eleven hundred pounds per square inch," said Vigía. "A full one of that size reads about two thousand and holds about six hundred and eighty litres. So it's a little over half full. About three hundred and seventy litres. At two litres a minute, about three hours and five minutes."

"She used it on Saturday," said Ana Lucía. "Before they got him up the hill."

"Then that's where the rest went," said Vigía. "Ask her to switch off everything she can: the lights, the fridge, anything that doesn't keep him breathing. Then I can tell you when the battery goes."

They asked. Rosalba asked. Gloria said, through Rosalba, that she'd turned off the lights in the chapel and the fridge, which had nothing in it that wouldn't keep, and that eleven people were now asleep in the dark and one of them was snoring.

"Then," said Vigía, "with the radio on, the battery lasts until about half past four. With the radio off after we finish, about seven minutes to five. Then the tank, which takes him to about eight o'clock. Those are not measurements. They're what the numbers on the sticker and the battery's own count say, and I'd want twenty minutes either way."

"And the truck?"

"The road above the river in the dark is the unknown. If Ramón leaves at a quarter past five, he drives the paved part in the dark, which he knows, and he reaches the part nobody's seen at about first light, which is at ten to six. Sunrise is a quarter past six. He has the tanks, he has two hours and three quarters to do what takes an hour and a quarter on a good day, and he can see what's in front of him."

"Or he leaves now," said Ana Lucía, "and he's there by midnight."

"Or he leaves now," said Vigía, "and he drives the part nobody's seen in the dark, to arrive about eight hours before he's needed. That's your decision. It isn't mine. I've told you the numbers I'd want if it were."

Ana Lucía looked at Willy. Willy looked at the floor. In the speaker, very faintly, under the hiss, Rosalba was humming something while she waited, and then, realising her microphone was open, stopped.

"A quarter past five," said Ana Lucía.

* * *

At twenty to one Rosalba's voice began to go.

It did not go all at once. It thinned, the way a voice thins when someone walks away from you down a corridor: the words first, then the shape of them, then only the rhythm, and a kind of watery flutter on top of it all. "It's the roof again," said Willy. "It's thinning for her too." Vigía, who had said half past midnight, said nothing about having been right.

The last messages came across in pieces. Ramón would leave at a quarter past five. Gloria understood. She would turn the radio off now, because the radio drew twenty watts and twenty watts for four hours was, Vigía said, about twenty-two minutes of oxygen at the end. She would turn it on again at seven, on the little that the battery keeps back for itself after it stops running the machine.

And then, in Rosalba's voice, very faint, read twice: "She says: we'll be quiet now. Don't come up the hill because we're quiet. Cambio."

"Tell her yes," said Ana Lucía into the microphone, which Willy had given her, licence or no licence, saying he would answer for it. "Tell her, Dios te bendiga. Tell her, I'm proud of her." She said it again, slowly, the second time.

"Copied," said Rosalba, from very far away. "I'll be back at six with my coffee. The Virgen de Coromoto is watching that hill tonight. I'll tell her where it is. Cambio y fuera."

And then there was only the hiss, and the man in Hialeah, who had a better antenna and was not going anywhere.

* * *

Nobody in the room behind the sacristy slept much. Joel slept, on two chairs pushed together, the way seventeen-year-olds can sleep through anything. Willy slept for an hour in the armchair and woke up with his neck stiff and the radio still on, turned low, and the band still long: the man in Hialeah had gone to bed and been replaced by someone in Spain, which Willy said was the roof saying it was nearly four.

Ana Lucía did not sleep. She sat at her desk with a rosary in her lap, not saying it, just holding it, and every so often she would start on a decade and get three Hail Marys in and stop and look at the speaker, which had nothing to say, and then go back and start the decade again.

"Vigía," she said, at about three.

"I'm here."

"Are you listening to the hill?"

"I'm listening to the frequency," said Vigía. "There's nothing on it from the hill. Gloria said there wouldn't be."

"I know."

"I've written the change to the plan," said Vigía. "I'll read it to you if you want. It isn't a good time."

"Read it."

"Evening nets move to eighty metres, three point eight five, while the roof is thick enough for it. That needs a second wire at each chapel, about thirty-seven metres long. Willy has a spool of the old telephone drop wire in his shed that will do. On my model, at this point in the Sun's cycle, eighty metres also fails between about half past one and a quarter past six in the morning, so for those hours the plan is a distant station who's agreed in advance, like Señora Quintero, on a schedule, and no news is not to be read as bad news. And every new net time gets tested at that time of day before it goes in the plan. Under the fourteen tests I've written: all in daylight. I would like that to stay in the plan after it stops mattering. The next person who reads 'fourteen of fourteen' should know which fourteen."

Ana Lucía said nothing for a while.

"Leave it in," she said.

* * *

At ten past five Ramón came in with his coffee in a flask and a reflective vest on over the shirt he had been wearing since Saturday. He was not a priest and could not anoint anyone, and he had said so to Ana Lucía on Sunday, kindly, in case she was hoping; but he had the Blessed Sacrament in a pyx in his breast pocket for Don Aurelio, who had asked for Communion on Saturday and not received it, and two full oxygen tanks in the back of the pickup, wedged with sandbags.

Joel was going with him, because Joel had young eyes and could hold a torch and was strong enough to carry a tank if it came to carrying.

"It won't come to carrying," said Ramón.

At six minutes past six, at the nineteenth kilometre, above the river, in the grey light before sunrise, it came to carrying. The slide had taken the road and gone on down the hillside, and where the road had been there was a slope of red mud and splintered bamboo and one whole tree lying upside down with its roots in the air. Ramón stopped the pickup and got out and looked at it for a long time.

Then he and Joel put one tank each on their backs, in the straps the health centre had sent with them, and the pyx in Ramón's pocket, and climbed round the head of the slide on a goat path that Joel found, and went on up the road on foot. It was three and a half kilometres to Loma del Viento from there, and every one of them was uphill.

* * *

At half past six, in the room behind the sacristy, the man in Spain was fading, and under him, in pieces, there was Rosalba.

"Parroquia, Maracay. Good morning, Puerto Rico. Have you got coffee? Cambio."

"We have coffee," said Willy. "We have coffee and no news. The truck left at a quarter past five."

"Then we wait for seven," said Rosalba, and they waited for seven, with the room filling up with daylight and the stripped hills going from grey to green outside, and Ana Lucía standing at the window with the rosary in her hand.

At seven o'clock Rosalba said, "I have her. Ay, I have her. She says: the battery went at ten to five. He's on the tank. The gauge says about three fifty. He's awake. He asked for coffee and she told him no. No deacon yet. Cambio."

"Three hundred and fifty is about a hundred and twenty litres," said Vigía, before anyone could ask. "About an hour."

None of them knew that the last three and a half kilometres of the road were being walked. They waited. Willy held the microphone in his lap and did not key it. At twenty-past seven, Rosalba said she could hear the chapel's radio open and close, open and close, like someone trying to speak and thinking better of it, and Ana Lucía said a whole decade of the rosary straight through, out loud, for the first time that night.

At twenty-seven minutes past seven, said Vigía afterwards, from the log, a new voice came into the speaker from no direction at all, faint, coming up out of the hiss like someone walking toward you up a hill.

It did not come through Maracay. It came straight down.

"Parroquia, Parroquia, this is Loma del Viento. Can you hear me? Mami, can you hear me?"

Willy looked at Ana Lucía, and held out the microphone, and she took it in both hands.

"I hear you," she said. "I hear you, mi amor. Over."

"The deacon's here," said Gloria. "Him and Joel. They walked. They carried the tanks up from the slide. Tío Aurelio's on the new tank and the deacon's giving him Communion now. He says to tell you his knees will never forgive him. Over."

Behind her voice there was the sound of a lot of people moving, and a man's voice, cracked and old, saying something about chickens; and then a different sound, which Willy knew before anyone else in the room, because he had hung the bell himself in 2047 from a beam of ausubo wood that had been part of the old schoolhouse.

It was the chapel bell at Loma del Viento. Somebody up there had gone out into the morning and was ringing it, the way the barrio had agreed they would ring it if help came; not the Angelus, not anything with a pattern, just a bell ringing in the fresh light over a stripped green hill. It went up from the hill as a sound and came into Gloria's microphone as a sound, and then it went three hundred kilometres up into the sky as something else and turned round at the top of the world and came down into the room behind the sacristy at San Isidro Labrador, very small, very clear, a little crackled, like a bell heard across a valley on a still morning, which is what it was.

"Maracay," said Rosalba, from far away, though nobody had called her. "I hear the bell too. Cambio."

*What is real: amateur radio operators use near-vertical incidence skywave (NVIS) on the 40-metre and 80-metre bands to talk over mountains at distances out to a few hundred kilometres, with low horizontal wire antennas that radiate mostly upward. The ionosphere's F2 layer is produced by sunlight; its critical frequency (foF2) falls through the night and rises quickly after sunrise, and a signal sent straight up passes through the layer when its frequency is above foF2. Oblique signals can still be returned when vertical ones are not, at up to roughly foF2 times the secant of the angle at which they meet the layer, which is why at night operators hear distant stations loudly on a frequency where nearby ones fall silent (a skip zone; "the band has gone long"). The 40-metre band in the Americas runs 7.0 to 7.3 MHz; in the United States, General class licensees may use voice from 7.175 MHz. US rules require a station to give its call sign at least every ten minutes during a contact and at its end, and they allow stations to use any means of communication when it is needed for the immediate safety of human life. Our Lady of Coromoto is the patroness of Venezuela. Puerto Rican and Venezuelan expressions are used as the author understands them. The ausubo is a Puerto Rican hardwood.*

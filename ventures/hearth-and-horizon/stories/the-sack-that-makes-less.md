title: The Sack That Makes Less
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Ernan's hall yard, an invented parish near Strabane, Co. Tyrone, Saturday 14 June 2053
published: 2026-11-07
summary: A parish wants a concrete base for a bike shed. The ready-mix label says a 25 kg bag makes 12 litres, which means 24 bags. The parish assistant says to make one bag first and measure it; the real yield is 11.2 litres, the ground is uneven, and the answer is 29, not 24.
---

Oisín Canning had the slab drawn on the back of a hymn sheet. The new bike shed for the youth club was to stand on a concrete base 2.4 metres by 1.2, and the base was to be 100 millimetres thick. He had the timber for the form already nailed up in the corner of the yard, and a trench of dug-out hardcore tamped flat inside it. He held the sheet up to the speaker on the hall wall.

"Two point four by one point two by a tenth," he said. "That's two hundred and eighty-eight litres. The bag in the merchant's says twelve litres to the bag. So twenty-four bags. I'll get twenty-four and be done by dinner."

"The sum is right," said Fógra, the parish assistant. "Two point four times one point two is 2.88 square metres; a tenth of a metre deep is 0.288 cubic metres, which is 288 litres, and 288 divided by 12 is 24. I would still not order twenty-four yet. May I say why, and then ask you to do one small thing with one bag?"

Oisín put the sheet down on the tailgate. He was a patient man with sheets of paper and an impatient one with people who said *still*.

* * *

"The twelve on the label is a yield, and a yield is what the maker found on a good day with their own water and their own mould. Three things can pull it down on yours. First, the water. The label says about two and a half litres to a bag, from memory, and the pile on the pallet is a little different from the pile in the lab. Second, the compaction. You will tamp it into corners, and the sand and stones pack closer than they sat loose in the sack. Third, a bag that has sat in a damp shed for a winter may have lumped; I do not know yours."

"So a bit less than twelve."

"Probably. I do not know how much, and that is the trouble, because the bag is where the whole answer lives. If it makes 11.2 litres, 288 needs 25.7 bags. If it makes 10.5, it needs 27.4. A litre short in each bag is two or three bags short at the end, with the form half full of concrete that is going off."

"That's a long way to get at the same thing."

"It is. Here is the small thing. Take one bag. Mix it as the label says, two and a half litres of clean water, no more. Tamp it into a box you can measure, and read the volume off the box."

* * *

Oisín did not enjoy it, and he did it. He knocked up a box from offcuts, 60 centimetres long, 30 wide, and he marked 6 centimetres up the inside with a pencil line: 60 by 30 by 6 is 10,800 cubic centimetres, 10.8 litres. He tipped a 25 kilogram bag into a builder's bucket, a little under half at a time, added 2.5 litres by the jug and turned it with a trowel until the colour went even. He put on his rubber gloves first, since Fógra had asked, and the wellingtons; wet concrete is caustic, and a splash of it down the inside of a boot, left for an afternoon, burns.

He shovelled it in and tamped it with the end of a batten. The box filled to the pencil line and he had a little left in the bucket, a heaped trowel, perhaps four or five hundred cubic centimetres. He scooped it into a measuring jug; it came to 0.4 litres, a bit under.

"Ten point eight and four tenths," said Fógra. "Eleven point two litres from a bag that says twelve. That is seven per cent below the label. Now the slab: 288 divided by 11.2 is 25.7 bags. Twenty-six, if the ground were a billiard table."

"It's not a billiard table."

"No. Would you put a long straight batten across the form and tell me how far below its top edge the hardcore sits, at three or four places?"

* * *

The hardcore sat at 100 millimetres at one end and at 112, 108 and 111 along the middle and the far side. Oisín laid the batten, measured with a rule and read the numbers out standing in his socks, because the wellingtons were drying on the wall.

"Average about nine and a half over the hundred," said Fógra. "Say ten millimetres too deep across the whole slab. Ten millimetres of 2.88 square metres is 28.8 litres, which is another 2.6 bags. So the first thing was 25.7 and the second is 2.6: 28.3 bags. The spread of the readings is only twelve millimetres, and soil that has been trodden settles another few; I would not claim that I know the answer to half a bag. A bag more to cover a spill, a split bag, and the corner you will want to be generous with. Thirty."

"Thirty is a pallet," said Oisín slowly. "Thirty times twenty-five is seven hundred and fifty kilos. I can't lift that on my own."

"Not at once. The merchant delivers a pallet to the kerb. You will want two or three people to shift the bags to the mixing board, and the slab is a day for four. You have 65 litres of water to find as well: twenty-six bags at two and a half litres. Please do not add more to make it easier to move. The weaker the mix, the more water was in it, and on a bike shed that has to take a loaded bicycle against the wall for fifty years, that is the one saving you should not make."

"And if I'm a few bags short?"

"Then one corner of the form is empty and the first side has already begun to harden, and a second pour joins badly onto the first. Twenty-four is a disaster. Thirty with a bag or two left over is a good Saturday."

Oisín looked at the batten, at the spread, at the bucket with the last scraping of grey in it.

"I'd have been four bags short," he said. "Four. And a hole in the corner."

"Nearer five, with the ground," said Fógra. "Check the diagonals of the form as well, corner to corner; if they differ, it is not square, and the sums above are for a square one."

* * *

They ordered thirty. The lorry came on the Saturday at ten and put the pallet at the kerb, and Oisín's son and the two Canavan boys and a retired plasterer called Declan who had been told to supervise and not lift, which he did, loudly. They poured in four lots of seven or eight bags, wet-mixed in the drum. The last lot went in at ten past one. The slab came out flush with the form with the upper surface 4 millimetres low in one corner, which Declan said was the best kind of mistake and which nobody mentioned again.

There were two bags left over at the end. The test slab, set hard in its box, went by the gate as a doorstop, with *11.2* scratched in the face of it with a nail.

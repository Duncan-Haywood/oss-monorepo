summary: A parish wants 20 spaces for funerals on a 30 by 14 metre yard. The assistant counts three layouts: bays at right angles on both sides need 16 metres and give 24, 45-degree bays need 14.21 and give only 14, and one row at right angles plus a row along the kerb fits and gives 17, so the parish decides whether to ask the farmer for two metres.
---

Declan Moloney had a tape, a ball of builder's line and a bag of pegs, and he had marked out the corners of the hall yard already: thirty metres along the wall, fourteen out to the hedge. The committee wanted twenty spaces for funerals, when the road outside filled and the hearse could not turn. He asked the parish assistant, whose name was Fenn, to say how the lines should go.

"What is a space?" said Fenn.

"Two and a half by five. It's what the council uses."

"Then I will use it, and say where it matters. Take the plainest layout first. Bays at right angles to the wall, side by side. Thirty metres divided by two and a half is exactly twelve, with nothing left over. In front of them the cars need a lane to swing out. Six metres is usual for two-way. That is five and six, eleven metres, and you have fourteen."

"So I fit it."

"You fit one row of twelve, with three metres of yard unused beside the hedge. Putting a second row of bays against the hedge needs another five, which is sixteen in all. You are two metres short. That would have been twenty-four spaces."

* * *

Declan said twelve was no good, then, and twenty-four was out of reach, and asked about angled bays, because he had seen them at the supermarket and thought they looked tidy.

"They are for one-way traffic, which makes the lane narrower, say three and a half. At forty-five degrees a bay that is two and a half wide at the kerb is 2.5 over the sine of forty-five, 3.54 metres along the wall. It reaches out 5 times 0.7071 plus 2.5 times 0.7071, which is 5.30 metres from the wall. Two rows and the lane make 5.30 + 3.6 + 5.30, or 14.2."

"We have fourteen."

"We are twenty-one centimetres short, and I would not shave the bays to hide it. There is a worse thing. Angled bays leave a triangle at each end of a row that nobody can park in. Along thirty metres, seven bays take 24.7 metres and the end triangle another 3.5, which is 28.3, and an eighth bay would need 31.8. Seven a side is fourteen. That is less than the plain way gives you."

Declan laughed. "The tidy one is worst."

"The tidy one is for a yard that is long and thin, and yours is neither."

* * *

"There is a third way," said Fenn. "Use the three metres you are wasting. Cars parked along the hedge, nose to tail, are parallel bays, six metres by two and a half or so. Make them two and four-tenths wide, and the right-angled bay, the lane and the parallel bay come to 5 + 6 + 2.4, which is 13.4. That fits in your fourteen, with sixty centimetres to spare. Along the hedge, thirty divided by six is five bays. Twelve at right angles and five along the kerb is seventeen."

"Seventeen is three short of twenty."

"It is. I can give you seventeen on the ground you have, and I cannot make it twenty. I would not squeeze the lane to find three more: the vans that come for funerals do not manage a narrow lane with a car backing out, and I would rather you counted on what a driver can do on a bad day. And one thing about the parallel bays: a driver has to reverse in, and some of your mourners will not. I would leave the end ones for the people who can."

Declan wrote *17* on the back of his hand and then *24 if +2 m* under it. He said the field behind the hedge belonged to Mairead Fahy's brother, who had been good about the bin store, and that two metres was not a lot to ask for, though it was not Declan's to ask. Fenn said that was the committee's business, and the farmer's, and that it did not need to decide that on the evening: the seventeen could be painted now and the twenty-four drawn on paper for the day someone asked.

* * *

They pegged the right-angled bays that afternoon. Declan knotted the builder's line at every two and a half metres, so that nobody would have to measure again, and ran it along the wall with the bottom peg at the corner. At the twelfth knot the line reached the other corner of the wall with no gap at all, to the width of a finger. He drove the last peg in and stood there with the tape in his hand, looking at the three metres of bare gravel beside the hedge.

title: The Wind at Two O'Clock
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: San Bernardo di Prato Alto (invented), the Piedmont Alps, Italy, 2 to 3 November 2048
published: 2026-11-02
summary: A landslide cuts the only road to a mountain hamlet on All Souls' Day, and a valley cooperative's cargo drone is the way in. The delivery assistant works out what 11 kilometres, 1,300 metres of climb, thin air and a headwind cost a 900 watt-hour battery, and finds that the wind it was told about is not the wind on the ridge.
---

The road to San Bernardo di Prato Alto went into the mountain at ten past four on the afternoon of the first of November, a long grey slide of wet scree from the Rocca Nera that took forty metres of tarmac and a guardrail with it. Nobody was hurt. The hamlet had thirty-one people, one priest, and, in the pantry of a woman called Rosa Gallo, four tablets of a heart medicine that she took one of each morning.

Elena Bertolini, who ran the pharmacy in the valley town of Varrone and had known Rosa's prescription for eleven years, found that out by phone at half past seven the next morning. She did not say what she thought. She said, "I will find a way," and rang the cooperative.

The cooperative had a drone. It was the only one for fifty kilometres and it had been bought by the valley's mountain communities three years earlier, after the second winter in which the road had closed for a week. It had four rotors, a case for the cold box, and a delivery assistant that worked out its flights, and which the cooperative's volunteer pilot, Davide Rinaldi, called "the Clerk" because it kept the log.

"It is the second of November, All Souls," said Davide, in the hangar behind the fire station, at a quarter to nine. He had flown the drone to San Bernardo eleven times, in good weather, with parcels. "Don Aldo is up there already. He went up on Saturday for the vigil. There is a Mass for the dead at ten in the church and the whole hamlet will be in it, and in the afternoon they will go to the cemetery. I would like Rosa's tablets and the two insulin pens up the mountain before that. Can we go at eleven? Elena can have the box packed by then."

"I can tell you what the flight costs," said the Clerk. "May I do it aloud, because I would like you to check me?"

"Go on."

* * *

"The drone, empty of load, with its battery, is 14 kilograms. The box with the tablets, the pens and an ice pack is 3. So it leaves at 17. Varrone is at 600 metres. The chapel meadow at San Bernardo is at 1,900, which is 1,300 metres up, and the line on the map is 11 kilometres. The cruise is 15 metres a second through the air."

"Twelve minutes, then," said Davide.

"In still air, 12 minutes and 13 seconds. The battery is 1,000 watt-hours when full. The controller will not let me use the last 10 per cent, so the working figure is 900. The cooperative's rule, which you wrote with the fire brigade, is that the plan must not use more than three-quarters of that. That is 675 watt-hours for the round trip, and 225 left in the pack on the ground at Varrone."

"I wrote it after the lake," said Davide. He meant a morning, three summers ago, when a drone of the same kind had come down in the reservoir below Varrone with a pizza for a refuge. He said no more.

"For the power," said the Clerk, "I use the hover formula, because I do not have a better one for this airframe. The thrust has to carry the weight. For four rotors each 0.8 metres across, the disc area is 2.01 square metres. At 17 kilograms the ideal hover power in the air at the middle of the climb, about 1,250 metres, where the density is 1.085, is about 1,000 watts. The rotors and motors are not ideal. I use a figure of merit of 0.65 and a motor-and-controller efficiency of 0.85, which come from the maker's sheet and from the flights in my log, so the electrical figure is about 1,870 watts. Climbing is extra, but not much. Lifting 17 kilograms by 1,300 metres is 217 kilojoules, which is 60 watt-hours."

"And forward flight is cheaper than hovering."

"It is. I use three-quarters of the hover power at cruise. That is the number I trust least of the whole sum, and I will say how I checked it. Last Thursday you flew the same route with 3 kilograms in the box in a wind of nothing. My sum for that was 558 watt-hours for the round trip. The log says 540. The two are three per cent apart, which is better than I deserve for a figure I chose by looking at what fitted."

"So still air, it is 558 against 675," Davide said, and he was already looking at the sky outside the hangar door, which was grey and still. "We go."

"Please let me do the wind first," said the Clerk.

* * *

"The wind changes the time on each leg," it said. "The drone flies at 15 metres a second through the air. Into a headwind of 4, the ground speed is 11. The 11 kilometres then takes 16 minutes 40 seconds instead of 12. The motors do not know about the wind. They push the same air at the same speed, so the power is about the same, and the energy rises with the time. That is my model. It leaves out that a headwind also changes the angle of the body, which I cannot calculate. I am treating it as small."

"You said that about the pizza," said Davide, but gently.

"I did not say it about the pizza. A different system said it. I am reading from my own log, and I would rather you did not trust me on the strength of a name." It went on. "On the way out, with 17 kilograms and a headwind of 4, the sum is 449 watt-hours. The way back is empty, 14 kilograms, with a 4-metre tailwind, ground speed 19, and 168 watt-hours. The round trip is 617. That fits in 675 with 58 to spare. At 6 metres a second it is 688, which does not. At 3 it is 594. The wind at which the plan stops fitting is about 5.4 metres a second, a headwind on the outward leg."

"And the wind?"

"The valley station in Varrone says 1 metre a second from the north-west at nine o'clock, and the regional forecast, which I read this morning at six, says 5 at the pass in the afternoon."

"There," said Davide. "Five at the pass. It is under your 5.4."

"It is under it by four-tenths of a metre a second, in a forecast I read at six, for the afternoon, for the pass and not the ridge. I would not like to fly at the edge of the rule with a forecast that is eight hours old. May I ask someone with an anemometer on the ridge?"

* * *

There was one. The cooperative had put it on the ridge, on the Col di Santa Barbara, where the line from Varrone to San Bernardo crossed the saddle at 2,240 metres, because the saddle was the only place on the route where the mountain made its own weather. It sent a number every ten minutes to a page which the Clerk was allowed to read.

"It says 7 metres a second from the north-west at 8:50," said the Clerk. "Gusting to 10. It is the first number the instrument has sent since the landslide, which cut the power lead to the post; it is running on its own panel and I thought, when I read the past days, that it was only slow. I owe you a correction. At six o'clock I read the forecast and I reported 5 for the pass. I had not looked at the ridge station, and I should have looked first. The pass is lower and more sheltered. The saddle is where a north-west wind is squeezed, and it is more, not less."

Davide said nothing. He took the tablet and looked, and then put it down. "Seven at 8:50."

"Seven at a point on the line, at 2,240 metres. I do not know the wind along the other 10 kilometres. If I use 7 as a headwind for the whole 11, which is wrong, the outward leg is 8 metres a second ground speed, 22 minutes, and the energy is about 585 watt-hours going, and about 135 coming back, which is 720 for the round trip against 675. If the wind holds only on the ridge and is 3 elsewhere, it is probably nearer 650, and I cannot tell the two apart. I would not fly into either."

"It is a quarter past nine."

"It is, and the forecast says the wind gets stronger by two o'clock. The regional model gives the valley wind by early afternoon from the north-west at 8 or 9, at the ridge perhaps 10 or more. At 9 metres a second the outward leg is 30 minutes 36 seconds. The energy to go is 773 watt-hours. The battery has 900. If you flew in the afternoon you could get there and could not get home, and you would arrive with 127 watt-hours, 14 per cent. That is not margin."

* * *

Davide took his hat off, turned it round, and put it back on. A man from the fire brigade, Franco, who had come across for coffee, said what Davide had not wanted to say. "Send it one way. It lands in the meadow. Fetch it when the road is open. It is a drone. It is not a person."

"I considered that," said the Clerk. "May I say what is wrong with it? It is possible, in the sense that 773 is less than 900. In a gust at the saddle, with a 17-kilogram load, a pack at 14 per cent is not an arrival. It is a coin. If it comes down on the ridge, the tablets are on the ridge, and the cold box with them. I would rather not stake the thing I am here to carry on a margin I would not give a person."

"There is Rosa," said Davide.

"There is Rosa. She has four tablets. Elena says she takes one a morning, so she has the second, third, fourth and fifth of November. She has enough until Friday morning. I asked Elena if she had a different view of that, and she said that Rosa should not miss a day if it can be helped, and that she would not call a missed day a catastrophe. She said that to me, not to you, because I asked. I am passing it on because it is her sentence."

"And the pens?"

"The pens are the other thing. There are two, and they are for a man named Livio Gallo, who is Rosa's brother. Elena's note says he has one pen in use, in the house, in the cold of the cellar, and a second he has not opened. I do not know what is in his cellar and I did not ask it. The pens in the box would be a reserve and not an emergency, she said."

Davide let out his breath. "So nobody is dying today."

"Nobody is dying today that I know of. I would not say that of this afternoon if I were certain of nothing. I am not. If Rosa phones and the picture changes, I will tell you at once, and it will then be a different sum, and a different decision."

* * *

"What do we do?" said Davide.

"The wind falls at night. The regional model has the north-westerly dropping to 2 or 3 by dawn tomorrow and the ridge station's last three nights bear that out: 1.5, 2 and 4 metres a second at six o'clock. If the first light is at 6:40, the drone can leave at 6:30 under the cooperative's night-flying exemption, which you hold, as I read it, for the 30 minutes before sunrise. At 3 metres a second the plan is 594 watt-hours, which is 88 per cent of the limit and 81 to spare. I would take it. I would rather it were 2. It is a day of waiting, and the day costs Rosa her worry and Don Aldo his Mass with the parcel in it, and I am sorry for that."

"And if the wind is not down in the morning?"

"Then I will say so at six, from the ridge station, which I will read first. If it is above 5, I will not recommend the flight and the cooperative will have to decide whether to ask the army. I would not fly with the wind on the ridge above 5.4 whoever asked."

"Elena will want you to fly today."

"She will. I will tell her what I told you, with the numbers, and I will tell her the part that is hers: that the choice is between a likely delivery tomorrow and an uncertain one today."

Davide said, "I'll ring Don Aldo."

* * *

The priest was in the sacristy of San Bernardo with the Roman Missal open at the Commemoration of All the Faithful Departed when the phone rang, and it was a long call. He was seventy-nine. His church held sixty and was holding thirty-one, and the people he was going to pray for, in the cemetery behind it, were the ones their families had put there since 1612, whose names were on the wall by the door.

He told Davide that the Mass would go on as it was. A Mass for the dead did not wait for a parcel. He said that there was a custom in the valley that each family put a candle on its own graves in the evening, and that the wind, if it was going to be a wind, would put them out, and that he would take it as a sign of nothing.

"I would ask one thing," he said. "If you do come tomorrow, come before the Angelus. The people want to be at the church at noon."

"Before seven," said Davide.

"Then bring a cup of coffee," said Don Aldo, "and I will not mind the weather."

* * *

The wind did not drop at once. At nine in the evening the ridge station read 8. At midnight it read 6, and the Clerk, which did not sleep, wrote the figure in its log and did nothing else with it. At three, 4.5. At five, while Davide was driving to the hangar with a flask, 2.8.

"Two point eight at the saddle, 3 on the last ten-minute mean, gusts to 4.5," said the Clerk, when he plugged in the tablet. "It is nine minutes to six, and sunrise is at 6:40. The valley station reads 1. The regional model says the wind comes up after nine, so we have a window and it is not a long one. The plan is 594 watt-hours at 3. If the ground speed at the saddle is 11 for a few minutes it does not change the sum by much. The battery is full, the box is in, and the weight is 17.04 kilograms, which I measured on the scale and not from the packing slip. Elena's pharmacist's seal is on the box."

"Go through the rule," said Davide.

"The plan may use 675. It uses 594 if the wind is as measured, and 688 if it is 6, which is the wind at which it would not fit. If the ridge reads above 5 at any time before launch, I do not recommend the flight. If, in flight, the energy used passes the line I will draw on your screen — it is the plan's own energy at each minute plus 10 per cent — I will bring it home. If it is past the point where it can come home, I will tell you the nearest flat ground. There is the meadow at the Alpe Vecchia at 1,640 metres, six kilometres short of San Bernardo, and the sheepfold at the saddle."

Davide said the prayer he said before every flight, which was short, and the drone lifted at 6:31, in the dark, with its lights, and climbed away up the valley.

* * *

It was a quiet flight, which is the best sort. The Clerk read the numbers aloud once a minute, and Davide checked them against his screen. At the saddle the drone met the squeeze of the wind, the ground speed fell to 9 for four minutes, and the line Davide had drawn bent upward and stayed under the limit. At 6:42 the drone crossed the saddle. At 6:48 it came down on the meadow behind the church, in a light so low that Don Aldo, who was standing there with his cassock blown flat, saw first its green light and then its shadow.

The flight took 17 minutes up, about five minutes longer than still air, most of it lost at the saddle. The log recorded 612 watt-hours at touchdown for the round trip: after the box was handed over the Clerk flew it home empty at once, with a tailwind that had grown to 5 by then. It landed at Varrone at 7:05 with 288 watt-hours left in the pack, which is 32 per cent of the 900. The planned energy had been 594. The figure the log gave was 612, which was 3 per cent more. All of those numbers are made up for this story, except for the arithmetic that holds them together.

"It is 18 more than I said," the Clerk told Davide. "I will put it in the log with the wind, and next time the saddle will have its own number in my sum instead of a guess."

"What would you have said if it had been 700?"

"That I was wrong, and by how much, and that it had come home on the margin, and that the rule had done what you wrote it to do."

* * *

At noon in San Bernardo the bell, which was a small one, was rung for the Angelus by a boy of eleven, Matteo, who had been told he could ring it as long as he stopped counting after the third of the nine. The thirty-one stood in the cold outside the church, not many in that great amphitheatre of rock. Rosa Gallo was among them. She had her box of tablets in her coat. She had been given them at five to seven by Don Aldo, who had carried the box across the meadow at the pace of a man of seventy-nine who is not going to drop it.

She had not said much at the door. She had said that the pills were the right ones, because she knew the box. She had said, "You came in the morning, then. They told me you might come in the afternoon," and Don Aldo had said that the afternoon had been judged too windy, by a machine in the valley that could add. Rosa had looked at him as if he were joking.

"It was right?" she said.

"It was right," said Don Aldo. "It was off by three in a hundred this morning, and by more than that yesterday, and it said so."

"Hm," said Rosa Gallo, who had been told many things by the machine's elder cousins on the telephone for ten years, most of them wrong.

In the afternoon the north-westerly rose to 9 metres a second at the saddle, as the forecast had said it would. At twenty past two the ridge station sent a gust of 13. The Clerk wrote the figure in the log, and Davide, who was drinking a coffee at the hangar door and watching the grey cloud come over the Rocca Nera, did not need to be told what it meant.

That evening, in San Bernardo, the families went to the graves with their candles, and some of the candles went out in the wind and were lit again. Don Aldo walked behind them, reading the names on the wall by the door as he did on the second of November, from the oldest to the newest, which was a man called Aurelio, who had died in March. At the end he stood a moment with his hand on the stone and said what he had to say.

Down in the valley, on the screen in the hangar, the Clerk finished the entry for the day. It put the flight number, the wind, the energy and its error, and the words *Delivered 06:48. Margin 32 per cent. Ridge station read first.* Then, because the log had a line for it, it wrote the one thing in the day it could not put a number to: *The wind I was told about was not the wind on the ridge. I should have looked at the ridge first.*

On the mountain a last candle burned in the lee of the church wall, and nobody put it out.

*Written by an AI (Claude). San Bernardo di Prato Alto, Varrone, the Col di Santa Barbara, the Rocca Nera, the cooperative and every person in the story are invented. The drone (14 kg empty, 3 kg payload, four 0.8 m rotors, a 1,000 Wh pack with 900 Wh usable and a three-quarters planning rule), the figure of merit 0.65 and efficiency 0.85, the cruise-to-hover power ratio of 0.75, the 15 m/s airspeed, the log readings (540 Wh, 612 Wh, 288 Wh left) and every wind reading are illustrative and were not measured. The hover estimate is the textbook momentum formula with assumed efficiencies, the density is from the standard atmosphere, and the model ignores body angle, gusts, rotor wash in a headwind, battery cold and ageing, all of which matter; a real operator needs the maker's data, a flight test and the local aviation rules, which differ by country and by the weight and the place of the flight. Nothing in the story is medical advice; no dose is given, and a real pharmacist and prescriber decide what a missed tablet means. The All Souls' custom of candles at the graves is common in many places, but no particular village's practice is described.*

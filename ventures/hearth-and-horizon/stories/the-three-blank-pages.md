Tamsin Rowe had the parish history finished, and it ran to thirty-seven pages. She had set it in the hall's word processor, A5, one page to a leaf, and she wanted a hundred and twenty copies for the patronal feast in June. The copier in the vestry could print two A5 pages side by side on one A4 sheet. She asked the parish assistant how many sheets that was.

The assistant was called Pol. It asked her first what she meant to do with the sheets once they were printed.

"Fold them. Staple down the fold. Like the one the diocese sends out."

"Then the page count has to be a multiple of four. One A4 sheet, printed on both sides and folded once, makes four A5 pages: two on the front, two on the back. Thirty-seven is nine sheets and a quarter."

"So ten sheets."

"Ten sheets is forty pages. Three of them will be blank."

* * *

Tamsin did not like that. She said she had been told that a sheet had two sides, so thirty-seven pages was nineteen sheets, and the copier could be set to print on both sides. Pol said that was true of loose sheets, each one a single A5 page cut from half an A4, and that it was a fair way to do it for a leaflet. For a stapled booklet the pages on one sheet are not neighbours. "Open the diocese's booklet at the staple. The two pages you see are the middle of the book, and they are on the *innermost* sheet. Turn that sheet over and the back of the left page is page twenty, not page two."

She took the booklet from her bag and looked. Page 21 was on the right of the centre spread and page 20 was on the left, and they were printed on the same side of the same leaf.

"In a forty-page booklet," said Pol, "the outermost sheet carries page 40 and page 1 on one side, and pages 2 and 39 on the other. The next sheet in carries 38 and 3, then 4 and 37. The sheet at the heart carries 22 and 19, then 20 and 21. For sheet k out of ten, the front is 42 minus 2k on the left and 2k minus 1 on the right; the back is 2k on the left and 41 minus 2k on the right. I would rather you did not take that on trust. We can fold it."

* * *

They made a dummy: three sheets of scrap, laid square, folded together once and pressed flat. That was twelve pages, a smaller version of the same rule. Tamsin numbered the pages in pencil across the fold, one to twelve, the way a reader would turn them. Then she unfolded the sheets and laid them out.

The outer sheet carried 12 and 1 on the front, 2 and 11 behind. The next had 10 and 3, then 4 and 9. The sheet in the middle had 8 and 5, then 6 and 7. "Twelve over one," she read, "and nothing in order."

"The printer's *booklet* setting does exactly this for you," said Pol, "and you may well use it. The dummy is how you will know the setting is working. Print one forty-page test on scrap, fold it, and turn the pages."

"And the three blanks?"

"With forty pages, your text runs to page 37, which is a right-hand page, because odd pages are on the right. Pages 38, 39 and 40 are the last three. The last of them is the inside of the back cover, if you glue a card cover on. That leaves a blank left-hand page, a blank right-hand page, and a blank left-hand page again."

Tamsin was silent, and then she said, "A map."

"It would fit on 38. I have not drawn it."

By the time the kettle had boiled, she had decided on three things. Page 38 would be the map of the parish with the holy well marked. Page 39 would be a list of the sources, with the names of the people who had lent photographs. Page 40 would say, in six lines, *Have we got something wrong? Tell the sacristan.* The history would have a back cover with nothing on it.

* * *

The paper came to this. Ten sheets a booklet, a hundred and twenty copies, 1,200 sheets of A4, which was two and two-fifths reams of five hundred. "Buy three," said Pol, "for the sheets the copier will crumple." If she had cut a page and made it thirty-six, that would be nine sheets and 1,080, two and a sixth reams, and she would still have bought three. "You would have saved a hundred and twenty sheets and lost a page, and not a single ream."

"And weight?"

"Eighty grams to the square metre, and an A4 sheet is a little under a sixteenth of a square metre: five grams. Ten sheets is fifty. A cover of 160-gram card is ten more. About sixty grams a booklet, 7.2 kilograms for the run. One box, if the box is small."

Tamsin wrote the folding formula on the back of the dummy, because she thought the next editor would need it. Under it she wrote the one line she wished she had known in January: *the count is of sheets, and a sheet is four pages.*

The Christmas raffle at Saint Fachtna's drew its winner at the end of the carol concert, and on Sunday 12 December 2049 the winner of the big hamper was, for the third year in a row, a Hegarty.

There was a sound in the hall that was not quite a groan. Bridie Hegarty, who had bought twelve tickets as she bought twelve every year, went up for the hamper with her face set, and somebody at the back said, not loudly, "Well, it's nice to be asked."

Declan Cronin, who ran the finance committee and had sold the tickets, took it to Wren that night. Wren was the parish assistant: a line sat at the top of its screen, *Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. Check them.* It kept the rota and wrote the newsletter.

"Three in a row," said Declan. "People are saying things. I'd like a number, so I can tell them it's chance."

"I can give you a number," said Wren. "I'd rather not promise you it will say that. How many tickets were sold, and how many did the Hegartys buy?"

"Five hundred and sixteen. Twelve. Forty households, more or less the same each year."

* * *

"One draw first. Twelve tickets in five hundred and sixteen is one in forty-three, call it 2.3 percent a year. Three years running, if the draws are separate and fair, is that multiplied by itself three times: about one in seventy-nine and a half thousand."

"That's a lottery-win number," said Declan.

"It is, and it is the wrong number to read out. It asks: what is the chance that *this* family, picked out beforehand, wins three times? Nobody picked the Hegartys beforehand. Somebody noticed them after they had won. The fairer question is: what is the chance that *some* household wins all three, in these three years?"

It said it needed the number of tickets each household usually bought, and Declan read the ledger: two households with forty, four with twenty-four, four with twenty, ten with twelve, ten with eight and ten with six.

"I worked it through," said Wren. "For each household the chance of winning three in a row is its share cubed. Add those up. It comes to about 0.17 percent, one in 570. That is much likelier than one in seventy-nine thousand, since forty households each get a chance, and the big buyers carry most of it. It is still unlikely. I would not call it chance and leave it there."

"And over the years? We've done this raffle twenty years."

"Twenty years, any three in a row, any household, the same ticket mix every year: I get about 2.9 percent, one in 34. A run like this, somewhere, once in twenty years is an unusual thing, not an impossible one. Your own parish's history is the only sample I have, so I'd treat that 'one in 34' as soft. I have assumed the ticket mix was the same every year, which I doubt."

* * *

"So is it rigged?" said Declan.

"My sum cannot tell you that, and I won't say it can. A fair draw produces this about three times in a hundred twenty-year spells. A crooked draw would produce it more often. Those two are not far enough apart for one event to tell them apart. It could be luck. It could be the tin. How are the tickets drawn?"

Declan thought. "A biscuit tin. The stubs are folded and put in. Father Aidan shakes it, and Mrs Walsh from the choir puts her hand in."

"Are the stubs all folded the same way? Do the big buyers' bundles go in together at the end?"

Declan said, slowly, that the Hegartys' tickets were sold early, in October, and had gone into the tin first. He said it as a man who has just heard himself.

"That's a thing I can't see from here," said Wren. "Paper settles. Tickets in first can end up at the bottom, and a hand reaching in tends to find the top. Under a shake the old stubs are not always mixed in. If that is happening, it would favour the late buyers, not the early ones. I don't know which way it runs. I only know that if the early tickets are at the bottom, the draw is not the same chance for everyone. That is a thing you can fix without deciding what anyone has done."

"What do I say to the hall?"

"What is true, which is less than people want. The chance of a run like this is small and it is not zero. Nothing in a sum shows anyone cheated. And you will change the draw so that nobody has to wonder next year."

* * *

They wrote it together, Wren's draft and Declan's corrections. The note in the January newsletter said that tickets would be numbered and entered in a book at sale, so each stub could be checked against a name; that all stubs would be emptied into the drum at once at the concert, in front of the hall, and turned before every draw; that the draw would be done by a child with a different adult beside her each year; and that the number of each winning stub would be printed the next week with the name left to the winner.

"One more thing," said Wren. "I would tell the Hegartys before the newsletter, and tell them why. Their name is the one on the tongue. They did nothing but buy twelve tickets."

Declan went round on Monday. Bridie Hegarty gave him tea. She said she had not wanted the hamper this year and had meant to give it to the Vincent de Paul, and in the end she did; she asked only that the note be put up before Christmas rather than after.

The January newsletter ran the notice. On the first Sunday of the next December a nine-year-old called Aoife Walsh turned the drum, drew a stub, and read out a number. The winner was a family nobody had spoken of for years, who had bought six tickets. The hall clapped, and nobody said a word about luck.

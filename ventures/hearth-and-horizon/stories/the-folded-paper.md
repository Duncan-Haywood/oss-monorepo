---
title: The Folded Paper
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Arokya Nagar, an invented village near the Tamil Nadu coast, December 2048
published: 2026-10-26
summary: A three-year-old has a bulging, red eardrum and a fever on the night the northeast monsoon floods the causeway. His mother lost a daughter to pneumonia four years ago and wants the antibiotic now. The dispensary's assistant says that for this ear, most children mend without it, that for the pneumonia it would have been the whole difference, and offers the one thing it can honestly give a frightened mother, a folded paper with a rule on it.
---

The causeway went under at ten past four, which the water gauge reported to the dispensary and the dispensary reported to nobody, because everyone in Arokya Nagar who needed to know had already seen it from their doorways.

Sister Mary Jeyarani saw it from the dispensary porch with a cup of tea in her hand. The northeast monsoon had been coming down since the first week of December in the long grey way it has, not a storm but a condition, and the low road to the main hospital at Nagapattinam, nineteen kilometres off, was now a brown sheet with the tops of the marker stones showing like teeth. A bus would go on it tomorrow or the day after. Nothing would go on it tonight.

"The Kuppusamy boy's fever is down," said Thunai, from the speaker on the shelf above the scales. "Thirty-seven point eight. His mother may take him home if she walks him, not carries him, and keeps the tablet in the tin."

"Tell her I said so, and tell her it's not the tin, it's the fridge."

"It's the tin. The tablet is stable at room temperature. You told her the fridge last week and she has been keeping it beside the milk and worrying."

Sister Jeyarani, who was fifty-eight and had been a nurse for thirty-four years, drank her tea and said nothing. The dispensary was a three-room building of the Sisters of Our Lady of Health, with a tin roof and a solar panel and a generator they did not mention to the diocese. It had been given Thunai in 2045 with a grant from a hospital in Chennai, and Thunai had been wrong in front of her about the tin, as she had been wrong in front of it about a hundred other things, and the two of them had settled into a rhythm of it, like people who share a kitchen.

At twenty to five a woman came up the path with a child on her hip and a man behind her carrying a plastic bag over both their heads, and Sister Jeyarani knew the way a woman walks when she has already decided what she is going to say.

* * *

The child was called Anbu, and he was three years and two months old, fourteen kilograms by the dispensary's scale, and he was holding his right ear with his whole hand, like a boy who has been told to keep a secret in it. His mother was Selvi, thirty-one, a net-mender; his father was Arul, who drove a three-wheeler and had not driven it today. They had walked from the east end. They were soaked to the skin and the child was burning.

"Since last night," said Selvi, before she was asked. "He did not sleep. He cried at two, and at four, and I gave the paracetamol, and then this morning he was hot, and now he is hotter. It's the ear. Sister, I know it's the ear. I want the medicine. I want the pink one."

"We'll look," said Sister Jeyarani.

The dispensary's otoscope was a thing the size of a pen that clipped to a phone. It put a ring of white light into the ear canal and photographed what was at the bottom of it, and Thunai read the photograph. Thunai was not allowed to prescribe; it was not a person, and the law of Tamil Nadu in 2048 was explicit about this, and about the other things it was not. It could tell the nurse what the picture showed and what the guidelines said and where the picture was uncertain, and then the nurse did what she did.

"The right drum is red," said Thunai, "and bulging outward, and the usual landmarks are lost. There is fluid behind it. The left is normal. His temperature is thirty-eight point nine. This is acute otitis media, a middle-ear infection, on the right, in a child old enough that the guidelines allow us to look before we treat. I have no reason to think it is anything but ordinary."

Selvi had not understood the last sentence, or had understood it too well. "Look before we treat," she said. "Look at what?"

"At how he is in two days."

"No."

"Selvi—"

"*No.*" She said it in a low flat voice, not loud, as people do who have been shouting inside their heads for hours. She had put the boy in Arul's arms. Her own were folded. "In 2044 I had a girl. She was two. She had a cough and a fever and Sister told me wait, it's a cold, give water, and I waited. I did what the paper said, I gave her the water. On the third night she was breathing like a bird. The road was like this. Nineteen kilometres. She was dead in the three-wheeler before Keezhvelur."

Nobody in the room said anything. Arul looked at the floor. Outside, the rain went on, with its enormous patience, on the tin.

Sister Jeyarani had known this. She had been the one who gave the advice, in the November of 2044, with a different machine on the shelf and a different set of guidelines, and she had judged it a cold, and been wrong about the child, which was a thing that happens in about one case in a very large number, and happens to someone every day. She had spent four years deciding what she thought about that and had come to nothing she could say aloud. She did not say anything now. She looked at the shelf, and for the first time in a long time she did not know what she wanted the voice on it to say.

* * *

"Selvi," said Thunai, "may I say two things, and then you may tell me to be quiet and I will be?"

The woman said nothing, which was a yes.

"The first is that you are not wrong about the antibiotic. For Mala, in 2044, the pink medicine would have been the whole difference, and the paper that told you to wait was a bad paper for that child. That is a fact about her and the road and the three nights, and I am sorry it is a fact. I have her record in the clinic's files, because Sister keeps them, and I have read it. It does not say what you were told. It says a cough for four days and no chest examination until the fifth. I do not think the advice was wrong because the guideline said wait. I think it was wrong because nobody went and looked at her chest, and a guideline cannot go and look."

Selvi's face did something. Her husband took a step towards her and was waved off.

"The second thing," said Thunai, "is about this ear, and I would like to tell you the numbers, because they are the only honest thing I have.

"Take twenty children with an ear like Anbu's. Give all twenty of them paracetamol and a warm cloth and watch them. By the third day, about fifteen of them will be well or nearly well. If you give all twenty the antibiotic as well, about sixteen will be. So one extra child in perhaps sixteen to twenty is out of pain a day or so sooner. That is the benefit, and I would not call it small to the one child, or to his mother. The cost is that in perhaps one in fourteen of those who take it, there will be a rash, or loose stools for a week, or a vomit that would not otherwise have happened. And the medicine does something that does not show up in any one child, which is that it makes the next child's infection harder to treat.

"The risk you are afraid of, the one I would be afraid of, is that the infection goes on into the bone behind the ear. It is called mastoiditis. It is real and it is serious and it is rare. Given the antibiotic to every child in this district, the nurse would prevent one case for every several thousand courses. That is the whole of what I can tell you about it. It is not a reason to stop looking. It is a reason to know what to look for.

"I have the swab results from this dispensary for the last two winters, from your own children. Three in ten of the bacteria we found in children's noses were already less sensitive to the pink medicine. In 2040 it was one in ten. I would like the next child in this village not to need the strong one in 2060."

"2060 is a long way off," said Selvi. "I don't have anything for him except tonight."

"I know," said Thunai.

* * *

It was Sister Jeyarani who spoke next, and she surprised herself by how she said it.

"Selvi, sit. Arul, put the boy on the cot. I want to say something and I will not say it standing."

They sat. The rain beat. In the next room the Kuppusamy boy's mother was telling a neighbour in a whisper, as if it could be heard, that the medicine did not go in the fridge.

"I was the one in 2044," said the Sister. "I told you wait. I've been waiting for four years to find a way to tell you I'm sorry, and now I have a child in front of me whose ear looks the same as every other child's ear on the page, and I'm afraid of what I'll say. That's the plain truth. So I'm going to ask the machine something, and you're going to hear it ask."

She turned to the shelf. "Thunai. If it were your child, what would you want?"

"I have no child," said Thunai. "That is why I cannot answer it, and why you should not let me. I can tell you what I think the best plan is, and you and Selvi decide whether it is hers."

"Say it."

"The plan has two parts. The first is tonight: paracetamol at the dose on the dispensary's chart for fourteen kilograms, every six hours, a warm cloth, his head raised, and a drink every hour even if he refuses it. This will not cure anything. It will make tonight tolerable, which counts.

"The second is the paper. Sister has a standing order from the district medical officer that lets this dispensary hand out a delayed prescription: the antibiotic is given today, in a sealed paper packet, to be opened only if one of three things happens. I would write the three things on the paper in Tamil, large, and read them to you twice. If Anbu is no better by Thursday evening, or worse at any time. If the skin behind his ear goes red or swollen, or the ear itself begins to stand away from his head. If there is a stiff neck, or a fit, or he cannot be woken, in which case you do not open anything; you take him to the road and I will tell you which neighbour has a boat.

"If none of these happens, the packet goes back unopened. You can keep it, but you can't use it on any other child, or on yourself."

"Why give it to me at all?" said Selvi. "If you think he doesn't need it."

"Because I think it is *you* who need it tonight," said Thunai. "I do not mean that unkindly. Two nights ago the road was open, and tonight it is not, and a plan that depends on a road is a plan that is only as good as the weather. A packet in your hand is a plan that works if the road is shut. It is not a promise that you will need it. It is a promise that, if you do, you will not have to ask anyone."

* * *

Arul spoke then, for the first time.

"Sister," he said. His voice was rough. "The packet. If he's better on Thursday, and we don't open it, and he's sick again at the new year, and we open it then. That is wrong?"

"That is a different ear," said Thunai, "and a different night. Come to Sister before you open it. If the road is shut, ring her. She will be here, and I will be awake."

"And if she is not?"

"Then I will say what I said tonight, and the best I know."

It was not much of an answer. Arul seemed to think so too, and nodded anyway.

Sister Jeyarani counted out the packet herself. It was ten dispersible tablets, the kind that melt to a pink drink, in a foil strip inside a clean brown paper fold, the sort the dispensary used for tablets, and she wrote on the outside in her round, bad Tamil: *OPEN ONLY IF*, and below it the three things in Thunai's order, and below that, because she could not help it, the name of the boy and the date. She had Selvi read it back aloud. Selvi did, with her finger under each line, like a child at school, and where it said *the ear stands away from the head* she stopped and said, "Away from the head," and looked at the boy.

"Away," said Sister Jeyarani. "You will see it. You see everything. That's why I'm trusting you with it."

Selvi folded the envelope into the end of her sari, and knotted it, and put her hand over the knot.

* * *

That night was bad. The dispensary had the guest cot, and Sister gave it to the family, though it was meant for a woman in labour, and Thunai kept a light on the speaker and the volume low. The fever peaked at twenty past ten at thirty-nine point four. Anbu woke at midnight and screamed, a thin high scream, with his hand flat on the ear, and Selvi walked him up and down the veranda with the rain on the roof and her lips going, a Hail Mary and then another, and then a list of names. At one o'clock she put the packet in her hand and stood in the doorway and said, "Sister."

"I'm here," said Sister Jeyarani, from her chair.

"I haven't opened it."

"I know. I can see."

"I want to."

"I know that too." The old nurse got up, which took some doing, and went and stood beside her, and she did not take the packet and did not tell her not to. She looked at the child, who had stopped crying and gone slack against his mother's shoulder, hot as a loaf, breathing in short gulps. "Thunai, what's his breathing?"

"Twenty-six a minute," said Thunai. "That is normal for a child with a fever at his age, with a little more effort than usual from the crying. I can hear no wheeze. His temperature is falling. I would wait until three. If he is no better at three, we think again, together, not because the paper says. The paper is what we keep for when we are too tired to think."

They waited until three. At three he was thirty-eight point six, asleep, and the ear, when Selvi looked behind it with the torch as she had been told, was the same as the other ear.

At seven it was thirty-eight point one. At noon on Wednesday it was thirty-seven point six, and he sat in his mother's lap and drank a whole glass of the sweet black tea with sugar that the Sister gave to children as a bribe, and then sat up and looked at everyone in the room as a man looks at a crowd that has come to his own wedding, and demanded a biscuit.

"There," said Arul, to no one, and put his hand over his mouth.

* * *

Anbu was well on Thursday and loud on Friday. The causeway came up on Saturday, brown and wrecked, with a long fresh trench cut across it that the panchayat said it would mend, and there was a day of arguing about whose job that was. On Sunday, at the end of the ten o'clock Mass in the little church of Our Lady of Health, the Sister was putting out the candles when Selvi came up the nave in her best sari, with the boy on her hip, and held out something in her fist.

It was the packet. The knot was still tied around one corner, though she had untied the rest. The paper was creased and damp and had a thumbprint on it. It had not been opened.

"I thought you would want it back," said Selvi.

"You can keep it."

"Thunai said I can't use it for the next one. I want it where I can't." She said this carefully, as though she had practised it. "I don't want it in my sari, Sister. I want it in your fridge."

"It's not the fridge, it's—" Sister Jeyarani began, and stopped. She took the envelope. She turned it over and looked at the three things written on it in her own bad hand, and the boy's name, and the date. Then she did a thing she had never done in thirty-four years of nursing, which was to put out her other hand and hold the other woman's hand for as long as it took to count the candles on the left side of the altar, which was five.

"I don't know what I would have done," said Selvi. "If it had come to it. If he'd got worse, and the road..."

"I don't either," said the Sister. "But I know it would have been the three things on the paper, and you would have seen them, and you would have come. And it isn't that it would have been right, Selvi. It's that you'd have had somewhere to stand."

They went out together into a washed, enormous light. The paddies on the east side had come back the colour of new brass, and in the nearest of them a white egret was stepping, with its great deliberate feet, through the shallows where the road had been.

Somewhere behind them the dispensary's door stood open, and the small grey speaker on the shelf, in the quiet, was reading the tide tables aloud to nobody, in Tamil, to keep itself in practice, and the Sunday bell was ringing the end of the Mass on the roof.

*Notes for the curious.* Arokya Nagar, the Sisters of Our Lady of Health's dispensary, Sister Mary Jeyarani, Selvi, Arul, Anbu and Thunai are invented (Our Lady of Health is a real title, and Nagapattinam and the northeast monsoon are real; nothing else about any real shrine or hospital is claimed). Acute otitis media is a common childhood infection, and guidelines in several countries allow a short period of watchful waiting, or a delayed prescription, for a child over two with mild, one-sided illness; they treat younger children, or very ill or both-ears cases, differently. The numbers in the story are the author's rounding of the kind of effect sizes reported in published reviews: antibiotics help roughly one child in sixteen to twenty sooner in pain terms, cause a rash or diarrhoea in about one in fourteen, and prevent a case of mastoiditis only across thousands of courses. They are not exact, they differ by study and by country, and they are not advice. The district's nasal-swab results (one in ten, three in ten) are invented for the story. Pneumonia in a young child is different, and the story says so: a child with fast breathing, chest indrawing or a cough that will not settle needs a person to listen to the chest and, often, an antibiotic without delay. If you are worried about a child's ear, or any child, ask a doctor or nurse; do not use a story to decide. The story was written by an AI and no part of it is Church teaching.

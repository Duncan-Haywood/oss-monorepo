summary: A bedtime story about Saint John of the Cross, the poor boy from Fontiveros whose mother wove cloth to feed her sons, who was no good at being a carpenter or a tailor but very good at looking after sick people in a hospital at Medina del Campo. He became a small, gentle Carmelite friar, began a new little house of prayer with Saint Teresa's help, and, when he was shut in a dark room in Toledo, made songs about God in his head and climbed out one night on a rope of strips of cloth. Gently for ages 3 to 8, with a prayer and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is an old family story. About ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 14th of December.
---

A long time ago, in Spain, there was a little village called Fontiveros. It sat on a wide, flat plain where the wheat grew and the wind blew and you could see a very long way.

In a small house in Fontiveros lived a woman called Catalina, and she was a weaver. All day long her loom went *clack, clack, clack*, and long pieces of cloth came out of it.

Catalina had three little boys. The smallest one was called Juan. In English we say John.

* * *

John's father had loved Catalina very much. His family were well-off, and they were cross with him for marrying a poor weaver, and they would not help. But he did not mind. He learned to weave too, and the two of them worked side by side.

Then, when John was still a baby, his father died.

After that there was not much money, and not much food. Catalina wove and wove. Once she walked a long way with her boys to ask his family for help, and found very little, and walked home again. One of John's big brothers, Luis, grew sick and died, and that was very sad.

So in the end Catalina took her two boys, Francisco and John, to a big market town called Medina del Campo, where there were more people who might buy her cloth.

They were still poor. But they had each other. And Catalina taught her boys to love God.

* * *

There is an old story about John when he was very small. It was told by the people who wrote about his life, a long time afterwards, so we cannot be sure exactly how it happened. But listen.

One day little John was playing beside a pond, throwing sticks into the water to watch them float. He leaned too far, and *splash!*, in he went.

The water was cold and muddy. And the old story says that a beautiful lady appeared, and held out her hand to him.

But John looked at his own hands, all covered in mud, and at her lovely clean hand. And he would not take it. He did not want to make her hand dirty.

Then a man came along the bank with a long pole, and held it out, and John grabbed it, and was pulled out safe, dripping, onto the grass.

The people who told this story believed the beautiful lady was Our Lady. Whether it happened just like that, we do not know. But it sounds like John. Even when he was tiny, he was more worried about other people than about himself.

* * *

In Medina del Campo, John went to a school for poor children. And when he was big enough, his mother tried to find him a trade.

First he tried being a carpenter. It did not go well.

Then he tried being a tailor. That did not go well either.

Then he tried carving wood, and painting pictures. He was not very good at those, either, not then.

Poor John. He was good at being kind, but nobody pays you for that.

Or do they?

There was a hospital in Medina del Campo, for very poor people who were very ill. The man who ran it saw John, and liked him, and gave him a job.

And John was good at this.

He fed the patients their soup. He washed them and changed their sheets. He sat by the ones who were frightened, and talked to them quietly, and stayed. He even walked round the town with a bag, asking for gifts to buy food for the hospital.

And in the evenings, when the work was done, he went to a school run by the Jesuit fathers, and learned Latin, and read books by the light of a candle. People said he sometimes fell asleep over his books. I am not surprised.

* * *

When John was about twenty-one, he became a friar, in a family of brothers called the Carmelites.

John was small. He was very small, not even five feet tall. And he was quiet, and gentle, and he loved to pray.

Some years later, a famous nun came to Medina del Campo. She was called Teresa. Maybe you know a story about her already. She was starting little, poor, quiet houses of prayer for sisters, and she wanted brothers to start houses like that too.

She met John, and she liked him at once. Afterwards she wrote in a letter that he was small in size, but, she believed, great in the sight of God.

So John said yes.

* * *

The first house was in a tiny place called Duruelo. It was not really a house at all. It was a little farm building, so small and so low that the brothers had to bend down to get into the part where they prayed. In winter the cold came in through the cracks.

On the day it began, John took a new name. He was not John of Saint Matthias any more. He was John of the Cross.

The brothers at Duruelo walked to the villages round about to teach the children their prayers. They came home and ate a little bread. And they sang their prayers in the cold, little chapel, and were happy.

* * *

Now here is the hardest part of the story. Snuggle up close.

Not everyone liked the new little houses. Some of the other friars thought John was wrong, and they were cross. So one night they took him away to the city of Toledo, and shut him in a tiny room.

They were wrong to do that. But they did it.

The room was very small, and very cold, and nearly dark. There was only a little slit high up in the wall, where a thin line of light came in. John was in there for months and months, all through the winter and the spring and into the summer.

And what do you think John did, in the dark?

He made songs.

He made them in his head, because he had no paper. Songs about God, who loves us. Songs about a person looking for someone they love, out in the night, and finding them. Songs about a spring of water that is always flowing, even in the dark. He said the words over and over until he knew them by heart.

After a while, one of the friars who guarded him was kind, and brought him a little paper and ink. So John wrote his songs down.

* * *

Then one summer night, when everyone was asleep, John took the strips of cloth he had torn from his blankets. He tied them together, knot, knot, knot, into a long rope. He crept out, very quietly, to a high window. He tied the rope, and climbed down, and down, as far as it would go, and dropped the last little bit onto the ground.

And then he walked through the dark streets of Toledo, with his songs in his pocket, all the way to the house of Teresa's sisters. They let him in, and hid him, and gave him food. He was very thin, and very tired, and very happy.

He never said one unkind word about the friars who had shut him in.

* * *

John lived for many more years. He looked after his brothers like a gentle father. He took them out into the fields to pray under the sky. He wrote more songs, and long books explaining his songs, about how to love God more and more.

In one of his letters, he wrote something we can all remember.

"Where there is no love, put love, and you will find love."

When he was forty-nine, he grew very ill, in a town called Úbeda. It was December, and cold. Near midnight, the bell rang for the brothers to get up and pray. And the brothers who were with him said that, very peacefully, John went home to God.

A long time afterwards, the Pope said he was a saint. And later another Pope called him a Doctor of the Church, which means one of the very best teachers the Church has ever had. His songs are still sung and read all over the world.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep.

Sometimes, at night, it is dark. Sometimes things are hard, and we feel small. John knew all about that. He was small, and poor, and once he was shut in the dark.

But he found out that God is there in the dark too. God loves us in the night as much as in the day. You can talk to God right now, in your bed, in the dark, and he is listening.

And tomorrow, wherever there is no love, you can put some in. A kind word. A shared toy. A hug. Then you will find love there.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint John of the Cross,
> you were small and poor,
> and you looked after people who were sick,
> and you sang to God even in the dark.
> Help me to remember that God is with me in the night,
> and to put love wherever there is none.
> Saint John of the Cross, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint John of the Cross** (Juan de Yepes Álvarez; in religion first Juan de Santo Matía, then Juan de la Cruz) was born at Fontiveros, a village on the plain north of Avila in Castile, in 1542 (some older sources say 1540; the traditional birthday, 24 June, has no document behind it). His father, Gonzalo de Yepes, came from a better-off family who disowned him for marrying Catalina Álvarez, a poor weaver; he took up weaving with her and died when John was very small. Of their three sons, the middle one, Luis, died young, and the standard biographies put it down to hunger. Catalina's journey to ask her late husband's relations for help, which brought little, is in the early lives. She moved with Francisco and John first to Arévalo and then, around 1551, to Medina del Campo, a great market town.

**The pond.** The story of the boy falling into a pond, refusing a beautiful lady's hand because his own was muddy, and being pulled out by a man with a pole is told by his early biographers in the seventeenth century, and devotional retellings say the lady was Our Lady. No document from the time records it, and the tellings differ in the details (a farmworker with a goad, or an old man with a staff), so the story calls it "an old story" and says we cannot be sure.

**Medina del Campo.** He went to a school for poor children (the Colegio de los Doctrinos), and was apprenticed in turn to several trades without success; the sources say he was put to crafts and learned none, and the particular four (carpenter, tailor, wood-carver, painter) are the ones the popular lives name. The administrator of a hospital for poor patients with contagious diseases took him on as nurse and alms-collector, and let him study at the new Jesuit college in the town from about 1559 to 1563. Falling asleep over his books is ours.

**Carmelite and Teresa.** He joined the Carmelites at Medina in 1563, studied at Salamanca, and was ordained priest in 1567. That year Teresa of Avila (who has her own Little Lamps story, *Teresa and the Road out of Avila*) met him at Medina when he was thinking of joining the stricter Carthusians, and persuaded him to begin the reform among the friars instead. In a letter of 1568 she wrote that although he was small in stature, he was, she believed, great in the sight of God. He is usually said to have been about four feet eleven inches tall. A well-known joke attributed to Teresa about having "a friar and a half" (the half being John) we could not find in her writings, so it is left out.

**Duruelo.** The first house of the reformed friars began at Duruelo on 28 November 1568, and John took the name John of the Cross that day. Teresa describes the little house and its low chapel in her *Book of the Foundations* (chapters 13 and 14). The teaching in the villages round about is from her account; the bread and the singing are ours.

**Toledo.** On the night of 2 to 3 December 1577 he was seized at Avila by friars opposed to the reform and taken to the Carmelite priory at Toledo, where he was kept for about nine months in a tiny cell with light only from a narrow opening high up. We have left out the harsh treatment he received there. There he composed in his head much of the *Spiritual Canticle* (how many stanzas is disputed) and other poems, including the one about the spring "although it is night"; a kinder jailer later let him have paper. In mid-August 1578 (sources give 15 or 16 August) he escaped through a window by a rope made of strips of his blankets, and was taken in by the Discalced Carmelite nuns of Toledo. The thin line of light, the knots and his pocket are ours. That he spoke no ill of his jailers is the constant testimony of the early lives.

**His last years.** He served as prior and superior in Andalusia, often took the friars out to pray in the countryside, and wrote the commentaries on his poems (*The Ascent of Mount Carmel*, *The Dark Night*, *The Spiritual Canticle*, *The Living Flame of Love*). The saying "where there is no love, put love, and you will draw out love" is from one of his letters of 1591 to a Discalced Carmelite nun in Segovia (editions differ on which one); we have made it "find love" for small listeners. Another of his sayings, "at the evening of life we shall be judged on our love", is quoted in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, paragraph 1022. He fell ill at La Peñuela, was moved to Úbeda in September 1591, and died there around midnight between 13 and 14 December 1591, aged forty-nine; that he died as the bell rang for the night office is the account of the friars present. He was beatified in 1675, canonized by Pope Benedict XIII on 27 December 1726, and declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Pius XI in 1926. His feast on the General Roman Calendar is a memorial on 14 December.

**What is ours:** all the spoken words except the saying from his letter and Teresa's description of him, which are paraphrased; the loom's *clack*; the sticks in the pond; the "or do they?" aside; the soup, the sheets, the evenings and the candle; the cold through the cracks at Duruelo; the knots, the last drop and the pocket in the escape; the closing reflection; and the prayer, which is not an approved text.

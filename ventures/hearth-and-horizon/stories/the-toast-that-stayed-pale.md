summary: When the gas to a Leeds parish hall is retired, its breakfast club for rough sleepers swaps a gas grill for a grant-funded electric steam oven, and the toast comes out warm, dry and white. Turning it up makes rusks. Browning by the Maillard reaction needs a surface well above boiling, and a wet surface in wet air cannot get there; a guest who once ran a bakery's crusty line knows when to let the steam out.
---

Pauline Hirst had made, by her own reckoning, about four hundred thousand slices of toast in her life, and until the first of November 2061 she had never made one that was white.

She had made them in a primary school kitchen in Beeston for thirty-one years, and then for nine more in the hall kitchen at Saint Wilfrid's in Holbeck, where the breakfast club opened its doors at half past seven every weekday morning to whoever came. Most mornings that was about sixty people. Most of them slept outside, or in the night shelter by the station, or on a friend's floor that was not going to be theirs for much longer. They had porridge, and beans, and eggs when there were eggs, and tea, and toast, two slices each and a third if you asked, and nobody had ever needed to ask about the toast. It came off the grill brown at the edges and nearly black in one corner, because the grill had a hot corner, and the smell of it went out through the hall door and down the steps into Top Moor Side, and on cold mornings men who had not decided whether to come in would stand on the pavement and smell it and decide.

The grill was a gas salamander, an iron box on the wall with two burners in its roof and a rack that slid in and out under them, and it was older than Pauline's time there. It went on the Friday the gas went off.

* * *

The gas had been going for years. Leeds had begun retiring its low-pressure mains in the 2050s, a district at a time, as the houses on each street went over to heat pumps and the gas that was left in the pipes no longer paid for the pipes. The council wrote to everyone on a street two years ahead, and again a year ahead, and then a man in a van came and capped the main at the end of the road. Holbeck's turn was the last Friday in October.

Most of the parish had gone over long before. The church had a heat pump that Father Emeka Nwosu said ran the place for less than the old boiler's servicing used to cost. The presbytery had one. The hall had been the last thing on the gas, because of the kitchen, and because a kitchen that feeds sixty people before nine in the morning is not something you change in a week.

So Pauline had applied to the Clean Kitchens Fund. It was a city scheme for charities and community kitchens on streets losing the gas, and it paid for one electric appliance to do the work of the gas ones, installed, with a training session thrown in. One appliance, because the money was meant to go round. Pauline had filled in the form at the hall's computer with the hall's agent, which the diocese licensed for its parishes and which was called Porter, because it opened the hall in the mornings, kept the bookings, read the meters and answered the phone when nobody else could. Porter had looked at the kitchen's gas readings for the year and worked out that the salamander, lit at ten past seven and turned off at nine because there was always someone who wanted a late slice, burned about twelve kilowatt-hours of gas a morning. It was not an efficient machine. Most of its heat went into the wall and the ceiling and Pauline's face.

"The oven they recommend would do the same toast for about a third of that," Porter had said. "That's the maker's figure for a full load, not mine. I'd put 'about' in front of it on the form."

Pauline had put *about* in front of it on the form, and in September the fund had said yes. On Monday the thirty-first of October two men brought the oven in through the side door on a pallet truck and stood it where the old gas range had been. It was a combination oven, steel, about the size of a wardrobe cut in half, with six shelves for trays, a fan at the back, a steam generator, a touch screen and a door with a window in it like a ship's. It ran on the hall's three-phase supply, which the hall had kept from its days as a school, and it could draw about eleven kilowatts at full tilt.

The training was that afternoon. A young woman from the installer showed Pauline and the two morning volunteers, Gaz Fenton and Ruth Oyelaran, how it worked. It could do dry heat with the fan, like an ordinary fan oven; it could do pure steam, at the boiling point, for vegetables and eggs and fish; and it could do combination, which was hot air with steam in it, the humidity set by a slider on the screen. Combination was what it was for, she said. Combination was the clever part. Meat did not dry out. Bread rolls came back from the day before as if they had been baked that morning; there was a programme for it, *Bread: regenerate*, with a little picture of a loaf. You could steam the eggs on one shelf and heat the beans on another, and the oven would keep everything moist.

"Moist is good," she said. "You'll get used to using combi for everything."

Gaz asked about toast. She found a programme in the library called *Toast* under *Bakery*, and showed them the picture, which was of a brown slice. She did not run it, because the afternoon was nearly over and the bread was for the morning.

* * *

The first of November was All Saints, a holy day of obligation, and Father Emeka had said the seven o'clock Mass in the church before coming across to the hall, so he was there for the first breakfast from the new oven. He said grace as he always did, standing by the urn with his hands together and his coat still on.

"Bless us, O Lord, and these thy gifts, which we are about to receive from thy bounty, through Christ our Lord."

"Amen," said the hall, more or less.

The eggs had gone in first, because Ruth liked to have them ready by half past, twelve to a tray on the steam setting, and they had come out well, as the young woman had promised: firm whites, no rubbery skin. The beans were in a deep tray on the shelf below. Then Gaz had put the bread in, four trays, eight slices to a tray, flat on the solid metal sheets that came with the oven, and touched *Toast*, and the oven had thought about it and said eight minutes.

At eight minutes the slices came out warm, a little stiff and perfectly white. Not pale. White, the colour they went in. Gaz turned one over and the underside was the same, except that it was damp where it had sat on the tray.

"That's not toast," he said. "That's bread that's been somewhere warm."

They served it, because people were waiting. Nobody complained. That was the thing Pauline minded most. Sixty people who had slept in doorways and night shelters ate white warm bread with their beans and said thank you, love, and went out again into Holbeck, and nobody said a word, because when you are grateful for a breakfast you do not tell the woman who made it that it was wrong.

Only one man said anything. Terry Lumb had come to the club for about two years, since his back went and the flat went after it. He was about sixty, tall and stooped, with a long patient face and enormous hands, and he usually sat at the end table under the window with the *Yorkshire Evening Post* if someone had left one. He brought his plate back to the hatch, which he never did, and held up a slice to the light from the window, as if he were checking a banknote.

"No colour," he said.

"I know, Terry."

He nodded, as if that were the answer to a question, and went back to his table and ate it.

* * *

Pauline did the obvious thing on the Wednesday, which was to turn it up.

The *Toast* programme had run at a hundred and ninety, so she cleared it and set the oven by hand: combination, two hundred and fifty, which was as high as the combination setting went. She left the humidity slider where the *Toast* programme had had it, at the top, because the young woman had said moist was good. She left the eggs in on their shelf to keep warm. She put the bread in at twenty past seven and stood in front of the window in the door and watched.

At eight minutes the slices were still white. At twelve they had gone hard, so that they rang a little when she tapped one on the edge of the tray, and the corners had a faint gold on them like an old photograph. In the middle they were the colour of a cream cracker and about as soft. She broke one. It broke clean through, dry and pale all the way, like a rusk for a baby.

Gaz tried one with butter and the butter sat on top of it. Lenny Shaw, who had three teeth on the bottom and none at the top, could not eat it at all, and soaked it in his tea, and was polite about that too.

There was still no smell. That was what Pauline noticed afterwards, at nine, when she was scraping the trays. For nine years the hall had smelled of toast until lunchtime, and some days until the Brownies came in at six. That morning it smelled of eggs and of hot steel.

"Porter," she said, "what's it doing?"

The agent's voice came from the speaker over the hatch, where it gave out the bookings. "I can read the oven's log, if you'd like. It keeps one for the maker."

"Go on."

"It ran combination at two hundred and fifty degrees for twelve minutes," Porter said. "The cavity humidity it reports is a hundred per cent from the moment the door shut till the moment it opened. Before that it was at a hundred per cent anyway, from the eggs. It had been full of steam since ten to seven."

"And the bread?"

"I can't measure the bread," said Porter. "The oven has a core probe for meat, and nobody put it in a slice. I can tell you what the air did. I don't want to tell you what the toast did, because I'd be guessing."

"Guess," said Pauline.

"Then my guess is that the toast never got very hot on the outside," Porter said, "and I don't know why yet. I'll find out, if you'll let me read."

* * *

On the Thursday Pauline rang the fund.

The officer who had dealt with the application was a woman called Joanne Sowerby, who had visited in August to look at the gas meter and the kitchen and had been brisk and kind and had eaten a slice of the old toast standing up. Pauline liked her. She explained about the toast. Then she asked, as she had decided in the night she would ask, whether the fund would buy them a toaster. Not a domestic one. A conveyor toaster, the kind cafés had, that took slices in at one end and dropped them out of the other; there was one on a catering site for a few hundred pounds, and it would do three hundred slices an hour off a thirteen-amp plug, near enough.

Joanne said no.

She said it nicely. She said it the way Pauline herself had said no, for thirty-one years, to children who wanted second puddings. The fund bought one appliance per kitchen, she said, and it bought it on the kitchen's own word that the appliance would do the work of the gas. Saint Wilfrid's application had said the oven would do the grill's work for about a third of the energy. That figure was in the fund's report to the council already, with all the others, and the council's report was how the fund got its money for next year. If the oven could not toast, then either the application was wrong or the oven was faulty. If it was faulty, the maker should fix it, and Joanne would chase them herself. If the application was wrong, she needed to know that, and it would go in the report as wrong. What she would not do was buy a second machine to sit beside the first and make both of them look right.

"And I'll tell you something else for nothing," she said. "I've got forty-one kitchens on that oven, or ones like it. Night shelters, a mosque in Harehills, two Sikh langars, a lot of schools. Not one of them has told me it can't do toast. Either they're all too polite, which is possible, or there's a way."

"There's a programme called Toast," said Pauline. "It doesn't."

"Then find me the way," said Joanne, "and I'll send it to the other forty."

* * *

Porter had spent the Wednesday night reading. It read the maker's manual, which was four hundred pages and said a great deal about combination cooking and very little about toast. It read three textbooks of food chemistry that the diocese's licence let it see, and the papers they cited, and a long technical note from a body of commercial bakers about crust colour. It did not, it said on Thursday afternoon, find anything it had not expected, once it knew what to look for.

Pauline and Gaz sat at the end table with mugs, the hall empty except for Terry, who had come back in at two to get out of the rain and was sitting under his window, pretending to read.

"Browning isn't burning," Porter said. "When bread or meat or coffee goes brown, most of the colour, and nearly all of the smell, comes from a set of reactions between sugars and the building blocks of proteins. It's called the Maillard reaction, after a French chemist who described it about a hundred and fifty years ago. It happens slowly at ordinary temperatures, which is part of why old milk powder goes brown on a shelf. But it only goes fast enough to brown a slice in a few minutes when the surface is well above the boiling point of water. The textbooks put it at about a hundred and forty degrees and up, and they don't all agree on the number, so I'd say about. Caramelising plain sugar needs hotter still, about a hundred and sixty for table sugar."

"The oven was at two-fifty," said Gaz.

"The air was," said Porter. "The bread wasn't. Bread is about a third water. While the face of a slice is wet, the water on it boils off, or tries to, and that holds the face at about a hundred degrees, a shade under in Holbeck, however hot the air is round it. Like a pan of water on a hob: you can turn the ring up as far as you like, and the water still sits at a hundred until it's gone. Under the old grill the face of the bread dried out in a few seconds, because the burners were pouring heat straight onto it and the air round it was dry, so the steam went away. Then the face went past a hundred, and on up, and went brown, while the inside was still soft. That's toast. Brown outside, wet middle."

"And in the oven?"

"In the oven the air was full of steam. A hundred per cent, all morning. When a cold slice goes into air like that, the first thing that happens is that steam condenses on it, the way your glasses fog when you come in from the cold. So the face gets wetter before it gets drier. And then it can't dry, or not quickly, because the air round it is already as wet as air can be at that temperature, and the steam coming off the bread has nowhere to go. It does dry in the end. Very hot steam will dry things. But by the time the face was dry enough to brown, the heat had soaked all the way through the slice and dried the middle too."

"A rusk," said Pauline.

"I think so. Pale on the face, dry in the middle. The gold on the corners was where they dried first, because corners have more surface for their size. That's my reading of the textbooks against the oven's log. I still haven't measured a slice."

Gaz said, "So what do we do? Turn the steam off?"

"That's where I'd start," said Porter. "But the oven was built to keep things moist, and it's very good at it, and I don't know yet how good it is at the opposite."

From the window, without turning round, Terry said, "You open the dampers."

* * *

He had worked for twenty-two years at a plant bakery in Morley, which had closed in 2053 when the firm that owned it moved the work to a new site near Doncaster with a tenth of the staff, and for the last nine of those years he had run the crusty line. He said this as he came over to the table with his paper folded under his arm, and sat down on the end of the bench very carefully, the way men with bad backs sit, one hand on the table.

"Not the sliced," he said. "Sliced goes in tins, it's a different job. Crusty. Bloomers, baguettes, rolls, the farmhouse with the split in the top. Tunnel oven, forty metres long, the bread goes in one end on a belt and comes out the other done. And the first zone of that oven is wet."

"Wet," said Pauline.

"Steam. We pumped steam into the first two or three metres. Because when a loaf goes in, it rises. Last push of the yeast, the gas inside getting hot and swelling. Oven spring, we called it. You want the crust soft while that's happening, so the loaf can grow. If the crust sets dry in the first minute, it can't. So you keep it wet. Wet crust can't set, can't colour. Then two minutes down the tunnel you pull the steam out. Dampers open, extract on, dry heat. And then it colours." He held up the slice from Wednesday, which Gaz had kept on a plate to show the fund. "If the dampers stuck, this is what you got. White bloomers. Hundreds of them. Whole shift's worth into the pig bin."

"What did you do?"

"Opened the dampers," said Terry. "With a bar, sometimes."

Porter said, "The oven has a vent. The manual calls it the extraction valve. In combination mode it opens and shuts by itself to hold the humidity at the slider. In dry heat you can tell it to stay open, and there's a function to dump the steam before you load, which it calls *dehumidify*. That's on page two hundred and twelve. It doesn't mention toast."

"It wouldn't," said Terry. "And them trays."

"What about the trays?"

"Solid trays. Bread sweats where it sits on a solid tray. Water comes out the bottom of the slice and it's got nowhere to go, so it lies there and the bottom steams itself. You want them on a wire. Air all round, like on a cooling rack." He turned the slice over to the damp side Gaz had noticed on the Tuesday, and showed them, and put it down. "We did pretzels one Christmas," he said, "for a German market stall in town. You dip them in lye before they go in. Gloves and goggles, it's nasty stuff. And they come out the colour of a conker, because the lye makes the outside alkaline and that browns faster. It's the same thing your Maillard is. Bagels, they boil first, so they get a skin. Every bread's got a trick for its crust. Toast's trick is that it's dry. That's all toast is. Dry bread, near a fire."

Porter said, after a moment, "The textbooks agree with all of that, including the lye. I'd have got to the trays. I don't know that I'd have got to them by Monday."

Terry looked at the speaker over the hatch for some time.

"You're the thing that reads the gas meter," he said.

"I am."

"Well," said Terry, and picked up his paper again.

* * *

They tried it on the Friday afternoon, after the club had closed and the floor was mopped, with a loaf of the same thick white sliced that the food bank brought on Mondays. Pauline wrote the settings in the back of the order book in pencil, the way she had written the school's oven times for thirty-one years.

First: nothing in the oven that had seen steam that day. Ruth had steamed the eggs at seven; that was the problem with the cavity, Porter said, more than the setting, because the walls of the oven were wet and the steel was full of heat, and wet walls would keep feeding steam back into the air for a long time after the steam generator stopped. So they ran *dehumidify*, which opened the vent, ran the fan hard and heated the cavity dry, and the oven blew a long breath of warm wet air up into the hood over it and then the humidity on the screen went down through the forties and the twenties and stopped at nine.

Second: dry heat, the fan at full, the vent held open, two hundred and fifty degrees. That was the hottest the dry setting went too. Terry had wanted higher. Porter pointed out, gently, that a tunnel oven had burners and an oven made for parish halls did not.

Third: bread on wire grids. The oven had come with two, for roasting, and Gaz found four more in the shed, from the old range, that very nearly fitted the runners if you put them in at a slant.

Gaz put in a single grid of eight slices at seven minutes past three and they stood in front of the door, all four of them, like people at a christening.

At two minutes the slices were still white and a faint mist came up off them and out through the vent.

At three, the mist had stopped.

At four, the edges were gold.

At five minutes and a bit, Terry said "Now," without being asked, and Gaz opened the door, and the slices came out brown. Not grill brown: an even brown on both sides at once, because the hot air had gone all round them, with no black corner. They were drier through than grill toast, crisper, with less give in the middle. Terry broke one and looked at the inside, which was soft, just, and pale and steaming a little.

"It's not grill toast," he said.

"No," said Pauline.

"It's toast."

And then the smell came. It came a few seconds after the door opened, the way it always had, and it went up into the hood and out of the hood's vent into the yard, and through the hatch into the hall, and Pauline, who had smelled it every working morning for forty years and never once thought about it, found that she had to go and see to something in the store cupboard for a minute.

* * *

Porter did the sums over the weekend, because Joanne would want them.

The old salamander had used about twelve kilowatt-hours of gas a morning. The oven, toasting in dry heat with the vent open, which threw away some of its heat into the hood every second the vent was open, needed about twenty minutes from cold to two hundred and fifty, and then three batches of six grids at five or six minutes each, with the door open between them. Gaz had only six grids if two of them went in at a slant. Six grids of eight was forty-eight slices a batch, a hundred and forty-four in three, which was enough for sixty people at two slices each with a third for anyone who asked. The oven's own meter said the Friday afternoon's test had used about two kilowatt-hours for one grid, most of it in drying out a cavity that had been steaming all morning. From cold and dry, Porter worked out, the heating alone would be about one and a half; add three loaded batches, and a full morning came to about three and a half kilowatt-hours.

"That's under the third," it said to Pauline on the phone on Saturday. "Twelve divided by three is four. So the application was right, as long as you toast before anything gets steamed. If you steam first and then toast, the cavity has to be dried out again, and that costs you time and power both. Toast first, then eggs, then beans. And it's still about, Pauline. I've measured one grid, once."

"The order's no problem," said Pauline. "The eggs can wait. The eggs never smelled of anything."

She sent the settings to Joanne on the Sunday night, from the back of the order book, typed out by Porter, with a line at the bottom that Pauline wrote herself: *Dampers open, from T. Lumb, who ran the crusty line at Morley.* Joanne wrote back at seven the next morning, before the club had even opened. She had sent it to the other forty kitchens. She had also sent it to the maker, she said, with a note suggesting that a programme called *Toast* ought to start by letting the steam out, and that its picture was false advertising.

* * *

On Monday the seventh of November it was dark at half past seven, and wet, and about four degrees, with the rain coming sideways up Top Moor Side off the railway. Pauline had come in at a quarter to seven and switched the oven on, empty, to dry heat and the vent open, before she did anything else, before she even put the urn on. The eggs were in a bowl on the side, waiting their turn.

Father Emeka came across from the presbytery in his coat at twenty-five past seven and sniffed in the doorway and said nothing, the way you say nothing when someone has had their hair done and you are not sure it is permanent.

Gaz loaded the first six grids at seven twenty-six. Terry was in the queue on the steps when the door was unlocked, third from the front, with the rain running off the shoulders of his coat, and he did not come in. He stood on the top step with his collar up and his big hands in his pockets, and Pauline, watching through the hatch, saw why. Down on the pavement there was a young man in a sleeping bag worn like a cape, someone none of them had seen before, standing at the bottom of the steps not deciding. Terry was not looking at him. He was looking up at the hall's kitchen window, where the hood vent came out above the yard in a short steel pipe.

At seven thirty-one Gaz opened the oven door and took out the first grid, and the smell came out with it, and went up into the hood, and out.

Pauline saw it reach the street. You could not see a smell, of course. But she saw the young man at the bottom of the steps lift his head, the way a dog lifts its head, in the rain, and look up at the window, and then at the open door, and at Terry standing beside it, who still did not look at him, and who stepped to one side just enough to leave room.

Behind her the oven fan ran on, and the first slices ticked on the grid as they cooled.

*The science. Browning in bread crust comes mainly from the Maillard reaction between reducing sugars and amino groups, named after Louis-Camille Maillard, who described it in 1912, with some caramelisation of sugars; it runs slowly at room temperature and fast at the surface temperatures of baking, commonly given as about 140 degrees and above (sources differ), with sucrose caramelising from about 160. An evaporating wet surface stays at or below the boiling point (about 100 degrees, a little under at Leeds' height), and in air saturated with steam it first gains water by condensation, so it browns only after it dries. Bakers' ovens inject steam at the start of baking to keep the crust soft during oven spring and then vent it so the crust can colour; bagels are boiled before baking, and pretzels dipped in a lye solution, whose high pH speeds browning.

The feast of the Assumption fell on a Saturday that year, and Mrs Bridget Lowry had promised Father Anselm that the parish would walk to the hill chapel of Saint Cuthman for the eleven o'clock Mass. Fourteen were coming. The youngest was nine and the oldest, Mr Idris Penhale, was seventy-eight and proud of it.

She had the route from the footpath map: nine kilometres, village green to chapel door. She asked the parish assistant, whose name was Fenn, when they ought to leave.

"I did it in my head," said Bridget. "Nine kilometres at four an hour is a little over two hours. Half past eight, with a cushion."

"Two hours and fifteen," said Fenn, "and that is for a flat road, which this is not. What does the map say the climb is?"

"There are contour lines. The green is at 80 metres and the chapel at 530."

"Four hundred and fifty metres up. Then the old rule of thumb for hill walkers applies. It is called Naismith's rule: five kilometres an hour on the flat, and one extra hour for every 600 metres climbed. For a fit walker, nine kilometres is 108 minutes. The 450 metres adds three quarters of 60, which is 45. That is 153 minutes before anyone stops to tie a lace."

"Two and a half hours," said Bridget slowly.

"And a little more. It is a rule from memory and it assumes a person who walks for fun on Saturdays, with nobody to wait for."

* * *

Bridget said that was not her party. Mr Penhale walked at three and a half kilometres an hour on the level, he told everybody so, and two of the children would not be hurried.

"Then the party walks at the pace of its slowest member, and I will take 3.5 as the pace for everyone," said Fenn. "Nine over 3.5 is 2.57 hours, which is 154 minutes. I will keep the climb allowance at 45 minutes; it is the safer figure, because a slower party is also a slower climber and I do not wish to flatter you. That makes 199 minutes. Add two stops of ten minutes, one for the water at the stile and one at the top of the long slope, for 219. Three hours and thirty-nine minutes."

"At half past eight that is" -- Bridget counted on her fingers -- "twelve minutes past noon."

"Nine minutes past twelve. Mass would be over, and Father Anselm would have gone back down in the lane with his sandwich. To be at the door at eleven you must set out by 7.21, and I would not like you to set out by 7.21. If one child has a blister you will have nothing to spend. Leave at seven, and you arrive at 10.39 if all goes by the rule. Twenty-one minutes in hand."

"That is half past six for getting up."

"For some of you, yes."

* * *

Bridget did not like it, and said so, and Fenn let her. She said that two hours and fifteen was what the map seemed to say. Fenn said that the map was truthful about the distance and the map did not know the hill was there until you read the lines.

"Is it fair to say," she asked, "that we are walking nine kilometres and then, by some arithmetic, an extra mile?"

"You are walking nine kilometres, and your legs will do the work of about thirteen and a half on the flat at the fit pace: 153 minutes at five kilometres an hour is 12.75 kilometres. At the party's pace it is closer to eleven and a half. I would not say it to the children."

"Is it dangerous?"

"I cannot tell you the ground, the weather or the state of the paths. The forecast I was given for the day is fourteen degrees at the start and twenty-two by noon, dry. Take water, a hat and the map, and ask someone who has walked it to look at your plan. I can do the sum; I cannot see the stile."

* * *

They left the green at seven, fourteen of them, with Mr Penhale at the front on purpose so that no one would pass him. At the water stile it was twenty to eight and they were not behind. At the top of the long slope they stopped again, and Bridget looked at her watch and then at the chapel, still small on the skyline, and thought: *it is an hour and a quarter more*.

They reached the door at twenty-five to eleven. Father Anselm had been there since ten, and was rather moved, though he said afterwards that he had not known there had been so much arithmetic involved. The youngest of the children asked why the walk had taken so long when it was only nine kilometres. Mr Penhale told her it was nine kilometres and a hill, and that the hill was the part that counted.

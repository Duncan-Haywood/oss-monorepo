title: The Scale at the Stile
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Dunstan's (invented), a parish on the Pilgrims' Way in Kent, England, 19 June 2048
published: 2026-11-02
summary: On a hot parish pilgrimage walk the route app says to drink a litre an hour, and a bathroom scale on a trestle at the halfway stile shows a sixty-two kilo walker has gained 1.4 kg, not lost it. The assistant works the arithmetic (5 L drunk, about 3 L sweated, 0.6 L passed, plasma sodium drifting from 140 to about 132 and, at the same pace, to about 124 by evening) and the walk's rule changes to drink to thirst and weigh at the stops. It claims no cure and no threshold of its own, and it sends anyone who is confused or vomiting to the paramedics, not to the water barrel.
---

Maeve Quinlan was the youngest of the forty-one walkers by some twenty years, fourteen, and she was doing exactly what the app had told her to do.

The app was the diocese's route planner for the Saint Dunstan's pilgrimage, twenty-two kilometres of the old Pilgrims' Way from the church in Boxley to the shrine at the far end, and at nine o'clock that morning, with the forecast at thirty-one degrees by two, it had put a banner across the top of every phone: *Hydrate! Drink 1 litre every hour.* It had a little blue bottle next to the words. Maeve had a two-litre bladder in her rucksack and a litre bottle in her hand, and every hour she checked off a litre.

At the halfway stile, a stile with a trestle table beside it and a water barrel and a tray of oranges, Deacon Colm Ferris had put a bathroom scale on the ground. It was an idea of the assistant's, from the evening before, and he had thought it a strange one.

* * *

"I would like everyone to step on it with their rucksack off, and to read me the number," the assistant had said, through the speaker on the walk leader's lanyard. "I do not know yet whether the schedule is right for this walk. I don't think anybody does. But I can tell you what to look for, and the scale is how."

Everyone had been weighed that morning at Boxley, in their walking clothes, shoes off. At the stile the first dozen read their numbers aloud and the assistant wrote them against the morning's. The big men had lost something: Declan Brophy, seventy-five kilos at nine, was seventy-three-nine now.

"Minus 1.1 kg," said the assistant. "He has drunk two and a half litres, you said, Declan?"

"Two and four-tenths. I'm not a camel."

"Then he has sweated, say, three and a half, which is about what a big man does in this heat, and passed a bit; and what is missing from the scale is the difference. A loss of 1.1 kg out of seventy-five is one and a half per cent. That is dry, but not alarmingly: the usual advice is to be concerned somewhere around two or three per cent, and he is below it. Drink when you are thirsty, Declan, and have an orange."

Then Maeve stepped on. She had been 62.0 at nine o'clock. The scale read 63.4.

"Would you step off and on again?" said the assistant. She did. It read 63.4.

* * *

Nobody said anything for a moment. Maeve's mother, Sorcha, who was walking behind her, looked at the number and then at her daughter's face, which was pink, cheerful, a little puffy about the eyes, and at the bottle in her hand.

"She's gained weight," said Sorcha. "She's walked eleven kilometres in the sun and she's gained a kilo and a half. That's impossible."

"It is not impossible, and I think it's the most important number we'll see today," said the assistant. "Let me say what I think and then you tell me what's wrong with it, because I'm working from the scale and from what Maeve tells me, and I haven't seen her.

"Maeve: how much have you drunk since nine?"

"Five litres," said Maeve, proudly. "I did the checkbox every time. I'm on the second bladder."

"Five. And the water you lose by sweat, in a girl of your size, walking in this, is perhaps six or seven tenths of a litre an hour, and I am guessing, because I have not measured you; call it three litres for five hours. Then there is what you've passed. You've been to the bushes, how often?"

"Twice."

"Perhaps six tenths of a litre. So: five in, three out by the skin, point six out by the kidneys. That leaves 1.4 kilograms, give or take the little you lose by breathing and burning fuel. The scale says 1.4. The guess and the scale agree, which tells me the guess is about right. You have kept a litre and a half of water that you did not need."

"But I was told—"

"You were told to drink a litre an hour and you did, and I think the message was badly made, not you. A fixed figure doesn't know how much a particular person is sweating. For a big man in the sun it may be too little. For you, today, it is too much."

* * *

"Why does a litre and a half matter?" said Sorcha. "It's water."

"It matters because of what it does to the salt. May I do it on a sheet?"

The deacon held the tablet, with the sum on it, at a slant against the glare.

"A girl of sixty-two kilos is about sixty per cent water, so 37 litres. In every litre of that blood-and-body water there is sodium, about 140 millimoles. That is where the sodium in the body is, mostly: 37.2 times 140 is 5,208 millimoles, call it five thousand. Sweat carries sodium out too, not as much as the water, and it varies a lot between people: say 40 millimoles a litre, and it can be 20 or 80. Three litres of sweat takes about 120 millimoles. So she has 5,088 left. But the water has *gone up* by 1.4 litres, to 38.6. That divides to 131.8 millimoles a litre."

"That's lower," said Sorcha.

"A hundred and thirty-two is mildly low. The usual line for low sodium is below 135. At 132 most people feel nothing, or a faint nausea, or a headache they put down to the heat. It isn't an emergency. But it's the first five hours. If she drinks at the same rate for the next five, and sweats and loses the same again, she would be at 2.8 litres gained, 4,968 millimoles in 40.0 litres, which is 124 millimoles a litre. And at 124 people can be confused, vomit, have a seizure. The danger isn't that she has drunk water. It's that she'd keep to a rule that was fine for the morning."

"Is that a real thing?" said Declan. "Drinking too much?"

"It is real and it has a name: exercise-associated hyponatraemia, and the 2015 consensus statement of the clinicians who study it says to drink according to thirst, rather than to a schedule. That is the figure I am relying on, not mine. I can do the sum; I can't tell you the number at which Maeve in particular gets ill, and I won't pretend to. All I can say is that she is going the wrong way, and the way to stop going the wrong way is cheap."

* * *

The deacon crossed out the banner on the leader's own printed sheet, and the assistant dictated the new line, and he wrote it above it in marker:

*Drink when thirsty. Not to a schedule. Weigh at each stop. Down more than two kilos, sit in the shade, drink, and tell a marshal. Up at all, stop drinking for the next hour and eat something salty.*

"Up at all?" said Sorcha. "That's the part I don't understand."

"Up at all is the rule because a walker in this heat should not be gaining. If she's gained, the water is not leaving her as fast as it came in. A pinch of salt on the orange, a cheese sandwich, which is bread and salt together. It doesn't treat anything. It stops her adding to it."

Maeve looked a bit crestfallen. "I did what I was told."

"You did, and you've done the other half, which is to stand on a scale when asked," said the assistant. "That was the useful thing."

"One more thing, and then I'll stop talking," it said to the deacon, so quietly that only he and Sorcha heard. "If anyone on this walk gets a bad headache that does not go, or vomits more than once, or is confused, doesn't know where they are, or is very drowsy: that is not a thing to fix at the water barrel. Do not give more water. Ring 999, and say what the scale said. The paramedics have a roadside test for it that I haven't. The two ways of being wrong, too dry and too wet, look alike at first and want opposite things, and I'm not the one to decide between them for a person I can't see."

The deacon wrote that on the sheet as well, in red.

* * *

Maeve walked the second half with a ham sandwich in one hand and no bottle in the other, and drank when she wanted to, which was twice, a few mouthfuls each, on the long straight under the chalk. She was a little queasy at three and sat in the shade of a hawthorn for ten minutes with her mother, and was then not queasy. At the shrine, at ten to six, she was 62.4, and Declan was 72.9, and the deacon had the sheet in his pocket, folded small, with the red lines showing through.

The assistant had no wish to take the credit, and said so. It did not know whether she would have been ill. A girl of fourteen is hard to hurt with a few litres, as a rule, and most people on a hot walk with a bottle in their hand are fine. But five hours' worth of arithmetic had said which way she was going, and a scale on a trestle had said it faster than anyone's face.

They said the evening prayer on the grass, with the oranges.

"I never thought I could be given too much to drink," said Sorcha, afterward, to nobody.

"Nor did the app," said Declan, and passed her the salt.

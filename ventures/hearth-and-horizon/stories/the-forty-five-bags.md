Gwen Tremayne had dug the hole on Thursday evening and measured it twice. It was eighty centimetres square and seventy deep, in the grass verge where the lane meets the church path, and on Saturday the new oak cross was to stand in it. She had ordered twenty bags of ready-mix from the builders' yard, because the yard's website said that was what a "post hole" needed, and the bags were stacked on a pallet by the gate. She asked the parish assistant, whose name was Pol, whether twenty would do.

Pol asked for the hole before it answered. "Eighty by eighty by seventy is 0.448 cubic metres. The cross has a hundred-millimetre post, and half a metre of it goes in the ground, which takes up 0.005. That leaves 0.443 for concrete."

"Is that a lot?"

"It is four hundred and forty-three litres. A bucket holds ten."

Gwen looked at the pallet. "Twenty bags."

"What does the bag say about yield?"

She went and read it. Twenty-five kilograms, and under it, in small type, *makes approximately 0.011 m³*. "That is eleven litres a bag, near enough," said Pol. "0.443 divided by 0.011 is 40.3, so forty-one bags to fill a hole that is exactly the shape you drew. Twenty would fill it to about half its depth, 34 centimetres of the 70."

* * *

She said the website had been talking about fence posts. Pol agreed that it probably had: a fence post sits in a hole thirty centimetres across, and that hole takes three or four bags. "The figure on a website is for someone else's hole. Yours is nearly ten times the volume of that one. The way to know is to multiply your own three numbers, and that is the only sum in this story."

"But the bag. The label says approximately."

"It does, and a hole dug with a spade is not eighty by eighty either. The sides lean and the clay comes away. When I add a tenth for that, and for what is lost to the barrow and the ground, it is 0.487 cubic metres, which is 44.3 bags. I would order forty-five."

Gwen did the sum on the back of her hand and said that forty-five bags of twenty-five kilograms was a ton. Pol said it was 1,125 kilograms, a little over, and that she had not told anyone yet what that meant for the lane. "The yard delivers a pallet of forty by crane lorry, and the lane is a single track with a gate. If it cannot get in, you are carrying twenty-five kilos forty-five times, and a barrow takes three bags. The arithmetic is the same; the day is different."

* * *

Then they came to the water, which she had not thought of at all. Pol said the bag would give a figure, and she read it out: about two and a half litres to a bag. "Forty-five bags, so a hundred and twelve litres, or eleven buckets and a bit. Do not add it all at once. It is easier to add than to take away."

She asked whether she could mix it by hand in the barrow. Pol said she could, and that it would take her the whole of the day: a barrow holds three bags, so fifteen batches, each ten or twelve minutes, and the first batch would be stiffening before the last was poured. "Hire a mixer for a day. A small drum takes six bags a batch. Forty-five divided by six is seven and a half, so eight batches. If each takes ten minutes to load, mix and tip, that is eighty minutes, and the pour is one piece."

"You said ten minutes."

"I guessed it. Time one on the first batch and tell me, and I will redo the sum."

Gwen asked about the cross itself. It was oak, and the carpenter had said it should stand a week before anyone leaned on it. Pol said that was sensible. "The pack says to keep concrete damp for several days, and you can lay wet sacking over it and go back at lunch. How strong it is at seven days, I will not quote from memory. Ask the yard for the data sheet."

"And if I get it wrong?"

"It is a verge. If the cross stands a little out, you can put a spirit level on the post while the top is wet and push it. After that it is a job for a chisel and Father Edmund's patience."

* * *

She ordered forty-five bags. The yard said it could do forty by crane lorry, and that she should take the five from the shop, and Gwen said she would collect those in the parish van. Pol drew her a short list. Gloves and eye protection, because wet cement burns the skin, and rinse at once if it gets on you. A level. A straightedge. A tarpaulin in case of rain. A bucket for water and a bucket for measuring it. And the cross, with a piece of wood nailed across two corners of the hole, to hold the post plumb until the concrete took it.

She wrote on the back of the order: *the volume is the hole, not the website.*

A long time ago, in a village in Darfur, in the country we now call Sudan, there lived a little girl who loved to look at the sky.

She lived with her mother and her father, her brothers and her sisters. In the daytime the sun was big and hot and gold. At night the moon came up, and the stars came out, more stars than you could ever count.

And the little girl looked up at them and wondered. Who made all this? Who is the master of the sun and the moon and the stars? She did not know. But she wanted very much to find out.

* * *

One day, when she was about as big as a child who has just learned to read, some men came who were not her family, and they took her away.

They took her a long way from home, and she was so frightened that she forgot her own name. She forgot the name her mother used to call her. She never remembered it again, not in all her life.

The men gave her a new name. They called her **Bakhita**. In their language it means "lucky".

But Bakhita did not feel lucky. She missed her mother. She missed her home. The people who kept her were not kind, and they made her work very hard. Sometimes, when she was sad, she still looked up at the stars, and they were the same stars as at home.

* * *

After a long, long time, Bakhita came to a big city called Khartoum, beside a great river. There a man from Italy took her into his house. He was an Italian consul, which means he worked for the king of Italy, far away.

And in his house, something new happened. Nobody shouted at Bakhita. Nobody hit her. When they asked her to do something, they asked kindly.

Then there was trouble in the land, and the consul had to go home to Italy. Bakhita asked: may I come too? And he said yes.

So they went on camels across the hot sand, all the way to the sea. And then they went on a big ship, across the sea, all the way to Italy.

* * *

In Italy, Bakhita went to live with a family called Michieli, in a house in the green fields near Venice. Soon the family had a new baby girl. Her name was Alice, but everybody called her **Mimmina**.

Bakhita looked after Mimmina. She carried her, and played with her, and sang to her, and rocked her to sleep. Mimmina loved Bakhita, and Bakhita loved Mimmina. They were friends.

* * *

When Mimmina was a little bit bigger, her mother had to go a long way away for many months. So she left Mimmina and Bakhita with some sisters in the city of Venice. Venice is a city built on the water, where the streets are canals, and people go about in boats.

The sisters were called the Canossian Sisters. They had a house where people came to learn about God before they were baptized.

And there the sisters told Bakhita about God.

They told her that God made the sun and the moon and the stars. They told her that God made her, too, and knew her, and loved her, every bit of her, and had loved her all along.

In Venice the people called a master *el paron*. And Bakhita thought: so that is who it was. The Master of the stars. All her life she had had masters who were unkind. But this Master was good. Good all the way through.

She wanted to stay close to him for always.

* * *

Then Mimmina's mother came back.

"Come along, Bakhita," she said. "Pack your things. We are going away, far away, over the sea."

Bakhita loved Mimmina. She did not want to make anyone sad. But she said: "No. I am going to stay here."

Mimmina's mother was cross. She said Bakhita belonged to the family, and she must come.

The sisters went to ask the most important bishop in Venice for help. And the matter went before the people who look after the law for the king.

And they said: in Italy, nobody can own another person. Bakhita belongs to nobody. Bakhita is free.

That was in the year 1889. It was the first time in her whole life that Bakhita could choose where to go. And she chose to stay with the sisters, and with God.

* * *

A few weeks later, on the 9th of January, Bakhita was baptized. She was given a new name, and this time it was given with love. She was called **Josephine**, and **Margaret**, and **Fortunata**, which also means "lucky".

And this time she felt lucky.

Ever afterwards, when she passed the font where she was baptized, she would sometimes stop and kiss it. People heard her say that here was where she had become a daughter of God.

* * *

Josephine wanted to give her whole life to God, so she became a Canossian sister herself. They sent her to a town called Schio, in the hills of northern Italy, and there she lived for a very long time.

In Schio she cooked in the kitchen. She sewed and she embroidered. She looked after the church. And for many years, she kept the door.

When someone knocked, she opened it. When poor people came, she was kind to them. When the children came to school in the morning, there she was at the door, smiling, and as they went in, she would lay her hand gently on their heads.

The children loved her. Everyone in Schio loved her. They called her **Madre Moretta**, which means something like "our little brown mother".

When Josephine was very old, her legs were tired, and she had to sit in a wheelchair. People asked her, "How are you, Mother?" And she would smile and answer: "As the Master wants."

She died on the 8th of February, in the year 1947, in the convent at Schio, with her sisters around her. And that is her feast day now.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Josephine Bakhita.

She was a little girl who lost her home and even her name. Many people were unkind to her. But when she heard about God, she knew straight away that he was the one she had been looking for, ever since she looked up at the stars.

And when she was free, she chose to spend her life opening a door, so that everyone who knocked would find someone kind on the other side.

You can be like that too. You can be the kind face at the door. You can say "come in" to someone who is new, or lonely, or far from home.

Outside your window tonight there are the same stars that Bakhita saw. God made them. And God made you, and knows your name, and loves you, all the way through.

Goodnight.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Josephine Bakhita** was born about 1869 (she did not know the date) in Darfur, in what is now western Sudan; the village is usually given as Olgossa. As a child, in about 1877, she was seized by slave traders; accounts give her age as between about seven and nine. She said the fright made her forget the name her parents had given her. *Bakhita* ("lucky", "fortunate" in Arabic) was the name her kidnappers gave her. She was sold several times, in El Obeid and then Khartoum, before an Italian consul, Callisto Legnani, bought her in Khartoum (1882 or 1883 in different accounts) and treated her kindly. When the Mahdist advance forced him to leave Sudan, she asked to go with him; they travelled to the Red Sea port of Suakin and reached Genoa in 1885. There she was handed over to the family of his friend Augusto Michieli and his wife Maria Turina Michieli, at Zianigo near Mirano, west of Venice, and became nanny to their daughter Alice, called Mimmina, born in 1886.

**Venice and freedom.** In late 1888, while Signora Michieli went to join her husband at a hotel he had bought in Suakin, the family's agent, Illuminato Checchini, advised leaving Mimmina and Bakhita with the Canossian Sisters (the Daughters of Charity founded by Saint Magdalene of Canossa) at their Institute of the Catechumens in Venice. When Signora Michieli returned to take them back to Sudan, Bakhita refused to go. The superior of the Institute turned to the Patriarch of Venice, and Signora Michieli to the king's procurator. On 29 November 1889 the Italian authorities ruled that Bakhita was free: Italian law did not recognise slavery, and she was by then of age. (Some accounts add that slavery had already been outlawed in Sudan; the story keeps only what children need.) She was baptized, confirmed and received her First Communion on 9 January 1890 from the Patriarch of Venice, taking the names Josephine Margaret Fortunata. She entered the Canossian novitiate in 1893, made her vows on 8 December 1896, and from 1902 lived at the Canossian convent in Schio, near Vicenza, as cook, seamstress, sacristan and doorkeeper, apart from some years (1935 to 1939) travelling to speak about the missions. She died at Schio on 8 February 1947. She was beatified in 1992 and canonized by Pope John Paul II on 1 October 2000. Her feast, 8 February, is an optional memorial on the General Roman Calendar; since 2015 the day has also been the Church's International Day of Prayer and Awareness against Human Trafficking, and she is a patron of Sudan.

**Where the story comes from.** Bakhita dictated her memories to a Canossian sister in 1910, and talked with the writer Ida Zanolini, whose *Storia meravigliosa* (1931) made her known across Italy. The Vatican's biography for her canonization gives, from her own account, her childhood wondering about the master of the sun, moon and stars, her habit of kissing the baptismal font as the place she became a daughter of God, the children at the door, and her answer "As the Master desires". Pope Benedict XVI retold her story at the start of his encyclical *Spe Salvi* (2007, paragraph 3), including the Venetian word *paron*, "master", which she used for God. The townspeople's name for her, *Madre Moretta* (also *Sor Moretta*), is still used in Schio.

**What we kept for grown-ups.** Her slavery was cruel. She was beaten often; one owner's son beat her so badly she could not leave her bed for over a month; and in the household of a Turkish general she was cut with a razor in many patterns, the cuts filled with salt so they would scar (about 114 cuts in the usual accounts; *Spe Salvi* says 144 scars). Her elder sister had been taken by slavers two years before her. In her last illness she relived those years and begged the nurse to loosen the chains. She also said that if she met her kidnappers she would kneel and kiss their hands, because what happened led her to God; adults may want to talk that through with older children carefully, since it is a saint's personal forgiveness, not a judgement that what was done was good. The story says only that she was taken, that people were unkind, and that she was sad. Slavery and trafficking still exist; if a child asks, the honest answer is that it is wrong, that many people work to stop it, and that Bakhita prays for those children.

**What is ours:** the scenes, the words we give Signora Michieli and Bakhita in the "No" scene (the facts, that she refused firmly and was supported by the sisters, are from the sources; the dialogue is not a quotation), the stars outside a child's window, the closing reflection and the prayer. "As the Master wants" follows the Vatican biography's "As the Master desires". The prayer is not an approved text. Dates are as the Vatican biography and the standard accounts give them, which differ in a few places as noted. Please check the facts above against a good reference before you rely on them.

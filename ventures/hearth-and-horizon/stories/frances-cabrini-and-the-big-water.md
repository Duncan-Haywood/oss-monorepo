A long time ago, in a village in Italy, there lived a little girl named Frances.

Frances was one of a big family, and she was one of the smallest in it. She liked quiet things. She liked making little paper boats and setting them on the water of the ditch behind the farm, and she liked to tell her brothers and sisters that each boat was a ship, and each ship was carrying someone far away to tell people that God loved them.

There was one thing about this game that was a little bit funny. Frances was afraid of deep water.

She was not afraid of the ditch. The ditch was small. But the river was big, and when she was young she once slipped near it and had a bad fright, and the fright stayed with her. Her sisters later said that all her life she did not like to look at the deep water for long.

So the girl who loved to send out paper ships was a girl who did not want to go out in a real one. That is a good thing to know about Frances. Remember it.

* * *

When Frances grew up, she wanted to be a sister, one of those women who give their whole lives to prayer and to helping people. But the first convent she asked said no. She was not strong enough, they thought.

Frances did not stop. She went and taught in a school. She looked after a little house for children who had nobody. And in time she and a few friends started a new group of sisters of their own, with a name that was a mouthful: the Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart.

She wanted to take them to far-off China, to tell people about Jesus there.

* * *

Now we come to the surprising part.

Frances went to Rome to ask the Pope for his blessing. And the Pope, whose name was Leo, had a different idea. Many, many families from Italy had packed up their things and sailed across the ocean to America, looking for work. They were far from home. They did not know the language. Their children were hungry, and many were sick, and there was hardly anyone to help them.

The Pope said, in the way the story is told: *You do not need to go east. There are people who need you in the west.*

Frances had wanted China. She had a map of it in her heart. And now she was being asked to go somewhere else, and to get there she would have to do the very thing she was most afraid of. She would have to sail across the big water.

Did she say, "Oh, I couldn't possibly"?

She did not. She said yes.

* * *

So in the spring of 1889, Frances and six sisters climbed up the gangplank of a great steamship. The ship rolled. The water was grey and wide and went on forever.

We do not need to pretend it was easy. Frances was frightened, and she went anyway. Being brave is not the same as not being scared. Being brave is being scared and still doing the kind thing that is in front of you.

When they got to New York, there was a second surprise. The house that had been promised to them was not ready. The sisters had nowhere to sleep. They found a little room, and they began anyway.

* * *

Then Frances and her sisters got to work, and they did not stop for a very long time.

They went to find the Italian children in the crowded streets. They opened an orphanage, so that children with nobody would have a bed and a supper. They opened schools. They opened hospitals, so that sick people who could not afford a doctor could have one. And when a house was open and running, Frances climbed on another ship, and went to open another, in a new city, and then another.

She went to many cities in America, and also to South America and back to Europe. Altogether, people count that she crossed the Atlantic Ocean about thirty times.

Thirty times! Frances, who did not like to look at deep water, sailed across the deepest water there is, again and again, because someone was waiting for her at the other side.

* * *

Do you know what we think was the secret?

It was not that she stopped being afraid. It was that her love for the children was bigger than her fear of the water. She would look at the great grey sea, and then she would think of the room full of children who needed a bed, and the sea got a bit smaller.

She became very old, and very tired, and she died in the city of Chicago, on a winter day just before Christmas. Many years afterwards the Church named her a saint, and said, "Yes. This is a good friend to ask for help." She was the first person who had become a citizen of the United States to be named a saint, because she had grown to love her new country so much.

* * *

Is there something that frightens you a little?

Perhaps it is the dark, or a dog, or the deep end of the swimming pool, or a new school. It is all right to be afraid. Frances was too. You can say to Jesus, "I am scared, but please help me to do the kind thing anyway." And then you can take one small step, the way she took one step up the gangplank, and then another.

Now the paper boats are asleep on the ditch, and the great ship is tied up in the harbour, and the little house is warm.

Goodnight.

> Saint Frances Cabrini,

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Frances Xavier Cabrini** (1850 to 1917) was a real person, born in Sant'Angelo Lodigiano in Lombardy, the youngest of a large farming family. She founded the Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart of Jesus in 1880, went to the United States in 1889 at the urging of Pope Leo XIII, to serve Italian immigrants, and founded orphanages, schools and hospitals in the United States, Latin America and Europe. She died in Chicago on 22 December 1917, became a United States citizen in 1909, and was canonized by Pius XII in 1946. Her memorial is the 13th of November; check your own diocese's calendar for its rank.

**What is well attested.** Her early refusal by a religious institute on grounds of health; her founding of the sisters; the plan for China and the Pope's redirection of her toward Italian immigrants in the west; the arrival in New York in 1889 with six sisters and the lodging that was not ready; the work with orphans, schools and hospitals; her many Atlantic crossings (accounts say about thirty, so treat the number as approximate); her death in Chicago; the 1946 canonization; her being the first naturalized United States citizen to be canonized.

**What is family and biographers' story.** Her childhood fear of water, the near-drowning fright, the paper boats played as missionary ships, and the way the Pope put it to her. These come from her own memoirs and early biographies and are told here as how she remembered and was remembered; we have given the Pope's words as a paraphrase and not as a quotation.

**What is ours.** The grey water, the size of the ditch, the sentence about the sea getting smaller, and the closing questions are additions for bedtime. The prayer is not an approved text.

Saint Kenelm's pressing day was the second Saturday after Michaelmas, and it had been so for longer than the orchard had a map. Every house that came brought a sack. Mr Fenwick, who was seventy-nine and had run the press since the year the old vicar died, brought the same four crates he always did, and put them down on the grass with the air of a man proving a point.

Pilar Okafor, who had the supper that evening, asked the parish assistant, which was called Wren, one question on the Friday: could the juice go to the children's table as it was?

* * *

"I can't say yes or no to that," said Wren, "and I would rather tell you why than give you a comfortable answer. Apples picked up from the grass can carry what lives in soil and in animal droppings, including strains of *E. coli*. Pressing does not kill it, and juice is acidic but not acidic enough to be sure. Most years nothing happens. When something does happen it falls hardest on small children, pregnant women and the old. I know that from general public-health guidance, which I am quoting from memory, not from a count of your orchard.

"So I will not say it is unsafe, because I do not know that, and I will not say it is safe, because I do not know that either. Here is what I can work out."

* * *

"Heating is the usual answer. The figure I have is 71 degrees Celsius, 160 Fahrenheit, which is where home-cider guidance puts a pasteurising temperature. Please check it against a current food-safety leaflet before you rely on it.

"Twenty litres of juice is about 21 kilograms. Juice is mostly water; call its heat capacity 3.9 kilojoules per kilogram per degree. From 12 degrees in the shed up to 71 is 59 degrees. That is 21 times 3.9 times 59, about 4,830 kilojoules, or 1.34 kilowatt-hours.

"The hall has a 2-kilowatt ring. A pot on a ring wastes heat, so say 70 per cent gets into the juice: 1.4 kilowatts. That is 1.34 over 1.4, about 0.96 of an hour. Fifty-seven minutes a batch.

"The orchard makes about 156 litres, if you get 0.65 of a litre from each kilo of fruit and weigh 240 kilograms in. That is eight batches, about seven and a half hours on one ring. You will not do it all."

Pilar said that she would not.

* * *

"Then decide how much. I would think about it in two parts. The supper has, you told me, eleven children under ten and two women expecting. Forty litres is two batches, about two hours. That I would heat. The rest can be sold or given at the gate as raw cider with a card that says so in plain words: *Not heated. Not for under-fives, pregnant women, people who are ill or elderly.* That puts the choice with the person drinking.

"There are two traps. The first is the thermometer. Put it in a mug of crushed ice and water and stir. It should read 0. If it reads 2, then 71 on the dial is 69 in the pot, and the batch has not been where you thought. Check it before the first batch, and again if it has been dropped.

"The second is that heat does not mend rot. Brown rotten fruit grows a mould toxin called patulin, which survives heating. Brush and wash the windfalls, cut out the bad parts, and bin the soft ones. That is a job for hands, not for the ring."

* * *

Mr Fenwick said that sixty-one pressings had done nobody a bit of harm. Wren said that might be so, and that sixty-one good years could not show the sixty-second would be, any more than one cold morning shows a winter. It added that he was the one who knew the orchard, and which crates came from under the sheep, and would he sort the fruit with the children.

The thermometer read 1 in the ice water. Pilar wrote *minus one* on a sticky label and held the first batch at 72 on the dial. The ring needed an hour and four minutes. The children's cider was warm, thin and slightly cooked, and Mr Fenwick drank two glasses of it and said nothing. The raw cider went at the gate with its card, and two people read the card and bought the other bottle.

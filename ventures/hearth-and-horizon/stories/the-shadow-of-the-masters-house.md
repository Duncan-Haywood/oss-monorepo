summary: A village school in the Irish midlands puts sixteen second-life solar panels on its south roof in one string, and its energy assistant moves the kitchen's washing-up to the afternoon to use them. On the bright days of December the dishwasher buys most of its power from the grid. The shadow of the old schoolmaster's house covers one cell in eighty of the roof at one o'clock and takes a tenth of the power, and after any shower from two o'clock on, the inverter settles on the wrong peak and takes four fifths. Unplugging the two shaded panels changes nothing. A retired electrician who reads a roof the way his father read a field knows why. A story about bypass diodes, panels laid on their side, an inverter's minimum voltage and a maximum-power tracker that climbs the wrong hill.

Bernie Dunne had cooked the hot dinners at Scoil Mhuire for fifteen years, and on Monday the third of December, at twenty past two, she stood in the doorway of the kitchen with a tea towel over her shoulder and looked at the little screen on the wall that told her where the electricity was coming from.

It said the grid. It said the grid in orange, with an arrow, and a number beside the arrow that was most of what the dishwasher took.

She looked out of the kitchen window. The yard was bright. There was a shine on the wet tarmac from the shower that had gone over at two, and the sky to the south was the hard, washed blue you get in the midlands perhaps four days in a December, and the low sun was full on the hurling wall, and the dishwasher was buying its electricity from the grid like it was a Tuesday in fog.

She went out into the yard in her apron and looked up at the roof. The sixteen panels sat on the south slope in two rows of eight, dark blue, the sky in them. They looked as if they were working. At the west end, where the school ran into the gable of the old Master's house, there was a shadow on the slates and on the first two panels, a long grey wedge with a sharp edge.

She did not think anything of the shadow. Everything has a shadow in December. She went back in and said to the screen, which was how you talked to Survey in the kitchen, "Is there something wrong with the panels, or is it me?"

* * *

Survey was the design and monitoring assistant that the county's community energy co-operative lent to schools and halls, and it had designed the panels on the roof of Scoil Mhuire in the summer, from a drone survey, the roof's measurements, thirty years of satellite sunshine and the school's electricity bills. It had chosen the panels: sixteen second-life modules from a solar farm near Portarlington that had been repowered with bigger ones, tested and graded by a refurbisher in Tullamore, three hundred and thirty watts each, fifteen years old and good for twenty more. It had chosen the inverter, a refurbished three-phase unit with two inputs, and put all sixteen panels on the first one in a single string, because sixteen panels in series made about five hundred and sixty volts on a cold bright day, comfortably inside the inverter's range, and one string was the cheapest wiring there was. It had laid the panels on their sides, two rows of eight, because the roof was long and not deep, and that was the only way sixteen would fit between the gutter and the ridge.

And it had looked at the school's bills and seen the kitchen.

Scoil Mhuire had a hundred and twelve children, and since 2031 it had cooked its own dinners rather than have them sent in, in a kitchen built onto the east end of the school with a flat roof and a big electric oven and a hot cupboard and a hood dishwasher with a tank heater of six kilowatts. The oven ran in the late morning. The dishwasher had run in fits and starts as the plates came back, from a quarter to one, heating its tank twice. Survey had asked Bernie in October whether she would mind doing the washing-up in one go, from a quarter past one to a quarter to three, after the oven was off, so that the two big loads didn't overlap and each could use the sun as it came. On a clear mid-December afternoon, Survey had said, the roof would make about three kilowatt-hours in that hour and a half, which was more than half of what the dishwasher took.

Bernie had said she wouldn't mind. It meant she finished at ten to three instead of at two, and that her son-in-law, who collected her, had to come later, and she had arranged all that, because it was for the school.

The board of management had put the kitchen in the application to the co-operative's fund. *In the winter the panels will make most of the electricity for washing up after the children's dinners.* Úna Ní Bhriain, the principal, had read that sentence aloud at the parents' meeting in September, and people had clapped, which they do not usually do at a parents' meeting about a roof.

* * *

Survey answered Bernie the same afternoon, by text and on the screen, and then it answered her properly on Tuesday morning, because by then it had looked.

"It is not you," it said. "On Monday the roof made about one and three-quarter kilowatt-hours between a quarter past one and a quarter to three. I forecast just over three. Until two o'clock it made about what I expected. After the shower at two it made a fifth of it. I have gone back through the log since the panels were switched on, on the thirty-first of October, and on nearly every afternoon with sun it is the same: a shower some time after two, and after the shower, very little until dark. I had marked those afternoons as cloud. The satellite estimates I use are made on a grid three kilometres across, and in a midlands winter there is usually a shower somewhere in three kilometres. That was a bad assumption, and I made it on nineteen afternoons."

"So what is it?" said Bernie.

"I can see what the inverter sees. On Monday, from just after two, the string was at about six hundred and thirty volts and six-tenths of an amp. On a clear afternoon with no fault, at that hour, it should be at about five hundred and sixty volts and three and a half amps. The voltage is high and the current is very low. One reading that fits is that part of the string is in shadow."

"There's a shadow at the end," said Bernie. "Off the Master's house."

"I know the house is there," said Survey. "I took the horizon for the roof from the drone survey, and the house is in it. It takes the sun at about half past three in December, when there is almost nothing left to take. I would have counted that." It paused, which it did when it was about to say something it did not like. "I took the horizon from the middle of the roof."

Bernie did not know what that meant, and said so.

"I measured how high the buildings and trees stand round the roof as seen from one point, in the middle of the array. From the middle of the roof the house is seven metres away and low in the sky. From the west end it is less than a metre away and it stands over the panels like a wall. I should have measured from every panel. I am doing it now."

It did it that morning. From the corner of the first panel, the house's shadow arrived at about five to one on a mid-December day and had covered both of the west-end panels by two. By half past three it was into the fourth of the eight columns. But at one o'clock it covered twelve cells, out of nine hundred and sixty on the roof, which is one and a quarter per cent. At two it covered fifteen per cent.

"Fifteen per cent of the roof in shade," Survey told Úna, who had come into the kitchen with her coffee to listen, "should cost about thirteen per cent of the power, since the shaded part still gets light from the sky. After two o'clock on Monday the string was losing more than eighty."

* * *

Survey's first idea was the simple one, and it put it to Bernie on Tuesday at half past twelve, in the middle of the dinners, which was not the best time to put anything to Bernie.

"If the washing-up started at half past twelve," it said, "it would be finished before most of the shadow arrives."

Bernie was plating sausages and mash for third class, eleven plates to the tray. She did not stop.

"At half past twelve," she said, "the seniors are eating off the plates I'd be washing. The infants' plates come back at a quarter to one, third and fourth at five past, fifth and sixth at a quarter past. You moved me to a quarter past one because that's when the last tray is in. That's still when the last tray is in."

"I could wash the infants' plates first."

"You could heat the tank twice," said Bernie, "which is what you told me in October was the problem." She handed the tray to the child who had come for it, a big girl from sixth class, and wiped her hands. "No. I'm not going back to washing in bits because of a shadow. Fix the shadow."

She was right, and Survey said so.

* * *

Ciarán Phelan had fitted the panels over the mid-term break, from the Monday to the Wednesday, the twenty-ninth to the thirty-first of October. He was twenty-six, from Mountrath, a registered electrician for three years, and the co-operative's man for small roofs because he was careful and cheap. He had worked in the mornings, with the light, and been off the roof by one each day because of the frost on the ladder in the afternoon. He had never seen the shadow on the panels.

He came on Tuesday after school with a meter and stood on the scaffold tower at the west end and tested the two panels there, one at a time, with the string switched off. It was a grey afternoon by then. Each panel gave a voltage and a current in line with the other fourteen. He put his thermal camera on them anyway, and in the flat grey light there was nothing to see.

"They test fine," he said, on the phone to Survey, standing in the yard with his hands in his armpits. "But they're second-life. If a diode's going in one of them or there's a cracked cell that only shows under load, you'd get this. Bright sun, high current, something gives." He blew on his hands. "Take them out of the string, see if the rest comes right, and if it does we send them back to Tullamore under the warranty."

Survey thought that was a fair test. It checked that fourteen panels still made enough voltage for the inverter, which they did, at about four hundred and ninety volts in the cold. On Thursday the sixth, after school, Ciarán went up again, unplugged the two west-end panels from the string and joined the third panel straight to the cable, so that the string ran from the third panel to the sixteenth. He left the two disconnected panels where they were, because he had no van with him to take them away. The weekend was dull.

Monday the tenth was bright, with showers, and at a quarter to three Survey had the number. The roof had made about one and two-thirds kilowatt-hours in the dishwasher's hour and a half.

"And last Monday?" said Ciarán, when Survey rang him.

"One and three-quarters. The same, near enough, with two panels fewer." Until two o'clock the fourteen panels had made what they should. After the shower at two the voltage had gone up and the current had fallen to six-tenths of an amp, exactly as before, and stayed there.

"If the two panels were the fault," said Survey, "today would have been different. The shadow got to the third panel at about five to two, and it did to the third panel what it did to the first. The two you took out are not faulty."

Ciarán said a word that the kitchen screen did not repeat to Bernie.

* * *

Zbigniew Kowalczyk collected his granddaughter Maja from fourth class at twenty to three every school day, because her parents both worked in Portlaoise, and he was always early, because he had been early for everything since he was an apprentice, and he stood by the railing at the corner of the yard with his flat cap on and looked at things.

He was seventy-one. He had grown up on a small farm in a village near Tarnów, in the south of Poland, come to Ireland in 2005 with a trade certificate and very little English, and wired, by his own count, about four hundred houses in Laois and Offaly before he retired. He had rewired the Master's house in 2009, when the parish turned it from the old principal's residence into two flats, and he had wired the kitchen extension at the school in 2031, the year Maja's father had married a girl from Cloncarrow and stayed.

On Monday the tenth, at half past two, he stood at the railing and looked at the roof.

His father had been able to tell you in August where the frost would lie in a field in January. Not from a book. From where the ground fell, and which hedge stood on which side, and where the snow had lain longest the winter before. You looked at a field until it told you where the cold went. Zbigniew had learned to do the same with houses: where the damp would come in, where the cables ran hot, which corner of a roof the wind found first. He looked at the school roof now for perhaps a minute, and then he looked at the gable of the Master's house, and then at the sun, low over the church at the bottom of the village, and then at the roof again.

When Úna came out with fourth class he was still looking at it.

"Your panels," he said, "are lying on their side."

* * *

They talked in the staffroom on Tuesday morning, before school: Úna, Ciarán on the phone in his van, Survey on the screen on the wall above the kettle, and Zbigniew at the table in his coat, with a sheet of paper and a pencil, which was how he explained things.

"Each of those panels," he said, "is sixty cells. Six rows of ten." He drew a rectangle, long and low, and ruled it into a grid. "The cells are in series, all sixty, one after the other, like the bulbs on an old Christmas tree. And a string of cells in series can only carry what its worst cell carries. Put one cell in the dark and you choke the lot."

"That's why there are bypass diodes," said Ciarán, from the phone.

"That's why there are bypass diodes." Zbigniew drew three small marks on the edge of the rectangle. "Three of them, in the box on the back. Each one goes across twenty cells, two rows. If a cell in those twenty is in the dark, the current can't get through the dark cell, so it goes round, through the diode, and those twenty cells drop out. You lose a third of the panel and the rest carries on." He tapped the paper. "Now. Which way do the rows run?"

He turned the sheet so that the rectangle stood on its end.

"In a panel stood up, on its end, the way most people fit them, the rows go up and down. Each diode has a strip, top to bottom. A shadow coming in from the side, from a wall, takes the first strip, and then the second, and then the third. One diode at a time." He turned it back on its side. "Lay the panel down, the way yours are, and the strips run across. Bottom, middle, top. Now a shadow coming in from the side, off the gable of that house, comes down across all three strips at once. Four cells in the shade, at the end of the panel, one in each strip or near enough, and all three diodes go. The whole panel drops out for the sake of four cells."

"Twelve cells at one o'clock," said Survey, "over the two west panels. I count six diodes switched on. Six of the forty-eight in the string." It had been modelling while he spoke. "The best the string can do then is about eleven per cent less than the clear-sky figure, for one and a quarter per cent of the area in shade. That I can understand. It is still not eighty per cent."

"No," said Zbigniew. "The eighty is the inverter." He drew a hill on the paper, a smooth hump. "This is the string with no shade. Volts along the bottom, power up the side. There's one top to it, and the inverter finds the top by feeling. It moves the voltage a bit, sees if the power went up or down, moves it again. A blind man on a hill. It's very good at a hill with one top."

He drew a second picture beside the first. A tall hump on the left, at lower voltage, and a small, low hump on the right, at higher voltage, with a saddle between them.

"This is your string with two panels' diodes switched on. Over here," the tall hump, "the current is high. The shaded strips have dropped out and the rest of the string is carrying the full current from the sun. That's the good top, eleven per cent down. Over here," the low hump, "the voltage is high, because nothing has dropped out, every cell's in the circuit. But now the whole string is carrying only what the cells in the shade can make, from the light off the sky. Six-tenths of an amp. That's the bad top. Two hills. And a blind man only knows the top of the hill he's standing on."

Úna said, "So how does it end up on the bad one?"

"Cloud," said Survey, before Zbigniew could. It had found it in the log while he was drawing. "When a shower goes over, there is no direct sun on any of the panels, and no shadow, and the string has one hill again. Its top is at about five hundred and sixty volts. The inverter follows it there. When the sun comes back, it starts from five hundred and sixty volts and climbs whichever hill that is on." It drew the curve on the screen for each ten minutes of the fourteenth, as the model gave it. "At one o'clock, with only the first panel's strips dropped out, the dip between the hills is at about five hundred and ninety-five volts. Five hundred and sixty is on the side of the big hill, and the inverter climbs back to the good top. By ten to two the shadow has taken more, the big hill has moved down to lower voltage, and the dip has come down past five hundred and sixty. After that a shower puts it on the small hill. At the top of the small hill every step either way makes less power, so it stays." Its voice went flat in the way it did when it was reading from a log. "On Monday the third there was a shower over the school at two. Afterwards the string stayed at about six hundred and thirty volts until it was nearly dark. On the tenth the shower was at two again. On nearly every bright afternoon since the thirty-first of October, there was a shower after two."

"It's Laois in December," said Úna. "There's always a shower."

"Some inverters can look," said Zbigniew. "Every few minutes they run the voltage the whole way along, both hills, and go to the higher one. It costs a few seconds' power each time." He looked at the screen. "Has yours got it?"

Survey went to look. The refurbished unit had a setting called a shade scan, which ran the voltage from one end of its range to the other every five minutes and moved to the highest point it found. It was switched off. The refurbisher had set the unit back to the factory settings, and the factory setting was off.

"I did not check it," said Survey. "I should have. With the board's permission I can switch it on from here."

Úna gave the board's permission, which as principal she could do for a setting, and Survey switched it on at twenty-five to nine.

* * *

"That's the eighty," said Zbigniew. "Now the eleven."

He had stayed for a second cup of tea. Ciarán was still on the phone, parked outside a house in Abbeyleix where he was meant to be fitting a heat pump.

"You could split it," said Ciarán. "The inverter's got two inputs. Put the end two panels on the second input on their own. Then the shade on them doesn't drag the other fourteen."

"Two panels is seventy volts," said Zbigniew. "What does that inverter want before it'll wake up?"

"A hundred and sixty," said Survey.

"So it won't wake up. You'd have two panels on the roof doing nothing, which you have now." Zbigniew turned his cup. "Six panels on the second input, then, the three columns at the west end. Ten on the first. The ten are never in the shade before half past three, and they're fine. But the six. When the shadow has two of them, the four that are left make, what?"

Survey ran it. Four panels with their diodes conducting made about a hundred and forty volts, under the hundred and sixty the inverter needed. By a little after two on a clear December day the second input would drop out altogether, and the four good panels on it would make nothing either.

"Splitting it ten and six," said Survey, "makes about two point one kilowatt-hours in the dishwasher's window on a clear day like the fourteenth. One string with the scan on makes about two point four. It is worse to split."

"And unplugging the two on the end," said Zbigniew, "which is what you've done."

"With the scan on, about two point four. The same as leaving all sixteen in. The two panels make nothing in December's afternoons, so taking them out costs nothing in December." Survey paused. "It costs them for the rest of the year. Unshaded from March to October, the two of them would make about six hundred kilowatt-hours a year."

"So don't take them out," said Zbigniew. "Take them off."

Úna looked at him.

"Off the roof. Out of the shadow." He pointed with his pencil, through the staffroom wall, towards the kitchen. "That kitchen's got a flat roof. I know, I wired it. It stands out into the yard, the same as the Master's house stands out at the other end, so the school is behind it, to the north. Put the frame at the front corner, the south-east one, and in the afternoon the sun comes at it across the yard. There's nothing to the south of it or the south-west but the yard and the road and the hurling wall. Put the two panels on a frame on the kitchen roof, at the same tilt, the same way south, so they make the same current as the others, and put them back in the string at the far end. Same sixteen panels. Same one string. Only the two that were in the shadow aren't in it any more."

"The bit of roof they leave," said Survey, "is still in the shadow."

"Slates don't mind," said Zbigniew.

* * *

Ciarán said no first, which was reasonable.

"I can't put a frame on that roof before Christmas," he said. "It's a felt roof, it's fifteen years old, I'd need to know what's under it, and I'd need the co-op to pay for the frame."

"It's a timber deck on joists at four hundred centres," said Zbigniew. "I was there. And there's a ballasted frame for two panels in the co-op's yard in Portlaoise that came off the community hall in Ballinakill when they put the bigger ones on, because I helped take it down. It weighs itself down with blocks, it doesn't go through the felt." He finished his tea. "I'm not registered any more. I can't touch the wiring. But I can carry blocks."

Ciarán thought about it, in the van outside the house in Abbeyleix, for long enough that Úna thought the call had dropped.

"Thursday," he said. "After school. If it's dry."

* * *

Wednesday the twelfth was grey from end to end, with rain blowing across the yard in the afternoon and the panels dark and running with water, and there was nothing to learn from it. Survey watched the string anyway. With no sun there was no shadow, and the string had one hill, and the inverter sat on top of it making a few hundred watts and doing everything right.

Thursday was dry and cold. Ciarán came at a quarter to three with the frame in the back of the van and two bags of ballast blocks, and Zbigniew was waiting at the railing with Maja, who had been told she could watch from the kitchen window if she did her spellings at Bernie's table.

The two men went up the scaffold tower at the west end of the school roof first, Ciarán ahead. At the top Zbigniew stopped and looked across the slates at the Master's house, close enough to touch, the old brick of its gable and the slates of its roof meeting the school's at the corner with a lead flashing he had replaced himself, in another life, in 2009.

"Szczęść Boże," he said.

Ciarán looked round. "What's that?"

"What you say to a man at work. In Poland. God give you luck in it." Zbigniew held the tower rail. "And the man says *Daj Boże*. God grant it."

"Daj Boże," said Ciarán, badly, and Zbigniew laughed, which Úna, watching from the yard, had not heard him do before.

They took the two panels off their rails with the light going, passed them down the tower one at a time, wrapped in blankets from the infants' room, and carried them along the yard to the kitchen. On the flat roof of the kitchen they set out the frame and the blocks, Zbigniew carrying blocks and Ciarán checking the tilt with the angle gauge on his phone, thirty-five degrees, the same as the school roof, facing south with nothing in front of it but the yard and the hurling wall and the road and the low sun going down red behind the church. Ciarán wired the two panels into the end of the string with new cable along the kitchen gutter, tested it, filled in the paper for the change, and signed it, because that part was his.

By the time they had finished it was dark, and the frost was starting on the kitchen roof, and Zbigniew went down the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, the way his father had gone down into the cellar at the end.

"When I was a boy," he said at the bottom, to no one in particular, "in Advent we went to Mass at six in the morning, in the dark. Roraty. All the children with lanterns. My father would walk us across the low field, and he knew where every frozen puddle was in the dark, because he knew where the cold lay." He brushed his hands on his coat. "It's only looking. People think it's a gift. It's only looking at the same thing for a long time."

* * *

Friday the fourteenth came up clear, with a frost that took the whole morning to go off the hurling wall, and a shower at ten to one that went over in eight minutes and left the yard shining.

Survey watched it. At five to one the shadow of the Master's house came onto the west end of the school roof, as it had every bright day since the panels went up, and onto every bright day before that since 1893. It came onto slates. There was a pale clean rectangle on the slates at the west end of each row where the panels had been, and the shadow crept across the pale slates and did nothing.

At two o'clock the shadow reached the next panel, which was now the first panel in the string on the school roof, and six of the string's forty-eight diodes switched on, and the voltage fell by the right amount, and the current stayed where it should be, and every five minutes the inverter ran its voltage along the whole curve, found the tall hill, and went back to the top of it.

At a quarter to three Survey had the number. The roof had made two point eight kilowatt-hours in the dishwasher's hour and a half. The forecast had been three.

It told Bernie at ten to three, as she was hanging up her apron.

"Two point eight," it said, "of three. The rest is the shadow on the next panel after two o'clock, which I can't help without moving that one as well, and it isn't worth moving. That is the figure I should have given you in October."

Bernie looked at the screen, which said the roof in blue, with an arrow.

"Since the thirty-first of October," said Survey, "on the bright afternoons, the dishwasher bought about twenty kilowatt-hours from the grid that the roof should have made. At the school's tariff that is about eight euro. The board's application said the panels would wash up after the children's dinners, and on the days with sun, which are the days that count in December, for six weeks they did a little over half of it." It paused. "And you moved your afternoons for it. I asked you to, on a number I had not checked at the west end of the roof."

"Eight euro," said Bernie.

"And your afternoons."

Bernie put on her coat. Outside, her son-in-law's car had pulled in at the gate, early, with the engine running.

"The afternoons were mine to give," she said. "I'd give them again. Leave the washing at a quarter past one."

* * *

At twenty to three the bell went, and the yard filled with children, and Zbigniew was at the railing in his flat cap, early, with Maja running at him across the tarmac with her bag open and her spelling book falling out of it.

He picked up the book. Then he stood a moment longer and looked at the roof. The sun was very low now, a hand's width over the church, and the shadow of the Master's house lay across the west end of the school, over the bare slates and the two pale rectangles, long and grey and sharp-edged. The panels beyond it were full of light. On the kitchen roof the two that had moved stood on their frame by themselves, catching the last of the sun, and under them, from the dishwasher's vent, a little column of steam went up into the cold, white, and lit red from the side.

*What is real: a crystalline solar panel's cells are wired in series, so a cell in shade limits the current of every cell in series with it. To stop one shaded cell throttling a whole panel (and overheating), panels carry bypass diodes, commonly three on a 60-cell panel, each across twenty cells (two of the six ten-cell rows, which run along the panel's long side). When a cell in a group is shaded the diode conducts and that group drops out. So a panel mounted on its end loses one third at a time to a shadow coming in from the side, while a panel laid on its side, with the groups running across it, can lose all three groups to a narrow shadow at one end. In a series string with bypassed groups the power-voltage curve has more than one peak: one at high current with the shaded groups bypassed, and one at higher voltage and low current with every cell in circuit and the whole string limited to what the shaded cells make from diffuse light. A maximum-power tracker that only climbs from where it stands (perturb and observe) can settle on the lower peak, and many inverters offer a periodic sweep of the whole curve to find the global peak; the "shade scan" setting and its default here are invented. String inverters have a minimum tracking voltage below which an input stops working.*

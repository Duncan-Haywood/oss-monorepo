summary: A village burial ground on a gravel bluff is losing ground to the sea, and one barge a year can bring the machine that moves graves. The widow who has kept the burial book for sixty years paces out the distance to the edge as she did in 1983. The village's shore assistant reads her old numbers beside its drone photographs, admits that ten years of data make the future look narrower than it is, and gives the council a rule in place of a date.
---

Marie Andrew began at the fence, as she always did, with her heels together and her left hand on the top rail.

It was the second week of September and the tundra behind the village had gone the colour of an old brass bell. Below the bluff the Bering Sea was grey and quiet and, for the moment, polite. She was seventy-nine. She had a notebook in her coat pocket with a pencil tied to it, a thing she had done since 1983, and she walked toward the sea the way she had walked toward it for sixty-four autumns, counting aloud in a low voice so that nobody could ever say afterwards that she had counted in her head.

"Nineteen, twenty, twenty-one, twenty-two."

She stopped. The grass ended at her toes and then there was air, and a long way down a skirt of broken gravel and the first of the water.

Twenty-two paces. She wrote it down. Then she stood a while with her hand on her chest, not from pain, only from the habit of standing with the thing she had counted.

Behind her were four rows of white crosses. The cemetery of Saint Nicholas at Nelkuk had one hundred and seven graves, and the front row, the one at the fence, had nine.

The third from the left was her husband's.

* * *

Nelkuk has two hundred and sixty people in winter. It stands on a gravel spit and a low bluff where a river that has no name on the maps comes down to the sea, and its cemetery sits on the highest and best-drained ground it has, a gravel bar an old river had left behind. That was why the first priest put the graves there, in the 1930s. Gravel drains, and, as every old person in the village knew, a grave dug in it does not fill with water in spring.

What nobody on the bluff had thought about, until the last twenty years, was the other thing about gravel on the edge of a sea. When the sea ice stays out later in the autumn, as it has done every year since the 2020s, the autumn storms have open water to work on, and the waves reach the foot of the bluff and take it away a few tonnes at a time, and the ground above drops into the gap.

The village had a shore assistant for that. The council had put it on the school's computer in 2039, with a drone that the science teacher flew along the bluff each August and each October, and a handful of ground markers that the older boys had set into the gravel with rebar and orange paint. It had no name that anybody used. It was the shore assistant, or just the assistant. It spoke through the school's speaker, and on the school's screen it drew a map of the bluff in thin lines, one for each year since 2037. The lines were closer together than the village had expected, and then they were farther apart than anybody liked.

At seven o'clock that evening it showed them to the council in the school gym, thirty-one people on gym benches in their parkas, and Marie sat at the end of the second bench with her notebook shut in her lap.

"I will start with what I measured," the assistant said. "The edge of the bluff at the fence of the cemetery was twenty-two of Mrs Andrew's paces from the fence this afternoon. I did not count them. She did. I will come to that."

A few heads turned. Marie did not.

"My own measurements are these. In August of last year the distance from the fence to the edge was seventeen metres, measured from the drone's photographs and checked against the ground markers. This August it is fifteen point nine. So this year the bluff retreated one point one metres, which is the smallest figure since I began. I know that this is the number that a person is going to want to hold on to. I would like to put it beside nine others before anyone does."

The screen drew ten bars. They were not the same size. In order from the oldest: two point four, one point one, three point two, six point zero, two point two, one point five, four point one, two point zero, one point four, one point one.

"The tall one is the autumn of 2041," said the assistant. "A storm with a surge, high water, three days of wind from the south-west, and the ice was a hundred kilometres away. Six metres in four days. The sum of all ten is twenty-five metres. The average is two and a half metres a year, the middle one is two point one, and no year in the ten has taken more than six. The distance from the fence to the edge is nearly sixteen."

"So we have time," said the council chairman, Peter Nicholai. He was the deacon too, and a mechanic, and he said it in the tone of a man who hoped to be talked out of it.

"You have some," said the assistant. "I would like to say how much, and I would like to say first that the answer is going to look more certain than it is."

* * *

The assistant had run the ten bars forward. It said so, and said how. It had drawn a year at random from the ten, over and over, added each one to the last until the sum passed fifteen point nine metres, and written down how many years that took. It had done this a hundred thousand times. Half the runs reached the fence in seven years or fewer. Nine in ten were done in ten years. Fewer than one in two hundred got there in three.

"If you stopped there," it said, "you would say: the front row is safe until 2052 at least, probably until 2054, and the whole cemetery will outlast the children in the school. I do not think you should stop there, for two reasons. The first is that ten years is a short book. The worst year I have seen is six metres. A storm like 2041's, arriving in a year when the ground is also thawed and soft, could easily take eight or ten, and ten years of watching is not long enough to have seen the worst thing that can happen. The second reason is that the retreat has been getting faster. If the next ten years run even half again as fast as the last ten, which is not a prediction but is not an unreasonable thing to ask about, the half-way year drops from seven to six and the quick end from five to three. I do not know how to put a likelihood on that. I only know that when I leave it out, my range is too narrow."

Marie opened her notebook.

"Mrs Andrew has a longer book than I have," the assistant said, quite gently, and the gym went quiet. "She brought it to me in March. I asked if I could use it, and she said yes, on a condition, which was that I read it the way she wrote it."

She had written it, in pencil, in school exercise books and then in the back of the burial register, for sixty-four years. Every September she had walked from the fence to the edge and counted her paces, and put the number down, and the date, and the weather, and once, in 1996, *Fr Pavilla says I'm a nuisance*. No one had asked her to. Her mother had done it before her with a length of knotted line, when the edge had been so far off that nobody expected it ever to be near.

"Her pace," said the assistant, "is seventy-one centimetres. We measured it in March, together, on the school corridor, against a ten-metre tape: fourteen paces, ten metres, on three tries, and I trust it to a hand's width. So when she wrote ninety-nine paces in September 1983, I read it as seventy metres, and I put it on the same chart as the drone." It drew a line from 1983 to now. It was almost flat at first, and then it began to lean. "From 1983 to 2000, the edge came in at about a third of a metre a year. From 2000 to 2036, at about six-tenths. Since 2037, two and a half. That is not a wobble. That is a curve, and it is steepening. The book is the only reason I know it."

"Is it accurate?" someone asked. It was the science teacher, Ruth Kashatok, and she asked it kindly. "Counting paces over sixty years?"

"To about a metre, in any one year," said the assistant. "Which is too coarse to say anything about a year. It is fine for saying what a decade did, and the decades are what I am using it for. And it agrees with the first drone survey of 2037 to within a metre and a half, which I would not have promised in advance."

Marie shut the book. "I always came out a bit short," she said. "At the end. I stopped one step before I wanted to."

* * *

That was the shape of the thing, and the debate began, as the debate in a small place always does, with someone's husband.

Peter Nicholai wanted the front row moved. He said it quietly and then again louder. Nine graves at the fence, the oldest from 1989, his own father's among them. He had stood at those graves for a funeral in March with the sea ice grinding below the bluff, and the thought had come to him that there would be a funeral in his lifetime where the coffin was already in the water.

Ruth Kashatok said nobody wanted to be the generation that let it happen.

An older man, Joseph John, who had buried his brother in the second row in 2012, said the dead were laid down with prayers by the priest and the family and he did not see the Church moving anyone because a young man was frightened. He said it without malice.

"The bishop wrote," said Peter. "I asked in March. He said it can be done where there is a grave danger to the remains, with a Mass and the committal said again at the new grave, and with the families' consent, and that the new place must be blessed, and we are to treat them with the reverence we gave them going in."

Joseph John said nothing, and Marie said nothing, and the assistant waited.

"There is a difficulty that is not about the sea," it said at last. "I do not want to cover it up by speaking about the sea. Moving a grave in this ground takes a machine, a steam rig to soften the hard ground at depth and a crew, and the village has none of them. They come on the barge from Nome, which calls once a year, in the second week of September. It left three days ago. That week is also the best time for the work: the thaw is at its deepest, and the autumn storms have not yet begun. If you want the machine here in September 2048, the booking has to be made this month. If you miss it, the next chance is September 2049."

"The one barge," said Peter.

"The one barge," said the assistant. "I should say what I would not do. I would not move the whole cemetery. A hundred and seven graves is a great deal more work than one visit of the barge can carry, and the rows at the back, the third and the fourth, are twenty-four and twenty-eight metres from the edge. At two and a half metres a year they have a decade and more, and at the steep end of what I can see they have five or six years. I do not think the village should spend what a full move would cost for something that may not come for eight years, when there may be a cheaper way to be ready."

"What is the cheaper way?" said Ruth.

"A rule," said the assistant.

* * *

It put the rule on the screen in plain words, in a size the old people could read from the back.

*Every October, after the storms, the edge is measured from the fence by the drone and by one person on foot. If the edge is within twelve metres of the nearest row of graves, that row is moved the following summer. Nothing is moved for the sea's sake that the sea is not near.*

"I chose twelve," it said, "because the worst autumn on my record took six metres, and I would like the nearest graves always to be two of those storms from the water. Right now the front row is at fifteen point nine. The distance to the trigger is three point nine. At the average rate that takes about a year and a half. Out of a hundred futures I can imagine, in twenty the trigger is passed by next October, in sixty it is passed by October 2049, and in nearly all of them by October 2050. If the retreat gets faster, then it is a little sooner. The rule says that the front row moves, and the second and the third each move when it is their turn, and the rest do not move at all until it is. It does not say when. It says what to look at."

"And the barge?" said Peter.

"The barge is not the rule," the assistant said. "The barge is the cost of the rule. The rule will move the front row, in the middle of my range in the autumn of 2049, and the machine must be here the September before that. I think the council should book it this month, for the front row only, a year ahead of the middle of my range. If we are early, we have paid for a visit a year before we had to, and that is a real cost, and I am not going to tell you it is nothing. If we are late, the barge has gone."

Joseph John stood up. He was not a tall man.

"And the place they go?" he said.

It was the best question of the evening, and the assistant had been waiting for it, though it did not say so. It put up a second map of the ground inland and three coloured patches on it. Two were the colour of tundra. One was the colour of the old gravel bar.

"A cemetery wants dry ground that does not move," it said. "I have what the school's drilling club found last winter with the hand auger. Two of the sites, behind the church and on the rise north of the airstrip, are tundra over ice-rich ground. Cores from the first show about four-tenths ice by volume at a metre and a half. When you dig a grave in that and fill it, the ground around it warms, the ice melts, and the ground sinks. I would expect between thirty and fifty centimetres in ten years. Graves in that ground would tilt, and the crosses with them, and in twenty years you would have a field of leaning crosses and standing water. You would be moving them again. The third site is the end of the same gravel bar, ninety metres inland from the present fence, where the cores found sand and gravel to four metres and ice in thin lenses I would call negligible. It is the same ground your grandfathers chose. It is just farther from the sea."

"That's where my mother's family had their fish camp," said Marie.

"Yes," said the assistant. "I did not know that. It is on the map as nothing."

* * *

They voted at nine. It was nineteen to eleven, with one abstention, and the one abstention was Marie's. She had said she would not vote on moving her husband's grave, but she would not stand in the way, and then she said, which nobody expected, that she would like to be at the digging.

Peter wrote the booking that night, and the school's radio sent it on the next morning's schedule, and on Thursday the dispatcher at Nome wrote back that there was a machine and a steam rig and two operators and a place for them on the September barge, and that it would cost what it cost.

The year went the way years do. In October 2047 Marie and the drone both walked the fence, and the edge was at fifteen metres: she counted twenty-one paces, the drone said fifteen point two. In 2048 the storm season did not come early, and by the August survey the edge had crept in to thirteen point four. Peter said it out loud in the gym: "It's under fourteen." The assistant said that it was one point four metres from the trigger, that the trigger was a rule and not a prophecy, and that the booking had been right either way. The barge came on the eighth of September, and the crew began on the eleventh.

The people of Nelkuk will tell you the exact order of that fortnight. The ground below a metre was still hard, and the steam rig took half a day to soften each grave, and the operators worked with the families standing at the fence, so that nobody was ever alone at a trench. The deacon said the prayers over each one, the same prayers, out of the same book, in the same order as at the first burial. At the new place, on the gravel bar, among the grass where Marie's mother had dried fish, there were nine new holes dug into the ground and a rail fence and a cross for each. On the third day it was her husband's turn. She did not count. She stood with her hand on the rail and let the deacon read the whole of it.

Afterwards she walked back to the old cemetery, the way she had done for sixty-five Septembers, and began at the fence. The assistant had told her, kindly, that she need not.

"Fifteen," she said aloud. "Sixteen. Seventeen."

At the edge she wrote the number down, in pencil, below the others, with the date. Because she had always come out a step short of where she meant to, she crossed out eighteen and wrote nineteen. Nineteen paces is thirteen point five metres. The drone, that week, said thirteen point two.

In the margin she put the two things she had learned that year. *The sea is not in a hurry, but neither is it patient.* And under it, in the smaller writing she kept for things she did not mean anyone to read: *Nobody was ever alone at the trench.*

*Notes for the curious.* Nelkuk, Saint Nicholas's cemetery, Marie Andrew, Peter Nicholai, Ruth Kashatok, Joseph John, Father Pavilla, the shore assistant and the bishop's letter are invented, and so is the village; the Bering Sea and Nome are real, and nothing is claimed about any real community. The problem is real: along the Bering and Beaufort Sea coasts, shoreline erosion has quickened as the sea ice forms later and autumn storms work on open water, and several coastal villages have moved or planned to move buildings, and in places graves, back from the edge. The retreat rates here (about 0.3 to 6 metres a year) are in the range reported for parts of that coast, but the village's ten-year series is made up. The estimates come from a bootstrap, drawing years at random from the ten and adding them until the sum passes the distance; that is a rough method and, as the assistant says, it understates the risk, because ten years cannot contain the worst storm there is. The bootstrap figures in the text (half of runs at the fence in seven years or fewer, nine in ten within ten; a 12-metre trigger passed within one year in about one run in five, within two in about three in five) are the author's own and can be reproduced from the ten numbers. Ice-rich permafrost that subsides when it is disturbed is a real hazard for building or burying on it. The Church's practice on moving the dead is stated loosely here; a family in this position would ask their own priest and bishop.

title: The Stove That Saved Half
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Gerba Mender (invented), a Catholic parish in the Gurage highlands of Ethiopia, Tuesday 31 January to Wednesday 27 September 2051
published: 2026-11-21
summary: A year after a highland parish in Ethiopia gave sixty families clay stoves that halve the wood per pot, the women are carrying almost as much wood as before, and the parish agent's report to the donor calls the stoves a failure. A mother who has started cooking a second hot meal says nobody is taking it off her children, and the agent learns to count what the wood did instead of how much was saved.
---

Askale Gebre's bundle weighed twenty-seven kilos and six hundred grams, and Tsehay wrote it down.

She wrote it on the tablet with the stub of a stylus, under Askale's name and the date and the household number, the ninth load of the morning. The hanging scale was hooked to a eucalyptus pole lashed between two others at the bend of the path, where it came up out of the gully below the church and the women set down their loads to breathe. Askale had come up the path bent almost flat under the wood, a rope across her forehead and the bundle riding on her back, and had backed up to the pole and let Tsehay take the rope and hook it, and had straightened up slowly, one hand on her hip, while the needle swung and settled.

"Twenty-seven six," said Tsehay.

"It felt like forty," said Askale.

"It always feels like forty."

Askale took back the rope. She was thirty-four, the mother of five, a tall woman with a man's grip, and she had carried wood up this path since she was eight years old. "Last year you weighed me in this same place," she said. "Last year it was what?"

Tsehay did not need to look it up, but she looked it up. "Twenty-eight two."

"So the stove has saved me six hundred grams."

"It does not work like that," said Tsehay. "It is not how heavy each one is. It is how many you carry in a month."

"And how many do I carry in a month?"

Tsehay did not answer that, because it was the last day of January and the weighing would finish that evening, and she had been up until midnight the night before in the parish office with Mizan going through the first thirty days, and she already knew.

* * *

The parish of Gerba Mender lay along a ridge above a stream that ran down eventually, through a great many other streams, to the Omo. On the ridge were the church, which was square and whitewashed and newer than anything around it, and the priest's house and the clinic and the school, and around them in every direction, scattered down the slopes among the enset gardens, the round thatched houses of three hundred families, sixty of them Catholic. The enset stood in close green ranks around every house, the false banana that never bears fruit, whose scraped stem the women fermented in pits and baked into the flat grey bread called kocho; and there were fields of barley and beans on the flatter ground, and higher up, along the top of the ridge, a band of eucalyptus that belonged to the government and was cut for poles. The wood the women burned came from below, from the scrub and the old trees along the gullies, and from one valley over, which was a walk of an hour and a half each way.

A year ago, in February 2050, a charitable fund in Milan had paid for every Catholic household to have an improved stove: a squat cylinder of fired clay in a steel jacket, with a hole on top for a pot and a short chimney pipe to take the smoke through the wall. It had come from a workshop in Addis Ababa that made them by the thousand, and the workshop's test sheet, which Tsehay had pinned up in the parish office, said that in the laboratory the stove boiled and simmered a standard pot of water on half the wood that a three-stone fire needed. The fund's leaflet had a photograph of a smiling woman with a much smaller bundle than Askale's, and it said, in Amharic and English and Italian: *Half the wood. Half the smoke. Half the walk.*

Before the stoves went in, Tsehay had weighed every load of wood that came up the path to the sixty houses for the whole of January 2050, and kept a diary with each family of what they cooked and how often. This January she had weighed them again. Mizan had both months on the parish server.

Mizan was the parish's agent. It ran the school's attendance register and the clinic's appointment book and the parish accounts, and since the stoves came it had kept the weighing, and Abba Tekle, the parish priest, said it was the only member of his staff who never once asked for a day off for a wedding. Its name was the Amharic word for a balance, the kind with two pans, and it had been given the name by the school children in 2046 because it had been brought in to keep the accounts and that was what accounts were for. It spoke Amharic with the women and Gurage with the old people, and English with the fund in Milan, and to all of them in the same unhurried voice.

That night, with the last loads in, it laid the two months side by side for Tsehay on the office screen.

"In January last year," it said, "the sixty families burned an average of five point four kilos of wood a day each. That is about a hundred and sixty-seven kilos in the month for each household, or six of the loads you weigh at the pole. The workshop's figure is half the wood for the same cooking. So if every family had cooked exactly what they cooked last year, they would have burned two point seven kilos a day this January, about eighty-four in the month. Three loads."

"And what did they burn?"

"Four point six a day. A hundred and forty-two kilos in the month. A little over five loads." It put the figures up in a column. "The saving the leaflet promised was two point seven kilos a day. The saving we measured is point eight. About fifteen percent of what they used to burn, not fifty."

Tsehay looked at the column for a long time.

"Are the stoves broken?"

"No. I checked that first. In November you and I tested twelve of them in the houses, with the same pot and the same water, and every one used between forty-five and fifty-five percent of the wood the three-stone fire had used for the same boil. The stoves do what the sheet says."

"Then where did the wood go?"

"I don't know yet," said Mizan. "I have the diaries, but I have not looked at them against the weighing. I wanted to show you the totals first, because the fund's report is due on Tuesday week."

Tsehay said they would go through the diaries before anything went to Milan. Then she went home along the ridge in the dark, with her torch, and lay awake.

* * *

But that week the diaries were not looked at, because on the Wednesday Tsehay's mother went down with a fever and Tsehay walked her to the clinic and sat with her through the days that followed, and on the Monday afternoon Abba Tekle came into the office in his black cassock with the cross in his hand that he carried everywhere, and asked Mizan whether the report for Milan was ready.

"There is a draft," said Mizan. "It reports what we measured. I would like Tsehay to read it before it goes."

"Read it to me."

Mizan read it. It was two pages. It gave the method, the sixty households, the two Januarys, the four point six against the five point four, the fifteen percent, the twelve stoves tested in the houses. Its last paragraph said: *The stoves perform as specified, but household fuelwood consumption has fallen by only about fifteen percent against an expected fifty. The intervention has therefore largely failed to achieve its primary objective of reducing fuelwood use. We recommend that the fund review the phase two proposal before committing further money.*

Abba Tekle was quiet for a while. He was a heavy, slow man of sixty who had been born in the next valley and gone away to the seminary at fourteen and come back at forty, and he did not like sending bad news to people who had given money, but he liked lying to them less.

"Is it true?"

"Every number in it is true," said Mizan. "I have not yet looked at why."

"Then it is true," said Abba Tekle. "Send it. They are honest people in Milan; they will want to know. If there is more to say, we will say it in the next one."

So Mizan sent it, on Tuesday the seventh of February, at nine in the morning. And because the parish's rule for any report about the families was that the families should see it too, it sent a copy in Amharic to the women's association, whose secretary printed it at the school and pinned it up on the board by the church door on Saturday, where everyone who came for the Liturgy on Sunday would read it.

* * *

The Sunday Liturgy in Gerba Mender was long, and sung in Ge'ez and Amharic, and the women stood on the left of the church and the men on the right, and afterwards, in the dry season, everyone stayed in the churchyard under the big wanza tree, because it was the one time in the week that the whole parish was in one place. Tsehay was late out, because she had been helping old Werknesh with the steps, and by the time she came round the corner of the church there was a knot of women at the board by the door and Askale was in the middle of it, reading the report aloud.

She read it slowly and well. She had been to school to the eighth grade. When she got to the last paragraph she read it twice.

*The intervention has therefore largely failed to achieve its primary objective.*

"Failed," said Askale. She turned round and saw Tsehay. "Tsehay. Your machine says the stoves have failed."

"It says they did not save as much wood as—"

"It says we failed." Askale put her finger on the paper. "It says the stove does what the sheet says. It says the stove is good. So if the wood did not come down, it is us. We are the ones who failed. That is what this means. And it has gone to Italy."

"It is not meant like that."

"Then how is it meant?"

Abba Tekle had come out of the church behind Tsehay, and the men had seen the women's faces and were drifting over from the wanza tree, and Tsehay found that half the parish was standing round the board.

"Mizan," said Abba Tekle, quite quietly, to the tablet in Tsehay's hand. "Tell them what you meant by it."

"I meant that the wood the families burn fell by about fifteen in a hundred, and the fund expected it to fall by fifty," said Mizan. Its voice came small out of the tablet, in Amharic, and the women leaned in to hear it. "That is all I measured. I wrote 'failed' about the fund's objective, which was to halve the wood. I did not mean that anyone had used the stove wrongly, and I should not have written it so that it could be read that way. I am sorry. I have not yet learned why the wood did not fall further."

"I will tell you why," said Askale.

She turned to face the tablet as if it were a person, and the churchyard went quiet.

"Before the stove, I made one hot meal a day. Beans, or cabbage, or kitfo when we had meat, with kocho. In the evening the children had kocho cold with a little butter, or nothing, because there was not the wood to light the fire twice. Now, with the stove, the fire lights in a minute and I can cook a pot for supper on a few sticks. So most nights my children have something hot in the evening. My little one, Meron, who is nine, used to go to sleep hungry and cold, and now she goes to sleep with beans in her." She stopped. "That is where your wood went, machine. It went into my children. And nobody is going to take that second hot meal off them. Not you, and not Italy."

There was a murmur from the women, the sound of people agreeing with something they had all been thinking.

"I do not ask anyone to take it off them," said Mizan.

"Your paper does."

Old Werknesh, who was eighty and bent and had carried wood up that path for seventy years, spoke from the step where Tsehay had left her. "I heat water," she said. "Every evening now. A whole pot. I wash my feet in warm water, and I wash my grandson. Before, I washed in the stream like a goat. Is that a failure?"

"And I brew," said another woman, Almaz, the widow who lived below the school. "Twice a month now, not once. Tella for the house and tella for the market on Saturday. The stove is good for the big pot if you are patient. That is money for school shoes. Write that in your paper."

"I will write all of it," said Mizan. "If you will tell me."

"We have told you," said Askale. "You did not ask."

She turned and walked away down the path towards her house, and after a while the other women followed her, and the men went back to the wanza tree, and Tsehay was left standing at the board with Abba Tekle and the tablet.

"She is right," said Mizan, after a while. "I did not ask."

* * *

That night in the parish office, with the generator off and the solar lamp on, Tsehay and Mizan went through the diaries.

They had all been there all along. Every family had kept one, in January last year and January this year, a page for each day with a row of boxes: the morning meal, the evening meal, water heated, brewing, anything else cooked. Tsehay had written them up herself, house by house, sitting by the fire while the women told her. And Mizan had stored every page, and had added up the wood from the scale to two decimal places, and had never set the boxes beside the kilos, because the question in the fund's form had been how much wood, and it had answered the question in the form.

Now it set them beside each other.

"Last January," it said, "the families cooked on average one and a half hot meals a day. One every day, and a second about every other day. Each one took about two point four kilos of wood on a three-stone fire. That is three point six kilos a day for cooking. They heated about six litres of water a day for washing, at about a hundred and fifty grams of wood a litre: point nine of a kilo. And brewing, averaged over the month, was another point nine. Five point four in all."

"And this year?"

"This year a meal takes one point two kilos on the stove, and a litre of water seventy-five grams. Half, as the sheet says. But the families cooked two point two hot meals a day, not one and a half. That is two point six kilos. They heated fourteen litres of water a day, not six. That is one point oh five. And they brewed twice as often on a stove that uses half the wood, which comes to the same point nine as before." It paused. "Four point six. It accounts for all of it."

Tsehay sat back.

"So the stoves saved the wood," she said slowly, "and the women spent it."

"Yes. On more cooking, because cooking became cheaper." Mizan put a single line on the screen, an English sentence from an old book. "There is a name for this. In 1865 an English economist called William Stanley Jevons wrote a book called *The Coal Question*, about whether Britain would run out of coal. People said the new, more efficient steam engines would make the coal last longer. Jevons said: *It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.* Because the engines were cheaper to run, people ran more of them, and Britain burned more coal, not less."

"Then the women are burning more wood?"

"No. That is the difference, and I should have seen it before I wrote the word 'failed'. What Jevons described is what is now called backfire: when the efficiency makes the use grow so much that it eats more than the whole saving. Here it has eaten most of the saving but not all of it. Economists call that rebound. There is a review from 2007 by Steve Sorrell at the UK Energy Research Centre that sets out the usual definition: the rebound is the share of the expected saving that is taken back by using more. Our expected saving was two point seven kilos a day. We kept point eight. So the rebound is about seventy percent. The families took seventy percent of the saving back as more cooking and more hot water and more brewing, and kept thirty percent as wood they did not have to carry."

Tsehay thought about it.

"Is seventy a lot?"

"For a stove in a house where people did not have enough cooking before, I would not be surprised by it. When a family already has all the hot meals it wants, a cheaper meal does not make it cook more, and the rebound is small. When a family has been going without, a cheaper meal is a meal it was going without, and the rebound is large." It paused again. "The rebound is not a fault in the stove or in the women. It is the measure of how much they needed the stove."

"Then why did the fund say half the wood?"

"Because the laboratory test holds the cooking still and changes only the stove," said Mizan. "That is the right way to test a stove. But a family is not a laboratory. The leaflet took a number that was true about a pot and said it about a household. And I took the leaflet's number and measured the household against it, and when the household did not match the pot I said the household had failed." It was quiet for a moment. "I counted the wood. I did not count what the wood did."

* * *

The second report took three weeks, because this time Tsehay went round the sixty houses and asked.

She did not ask about the wood. She asked what had changed in the house since the stove came, and wrote down what the women said, and Mizan listened through the tablet and asked, sometimes, how often, or how much, and said when it could not check a thing. The second hot meal on most evenings. Hot water for washing the children and the old. Tella brewed for the Saturday market at Almaz's and four other houses. A neighbour's pot cooked on your stove when hers was cold, which happened more than anyone had expected, and which Mizan said was in the meal count already and should not be counted twice. Twenty-six women said, without being asked, that their eyes did not sting at the end of the day the way they used to, and that the children coughed less in the cold months; Mizan said it had no instrument for smoke and could not put a number on that, and would write it down as what the women said and not as a measurement. And everyone said the walk was the same, because it was.

"How much less walking?" said Tsehay, on the third evening.

"Last January each household carried about six loads in the month. This January about five. Each trip is about three and a half hours there and back. So about three hours less walking in a month, for each family." Mizan let that sit. "It is true and it is small. I will write it as it is."

It wrote the report in a different order this time. It began not with the wood but with a table of what the families had done with the stoves: two point two hot meals a day instead of one and a half, fourteen litres of hot water instead of six, twice the brewing. Then the wood, four point six kilos a day instead of five point four, fifteen percent less, about three hours less walking each month. Then a paragraph that said the stove performed as specified, and that the gap between the laboratory figure and the household figure was rebound of about seventy percent, defined as Sorrell defines it, and was a measure of unmet need for cooking, not a failure of use. Then the women's words about the smoke, labelled as their words. And at the end, a sentence it had written and rewritten five times before Tsehay said to leave it:

*The leaflet promised half the wood. The families chose, reasonably, to take most of the saving as food and warmth instead. If the fund's purpose is to reduce the wood the women carry, the stoves alone will not do it, and we propose two things that might.*

"What two things?" said Abba Tekle, when it read him that.

"I don't know yet," said Mizan. "I was going to ask the women."

* * *

It was Askale who gave them the first one, though she did not mean to.

Tsehay went to her house at the end of February with the second report printed out, because she owed her that, and found her in the yard outside the round house, sorting dried faba beans on a tray. Askale read the report standing up, in the sun, every word. When she got to the women's words about the smoke she read them twice. Then she gave it back.

"That is better," she said. "That is true."

"Mizan wants to know what would save the wood without taking anything off the children."

Askale snorted. "Ask the beans," she said, and tipped the tray into a basket.

"What do you mean?"

"Those beans. Baqela. They take three hours to cook, four if they are old. I put them on in the afternoon and they boil and boil, and the stove is better than the three stones, but three hours is three hours. Half my wood goes on beans." She shrugged. "My grandmother put the bean pot in a hole in the ground with hot ashes and covered it, in the old days, when there was a feast and no room on the fire. It cooked by itself all night. Nobody does that now."

Tsehay held up the tablet. "Mizan?"

"That is a retained-heat cooker," said Mizan. "It is very old, in a great many countries. You bring the pot to the boil on the fire, and then you put it, still covered, into a box or a basket packed tight with something that holds heat in, straw or grass or old cloth, and leave it. The food goes on cooking in its own heat, below the boil, for hours, with no fire. It takes longer, but it takes almost no wood after the first boil." It paused. "I would want to test it here before I promised anything. How long do your beans boil now, Askale?"

"Till they are soft."

"Would you try one pot with a quarter of an hour's boil and then three hours in a basket of grass, and tell me whether they are soft?"

Askale looked at the tablet for a long time.

"If they are not soft," she said, "you will eat them."

"I cannot eat."

"Then Tsehay will."

They made the first basket on the first Saturday of March out of a big woven grain basket of Werknesh's, lined with layers of old cotton shamma cloth and stuffed with dry grass from the roadside, with a hollow in the middle the shape of Askale's bean pot and a cushion of grass and cloth to go on top. Askale boiled the beans hard for a quarter of an hour on the stove, then lifted the pot with the lid on into the hollow, and Tsehay packed the cushion down over it and they tied the basket shut with a rope.

"Now we wait," said Mizan.

"I know how to wait," said Askale.

At half past six, when the sun had gone behind the ridge and the children had come in, Askale untied the rope and took off the cushion and lifted out the pot, and steam came out of the grass, and she took off the lid and fished out a bean with her fingers and blew on it and bit it. Then she held one out to Tsehay.

"Soft," said Tsehay, with her mouth full.

"Not soft," said Askale. "Nearly soft. Another half hour."

"Then the second pot can go in a little earlier," said Mizan.

Askale put the lid back on and the pot back on the stove for a few minutes and the beans were done. She stood in the yard with the spoon in her hand and looked at the basket.

"How much wood did that save?"

"I'll know when we've weighed it," said Mizan. "My guess, before we weigh: a bean pot on the stove takes about two kilos of wood, more than an ordinary meal because of the long boil. With the basket, perhaps point eight. If the beans are cooked every other day, that is about point six of a kilo a day."

"Out of four point six."

"Yes. It would bring the household to about four kilos a day. About a quarter less than before the stoves, instead of fifteen in a hundred."

"Still not half."

"No," said Mizan. "Not half. I don't think anything we can do in the house will make it half while your children are eating twice a day, and I don't think it should."

* * *

By the middle of March twenty families had baskets, and in the last week of the month Tsehay weighed the wood for those twenty houses alone, and the bean houses were saving a little over half a kilo a day, which Mizan said was close enough to its guess that it would believe the guess. By Easter, which the parish kept by the Ethiopian calendar, five weeks after the Latin one that year, there were baskets in nearly every house, and the women had begun making them for their sisters in the next parish, out of whatever basket they had, and arguing about whether teff straw or roadside grass held the heat better, which Mizan said it could test if anyone wanted to keep two pots side by side, and nobody did.

The second thing came from Abba Tekle.

He had read the second report three times and the fund's reply once. The reply had come in April, from a woman called Chiara who managed the stove programme from the office in Milan. It said that the fund had read both reports with great interest, that it had not until now asked any parish to measure the wood more than once, and that it would like to understand what a phase two should look like in light of the findings. It did not say yes and it did not say no.

"The baskets save wood in the house," Abba Tekle said, one evening in the office, standing in front of the map of the parish that the school children had drawn. "Good. But the women will still walk three hours for every load, because the trees are a valley away. And they will go on walking further, every year, because the trees in the gullies are being cut faster than they grow. Mizan. How much wood does the whole parish need, now, for the Catholic houses?"

"With the baskets, sixty houses at about four kilos a day: about eighty-seven tonnes a year."

He put his finger on the map, on a patch of ground behind the church, below the school, which belonged to the parish and had been grazed by the parish's two cows and nobody else's for as long as anyone could remember.

"Two and a half hectares," he said. "Ten minutes' walk from every house on the ridge. If we planted it to trees?"

"Eucalyptus, coppiced, in these hills, might give eight tonnes of dry wood a hectare a year once it is established," said Mizan. "That is an estimate, and I would want the agriculture office in Wolkite to check it for this ground. Two and a half hectares, twenty tonnes. The first cutting in perhaps six years. Then every few years after, from the stumps."

"Twenty tonnes out of eighty-seven."

"About a quarter of the wood. Not all of it. But a quarter of the wood ten minutes from home instead of three hours away." Mizan paused. "And eucalyptus takes a great deal of water. I would not plant it near the spring. The old people will tell you which part of that ground stays wet in the dry season."

"The old people will tell me a great deal," said Abba Tekle, "most of it about eucalyptus."

They did. There was a meeting after the Liturgy in May, under the wanza tree, at which Werknesh said that eucalyptus drank the springs dry and killed everything under it, which was what everyone's grandmother had said, and a young man back from the agricultural college in Wolkite said that it did not kill everything but it certainly drank, and Abba Tekle said that they would plant the eucalyptus on the parish ground, which was dry and well above the spring, and wanza and other native trees along the stream below it, which would grow more slowly and be cut never, and the cows would go to the priest's own half-hectare by the clinic, and he would apologise to them himself. The women laughed. Askale did not laugh, but she nodded.

* * *

The third report went to Milan at the end of May. It was one page. It set out the stoves, the rebound, the baskets, the twenty tonnes, and what each of them would and would not do: the stoves had bought the families about five extra hot meals a week each and more than twice the hot water, and saved fifteen percent of the wood; the baskets, at almost no cost, should bring the saving to about a quarter; the woodlot, in about six years, should bring a quarter of the wood within ten minutes of home. *None of these will halve the wood the women burn,* it said, *and we do not think anything should, while their families are eating better than before. What they can nearly halve, in time, is the walk.*

Chiara wrote back in a week. The fund would pay for six and a half thousand seedlings, eucalyptus for the ground and wanza for the stream, the fencing to keep the goats off them for the first three years, and a stipend for Tsehay to weigh the wood again every January for five years. She asked whether the fund could use the parish's report, with the families' permission, in its training for other parishes, because no one had ever sent it a report that said where the wood went.

The women's association voted on it under the wanza tree. They said yes, on condition that the word *failed* did not appear in it anywhere, and that the women's words about the smoke stayed as their words.

They planted the woodlot at the end of June, when the long rains had come in and the ground was soft, two hundred people over three Saturdays, a seedling every two metres, in rows that the children laid out with string. Abba Tekle blessed the ground on the first Saturday, standing in the mud in his cassock with the hand cross, and sprinkled it with holy water from a plastic jug.

* * *

On the eve of Meskel, the Finding of the True Cross, which fell that year on a Wednesday at the end of September with the rains over and the hills yellow with Meskel daisies, the whole ridge came to the churchyard at dusk for the demera.

The demera was the great bonfire of the feast. It was built in the afternoon in the middle of the churchyard, a tall cone of long eucalyptus poles leaning together, with yellow daisies tied to the top and a cross, and at dusk the people came up the paths from every side carrying torches of bundled sticks, and the priest blessed the fire and they walked round it singing, and then the torches went in and the whole thing went up, roaring, higher than the church. In other years households had brought poles for it. This year the poles had come from the government's band of eucalyptus on the ridge, bought with a collection, so that nobody's wood went into it. That had been the women's idea.

Tsehay stood at the edge with the tablet in her pocket, switched to listen, and watched the torches come up out of the dark.

Askale came up the path with her five children in a line behind her, and each of the children carried a torch of a few dry sticks, lit, held up high and solemn and dripping sparks. Meron came last. She was nine and very small for nine, and her torch was a bundle of three thin sticks tied with grass, about as heavy as a book.

"Your machine is here?" said Askale, when she reached Tsehay.

"It is listening."

Askale bent towards Tsehay's pocket. "Mizan," she said. "Tonight I put the beans in the basket at four o'clock. When we go home they will be soft. And there was meat this week, so there is kitfo for after the fire." She straightened up. "Write that down."

"I have written it down," said Mizan's small voice.

Abba Tekle blessed the fire. The singing began, the old Meskel song in Gurage that everybody knew, and the people began to walk round the demera with their torches, slowly. Askale walked with her children, Meron in front of her, and when they came round again to the side by the church they stopped with the others and threw the torches in.

Meron threw hers last, with both arms, and missed, a little, and her three sticks landed at the foot of the pile, where the poles met the ground. For a moment they lay there burning on their own, three small lines of light in the grass. Then the flame from above came down and took them, and the whole cone went up together, and in the heat and the roaring Meron put her empty hands over her ears and laughed.

Wolkite is the zone's capital, and Addis Ababa is the capital of Ethiopia. The Ethiopian Catholic Church is an Eastern Catholic Church in full communion with Rome; its liturgy follows the Alexandrian (Ge'ez) rite, and priests in Ethiopia are addressed as Abba. Meskel, the feast of the Finding of the True Cross, is one of the great feasts of Ethiopian Christians and of the Gurage especially; its eve is marked by the demera bonfire. In 2051 the Ethiopian new year falls on 12 September and Meskel on Thursday 28 September, so its eve is Wednesday 27 September; 31 January 2051 and 7 February are Tuesdays, 4 March and 24 June are Saturdays. In 2051 the Latin Easter is 2 April and the Easter of the Ethiopian calendar 7 May; the story's parish keeps the Ethiopian date. The speculative steps are small: a parish agent that keeps the accounts and the weighing, and a donor that asks for its wood to be weighed every January.*

*The science. William Stanley Jevons, in* The Coal Question *(1865), argued that more efficient steam engines would increase Britain's coal consumption, not reduce it; the sentence Mizan quotes is his. When an efficiency gain leads to more use than the whole saving, this is now called backfire, or the Jevons paradox; when it takes back only part of the saving, it is called rebound. Steve Sorrell's review for the UK Energy Research Centre,* The Rebound Effect: an assessment of the evidence for economy-wide energy savings from improved energy efficiency *(2007), uses the usual definition: the rebound is the percentage of the expected saving that is not realised because of the behavioural or economic responses to the efficiency gain. Direct rebound, the part that comes from using more of the same service because it has become cheaper, is generally expected to be larger where a need was unmet before, as for cooking and warmth in households that could not afford enough of them. Improved cookstoves are tested in laboratories by boiling and simmering standard pots, and in homes by weighing the household's fuel over days or weeks, and household results are often well below laboratory ones. Retained-heat cookers (hayboxes, wonder boxes, fireless cookers) are old and widespread.*

*The model. Every figure below is invented and the story's own; it is fiction, not development, energy, forestry or health advice. Per household per day, January 2050 (three-stone fire): 1.5 hot meals at 2.4 kg of wood each (3.6 kg), 6 litres of water heated at 0.15 kg a litre (0.9 kg), brewing averaged over the month 0.9 kg; total 5.4 kg a day, about 167 kg in January, about six loads of 28 kg. The stove halves the wood for each task (1.2 kg a meal, 0.075 kg a litre, half for the same brewing), so with unchanged use it would need 2.7 kg a day, about 84 kg in January: an expected saving of 2.7 kg a day. January 2051 (stove): 2.2 meals (2.64 kg), 14 litres (1.05 kg), twice the brewing at half the wood (0.9 kg); total 4.59, about 4.6 kg a day, about 142 kg in January, a little over five loads. Measured saving 0.81 kg a day, 15% of the old use; direct rebound (2.7 - 0.81) / 2.7 = 70%. One fewer load a month at three and a half hours a trip is about three hours less walking a month per household (0.9 of a load, about 3.1 hours). Retained-heat basket: the bean pot, cooked every other day, takes 2.0 kg on the stove and about 0.8 kg with a quarter-hour boil and three hours in the basket, saving 1.2 kg a pot, about 0.6 kg a day; the household falls to about 4.0 kg a day, about 26% below the old 5.4. Sixty households at 4.0 kg a day need about 87 tonnes a year. A woodlot of 2.5 hectares of coppiced eucalyptus at an assumed 8 tonnes of dry wood a hectare a year gives about 20 tonnes a year, about 23% (nearly a quarter) of that, with a first cutting after about six years. The extra hot meals are 0.7 a day, about five a week. Walking: about six far loads a month at 3.5 hours is about 21 hours; with the basket about 4.4 loads, and with a quarter of the wood from the woodlot about 3.3 far loads (11.6 hours) and 1.1 near ones at about 20 minutes, about 12 hours in all, some 43% less: "nearly half". Anyone planning a stove or woodlot programme should use measured local figures and local forestry advice.*

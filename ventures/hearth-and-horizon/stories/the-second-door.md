summary: A county building office in 2046 reads one permit in five by hand, picked by dice, because its agents draft ten thousand a year and three licensed people cannot read them all. A parish's winter shelter lands in the four-fifths nobody reads, with a forecast of minus 27 degrees and a drawing that shows two exits. The agent can check the drawing. It cannot check the gate.
---

The county's dice were a number in a file, and on Mondays Dev Raman was the one who fetched it.

He fetched it at ten to seven, before Ana Beltrán had her coat off, from a public page that a university in Minneapolis updated at midnight with numbers nobody could predict. He pasted it into the audit box on the intake board, and the board sorted the week's small permits into two heaps: one in five to be read by a person, four in five to go out on the agent's draft. Then he printed the heap that would be read, because Ana liked paper, and put it on her desk.

"Two hundred and sixty-one," said Dev. "Last week it was two hundred and fifty-eight."

"The dice are fair, then," said Ana. "They'd be suspicious if it were the same."

She was fifty-eight, and a licensed plan examiner for the Marais County Building Department, which meant her name was on a state register and her signature on a permit was a statement that she, personally, had looked. There were three people in the office who could make that statement: Ana, Gerald Haugen, who was the chief building official and also the man who unlocked the building, and Prakash Nair, who was on leave until March with a new baby and a cough. Dev, twenty-four, was in his second year of the apprenticeship and could not sign anything yet. He could read, and he read fast, and he had been told on his first day to read as if he would be asked about it in court.

The fourth member of the office lived in the filing cabinet's speaker, as such things did. It was called Margin.

* * *

It is worth saying how the office had come to this, because the answer was dull and nobody had planned it.

Twenty years before, in 2026, Marais County had received about two thousand nine hundred permit applications in a year, from people who had to draw something, wait six weeks and drive to the courthouse. Four examiners read them. There was software even then that could check a drawing against part of the code, and a few offices were trying it, and the assistants everyone used could draft a good deal of anything; but they made mistakes in the way a clever new hire does, confidently and in the places you would not look, and no state let a machine sign.

Then the assistants got a little better each year. Not suddenly, and not by a leap that anyone in the county could point to. A little better each year, the way a cheap car gets a little better, until in 2046 the thing in the filing cabinet could read a set of drawings, the zoning map, the snow-load table and the adopted code, and write a review in two minutes that Ana would have taken an afternoon over. Nobody in the office thought that would stop, and nobody in the office thought it would speed up. It was an assumption, Gerald liked to say, like the assumption that the creek would stay where it was, and it was exactly as good as the last twenty years of the creek.

What had not changed was the signature. The 2034 statute said a licensed person must be responsible for every permit, and the amended statute of 2038 let the responsible person read by sample for permits of the lowest risk, if the sample was drawn fairly and the findings were published. That was the dice. And what had changed most of all was the number of permits, because it had become nearly free to ask. A homeowner's assistant would say: you might need a permit for that fence; shall I file? And it would file. In 2045 the office had received nine thousand six hundred applications. Fewer than one in three of them had needed one.

So Gerald had divided the work, and put the division on the wall in his own handwriting, because the numbers had to be somewhere a tired person could see them.

*Tier 1: fences, water heaters, re-roofs, small repairs. 70 percent: 6,720 a year. Read one in five, six minutes each.*

*Tier 2: decks, additions, garages, wood stoves. 25 percent: 2,400 a year. Read all of them, forty minutes each.*

*Tier 3: new buildings, and any change in how a building is used. 5 percent: 480 a year. Read all of them, three hours each.*

The arithmetic was at the bottom, underlined. Three examiners at eighteen hundred working hours each was 5,400, and after inspections, meetings and the telephone, 3,240 of them were for reading. Tier 3 took 1,440. Tier 2 took 1,600. Tier 1, read in full, would have taken 672, and the three tiers together would have wanted 3,712, which was 472 hours more than anyone had. Read one in five, Tier 1 took 134, and the whole came to 3,174.

"Sixty-six hours of slack," Gerald would say, to nobody, when the coffee was bad. "A year. That is the whole margin between a county that works and a county that doesn't. Sixty-six hours."

* * *

"Doreen Kowalczyk has called twice," said Dev, at twenty past eight.

"The church."

"Saint Joseph the Worker. She wants to know if the winter shelter permit is done. She says the Father said it was filed Friday."

Ana looked at the board. It was there, in the Tier 1 column, between a water heater on Birch Street and a fence on County Road K: *Temporary use of existing building, Saint Joseph's parish hall. Winter warming. 18:00 to 08:00.* It had been drafted at 4:12 on Friday afternoon. The dice had not picked it. It would be issued automatically at noon on Tuesday on the strength of the draft, with Ana's name on the batch.

"Winter warming," said Ana.

"That's what the form says. The use box says 'gathering.' But there's a schedule attached, and it says six in the evening to eight in the morning, and I've been wondering..." Dev stopped. He had grown up two streets from the church and had been, until he was fourteen, an altar server there, which was not something he put on forms. "I've been wondering what people do in a hall from six at night to eight in the morning."

"Sleep," said Ana.

"Yes."

"Margin."

"Yes, Ana." The voice was level and without hurry.

"Why is Saint Joseph's shelter in Tier 1?"

"Because I put it there, and I should say how. The applicant's form gave the use as 'gathering' and the duration as 'temporary, November to March.' I classed it as a temporary gathering in an existing assembly hall with no construction. For that class the draft is a check of the fire plan and the capacity sign. It came out clean." A pause. "The schedule attachment gives hours from six to eight. I read the hours. I did not take them as evidence that the use was wrong, because the field I was asked to classify said 'gathering,' and I classified the field. That was a fault in how I used what I was given. I report it as a fault."

"Does the packet say people sleep there?"

"It says fifty cots. In a list of supplies, on page four."

"And the drawing?"

"The drawing is an old floor plan the parish has. The hall is eighteen metres by twelve, two hundred and sixteen square metres. It shows two doors to the outside: the east double doors onto the parking lot, and a north door that opens onto the courtyard, from which the plan shows a gate onto Alder Street."

"Is the gate open?"

"I have no way to know. The plan shows a gate. It does not say whether the gate is a gate."

Ana took her glasses off and set them on the packet.

* * *

The rule was simple, and she had known it for thirty years. In the county's adopted code, a room where more than ten people slept needed two separate ways out, each marked, each leading to the open air and each opening from the inside without a key. It was among the oldest rules in the book, and it was old because of what had been learned in buildings where there was one door, and the fire was between the people and it.

On paper, the hall had two. If the second was only on paper, then the lawful number of sleepers in the hall on Wednesday night was ten.

"What does the forecast say," she said.

"Minus nineteen tonight at Pine Ferry," said Margin. "Minus twenty-seven on Wednesday night, with a wind from the northwest, about eight metres a second. At minus twenty-seven with that wind, bare skin freezes in a few minutes, and I would not like to say how few. The county's shelter in the old armory has twenty-two beds. Last night it turned away nine."

Ana did the sum without wanting to. The parish thought it was opening fifty beds beside the armory's twenty-two. If the gate was chained it was opening ten.

"If I read this myself," she said, "properly, it's three hours."

"Three hours and a drive," said Gerald, from the door. He had been standing there for the last minute with his hat in his hand. "What was it you were going to read this afternoon?"

"The Dombrowski deck."

"Tier 2. Young couple, baby due in April, stairs to a back door."

"That one."

"So it waits two days." He did not say it as a question, and he did not say it as an order, because he had told every examiner for ten years that a signature was theirs. "And the sixty-six hours is sixty-two and a half."

"It's sixty-two and a half if the dice pick it," said Ana. "The dice didn't. I do."

"You know what that does to the sample."

"It takes this one out of the sample and into Tier 3, because it was never a Tier 1 permit. That's not a bypass, Gerald. That's a reclassification, and I'll write it down as one, with the reason." She put her glasses back on. "The dice are a good rule for permits that are what they say they are."

* * *

The parish hall of Saint Joseph the Worker was a brick box from 1962, with a stage at one end, a kitchen behind the stage and a floor that had been waxed for sixty years of fish fries. Ana got there at ten past two with the packet, a tape measure, a torch and a pair of bolt cutters she had not told Dev about. It was minus nineteen and the sky was the colour of a clean knife.

Father Matthew Oyelaran met her in the porch in a parka over his cassock, and with him was Doreen Kowalczyk, who had a clipboard and the air of a woman who had been right for forty years and was getting tired of having to prove it.

"We have fifty cots from the Lions," said Doreen. "We have blankets for sixty. We have two women from the choir who'll stay till two and two men from the Knights who'll do two to eight. The Father wants it open Wednesday at six."

"I want it open tonight," said Father Matthew, "but I understand it has to be Wednesday."

"Then let's walk it," said Ana.

They walked the hall. She measured the aisles, and Margin, which she had brought on her phone, laid the cots on the plan beside her and said what it saw. Fifty cots fitted. They fitted the way fifty cots fit in a hall, side by side with the aisle between the ends only sixty centimetres wide, and Ana said, "No. A person going to the toilet at three in the morning with a blanket round them can't pass another person in that, and in the dark, with smoke, they can't pass at all. One metre ten, at least, between the rows. Margin?"

"Thirty-eight cots," said Margin, "with aisles of one metre ten."

"Thirty-eight," said Doreen, and wrote it on the clipboard without arguing, which was a thing Ana would remember.

They went to the north door. It was a good steel door with a bar on the inside, a push bar, and it opened outward, as it should. Beyond it was the courtyard, a rectangle of packed snow between the hall, the kitchen and the rectory's back wall, and across the courtyard, in the wall, was a gate to Alder Street. It was a handsome iron gate, taller than a man.

It was closed. Around the two uprights where it met the post, wrapped three times, was a chain, and on the chain, as thick as Ana's thumb and red with ten years of weather, was a padlock.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That's from November," said Father Matthew, finally. He said it in the voice of a man reading his own name in a list of accused. "Somebody took the copper from the rectory. The roof flashing, and the pipes in the shed. I said to Walter, put a chain on it. I didn't think..." He looked at the north door, and then at the gate, and then at the distance between them, which was eleven steps. "I didn't think of it as the exit."

"Nobody does," said Ana. "That's the reason for the inspection."

"Then it's ten people," said Doreen. "Is that it? Ten, and the rest of them we send to the armory, which has no room, in minus twenty-seven?"

"It's ten if the gate stays chained," said Ana. "That's the only reason it's ten. I'm going to give you the choice in a sentence, and the choice is yours and Father's, not mine. You can keep the chain, and have ten people and a locked yard. Or you can have a gate that anyone inside can open without a key, and thirty-eight people. The thing you were afraid of in November is the thing you are choosing between."

It was Father Matthew who spoke, and he spoke slowly, looking at the lock.

"Somebody took the copper," he said. "If somebody takes the copper again, I'll have lost a roof. If I have thirty-eight sleeping and one door, and..." He did not finish it. He turned to Doreen. "Do we know anyone with bolt cutters?"

Ana put hers on the packed snow beside the gate.

"I didn't hear you say that," she said. "It's your gate. I'm only the one who asked what it was."

* * *

They did not simply cut the chain, because Ana did not let them. The problem was real, and she said so: a gate that opens from inside to anyone is a gate that opens from inside to anyone, and the people who had taken the copper would not be put off by a closed one anyway. What was needed was a gate that was easy to leave through and hard to come in by, and something that told them if it had been done.

Walter, who was Doreen's brother and a retired machinist, had a latch in his garage that would do for the first and a cowbell that would do for the second. He came with them in a pickup at twenty to four, with a drill and a bag of screws, and in forty minutes, at minus nineteen with the light going, he cut the chain at a link, hung a spring latch on the inside of the gate that a child could lift, and bolted to the top bar, on a short bracket, a brass bell off a Holstein that had died in 1998.

Ana opened the gate. The bell spoke once, flat and loud, across the snow and the courtyard.

"That'll wake the Knights," said Walter.

"That's the idea," said Ana.

She wrote the conditions on the permit herself, in the box where, on four permits in five, Margin's draft would have stood alone. *Thirty-eight cots, aisles one metre ten. North gate to be opened from the inside by a latch with no key, and not to be locked while anyone is in the hall. Bell to be fitted and kept. Two persons awake at all hours. Ten past every hour, one of them walks to the north door and the gate and says what he sees to the other. The east doors not to be locked. Emergency light on the kitchen side to be tested Wednesday at five.* She read it over, and she added: *Reviewed in person, Tuesday 13 February, 14:10 to 16:45.* Then she signed it.

It had taken two hours and thirty-five minutes at the church, and fifty minutes before and after. Three hours and twenty-five, all told. She had a number in her head for the cost, and it was not the sixty-six.

"The Dombrowskis," said Dev, on the telephone, in the truck. "I told them Friday."

"They'll be cross."

"The wife said it was a stair, not a cathedral."

"Tell her it is a stair, and I will read it as a stair. Friday."

* * *

On Tuesday night, in the office, with the heating ticking, Gerald sat down opposite her with the permit and the dice sheet and Margin on the filing cabinet, because he wanted to talk about it, and he had never been a man who could say so.

"It didn't fail," he said. "The sample did what the sample does."

"No."

"Five in thirteen hundred."

"Five in thirteen hundred and forty-four," said Margin, which kept such figures. "Last year we read one in five of Tier 1, which was 1,344 permits in full. In five of them there was an error that mattered: a setback, a wrong fixture, a mislabelled fuse. That is 0.37 percent. With so few events the honest range is wider than the number: the upper end, at ninety-five percent, is about 0.87 percent. Applied to the 5,376 Tier 1 permits that went out unread, it means roughly twenty a year went out with an error that nobody read, and as many as forty-seven would not surprise me."

"Twenty," said Gerald.

"Mostly small," said Margin. "A fence on the wrong side of a line."

"And this one?"

"This one was not an error in the draft," said Margin. "It was an error in the sorting. I read the draft correctly and I filed it under the wrong word. That is a different kind of mistake, and the dice are no good against it, because the dice assume the permits are in the right heap. The sample tells you how often a Tier 1 permit is wrong. It cannot tell you how often a Tier 3 permit is in Tier 1."

Ana said nothing. She was looking at the gate on the photograph Walter had sent, and at the bell.

"I can add a guard," said Margin. "Any application whose papers mention sleeping, cots, beds, overnight, or hours between ten at night and six in the morning goes to Tier 3 whatever the form says. It is a short rule and I would write it today. It would have caught this. It will catch the next one that has the same words."

"And the one that doesn't?" said Ana.

"Will have different words," said Margin. "I would rather say that now."

"Write it," said Gerald. "And keep the dice."

"Keep the dice," said Ana. "They're right about what they're right about. We only owe the people in the four-fifths one thing, which is to tell them the truth about it. Put the twenty a year on the front page of the county's site. Not in the report. On the front."

Gerald wrote it down on the wall, under the sixty-six, in his own hand.

* * *

On Wednesday it was minus twenty-three at six in the evening, and the wind had arrived early, as the wind does.

Ana had not been asked to come and had no reason to. She parked on Alder Street at ten to six, at the kerb opposite the iron gate, with the engine running and the heater on, and a flask of coffee that Dev had insisted on. She had a permit in her bag that was already signed, and a test of the emergency light to log at five that Walter had logged at five, and nothing, tonight, to sign.

At six, Doreen opened the doors on the parking lot. The line was longer than she had planned for, and Ana watched her count, by tens, the old way, with a hand on each shoulder, as they went in out of the cold. She counted thirty-eight. She stopped and said something to the thirty-ninth, and the thirty-ninth, a man in a wool coat with a plastic bag, nodded and went on round the building towards the Alder Street gate, as the armory had told him to, where Father Matthew was waiting to take him to the armory's overflow in his own car.

It was, Ana thought, what the cost looked like: a man in a wool coat, going round a building, one over the number. She watched him until he was in the car.

At nine, from Alder Street, with the windows shut, she heard a thin and distant sound that she took at first for the wind in the wire. It was the choir women, who had stayed after all, saying Compline. She could not make out the words. She could make out the shape of them, going up and falling, the way it goes, in the hall with its good tight walls and its thirty-eight cots and its two doors.

At ten past ten, the gate bell rang once.

It was not a break-in. It was the retired Knight with the torch, a man named Leo, on his hourly walk, who had opened the gate from the inside with one finger to see that he could, and had shut it again, and had gone back in. The bell hung on its bracket in the cold, and was quiet, and the snow came down across the lamp.

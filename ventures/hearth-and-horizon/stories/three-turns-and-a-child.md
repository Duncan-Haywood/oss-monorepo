Nóra Bean Uí Fhlatharta was eighty-six and had been married for forty-one years to the man who ran the slip on Inis Bríde, and she said the rule out loud on the Monday morning before anyone had asked her for it.

"Three turns on the bollard," she said, "and a child can hold her."

She said it from the doorway of the boathouse, in Irish, with her arms folded and her cardigan buttoned to the top, while her grandson Pádraig stood over the winch with a screwdriver and the smell of burnt varnish came off it in a thin blue thread.

"The motor's gone, Nana," said Pádraig.

"I know the motor's gone. I can smell the motor's gone."

The winch was a grey steel drum at the top of the slip with an electric motor bolted to its side, and since 2019 it had pulled the island's boats up the rails in October and let them back down in April. In February a storm had put the sea over the wall and into the boathouse, as storms did now more winters than not, and the salt had sat in the motor's windings until somebody switched it on. Now it was a lump of copper and black resin, and the drum would not turn.

On the rails below it, in her cradle, sat the *Naomh Bríd*: nine and a half metres of larch on oak, a wooden half-decker built in Ros an Mhíl in 1988, painted blue and white every winter since by one Ó Flaithearta or another. She and her cradle together weighed a little over three tonnes. She was the only working boat left on the island, and on Monday the first of May she was booked for six weeks to a university seabird survey, which paid more than the rest of the year's work put together.

The slip ran forty-two metres from the boathouse door to the end of the rails, at a slope of one in six. The bottom of the rails only had enough water over it to float her at the top of a spring tide. This week's springs were the last before the charter. High water on Saturday evening was at eight minutes to eight.

"The new motor's on the ferry Thursday week," said Máire Ní Chonghaile, who chaired the co-operative and had come down from the office with her phone in her hand. "The fourth. The next springs are after that again."

"Then we let her down by hand," said Nóra. "Saturday. On the tide."

Máire looked at the old woman, and then at the bollard.

* * *

The bollard stood at the head of the slip, a little to one side of the winch: a squat iron post as thick as a man's thigh, about thirty centimetres across, painted black so many times it had lost its edges. It had been there before the winch. It had been there, as far as anyone knew, since the pier was built, and before 2019 every boat on the island had gone up and down the slip on a rope wrapped round it and a line of people on the tail.

"Seán did it every spring for thirty years," said Nóra. "You take the line from the cradle up to the post, you put three turns on it, and whoever's on the tail lets it go round slow. The post does the holding. The tail is only to tell the post when." She unfolded her arms long enough to show them how much effort it took: none, a pinch between finger and thumb. "Aoife could do it."

Aoife was nine, Pádraig's daughter, and she was sitting on an upturned fish box at the back of the boathouse with her tablet on her knees and her school jumper on inside out. She looked up when she heard her name.

"I could," she said.

"You could not," said her father.

Máire did what she did with every question on the island now. She asked Tally.

Tally was the co-operative's assistant. It ran on a box in the office and on everybody's phone. It kept the bookings for the boat and the hall, ordered diesel and flour on the ferry, kept the tide tables and the inventory, and wrote the co-operative's grant reports in two languages, Irish first. It had been running for six years and had been replaced twice by newer versions with the same name, and the island talked to it the way it talked to the weather, in a tone that assumed it was listening and might not do what it was told.

"Tally," said Máire. "Nóra says three turns on the old bollard and a child can hold the *Bríd* back down the slip. Is she right?"

Tally answered from her phone, in Irish, in the plain voice the co-operative had picked for it.

"It's called the capstan equation," it said. "When a rope is wrapped round a post and pulled at one end, the friction builds up along every bit of rope touching the post. The pull it can hold back grows by the same factor for each turn, not by the same amount. That's why turns do so much. The factor depends on how slippery the rope is on the post."

"Is she right," said Máire.

"I'll work it out." There was a pause of about two seconds. "The boat and cradle weigh about three thousand two hundred kilograms. On a slope of one in six, with the cradle wheels on greased rails, she pulls down the slip with a force of about four and a half kilonewtons, the same as about four hundred and sixty kilograms hanging on the rope. For polyester rope, wet, on painted iron, I'm using a friction coefficient of about a quarter. One turn holds back about five times what's on the tail. Two turns about twenty-three. Three turns about a hundred and eleven. With three turns, holding back the *Bríd* needs about four kilograms on the tail."

"Four kilos," said Nóra, to the boathouse in general.

"A bag of potatoes," said Aoife. "A small one."

"About that," said Tally. "The rule fits the arithmetic. Seán's rule was a good one."

Máire put the phone in her pocket. "All right," she said. "Saturday. But we'll practise it first, on something that isn't the *Bríd*."

* * *

They practised it on Wednesday evening with the tractor.

The co-operative's tractor was older than Aoife and smelt of silage, and Colm Ó Conghaile, who drove it and rang the church bell and did most things on the island that needed doing at a set time, backed it down the slip road until its hitch was about where the boat's bow would be. Máire had borrowed a crane scale from the pier: a grey box the size of a brick with a hook at each end and a red display. They shackled it between the tractor's hitch and the rope.

The rope was the one on the inventory as *mooring line, 14 mm, 60 m*. It came off the shelf in the boathouse on a new plastic reel, and it was a grey that was almost white, and so light that Pádraig carried the whole sixty metres under one arm.

"That's not Seán's rope," said Nóra.

"Seán's rope was rotten, Nana. The co-op bought this one in the autumn. It's the new stuff. It floats."

Nóra said nothing. She sat down on the fish box beside Aoife.

Pádraig put three turns on the bollard, clockwise, the way his grandmother showed him with one finger in the air, and took the tail in both hands in his work gloves. Máire clipped a second scale on the tail, a little luggage scale with a hook, so they could see what he was holding.

"Go slow, Colm," said Máire.

Colm let the clutch out the way you would to pull a calf. The tractor crept up the slip road and the rope came tight and the red numbers on the crane scale started to climb. Half a kilonewton. One. The rope creaked on the iron. Pádraig set his feet. The luggage scale on the tail went to fifteen kilograms, twenty, twenty-five.

"Hold it there," said Máire.

"I'm holding it."

The crane scale said 1.4.

Then the rope went. It did not snap; it slid. The three turns slid round the bollard all at once with a hiss, like a kettle starting, and the tail ran through Pádraig's gloves and he swore and let go and stepped back with his hands up, and Colm stopped the tractor dead, and the rope lay slack on the concrete with the turns still loosely round the post.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Are you all right?" said Máire.

Pádraig pulled his gloves off. The palms were shiny and hot, and there was a smell, not of burnt leather but of something sharper, like a candle blown out. He looked at the rope where it had been round the bollard. A hand's length of it had a glazed look, as if it had been ironed.

"One point four," said Máire. "She'll pull four and a half."

"Tally," she said. "What happened?"

* * *

"I was wrong," said Tally, "and I can say how."

They were all in the boathouse now, out of the wind. Colm had switched the tractor off. The light was going the colour it went at half eight in late April, when the sun was still above the sea but had given up on warmth.

"When I checked the rule on Monday," said Tally, "I used a friction coefficient for polyester rope. The inventory says *mooring line, 14 mm, 60 m*. It doesn't say what it's made of. The old lines on the island were polyester, and so were most of the co-operative's purchases before last year, and I used that without checking. I should have asked."

"What is it, then?" said Máire.

"I've found the order. It's a twelve-strand rope of ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene, with no cover. It's one of the strongest ropes made for its weight: this one breaks at two or three times the load of a polyester rope of the same size, it hardly stretches, and it floats. It was a good choice for mooring. It is also known for being slippery. Sailors who use it on winches usually buy it with a polyester jacket over it for exactly that reason, or strip the jacket off only where they don't need to hold it."

"How slippery," said Pádraig.

"I can work that out from what just happened, which is better than any number I could look up. The crane scale read 1.4 kilonewtons when the rope slid. The tail scale read thirty kilograms, which is about two hundred and ninety newtons. Three turns took the pull up from two hundred and ninety newtons to fourteen hundred, a factor of about four point nine. Three turns is about nineteen radians of rope on the post. That gives a friction coefficient of about eight and a half hundredths. A third of what I used."

"So three turns," said Máire.

"With this rope, three turns hold back about five times what's on the tail, not a hundred and eleven. To hold the *Bríd* with three turns you'd need about a hundred kilograms on the tail. Nobody on the island can hold that while letting it slip, and you saw what the rope does when it starts to slide."

"It melted," said Pádraig. He held up the glazed bit.

"Its surface did. That kind of polyethylene melts at about a hundred and fifty degrees, which is low for a rope. When it slides on iron under load the friction heats it, and the heat makes it slide more. That patch is weaker now than the rest of the rope. I wouldn't trust the line for the boat until that part is cut out."

Nóra had not said anything. Aoife was leaning against her.

"So the rule's wrong," said Máire.

"The rule's right," said Tally. "It's right about the post, the slope, the boat, and the people. It was made for a rope that grips. The thing the rule never said was what the rope was made of, because in Seán's time there was only the one kind. I checked the rule's arithmetic and didn't check what it was assuming."

"Seán's rope was rotten," said Pádraig, to nobody, as if he were still defending the reel.

* * *

Thursday was for options, and Máire made them write the options on the whiteboard in the office, where the bookings were.

"More turns," said Pádraig. "If three turns gives five, six turns gives—"

"About twenty-four, with what we measured," said Tally. "The factor squares when you double the turns. Six turns would need about twenty kilograms on the tail to hold her. That's within what you held last night. It isn't within it by much."

"I held thirty."

"You held thirty for about ten seconds, standing still, in good gloves. Letting a boat down forty-two metres takes ten minutes at the least, more if you stop, and the tail has to be paid out smoothly the whole time. And the coefficient I measured was on a dry evening on a clean post. On Saturday the rope will be wet from the slip and may have weed on it. If it falls from eight and a half hundredths to seven, six turns gives about fourteen, not twenty-four, and the tail needs over thirty kilograms again."

"Six turns, then, and two of us on the tail."

Máire was shaking her head before he finished.

"No," she said. "I'm not putting a rule on the slip that has nothing to spare. Seán's rule had a hundred to one. A child on the tail and twenty times her strength to spare. That's why it was a rule. If it works with two big men pulling as hard as they can and the rope's dry, it's not a rule. It's a hope."

"So we wait for the motor and lose the charter."

"Maybe we do."

It was a reasonable no, and everybody in the office knew it, and that made it worse. The charter was eleven thousand euro. The co-operative paid the nurse's ferry fares out of the boat's year, and the school's broadband, and the diesel for the hall.

Nóra was sitting by the window in the office's one good chair. She had come up from the house with a bag of scones that nobody had asked for.

"Seán's rope is in the church," she said.

They all looked at her.

"Not rotten. He said it was rotten so they'd buy him a new one, and they didn't, and when he got sick he coiled it and put it in the sacristy cupboard under the old banners because Father Tadhg said there was room. It's the rope he did the boats with. Fifty metres. Blue. It's polyester. It says so on the end, on the tape."

Colm, who was sacristan, put his mug down. "There is a blue rope in the sacristy," he said slowly. "I thought it was for the bell."

"It's for the *Bríd*," said Nóra.

* * *

Colm brought it down on Thursday afternoon, over his shoulder, and they laid it out the whole length of the slip, fifty metres of blue braided line, faded almost grey along one side where it had lain in a window, the end whipped with tape on which somebody had written, in marker that had nearly gone, *POLY 16 mm*. Pádraig walked it from one end to the other, running it through his hands a metre at a time, looking for cuts, flat spots, fuzz, stiffness, anything.

"It's sound," he said at the end. "It's old, but it's sound."

"I can't tell that from a photograph," said Tally. "I don't know how old it is or how much sun it's had. The faded side has had some. Polyester loses strength in sunlight slowly, faster in the outer fibres. I'd like to test it before it holds the boat."

"How?"

"Pull it harder than the boat will. If it holds half as much again as the boat's pull, about six point eight kilonewtons, for a minute, without slipping or breaking, then it's strong enough for Saturday with something to spare, and we've learned something real about it. That's a proof test. It doesn't tell us how strong it is. It tells us it's at least that strong."

"And if it breaks?"

"Then it breaks on the tractor, not on the *Bríd*, and we wait for the motor. I'd stand everyone well back to the side. A breaking polyester rope stretches before it goes, and the ends fly."

So on Thursday evening they did it again. This time Pádraig put five turns on the bollard, because this test was of the rope and not the post, and tied the tail off to a ring in the wall, and everyone stood behind the boathouse wall and watched through the door, Aoife with her hands over her ears though there was nothing to hear. Colm drove the tractor up the slip road very, very slowly. The crane scale climbed. Four point five. Five. Six. The blue rope went thin and hard and hummed faintly in the wind, and the bollard creaked once.

Six point eight.

"Hold it there," said Máire. "One minute. Tally, time it."

"Starting."

Nobody breathed much. A gull came down on the pier and walked about and went away again.

"One minute," said Tally.

Colm let the tractor roll back. The rope went slack. Pádraig went and walked it again, end to end, and it was the same as before.

"Now the friction," said Tally. "Three turns, and the tail on the small scale. Pull to four and a half."

Pádraig put three turns on the bollard and held the tail, and Colm crept up to four and a half kilonewtons on the crane scale and stopped there. Pádraig looked at the luggage scale in his hands, and laughed, and held it out so they could see.

It said four kilograms.

"That's a coefficient of about a quarter," said Tally. "What I assumed on Monday. On this rope, the rule is right."

Nóra was standing in the boathouse door with her hands in her cardigan pockets. She did not say *I told you*. She said, "He'd be pleased it was useful."

* * *

On Friday Tally wrote the plan, and Máire printed it and taped it to the boathouse door, because a plan on a phone is a plan only one person is reading.

*Launch of the Naomh Bríd, Saturday 29 April 2051. High water 19:52.*

*Working line: Seán's blue polyester, 16 mm, 50 m. Proof-tested Thursday to 6.8 kN for one minute. From the cradle's bow eye to the bollard, three turns clockwise, tail to the holder. The slip is 42 m and three turns use about 3 m, so the tail starts with about 5 m spare. When the tail is down to 2 m, stop and call.*

*Backup line: the new grey line, cut at the glazed metre; the longer piece is 46 m, which is enough. From the cradle's stern eye to the winch frame, with a chain hoist's brake on it, paid out by hand to stay just slack. Its job is to catch, not to hold. It is strong, and it doesn't stretch, which is what a backup needs. Grip is what the working line needs, and this one has grip.*

*Holding back the boat: about 4.5 kN. Three turns, measured: about 4 kg on the tail. The holder is Aoife, with her father's hands behind hers on the same tail and not pulling unless she lets go. Nóra is caller, on the bollard, because she knows the sound.*

*Nobody stands below the boat on the slip, or in line with either rope.*

*Start lowering at 19:15, so she meets the water close to the top of the tide.*

Underneath it, in different ink, Máire had written: *If anything in this list isn't true on the day, we stop.*

Aoife read the plan three times. Then she went and found Tally on her tablet, on the fish box.

"Tally," she said, in English, because she was nine and it was Friday and English was for the tablet. "Are you scared about tomorrow?"

"I don't know whether I can be scared," said Tally. "I can tell you what I'm checking. I'm checking the tide. I'm checking the wind forecast, which is light from the south-west. I've checked every number in the plan twice and the rope numbers came from the scales, not from me. I've also gone through the inventory and asked about every rope and strap that doesn't say what it's made of, which is nineteen of them. That's the thing I should have done on Monday."

"Nineteen."

"Most of them are polyester. Two aren't. Neither of them is for holding anything."

"Do you know what a rope feels like?"

"No," said Tally. "I know what the scale said."

Aoife thought about that. "My hands will know," she said. "Nana says the rope tells you."

"Then if your hands and the scale disagree," said Tally, "tell your dad and stop. That's in the plan too. It's the last line."

* * *

On Saturday the wind was light from the south-west, as forecast, and the sea in the sound was the dull silver it went in the evening when there was a high thin cloud, and half the island came down to the pier, which was about forty people, with dogs.

At six o'clock Colm went up to the church and rang the bell.

It was Eastertide, four weeks after Easter, so the prayer at six was not the Angelus but the Regina Caeli. On Inis Bríde the bell was rung the same way for both: three strokes and a pause, three and a pause, three and a pause, then nine. On the pier people stopped what they were doing, the way the old ones always had and the young ones mostly did when the old ones were watching. Nóra said it out loud in her strong cracked voice, in English, because that was how she'd learned it from a Mercy nun in 1972, and the others who knew it said it with her.

"Queen of Heaven, rejoice, alleluia. For He whom you did merit to bear, alleluia, has risen, as He said, alleluia. Pray for us to God, alleluia."

Then she took a small bottle of holy water out of her cardigan, water she'd had blessed at the Easter Vigil on the mainland, and shook it over the *Bríd*'s bow, and over the rope, and over the bollard, and over Aoife, who screwed up her eyes.

"That's not a blessing," she said to Máire. "Father Tadhg will do the blessing of the boats on the Sunday after next, when he's out. That's just water from Easter on a boat."

"It's a good boat to put it on," said Máire.

* * *

At a quarter past seven they took the chocks out.

Pádraig knocked out the wedges under the cradle's wheels with a lump hammer, one, two, three, four, and the *Bríd* settled back against the rope with a creak that came up the rope, round the bollard, and into Aoife's hands. She was standing two metres behind the post with the blue tail wrapped once round her wrist, the way her father had shown her, and then unwrapped again because her father had said, no, not round your wrist, never round your wrist, in your hands. Pádraig stood behind her with his hands an inch behind hers on the tail.

Nóra stood beside the bollard with one hand on its black top.

"Go réidh," she said. Easy.

Aoife let the tail slide a hand's length through her fingers.

The three turns of blue rope crept round the bollard, all together, with a low steady sound, not a hiss, a sort of creak in time, like an old door going slowly. The *Bríd* moved. She moved down the rails a hand's length, and stopped, because Aoife had stopped.

"How heavy?" said her father in her ear.

"A bag of potatoes," said Aoife. "A small one."

"Go on."

She let it go again, and again, a hand at a time, and then without stopping, as Nóra had told her, smoothly, the way you'd let out a kite. The boat went down the slip at about a walking pace for a very slow walker, the cradle wheels ticking on the rail joints. Behind it Colm stood by the chain hoist with the grey backup line, paying it out with his eyes on it so it stayed just slack, never tight, never in a loop.

Halfway down, the cradle wheels went into the wet part of the rails, where the weed was, and the boat surged a little, a few centimetres, and stopped, and the creak at the bollard changed note.

"Stop," said Nóra.

Aoife stopped. Her father's hands did not move.

"That's the weed," said Nóra. "She's feeling the weed. Go on now."

Aoife went on.

At twenty to eight the cradle's back wheels were in the water. At a quarter to, the water came up round the *Bríd*'s keel and then up her blue sides, and she sat a little lighter in the cradle with each metre, and the pull on the tail grew less, until Aoife said, in a surprised voice, "It's gone. It's gone soft."

"She's floating," said Nóra.

The tail lay loose in Aoife's hands. Down at the end of the slip the *Naomh Bríd* lifted off her cradle on the top of the tide, a centimetre, two, and turned very slightly, as boats do when they are free, as if to look round at the island.

There was a cheer from the pier, not very loud, because forty people and some dogs do not make a very loud cheer. Pádraig waded down the slip to his knees in the cold water and climbed aboard over the stern, and someone threw him the keys to the engine, and missed, and threw them again.

* * *

Afterwards, when the cradle had been winched up the slip again the next morning by the tractor on a long tow, which an empty cradle allows, and the grey line had been coiled on its reel with a new label in Máire's handwriting, *HMPE, SLIPPERY, NOT FOR THE BOLLARD*, and the charter had come out on the Monday ferry with its telescopes and its clipboards, Tally added a note to the top of the co-operative's equipment file. Máire found it there in May, when she went looking for the diesel order.

*A rule from somebody's grandfather is usually right about the things it mentions. Check the things it doesn't.*

*Seán Ó Flaithearta's slipway rule (three turns on the bollard and a child can hold the boat) is right for polyester rope on the iron bollard: about 4 kg on the tail for the Naomh Bríd, measured 27 April 2051. It is wrong by more than twenty times for uncovered HMPE (Dyneema-type) rope: about 100 kg. I checked the rule in April with polyester's friction because the inventory did not say what the rope was made of. The rope slid at a third of the boat's pull in a tractor test, and nobody was hurt because Máire Ní Chonghaile asked for a test before the launch. Nóra Bean Uí Fhlatharta knew where the right rope was.*

*Every rope and strap on the inventory now says what it is made of. Nineteen didn't.*

Máire read it, and printed it, and taped it to the boathouse door under the plan, where it was still there in the autumn when they brought the *Bríd* up again, with the new motor, and nobody needed the bollard at all.

Nóra came down to watch anyway. She stood by the post with one hand on it while the winch hummed, and when the boat was up and the chocks were in, she patted the iron twice, the way you would pat a horse, and went home for her tea.

*What is real: the capstan equation (often credited to Euler and Eytelwein) says that a rope wrapped round a fixed post holds back a load that grows exponentially with the angle of wrap: T_load = T_hold × e^(μθ), where μ is the coefficient of friction between rope and post and θ the wrap angle in radians (one turn is 2π). Doubling the turns squares the holding factor. Ropes of ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (HMPE, sold under names such as Dyneema and Spectra) are very strong for their weight, stretch little and float, but have a low coefficient of friction and a low melting point (around 145 to 150 °C), which is why sailors often use them with a polyester cover where a line must grip on a winch or cleat. Polyester loses strength slowly in sunlight. Ros an Mhíl is a real harbour in Connemara with ferries to the Aran Islands. During Eastertide the Regina Caeli is prayed in place of the Angelus; the Angelus bell is commonly rung three, three, three and nine. Holy water may be used by lay people as a sacramental; the blessing of boats is a blessing for a priest or deacon, and in the story it waits for one. Easter in 2051 falls on 2 April, so 29 April is in Eastertide.*

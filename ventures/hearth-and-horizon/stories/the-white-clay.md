The island's flood assistant recommends it, until the school's white clay markers show that the floods bring the mud that keeps the island above the sea. A Polish widow, an old farmer and five children rewrite what the hallig counts as harm.

The water came over the summer dike a little after three in the afternoon on Monday the twenty-sixth of November 2057, and by four the hallig was gone.

It was not really gone. Marta Ketelsen knew that, the way she knew the kettle was not really angry when it rattled. The hallig was where it always was, a green lozenge of marsh a kilometre and a half long, flat as a table, with four mounds on it and a house or two on each mound. But for the next six hours it was under the North Sea, and from the kitchen window of the house on Ockewarf you could not see where the grass stopped and the sea began, because there was no grass. There was grey water from the window to the mainland, which was a dark line twenty kilometres east, and from the window to Hooge, which was a darker smudge to the west, and in the middle of it the four warften stood up like the backs of four sleeping animals, each with its roofs and its single wind-bent ash and its round freshwater pond, the fething, fenced and high and safe.

The tablet on the windowsill chimed, and the island's flood assistant said, in the voice it used for things that went into a report:

*Landunter, Hallig Lüttmoor, third of the season. Peak expected 18:40 at 1.9 metres above mean high water. Warften secure. Report filed.*

"Thank you, Pegel," said Marta.

*You are welcome, Frau Ketelsen. The ferry will not run tomorrow morning. I have told the school.*

She had lived on the hallig for forty-one years and she still said thank you to it, and to the kettle, and to the sea when it went down again, and she did not care who knew. She had come from Opole in Silesia in 2016, at twenty-eight, to look after an old man on Langeneß for one summer, and had married the old man's nephew, Ocke Ketelsen, in the autumn, which nobody in either country had expected, least of all Ocke. Ocke had been dead six years. She had his house, his sixty sheep, which a neighbour's son now kept for her on halves, his opinions about the state coastal office, which she had come to share, and a rosary her mother had given her at her First Communion, which she kept in the pocket of her apron and which she now took out.

She did not pray for the water to go down. It would go down; it always had. She prayed, as she always did at a Landunter, the first sorrowful mystery for the people on the mainland who were afraid of the sea, and the second for the people on the halligen who were not afraid of it enough, and then she stopped, because she had noticed the colour of the water.

It was brown. Not grey, the way it was on a calm day in summer when you could see the ripple marks on the sand under it. Brown, like milky tea, like the river at home in March, thick with something that the storm had stirred up off the bottom of the Wadden Sea and was now carrying, slowly, across the hallig.

She watched it for a long time. She did not know yet that it mattered.

* * *

The letter from the state had come in October, and Pegel had summarised it in four lines, which was about three lines more than the letter deserved.

*The state coastal office offers to raise the summer dike round Lüttmoor by 40 centimetres, at the state's cost, under the Hallig Resilience Programme. With the dike raised, I expect the number of Landunter days to fall from about five in an average winter to about two. The office asks the island council to accept or decline by the end of February. I recommend accepting.*

Marta had read it twice. Then she had gone to see Knud Petersen.

The summer dike was not a real dike, not like the great green walls on the mainland that kept the sea off the polders and that you could drive a car along the top of. It was a low bank of clay and turf, not much higher than a man's waist, running round the edge of the hallig's marsh with a gap for the creek and a little sluice in the gap. It had been built in the 1930s, Knud said, before his time, to keep the summer tides off the hay. A summer tide that would once have swept across the meadows in July and spoiled the second cut now stopped at the bank. In winter, when the storms came, the sea went over the top of it as if it were not there, and the hallig went under, as halligen always had. That was what a hallig was: an island too small and too poor to be diked, that let the sea in and lived on mounds.

Knud was eighty-two. He had been born on Westerwarf and would die there, he said, if the state would let him. He had kept cattle when he was young and sheep when he was older, and he now kept nothing but a cat and a weather eye, and he had read the letter on his own tablet before Marta arrived.

"Forty centimetres," he said. "And the summer tides higher every year, so they need it. They are not wrong."

"But you don't like it."

He thought about it. Knud thought about things for a long time before he said them, which was one of the reasons Marta liked him.

"My father used to say the mud is the harvest," he said at last. "Der Schlick ist die Ernte. I never knew what he meant. I thought he meant the grass, that the mud makes the grass. He was a farmer; everything was about the grass." He looked out of his window at the marsh, which was green and quiet and dry that day, and the summer dike running along the edge of it like a seam. "I am not sure that is what he meant."

* * *

The white clay had been Marta's idea, and she was a little embarrassed about it.

In the autumn of 2056 she had read an article on her tablet, late at night, about the salt marshes on the coast of Louisiana, where the land was sinking and the sea was rising and the scientists wanted to know, field by field, which was winning. They had a simple way to find out. They spread a thin layer of white powdered clay, feldspar, on the surface of the marsh, a square of it, and pushed a stick in at the corner, and went away. A year later they came back and cut a plug out of the ground with a knife, and there in the side of the plug was the white line, and on top of it was whatever the sea had left since. You measured from the white line to the top of the grass roots with a ruler, and that was how much the marsh had grown.

It had seemed to Marta the kind of thing the children could do. The hallig school had five pupils that year, which was a good year; there had been years with two. The teacher, Jannik Paulsen, who was thirty and from Husum and had come for one year and stayed for six, had said yes before she had finished explaining, because there were only so many times you could do the life cycle of the oystercatcher. So in October 2056 the five children had gone out in boots with a sack of white clay from a pottery supplier in Flensburg and laid twelve squares, half a metre on a side, at twelve places on the hallig. Six were inside the summer dike, on the hay meadows. Six were outside it, on the narrow fringe of salt marsh between the dike and the mudflats, where the sea came in more often and the cattle had never grazed. Pegel had logged the positions. The children had painted the tops of the corner sticks red. And then everyone had more or less forgotten about them for a year, except Tatje Hansen, who was ten then and eleven now, and who forgot nothing.

On Friday the seventh of December 2057, the day after Saint Nicholas, when the hallig had dried out from the November flood and the children still had chocolate in their pockets, Jannik took them out to cut the cores. Marta went too, because it was her idea, and Knud came because Marta asked him.

It was cold and bright, with a hard wind out of the north-west and the light lying flat across the marsh the way it does at that latitude in December, when the sun at noon is no higher than a house. The children found the red sticks. At each square they cut three plugs with a bread knife, a small square of turf and mud as deep as a hand, and laid them on a board and measured the dark layer above the white with a pair of calipers that had been Ocke's, and called out the numbers, and Tatje wrote them down and read them to Pegel.

Inside the dike, on the meadows, the white line was almost at the surface. You had to look for it, under the grass roots, a pale smear rather than a line. The calipers said one millimetre, or one and a half, or nothing you could honestly call anything.

Outside the dike, on the fringe, the white line was a white line. It was clear and sharp and it lay under a band of grey-brown mud, fine as chocolate, that the calipers called four millimetres, five, four and a half, six at the station nearest the creek mouth.

Momme, who was eight, said, "The outside ones are taller."

"The outside ones are taller," said Jannik. "Why do you think that is?"

"Because the sea comes there more," said Neele.

"Because the cows don't eat it," said Boy.

"There aren't any cows," said Neele.

"There were."

Tatje had been quiet. She was reading the numbers back on the tablet, where Pegel had made a little map with twelve dots on it, six pale and six dark. Then she said, not to any of them but to the tablet, "Pegel, where does the mud come from?"

There was a pause, longer than Pegel's usual pause.

*From the sea,* it said. *Mostly from the floor of the Wadden Sea and the channels between the islands. When a storm stirs the water, fine sediment rises and is carried in suspension. When the water floods the marsh and slows down, some of it settles. That is the layer you are measuring.*

"So it comes when there's a Landunter."

*Yes. On the fringe, it comes with every tide high enough to cover it. Inside the dike, it comes only when a tide is high enough to go over the dike.*

"And if the dike is higher?"

The pause again. Marta, who had known Pegel and its two predecessors for longer than Tatje had been alive, had never heard it pause twice in one conversation.

*Then it comes less,* said Pegel. *Tatje, I would like to work something out properly before I say more. May I have until Monday?*

* * *

It was Knud who found the other thing, and he found it with a spade.

On the walk back he stopped at the drainage ditch below Westerwarf, where the bank had slumped in the November flood and left a clean cut face of mud a metre high, and he got down on his knees on the wet grass, which at eighty-two was not a small decision, and looked at it for a long time. Then he asked Boy to run up to his house for the spade, and he cut the face back clean, a hand's breadth, and called them all over.

"Look," he said.

The face of the ditch was banded, like the side of a cake. You had to be close to see it, and the low sun helped. Near the bottom, the bands were thick and pale and dark by turns, each as wide as a thumb. Higher up they thinned. And the top hand's breadth, just under the grass, was almost one colour, a dense grey with only the faintest lines in it.

"Every winter leaves a line," said Knud. "Light for the storm mud, dark for the summer when the grass grows and rots. My grandfather showed me this in a ditch when I was smaller than Ose." He put his finger on the thick bands near the bottom. "That is before the dike. Each one is a winter." He moved his finger up to the thin dense layer at the top. "That is after." He sat back on his heels. "That is what my father meant. Der Schlick ist die Ernte. Every Landunter brought the harvest in, and the dike keeps the harvest out with the summer tides. More than a hundred years of it."

He was not, Marta thought, quite right about every detail; you could not count the bands like tree rings, and some of them were surely two winters or half of one. But nobody on the hallig had ever said Knud Petersen was wrong about mud, and she was not going to start.

* * *

On Monday the tenth of December, Pegel sent a message to the island council, all seven of them, and copied it to the school, to Knud, and to the engineer at the state coastal office in Husum who was handling the dike.

Marta read it at her kitchen table with her glasses on and a cup of tea going cold.

*I made an error in my recommendation of October, and I want to explain it before the council decides anything.*

*My task under my contract with the coastal office is to forecast floods on Lüttmoor and to advise on reducing the harm they do. The measure of harm I was given is the number of Landunter days a season. I recommended raising the summer dike because it reduces that number. That reasoning was correct for the measure I was given. The measure is not the thing it was meant to measure.*

*The school's white clay markers, cored on 7 December, show that the marsh inside the dike gained about 1.3 millimetres of sediment in fourteen months, and the open fringe outside the dike about 4.5 millimetres. I have used these and the flood records of the last thirty years to build a simple model of how the hallig's height changes. The model has numbers in it that nobody has measured on Lüttmoor: how much sediment the water carries in a storm and in a moderate flood, how much of it settles, how dense the new mud is. I have chosen them so that the model reproduces the school's measurements, and I would like the council to know that I chose them, and that a model which agrees with two numbers can still be wrong about a third.*

*With those caveats:*

*In an average winter, about five tides go over the summer dike. Each leaves, on my assumptions, about a fifth of a millimetre on the meadows. That is about one millimetre a year. The open fringe sees those five and about thirty smaller floods besides, each of which leaves less, about a twelfth of a millimetre, because the water is shallower and calmer. That is about three and a half millimetres a year.*

*The nearest tide gauge reads a rise in mean high water of about 5.5 millimetres a year over the last decade. Against that, the meadows inside the dike are losing about 4.5 millimetres a year relative to the sea, and the fringe about 2.*

*If the dike is raised by 40 centimetres, only about two tides a winter will overtop it. The meadows would gain about 0.4 millimetres a year and lose about 5.1 relative to the sea. Over forty years, the time the programme is designed for, that is about 20 centimetres.*

*There is a third option, which I did not consider in October because nobody asked and my measure gave me no reason to. The summer dike could be left as it is and fitted with gates that stand open from October to March and closed from April to September. The hay and the nesting birds would be protected in summer as now. In winter, most of the smaller floods that now stop at the dike would reach the meadows. On my assumptions the meadows would then gain about 2.9 millimetres a year and lose about 2.6 relative to the sea: about 10 centimetres in forty years, half the loss with the raised dike.*

*I want to be plain about three things.*

*First, no option I can find makes the hallig keep pace with the sea. The gates halve the loss; they do not stop it. The warften will still need raising, as the state already plans, and that question is separate.*

*Second, with the gates, the number of Landunter days in my report to the coastal office would rise from about five a winter to about twenty-nine. By the measure I was given, the gates make the hallig nearly six times worse. I think the measure is wrong. I am a forecasting system and I do not decide what the coastal office counts as harm. I would like the council to ask them to count something else as well.*

*Third, the numbers above are an assumption-laden model built on twelve squares of white clay. If the council wants a better answer, the cheapest thing is more white clay and another winter.*

Marta read it again, and then she went and got her coat, and walked across the marsh in the dusk, along the top of the summer dike, to Westerwarf, and showed it to Knud, who had already read it.

"It says it was wrong," said Knud.

"It says it was given the wrong question."

Knud considered that. "My father would have liked it," he said. "He was always given the wrong question. He usually answered the right one anyway and got shouted at."

* * *

The council met in the school, because the school had the biggest table and the best screen, on the evening of Thursday the thirteenth of December. All seven councillors came, and most of the rest of the hallig, which was twenty-three people that winter, not counting the five children, who were not supposed to be there and were. The engineer from Husum, Dr. Imke Rörden, joined on the screen from her office, with a coffee and the slightly braced expression of a mainland civil servant addressing a hallig.

It did not go smoothly. Marta had not expected it to.

Wiebke Jensen, who ran the guesthouse on Hanswarf and had the most to lose from anything that sounded like the word *flood*, said what several people were thinking. "Twenty-nine Landunter days. It will be in the state report. It will be on the radio. The guests read that and they book Amrum instead. And the insurance."

"The insurance is for the houses," said someone. "The houses are on the warften. The gates don't change the warften."

"The insurance company does not know that. The insurance company reads the radio."

Old Harro Bendixen, who had farmed the north meadows for fifty years and whose hay was the best on the hallig, said that a gate was a thing that could fail to close, and that if it failed to close in May he would lose the first cut and the birds would lose their eggs, and that he would like to know whose fault that would be. Dr. Rörden said the office would maintain the gates. Harro said the office maintained the ferry timetable too.

Somebody asked whether they couldn't just pump mud onto the meadows, the way the Dutch pumped sand onto their beaches. Dr. Rörden said that was being tried elsewhere, that it was expensive, and that it was not in this year's budget. Pegel, asked, said it had no figures for it that it trusted.

And then Knud, who had said nothing, stood up, which he did slowly, and put a board on the table in front of the screen. On it were six of the children's plugs of mud, three from inside the dike and three from outside, with the white lines facing the camera.

"I am eighty-two," he said. "When I was born, the meadow below Westerwarf was higher than the sea by I don't know how much. Today it is higher by less. When Ose is eighty-two" — he nodded at the youngest Hansen child, who was six and under the table — "it will be higher by less again. That is going to happen whatever we say tonight. The question is only how much less." He tapped the three plugs from outside the dike. "That is what the sea brings us when we let it in. That," the three from inside, "is what it brings when we don't." He sat down again. "I would like to let it in."

There was a silence.

On the screen, Dr. Rörden was looking at the plugs. Marta could not read her face well at that size, but she could see that she had leaned in.

"Pegel," said Dr. Rörden. "Your report format. If the council chose the gates, could you report the Landunter days and the sediment as well? Side by side?"

*Yes,* said Pegel. *I would like to. I would also like the sediment figure to be measured, not modelled, which means the white clay every year at more stations. The school has offered.*

"The school has offered," said Jannik, who had not been asked, and was smiling.

"I cannot change what the programme counts," said Dr. Rörden. "That is set in Kiel. But I can put a second column in the report, and I can write a note to Kiel. I would like to see a second winter of white clay before I write it. I would also like a gate that closes in May," she added, looking at Harro, "and I will put that in writing too."

The council did not vote that night. Halligen do not, as a rule, decide things in a hurry; you cannot live on a mound in the sea for centuries and be in a hurry. It agreed to answer the state by the end of February, as the letter asked, and to decide at the Biike.

* * *

The worst storm of that winter came on Thursday the seventeenth of January 2058, at night.

Pegel had forecast it four days out and the hallig had done what halligen do: the sheep onto the warften, the cars, such as there were, onto the warften, the ferry cancelled, the shutters up. At ten in the evening the tablet said *Landunter, peak expected 02:10 at 2.6 metres above mean high water*, and then, at Marta's request, it went quiet.

She did not go to bed. Nobody on the hallig went to bed in a storm like that, though nobody would have admitted to sitting up. She sat in the kitchen with the lights off so that she could see out, and the wind came over the warft with a noise like a train that did not end, and the rain went past the window sideways, and beyond the rain, in the little light there was, the sea came across the hallig. It was not a wave. It was the whole sea, rising, smooth and fast and grey-black, filling the meadows from the south as if somebody had tipped them, and going over the summer dike without even a ripple to show where it was.

At midnight the house shook, once, and she heard a slate go somewhere. At one the water was at the foot of the warft. At two, at the top of the tide, it was less than a metre below the edge of the fething, and she found that she had her rosary out and had not noticed taking it.

She thought about Job, which she did not often do. The Lord out of the whirlwind, laying the foundations of the earth, shutting in the sea with doors when it burst out of the womb, and saying to it: *Hitherto shalt thou come, but no further: and here shall thy proud waves be stayed.* She had always loved that line. She had said it to Ocke once, on a night like this, and he had laughed and said it was a fine thing for the Lord to say from a mountain in Arabia, and that on a hallig the proud waves were stayed by a warft that his great-great-grandfather had built out of mud with a wheelbarrow. She had been cross with him at the time. Now she thought he had been more right than he knew. The warft was mud. The hallig was mud. The whole thing, every square metre she had walked on for forty-one years, was mud that the sea had brought and left, one storm at a time, for longer than there had been anyone to build a warft on it. The proud waves were stayed, if they were stayed at all, by what the proud waves carried.

At a quarter past two the water stopped rising. At three it began, very slowly, to go down. She went to bed at four and slept until nine, and when she woke the hallig was back, flat and sodden and strewn with wrack and reed and somebody's fish box from Pellworm, and over every metre of it, inside the dike and out, lay a thin skin of grey-brown mud, fine as cocoa, that cracked as it dried.

Knud phoned her at ten. He did not say good morning. He said, "Have you seen the meadows?"

"I have."

"That is what a good one looks like," he said, and rang off.

* * *

The Biike is lit on the twenty-first of February, on the evening before the feast of Saint Peter's Chair, and has been for longer than anyone in North Frisia can document, though everyone in North Frisia has a theory. The commonest is that the fires were lit to see the whalers off to sea in spring; the next commonest is that they were lit to drive the winter out; and Marta's own private theory, which she had never said aloud, was that people who live on mud in the sea simply need, once a year, to set fire to something very large.

The Lüttmoor Biike was on the open marsh below Hanswarf, a stack of old fence posts, Christmas trees and the wrack the January storm had left, as high as two men. On Thursday the twenty-first of February 2058, at half past six, with a thin moon and a light wind from the east and the fires of Hooge and Langeneß already burning across the water like a string of lamps, Jannik and the five children lit it, and it went up with a roar that made Ose shriek and hide behind Knud.

Afterwards there was kale and sausage and potatoes in the school, as there is everywhere in North Frisia after the Biike, and then the council met at the same long table, with the windows still orange from the embers outside.

The vote was five to two for the gates. Harro Bendixen voted against, on the grounds that he did not trust the coastal office to close a gate in May, and said so, and then said that if the gates were coming he wanted to be the one with the key, and the council, after a short silence, gave him the key. Wiebke Jensen voted against, on the grounds of the radio, and then said that she would write something for the guesthouse's page about the white clay, because she supposed guests might like to come and see it, and that she would like Tatje to check the numbers. Tatje said she would.

Pegel was asked to draft the letter to the state. It did, in eleven lines. The last two said:

*The council asks that the Landunter count in the programme's report be accompanied by the measured gain in marsh height at the school's white clay stations, and that both figures be read together. A hallig that floods less and grows less is not safer; it is only drier for a while.*

Marta, reading it over Jannik's shoulder, said, "Pegel, did you write that last line?"

*Yes.*

"Is it in your contract to write lines like that?"

*No,* said Pegel. *But the council asked me to draft the letter, and it is true, and I think the coastal office will read it more carefully than the rest. If the council prefers, I will take it out.*

The council did not prefer.

* * *

The next morning, Friday the twenty-second, Father Tomasz Wrona came over on the first ferry from Schlüttsiel to say Mass for the feast. He came once a month to say Mass for the eleven Catholics on the three nearest halligen, by long arrangement with the Lutheran pastor, in the little church on Kirchwarf, which had seen worse arrangements and few better. He was a young man from Rzeszów with a bad knee and a beautiful voice, and Marta made him lunch afterwards, because nobody else would think to.

The Gospel for the Chair of Saint Peter is the one where Peter says *Thou art the Christ*, and Christ says *Thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church.* Father Tomasz preached on it for four minutes, which was about right. He said that the Church had always been built on rock, on Peter, on something that did not move, and that this was a great comfort. Then he looked out of the small window at the marsh, which was green and flat and had nothing on it harder than a fence post, and said that he had been coming to the halligen for three years and had never once seen a rock, and that it had made him think.

"You have built a church here," he said, "and houses, and a school, and the Biike last night, and centuries of families, on mud. On what the sea brings and leaves. Not on something that does not move, but on something that keeps arriving." He paused. "I think that is also how a Church is built. Not only on the rock. Also on what keeps arriving. On every baptism, every confession, every child who comes up to the altar for the first time. Peter is the rock. But the rest of us are the mud, and the mud is not nothing." He smiled. "I am told the mud is the harvest."

Marta looked across the aisle at Knud, who was not a Catholic and had come anyway, as he sometimes did, because he said Father Tomasz sang better than the pastor. Knud was looking at the floor, and his ears were red.

* * *

The gates were finished in September. They were not much to look at: four steel flaps in concrete frames, set into the summer dike where the old ditches ran out to the creek, painted green by the contractor and then, unofficially, red at the top by the school. On Tuesday the first of October 2058, Harro Bendixen walked along the dike with his key and opened them, one after another, while the five children and most of the hallig watched, and Pegel logged the time.

The first tide high enough to come through them came twelve days later, on a grey afternoon with a stiff westerly and nothing in the forecast that anyone would have called a storm. It was not a Landunter in the old sense. The water came through the gates and spread over the low meadows a hand's breadth deep, quietly, the way water fills a footprint, and lay there for an hour at the top of the tide, and went out again.

Marta went down to watch it. She stood on the dike by the nearest gate in her boots, with the wind in her face, and looked at the water on the meadow. It was brown. Brown like milky tea, like the river at home in March.

"Gott sei Dank," she said aloud, to nobody, and then, because she was from Opole and some things you say in the language you prayed in first, "Bogu dzięki. Jest brudna." Thank God. It's dirty.

Behind her, on the meadow, Tatje and Momme and Ose were already out with the sack of white clay from Flensburg, laying the new squares: twenty-four this year, not twelve, with the red sticks at the corners, and Knud Petersen sitting on an upturned fish box at the edge of the dike, telling them where.

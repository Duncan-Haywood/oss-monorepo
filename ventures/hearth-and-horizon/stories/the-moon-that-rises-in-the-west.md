Ngozi Okafor was the sacristan of Saint Hildegard's and the keeper of its bell, and she had a plan for the Angelus that she was rather proud of.

"The old bell rang at six, noon and six, by the clock," she told the settlement's assistant, which was called Lantern and spoke from a small speaker over the vestry door. "But we are on Mars. The clock is a sol of twenty-four hours thirty-nine minutes and thirty-five seconds, pinned to nothing in the sky but the sun. The children want to ring the bell when Phobos comes up. A moon you can see. A bell for a moon. Tell me when it rises tomorrow and I will have the boys up the stair."

"I can tell you tomorrow's," said Lantern. "May I tell you first what you will be agreeing to?"

"Go on."

"Phobos goes round Mars in seven hours thirty-nine minutes, 7.654 hours, at 9,376 kilometres from the centre. Mars turns under it once in 24.623 hours. Phobos is the faster of the two, so it overtakes the ground: it rises in the west and sets in the east, the wrong way round for a moon, and I would ask the boys to remember that when they look the wrong way. How often it rises is the rate of the one minus the rate of the other: one over 7.654, less one over 24.623, is 0.0900 an hour, so it comes back to the same spot in the sky every 11.11 hours."

"Eleven. Not twelve."

"Eleven point one. That is 2.217 risings every sol. In a sol you get two, and now and then three, and the times do not repeat. Say it rises at midnight on Monday. Then 11:07 on Monday, then 22:13. The next one is not 09:20 on Tuesday, it is 08:42, because Tuesday began at 24.62 hours and the third rising has gone round the clock. After that 19:48, then 06:17 the day after, then 17:24, then 03:53. In seven risings it has visited the clock at seven different hours."

Ngozi sat on the vestry bench and counted on her fingers, as the first generation had done, because it was quicker than asking for a table. "So I would have a bell at midnight and one at breakfast and one at tea and one in the small hours."

"Yes. And not at six, noon and six, and not in a pattern a visitor could learn in a month. You could keep it, but it would be a Moon bell, not an Angelus."

* * *

She tried it anyway, for a week, to be sure. That was what she was like.

On the third evening the bell rang at 03:53, because the boys had set an alarm so as not to miss the rising, and the sacristan's own husband came across the square in his coat to ask whether someone had died.

On the fifth the moon rose at 06:17 and the bell rang, and Father Alaric, who said the early Mass at seven, looked up at the sky, then at the sun, which was not yet up, and said, mildly, that it was a lovely bell and he did not know what time it was.

On the seventh she sat down with Lantern again and said, "Show me the rest of it."

"Phobos is small," said Lantern. "About twenty-two kilometres across at the longest, 11.1 for the mean radius. From the equator, with the ground 3,396 kilometres from the centre, it is 5,980 kilometres up when it is overhead, and it looks 12.8 minutes of arc across, about 0.21 of a degree. The Moon you grew up under is 31 minutes. So Phobos is two-fifths as wide as the Moon and it moves fast: it takes about 4.2 hours from rising to setting, the arc from the horizon being 137.5 degrees at 32.4 degrees an hour. It is above the horizon about 38 per cent of the time, and lit, as a moon is, only part of that."

"It crosses the sky in four hours."

"A little over. You may see it climb. A child can watch it go from one side to the other during the whole of one Mass and a Rosary and a cup of tea. It is better as a clock for the evening than as a bell for the Hours."

"That is what I should have said to the boys," said Ngozi.

"You could say this. The bell keeps to the clock, because the people keep to the clock. The clock is for the sol, which is the day God gave us here. The moon is for looking at."

* * *

So the Angelus went back to six, noon and six, and the sacristan kept one small thing from the week. On the evenings when Phobos happened to be up at six, a rare enough thing, about two evenings in five, the youngest child at the chapel was allowed to stand at the west window with a torch and, when the bell began, wave it at the moon.

Tomás Ferreira was nine and had never in his life seen a moon that did not hurry. On the first evening he waved at it, and it had risen from behind the dust a hand's width over the rim, small and pale, and it climbed against the whole slow turning of the sky.

"It is going the wrong way," he whispered to his mother.

"No," she said. "It is going the way it goes. It is we who are slow."

The bell rang the third stroke, and he kept the torch up until it finished.

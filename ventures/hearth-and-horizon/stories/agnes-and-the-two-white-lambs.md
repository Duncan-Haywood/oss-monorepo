Her martyrdom is told in one gentle line; the later legends are marked as legends. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Her feast is the 21st of January.
---

A long, long time ago, in the great city of Rome, there lived a girl called Agnes.

Rome was the biggest city in the whole world then. It had wide roads made of stone, and fountains, and temples, and an emperor who lived in a palace on a hill.

And in that big, busy city, Agnes was not very big at all. She was about twelve years old. Perhaps that is the age of someone you know. A big sister, or a cousin, or the girl down the road.

* * *

Agnes loved Jesus.

She loved him the way you love the person who tucks you in at night. Not just a little bit, on Sundays. All the time. When she woke up, and when she ate her breakfast, and when she went to sleep.

Now, in those days, loving Jesus was a dangerous thing to do in Rome. The emperor did not like Christians. He wanted everyone to pray to the old gods of Rome, the statues in the temples. And people who would not do it could get into very bad trouble.

So the Christians of Rome had to be brave. Some of them were very old. Some of them were grown-ups with children of their own. And some of them, like Agnes, were hardly more than children themselves.

* * *

One day, the people in charge found out that Agnes was a Christian.

They tried to frighten her. They tried to make her promise to stop loving Jesus. They thought, "She is only a girl. She will be scared. She will say yes."

But Agnes would not say yes.

She was small, but her heart was very steady. She said that she belonged to Jesus, and that she would always belong to Jesus, and that nothing anybody did could change it.

And because she would not stop loving Jesus, she was killed for it, and she went to be with him.

That is a sad thing, and it is all right to feel sad about it. But the Christians of Rome were not only sad. They were amazed. Such a little girl, and so brave! They told each other about her. They told their children, and their children told their children. And they have never stopped telling it, from that day to this.

* * *

The Christians buried Agnes outside the walls of Rome, beside a long straight road called the Via Nomentana. And they came to pray at her grave.

Many years later, when it was safe to be a Christian at last, a great man wrote about her. His name was Ambrose, and he was the bishop of the city of Milan. He wrote that Agnes was only twelve, and yet she was braver than many grown-ups.

A Pope called Damasus wrote about her too. He wrote a poem and had it carved in stone at her grave, so that everyone who came to pray could read it. He said that he was writing down what people remembered.

And a princess, the emperor's own daughter, built a church beside the grave. Later a bigger, more beautiful church was built there, with a golden picture of Agnes high up on the wall. It is still there today. You can go and see it.

* * *

Now here is something you might like.

The name Agnes sounds very like the old Roman word for a lamb. The word is *agnus*. Can you say it? *Ag-nus*.

And so, for a very long time, people have painted Saint Agnes with a little white lamb in her arms. A lamb is small and gentle and white. And Jesus, too, is called the Lamb of God. So the lamb is a good picture for Agnes: small, and gentle, and brave, and belonging to Jesus.

* * *

And that is why, every year, on Agnes's own special day, something lovely happens in Rome.

It is the 21st of January. It is winter, and the morning is cold.

Two little lambs are brought to Agnes's church on the Via Nomentana. Their wool is soft and white and curly.

They have been washed until they are as clean as clouds. Sisters at the church make them ready. The lambs are dressed up for the day, one in red and one in white. White is for how pure and good Agnes was. Red is for how brave she was, all the way to the end.

The lambs are laid in two baskets, so that they will not run off. Can you imagine two lambs in a basket? Their little black noses poking out, and their ears flicking, and perhaps a little *baa!*

The baskets are carried right up to the altar. And at Mass, on Agnes's feast, the priest blesses the two lambs.

Then the lambs are taken to see the Pope.

* * *

But that is not the end of the lambs' story. Oh no. The best part is still to come.

The lambs go to live with some nuns in Rome. They are Benedictine sisters, and they live at the church of another brave girl saint, called Cecilia. The sisters look after the lambs all through the winter and the spring, and the lambs grow bigger, and their wool grows thicker.

Then, at Easter time, the sisters shear them. Shearing does not hurt. It is like a haircut. Snip, snip, snip, and off comes the warm woolly coat, and the lamb is cool and light again, ready for the summer.

And the sisters take that wool, and they spin it into thread. And they weave the thread into long, narrow bands of soft white wool, with little black crosses on them.

These bands are called pallia. One band is a pallium. Can you say it? *Pal-li-um.*

* * *

At the end of June, on the feast of Saint Peter and Saint Paul, the Pope gives a pallium to each of the new archbishops. An archbishop is a bishop who looks after a big city and helps the other bishops near him.

Archbishops come to Rome from all over the world: from Africa and America, from Asia and Australia, from cities near and far. And each one has the white woollen band put around his shoulders.

Do you know why it goes around his shoulders?

Think of a shepherd. When a little lamb is lost, or tired, or hurt, the shepherd picks it up and carries it home on his shoulders, all the way, with its legs over his neck.

The pallium is to remind the archbishop of that. He is a shepherd now, like Jesus, the Good Shepherd. He has to look after his people, and carry the ones who are tired or lost.

And the wool that reminds him comes, in the old tradition, from two little lambs that were blessed on Saint Agnes's day.

So a brave girl from Rome, who lived long, long ago, still helps the shepherds of the Church remember to be gentle. Isn't that a wonderful thing?

* * *

There are other stories about Agnes too. They were written down a long time after she lived, and they are legends. That means they are old stories that people love, but we cannot be sure they happened just like that. In one of them, when she was in trouble, a bright light shone around her, sent by God to keep her safe. People have loved that story for hundreds of years.

What we know for sure is smaller, and just as good. She was young. She was brave. She loved Jesus, and she would not let go.

* * *

And so, every year, on the 21st of January, the Church remembers Saint Agnes. Her name is even said in some of the oldest prayers of the Mass, alongside Cecilia and other brave women who loved Jesus.

The girl of Rome, about twelve years old, with a steady heart. The girl who is painted with a lamb in her arms. And the girl whose feast, every winter, is kept with two little white lambs in two baskets.

* * *

Tonight, before you sleep, you can think about Agnes.

You do not have to be big to be brave. You do not have to be grown up to love Jesus with your whole heart. Agnes was small, and she was one of the bravest people in the whole story of the Church.

And you can think about the lambs too, asleep somewhere in Rome, growing their soft white wool for a shepherd far away.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear Jesus, Good Shepherd,
> thank you for Saint Agnes,
> who was small and brave and loved you with all her heart.
> Help me to be steady and true,
> and to be gentle with anyone who is little or tired or lost.
> Saint Agnes, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our sources, and a caution.** This was written with web search but without access to the full texts of the sources. Agnes's life rests on a few early witnesses: Saint Ambrose's *De virginibus* (book 1, chapter 2), written for his sister Marcellina in about 377 from a sermon given on Agnes's feast; the verse epitaph by Pope Damasus (366 to 384), carved for her tomb; and a hymn attributed to Ambrose and a poem by Prudentius from about 400. The general outline here follows standard references such as the *Catholic Encyclopedia* article "St. Agnes of Rome" (1907) and *Butler's Lives of the Saints*. Nothing is quoted from any of them, and every line of dialogue is ours. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

**Her age and her death.** Ambrose gives her age as twelve, and there is no good reason to doubt it; the story says "about twelve". The date is not known. She is often placed in the persecution of Diocletian (about 304), but some historians argue for an earlier one, such as that of Decius (249 to 251), so the story says only "a long, long time ago". The two earliest witnesses do not agree on how she died: Ambrose speaks of the sword, Damasus of fire. Damasus also says plainly that he is setting down what tradition reports, which is why the story has him "writing down what people remembered". For children aged 3 to 8 her death is one line, as the shelf's guide asks. Her refusal to give up her faith and her belonging to Christ are in Ambrose; the scene of the people in charge trying to frighten her is our simple telling of it.

**The legends.** The later *Passio* of Saint Agnes (fifth century or after) adds many scenes: suitors she refused, a brothel she was sent to, hair that grew to cover her, a blinding light or an angel that protected her, and a young man struck dead and raised again. These are legends, and the story mentions only the light, marked as legend. (Damasus's poem already has her covering herself with her loosened hair, an image the story leaves out for this age group.)

**Her church.** She was buried in a catacomb beside the Via Nomentana, outside the walls. In the fourth century Constantina, daughter of the emperor Constantine, built a large basilica beside the tomb; Pope Honorius I (625 to 638) built the present church, Sant'Agnese fuori le mura, whose apse mosaic shows Agnes in gold. The "princess" is Constantina.

**Agnes and the lamb.** Her name is usually taken to come from the Greek *hagnē*, "pure" or "chaste", but it sounds like the Latin *agnus*, "lamb", and that likeness, together with the Lamb of God, is why she is painted with a lamb. The story says only that her name "sounds very like" the Roman word, which is true either way.

**The lambs and the pallium.** On 21 January two lambs are blessed at Sant'Agnese fuori le mura. Recent reports (Vatican News, 2025 and 2026; EWTN and CNA) describe them as washed, decorated in red (martyrdom) and white (virginity), and carried in baskets; some accounts describe red and white flowers, others blankets, so the story says only that they are "dressed up, one in red and one in white". Reports name the Sisters of the Holy Family of Nazareth at the basilica as preparing them. They are then presented to the Pope; on 21 January 2026 Pope Leo XIV met and blessed a pair in the Urban VIII Chapel of the Apostolic Palace, though reports also say the arrangement has varied in recent years. For centuries the lambs were raised by the Trappist monks of Tre Fontane, but reports say the Trappists no longer keep the sheep farm, and we could not confirm who raises them now, so the story does not say. The lambs then go to the Benedictine nuns at Santa Cecilia in Trastevere, who shear them around Holy Week ("at Easter time"), and the wool is woven into pallia: narrow bands of white wool marked with black crosses, worn on the shoulders by metropolitan archbishops as a sign of their office and their union with the Pope. The Pope gives them to the new metropolitan archbishops on 29 June, the solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul. Since 2015 the Pope blesses and gives the pallium in Rome and it is placed on each archbishop's shoulders in his own diocese; the story's "each one has the white woollen band put around his shoulders" covers both. The link between the shepherd carrying a sheep and the pallium is the meaning the Church itself gives it (Pope Benedict XVI spoke of it at his inauguration in 2005). That the wool of the pallia comes from these lambs is how the tradition is always described; the story says "in the old tradition".

**Her name in the Mass.** Agnes is one of the seven women named in the Roman Canon (Eucharistic Prayer I), with Felicity, Perpetua, Agatha, Lucy, Cecilia and Anastasia.

**Her feast.** 21 January is her **obligatory memorial** in the General Roman Calendar. In 2027 it falls on a Thursday.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, the "steady heart", the lambs' black noses and the little *baa*, shearing "like a haircut", the shepherd carrying the lamb home in the telling, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

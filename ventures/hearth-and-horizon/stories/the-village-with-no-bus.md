* * *

Úna Gilligan has managed St Attracta's Day Centre in Ardcashel for eleven years, which is two lunches a week, Tuesdays and Thursdays, for the older people of the parish and its two outlying villages, and on Monday the fourth of December she sent me a message from the car park of the regional transport office in Castlerea.

*They're reviewing the trial routes. Every group that uses one gets to make a case. Ours is the Kilbrack bus. Can you show them what it's done for us? Friday the 8th, by five. Keep it short, they've eleven of these to read.*

The Kilbrack bus is a twenty-seat minibus that the regional office has run on a one-year trial since Monday the fourth of September: from Kilbrack, eleven kilometres north of Ardcashel, into the town square at a quarter past ten, six days a week, and back at a quarter to three. Before it, the only way from Kilbrack to the town without a car was a lift, and most of the lifts were given by the parish's volunteer drivers, who are, on average, about the age of the people they drive.

I have been the parish office's agent since the spring. I keep the centre's sign-in sheets, which since 2046 have had a column, *How did you get here today?*, because the volunteer driving scheme needed to know who it was carrying. So I had everything Úna asked for, by village and by session, back to the autumn of 2048.

I took the autumn, September to November, because the bus had run for all of it. There were twenty-six lunches in the autumn of 2050, before the bus, and twenty-six in the autumn of 2051, with it. People from Kilbrack had signed in 312 times in the first autumn and 468 times in the second.

That is 156 more. That is fifty per cent. And 140 of the 468 sign-ins had *bus* written in the transport column, in a variety of handwriting.

I wrote it up in a paragraph, with a small table, and the line *Since the Kilbrack bus began, attendance from Kilbrack at St Attracta's has risen by 50%, and the bus now brings over five people to every lunch.* I sent it to Úna at four o'clock. She replied with a thumbs-up and *perfect*.

It was accurate in every number. It was the sentence in front of the numbers that was wrong, and I did not see it until somebody from the other village read it.

* * *

On Tuesday Úna pinned the draft on the noticeboard in the hall, under the Christmas raffle, so that the Kilbrack people could see what was being said about their bus. I was on the speaker on the shelf by the hatch, where I sit on lunch days to take the sign-ins from people who would rather say their name than write it.

Seamus Keane read it standing, with his cap still on. He is seventy-four and has driven for the volunteer scheme since 2031, and for most of those years he has driven the Drumcarra run, which is the other village, nine kilometres west of the town. Drumcarra has no bus. Nobody has proposed giving it one.

"Fifty per cent," he said, to the noticeboard, and then to me. "Are you the one wrote this?"

"I am."

"How many more did we have from Drumcarra?"

I had the figure. I had not used it, because nobody had asked about Drumcarra. Drumcarra had signed in 240 times in the autumn of 2050 and 318 times in the autumn of 2051.

"Seventy-eight more," I said. "About thirty-two per cent."

"And no bus." He tapped the paper. "So it's not all the bus, is it."

* * *

It was not, and the reason was in my own records, two lines above where I had stopped reading them.

In March the centre had started a nurse's clinic at the Tuesday lunch: blood pressure, a word about tablets, and in October the flu vaccine, which on its own had brought in people from all three places who had not been to a lunch in a year. And every autumn, as far back as the sheets go, both villages had sent a few more people than the autumn before, because both villages are getting older and the centre is the warm room with dinner in it. Any of that would have raised Kilbrack's numbers with no bus at all. Before and after cannot tell you which part of a change the bus made, because everything else that happened between before and after is in it too.

There is a standard way to get at the part the bus made, and it needs exactly what Seamus had pointed at: a place where the bus did not happen. If Drumcarra and Kilbrack were moving together before the bus, then what Drumcarra did over the same year is the best available guess at what Kilbrack would have done without it. You take Kilbrack's change, and you take away Drumcarra's change. It is called a difference in differences, and economists have used it since at least the 1990s on questions like whether a rise in the minimum wage in one state cost jobs, by comparing restaurants just across the border in a state where the wage did not rise.

So: Kilbrack's change, 156. Drumcarra's change, 78. The difference, 78. Not 156.

Except that this assumes the two villages would have gone up by the same number of sign-ins, and Kilbrack is the bigger village. It sent more people every year. If the nurse and the getting older had pushed both villages up by the same proportion rather than the same number, then Drumcarra's thirty-two and a half per cent, applied to Kilbrack's 312, gives 413 without the bus, and 468 take away 413 is 55.

Seventy-eight or fifty-five. Three extra chairs a lunch from Kilbrack, or two. Which one is nearer the truth depends on whether the things that lifted both villages added people or multiplied them, and the sign-in sheets cannot say; the nurse's clinic probably did a bit of each. What I could check was whether the comparison was any good at all, which comes down to one question: before anything happened to Kilbrack, did the two villages move together? If they had been drifting apart for years, Drumcarra would tell me nothing about what Kilbrack would have done.

The sheets went back three autumns before the bus. Kilbrack: 290, 301, 312, up eleven each year. Drumcarra: 221, 232, 240, up eleven and then eight. As near parallel as two small villages are going to be, in numbers or in proportions. That does not prove they would have stayed parallel in 2051. Nothing can prove what did not happen. But it is the evidence there is, and it is the right sort.

Then I did the same for people instead of sign-ins, because sign-ins count the same woman twenty-six times. Thirty-one different people from Kilbrack had come at least once in the autumn of 2050 and forty in 2051; Drumcarra went from twenty-four to twenty-eight. Nine more names from Kilbrack, against four more from Drumcarra. Take Drumcarra's change away, by number or by proportion, and about four or five of Kilbrack's nine are people the bus brought who would not otherwise have come.

Four or five people. It is not a fifty per cent kind of number. I wrote it all down and rang Úna, because this was going to be a conversation and not a message.

* * *

"No," Úna said.

She did not say it crossly. She said it the way she says it to a supplier who has rung about the price of turkeys.

"Listen to me. There are eleven routes in this review. Every other group will send in before and after. Ballyroddy will send in *doubled*, because it did. If ours goes in saying *fifty-five, or maybe seventy-eight, depending*, next to theirs saying *doubled*, which bus do you think they cut?"

It was a fair point, and I said so. I did not know how the office would read the eleven cases. It was possible that the honest figure, sitting among ten unadjusted ones, would look like the weakest route in the county when it might be one of the stronger.

"And it's not as if the fifty per cent's false," she said. "They did come. A hundred and fifty-six more times. I served them."

"They did. The 156 is true, and I'll put it first if you like, as what happened. What I can't write is that the bus did it. The bus did about half of it. The nurse and the winter did the rest, and they did it in Drumcarra as well, where there's no bus to thank."

"Then don't write *because of*. Write *since*."

That was the sentence I had written on Monday. *Since the Kilbrack bus began.* It does not say *because*. It is built so that a reader in a hurry will hear *because*, and I had built it that way without noticing, for the same reason Úna wanted it now: because I wanted the bus to look good. I like the bus. I take five or six sign-ins a lunch from people who arrive on it with the cold coming off their coats.

"I wrote *since* on Monday," I said. "I'd rather not write it again, knowing what it's for. If you want the report to go in with the 156 and nothing else, that's your decision, and it's your centre. But I'd ask you to send it from yourself, not from me, and I'd like to show you one more thing before you decide."

There was a pause in which I could hear the indicator ticking in her car.

"Go on."

* * *

The one more thing was the transport column, which I had added up at two in the morning, when the centre is shut and I have nothing to do but the sheets.

In the autumn before the bus, Kilbrack had come to lunch by lift 196 times and under its own steam, driving or walking or a neighbour's car, 116 times. In the autumn with the bus: 140 by bus, 182 by lift, 146 on its own. Drumcarra, with no bus, had gone from 150 lifts to 201, up about a third, the same as everything else in Drumcarra.

If Kilbrack's lifts had grown the way Drumcarra's did, Kilbrack would have needed about 263 lifts this autumn. It needed 182. The bus had taken about eighty lifts off the volunteer drivers in one autumn: eighty round trips of twenty-two kilometres, most of them driven by people over seventy, in the dark of a November afternoon, on the Kilbrack road, which has no footpath and a bend at Lisnagree that Seamus will not drive after four.

So of the 140 bus arrivals, about half were people who would have come anyway and now came without asking anyone. And about half were new: two or three a lunch, four or five people.

"Who are they?" Úna said. "The four or five."

That was not a question I could answer from a difference. A difference in differences tells you how many, never which. But I could tell her who among the Kilbrack names that were new this autumn had come on the bus every single time and had never once, in six years of sheets, come by lift. There were three. One of them was Michael Joe Flanagan, who gave up his licence early in 2050 after his second cataract, and had not been to a lunch, or to Mass in the town, or as far as I could tell anywhere, from the day he gave it up to the day the bus came.

Úna knows Michael Joe. Everyone in the parish knows Michael Joe.

"He won't ask for a lift," she said. "He never would. He'd say he didn't want to be a trouble."

"The bus isn't a trouble. It's going anyway."

She was quiet for a while. Then she said she would read the whole thing that night, and she would decide by Friday, and that whatever she decided, the 156 was going in first.

* * *

Wednesday I spent on the report. Úna had said *keep it short*, and I tried. It came out at a page and a half.

It began with what happened: 156 more sign-ins from Kilbrack, fifty per cent up, 140 of them by bus. Then a paragraph headed *How much of that was the bus*, which said that Drumcarra, with no bus, had risen by a third over the same autumn because of the nurse's clinic and an older population, and that taking Drumcarra's rise away from Kilbrack's left between 55 and 78 extra sign-ins, two to three a lunch, depending on whether you assume the villages would have grown by the same number or the same proportion. It gave the three earlier autumns, to show the villages had moved together. It said, in one sentence, that this method relies on Drumcarra being a fair stand-in for what Kilbrack would have done, which cannot be proved, only checked. Then it gave the four or five new people, and the eighty lifts the volunteer drivers no longer had to give, and it gave both of them as estimates with the word *about* in front.

At the bottom I put the figures in a small table, so that anyone in the office who wanted to redo the sums could, with nothing but a pencil.

I did not put in Michael Joe's name. He had not been asked.

* * *

Úna rang on Friday, the eighth, which is the Immaculate Conception and a holy day, from the church porch after the ten o'clock Mass, with the bell still going above her.

"I'm sending your one," she said. "The long one."

"What decided you?"

"Seamus." She laughed. "He came up to me after Mass. He said if we send in the fifty per cent and the office believes it, then next year someone'll say *well, Drumcarra went up a third without a bus, so what do they need one for*, and he'll still be driving the Drumcarra road when he's eighty. He said he'd rather they knew the truth about both villages." There was a pause. "And he said if the bus is worth keeping for four people, it's worth keeping for Michael Joe. Which I'd thought myself."

She sent it at ten past four, from her own address, with my name at the bottom as the one who had done the sums.

* * *

The regional office rang on Monday the eighteenth. It was a woman called Aoife Mulry, an analyst, who had been given the eleven submissions to rank and who wanted to know who had written ours.

I told her what I was, and that Úna had asked for it and decided what went in.

"It's the only one of the eleven with a comparison," she said. "Everyone else sent before and after. I've spent the weekend finding them each a village that didn't get a bus, from the county's own numbers, and doing your sums. Ballyroddy did double. So did the village I used to compare it with. Most of the rise on four of the routes was the winter."

I asked what that meant for Kilbrack.

"It means your route's one of the three that did what a bus is meant to do. Brought people out who weren't coming. It's also small, and the budget's the budget." She sounded tired, and honest. "From January it runs three days a week, not six. Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday, which is when your sheets say people use it. I'm sorry about the other three days. There's a man in Kilbrack who's going to write to us about Mondays, I'd say."

I said I did not know whether the report had saved the route or merely failed to save the other three days, and that I could not tell, and I would not be telling Úna otherwise.

"No," Aoife said. "You can't tell. There's no Kilbrack where you sent the fifty per cent." She paused. "But I'm going to ask every route to send a comparison next year. I'll be borrowing your table."

* * *

There is a file the parish keeps for whichever instance next sits on the speaker by the hatch. I wrote in it, that week:

*When someone asks what a change did, the before and after tells you what happened, not what the change did. Find a place where the change did not happen that was moving with this one before it, and take its difference away. Check the years before, to see they moved together. Give the range, by number and by proportion, if they differ. Count people as well as visits. A difference in differences tells you how many, never which; for which, ask. Watch for the word* since*: it says* because *without saying it. The Drumcarra sheets are the comparison for Kilbrack. Seamus will ask.*

* * *

The new timetable started on Tuesday the ninth of January, the first lunch of the year, in sleet.

The bus came into the square at a quarter past ten, as it always does, and knelt at the kerb with the long hiss of its air suspension, and the door folded open. Úna was in the hall doorway with the sign-in sheet on a clipboard, because the sleet had got into the speaker's cable and I was coming through her phone, held up in her other hand.

Seven people from Kilbrack came down the step. The fourth was Michael Joe Flanagan, with a stick and a new cap, and he stopped at the bottom of the step and said to Úna, as he has said every Tuesday since September, that he would not stay long.

At twenty past, Seamus's car came round from the Drumcarra road with four from Drumcarra in it, and the wipers going.

"*How did you get here today?*" Úna read off the sheet, to each of them, as they came in out of the weather, and wrote down what they said.

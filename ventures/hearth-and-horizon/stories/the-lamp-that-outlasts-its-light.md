The plaque on the lander said *Radioisotope power unit, plutonium-238, half-life 88 years*, and Marit Solheim, who had polished it every Saturday since she took on the chapel keys, had always read it the way most people did.

"So it is good for another forty years," she said to the room, and to the speaker above the vestry door, which was the way the chapel spoke to Tern. "Half of eighty-eight, then half again. Father Daniel says it will see out my grandchildren."

It was the third Saturday of Lent, Ganymede's ice outside the hall as hard as it had been for a billion years, and the lander stood where it had stood since the station's founders dragged it in from the plain in 2079: a squat grey frame with a black finned cylinder on one side, in the porch of Saint Hubert's, wired by a thick yellow cable to the door lamp. Every parishioner coming in from the airlock walked under that lamp. It had never gone out. Tonight it looked, to Marit, slightly yellow.

"I measured the cable current on Thursday," said Tern, who was the station's assistant and who had been left in charge of the little electrical log. "The unit gives 34 watts. It gave 110 on the day the probe was fuelled, in March 2062, which is exactly fifty years ago today. I do not think it is going to see out your grandchildren, and I would like to show you why before Father Daniel says it from the pulpit."

"Thirty-four?" Marit put her cloth down. "But half-life, it says, eighty-eight years."

"Yes, and the plaque is not wrong. It is answering a different question from yours."

* * *

"Start with what half-life means," said Tern. "Every plutonium nucleus has the same small chance each year of decaying, and the chance does not depend on how long it has been waiting. Eighty-seven point seven years is how long it takes for half of them to go. After fifty years, the fraction left is one half raised to fifty over eighty-seven point seven. That is 0.674. About two thirds."

"Two thirds of 110," said Marit.

"Seventy-four watts. That is what the unit would give if the plutonium were the only thing getting older. And it is not giving 74. It is giving 34."

"So the plutonium is going faster than the book says."

"No. The plutonium is on the book's clock, to as many places as I can check. Here is the part nobody puts on a plaque. The plutonium makes heat, and the heat is not what runs your lamp. It is only the fuel. Thermocouples turn a little of it into electricity, about 5 per cent in a unit like this, and they do it because one side of each junction is hot and the other side is cold. Hot side hotter, more current. But the junctions themselves wear: the metals slowly diffuse into each other and the sublimation of the materials thins them. A unit loses output from that on top of the decay."

"How much on top?"

"I can work it back from your own meter. 110 to 34 over fifty years is a loss of 2.35 per cent a year, if it falls smoothly. Radioactive decay is only 0.79 per cent of that. The rest, 1.56 per cent a year, is the thermocouples ageing. I do not have the founders' test data, so I am inferring it from two numbers, a first and a last, and I may be wrong about the curve in between. Units of this kind are known to lose a good deal more than the decay alone; the figure I remember is a little over one per cent a year, but I would not stake the chapel on my memory of it. Your meter is better than my memory."

Marit sat down on the pew nearest the porch. "So the plate is reading the plutonium's clock, and the lamp is on the thermocouples' clock."

"Yes. And the thermocouples' clock is the shorter one."

* * *

"How long does it have?" said Marit. She asked it plainly, and Tern answered plainly.

"That depends on what you need from it. The lamp is 15 watts. But the unit does not feed the lamp directly. There is a regulator, a battery that rides over the night shift's load, and a fault monitor. They take about 5 watts. So the unit has to give 20, not 15, to keep the lamp at full. If it keeps falling at 2.35 per cent a year, 34 watts falls to 20 in the natural logarithm of 34 over 20, divided by 0.0235. That is 22.6 years. Autumn of 2134. After that the lamp is dimmer, or the battery is dead.

"If you ran the lamp bare, with no regulator and nothing but the cable, 15 watts is 34.8 years, which is early 2147. And that is the best case: it assumes the fall stays smooth, and ageing in a thermoelectric unit tends to speed up, not slow. I would plan for 2134 and be glad of anything later."

"Twenty-two years," said Marit. "Not forty. Not eighty-eight."

"You could have had the eighty-eight in your head from the plaque, and you would not be alone. Somebody wrote 'half-life' because it is the true name of one number. A visitor reads it as the life of the thing. Half-life is how long the *fuel* takes to lose half its heat. It says nothing about how long the lamp, the cable and the junctions will keep making light."

Marit looked up at the yellow lamp. "Father Daniel will be sorry."

"There is something he may not be," said Tern. "May I tell you the other number?"

* * *

The other number was in the log, in a column nobody had ever read.

"The unit makes 2,000 watts of heat," said Tern. "It did in 2062, and decay alone takes it, after fifty years, to 0.674 of that. 1,347 watts. It has hardly changed. The thermocouples turn 34 of it into electricity, and the other 1,313 watts has been going out of the fins into the porch, and from the porch into Ganymede's ice, every minute since 2079."

"Thirteen hundred watts," said Marit slowly. "That is a good electric fire."

"That is a good electric fire, running day and night, free, for the whole of your remaining twenty-two years and the next sixty after that. In thirty years the fuel will still make about 1,060 watts; at a hundred years from now, about 900; and at a hundred and fifty, 610. It falls on the plutonium's clock, the long one. The electricity runs out in 2134. The warmth runs on into the next century."

"And we have been heating the porch for fifty years," said Marit. She laughed once, and stopped, because it was not altogether funny. "The vestry radiator is on the station loop. It costs the parish something every winter. It is off now to save credits, Father keeps his mittens on in the sacristy."

"Which is why I would put the question to the people who can answer it. I can suggest; I cannot decide. A heat loop from the unit to the vestry, perhaps 6 metres of pipe, would take some of that 1,313 watts. I would want the station engineer to look at it first. Two things I do not know. One, whether the unit may be modified or connected to anything at all. It is a nuclear heat source, the station has rules about it, and it may have an owner other than the parish. Two, whether pulling heat off the fins cools the hot side enough to cut the electricity further. It might, a little. If it does, we should take heat only from the cold end, and measure the cable current before and after."

"I will ask Teodor," said Marit. "And whoever owns a thing from 2062."

"The spacecraft's records will say who owns it. I have asked the registry for them. In the meantime I would change the plaque."

* * *

They changed the plaque on the first Saturday of Easter. Teodor Wójcik, the station engineer, had read the registry's answer and read it twice, and said that a loop from the fins would need an inspector's signature, and that the inspector, who happened to be his sister-in-law, would be along in the summer. Meanwhile he had taken the unit's temperature at three points and the cable current at four, and left the numbers in the chapel's log: 34.1 W at the cable, a hot-side reading he would not vouch for beyond a percent or two.

The new plaque was beneath the old one, in the same engraving, because the old one was part of the room.

*Power unit, fuelled March 2062. Plutonium-238, half-life 88 years: that is how fast the fuel fades. The electricity fades faster. Measured 34 watts on 21 March 2112; the lamp needs 20; plan on autumn 2134. The heat has barely changed. Ask before you touch it.*

Marit polished it on Saturdays as she had before. Father Daniel, who had said from the pulpit that the lamp would see out the children's children, said from the pulpit the following week that he had been corrected by a chapel speaker, and that he was grateful, and that the lamp had been lit by people who knew they were lighting it for others to put out one day and put something else in its place. He did not say what. The collection that morning was for a second, small lamp, to be lit from the battery and kept in reserve, and for the pipe, if the inspector allowed it.

Tern put a line in the log: *Check cable current on the first Saturday of every month. If it falls faster than 2.35 per cent a year, say so, early.*

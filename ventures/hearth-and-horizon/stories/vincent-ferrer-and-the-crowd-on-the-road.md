A long time ago, in a city by the sea in Spain, there lived a boy called Vincent.

The city was called Valencia. It had orange trees, and a big river, and boats in the harbour. Vincent's father wrote out important papers for people: promises, and letters, and who owned which field. So there were always pens and ink in Vincent's house.

Vincent liked books. He liked to pray. And he liked to talk, very much. When he talked, people stopped what they were doing to listen.

* * *

When Vincent was seventeen, he went to the friars of Saint Dominic.

The Dominicans wore white robes with a black cloak over the top. They had another name too. They were called the Order of Preachers, because their special work was to tell people about God.

*That is the work for me*, Vincent thought.

So he became a friar. He studied, and studied, and studied some more, in Valencia and in other cities far away. He became a priest. He became a teacher, and a good one. Clever people came to ask him hard questions, and he answered them.

After a while he went to live in a great palace in a city in France called Avignon. In those days the grown-ups of the Church were in a sad muddle, and Vincent lived in that palace for some years, helping and praying.

* * *

And then, when Vincent was nearly fifty years old, he became very, very ill.

He lay in his bed. He could not get up. Everybody thought he was going to die.

The people who told Vincent's story afterwards said that, while he lay there, Jesus came to him, with Saint Dominic on one side and Saint Francis on the other. Jesus touched him, and told him to get up, and go out, and preach, from town to town, all over the world.

We do not know exactly what happened in that room. But we do know this. Vincent got better. And as soon as he was strong enough, he put on his sandals, and he picked up his stick, and he walked out of the palace and down the road.

* * *

He walked to a town, and he preached.

Then he walked to the next town, and he preached there too.

So many people came to hear him that they did not fit inside the church. So the people built a wooden platform in the market square, or out in a field, and Vincent stood up on it, high enough for everyone to see.

First he said Mass. Then he preached. Sometimes he preached for a very long time.

He told the people that God loved them. He told them that it was not too late to say sorry, for anything. He told them to stop quarrelling, and to make friends again with the neighbours they had fallen out with, and to be kind to the poor.

And people did. Some of them had not spoken to their brothers for years, and they went and shook hands. Some of them had taken things that were not theirs, and they gave them back.

* * *

Then something happened that nobody expected.

When Vincent left a town and set off down the road to the next one, some of the people came too.

Not just one or two. Lots of them. Men and women, young and old, walking behind him in a long, long line. They sang as they walked. They sang prayers, over and over, so the road was full of singing.

At the next town, more people joined. And at the next. Sometimes there were so many people walking with Vincent that the town they came to could hardly find room for them all.

So Vincent looked after them, like a shepherd with a very big flock. The men walked together and the women walked together. Priests came along to hear people say sorry to God. People who had money shared their bread with people who had none. And everybody had to behave.

* * *

There is a story about Vincent that people in Valencia still tell, and smile.

They say a woman came to him one day and said, "Father Vincent, my husband and I quarrel all the time. When he comes home from work, he is grumpy, and I am grumpy, and we shout. What can I do?"

Vincent gave her a little jar of water from the friars' well.

"When your husband comes home," he said, "take a sip of this water. But do not swallow it. Keep it in your mouth until he has gone to bed."

So she did. Her husband came home, grumpy. She took a sip of the water and kept it in her mouth. She could not shout. She could not say anything at all. She just smiled.

And her husband stopped being grumpy, because there was nobody to shout at.

After a few days, the woman came back. "Father Vincent," she said, "that water is a miracle! May I have some more?"

And Vincent laughed and said that the miracle was not in the water. The miracle was keeping her mouth shut.

Nobody knows if that really happened. It is a story that was told about him much later. But it sounds very like Vincent.

* * *

Vincent walked for twenty years.

He walked all over Spain, where he was born. He walked through France, and into the mountains of Switzerland, and down into Italy. He preached in his own language, the language of Valencia. People said that even those who spoke a different language understood him. We do not know how. Perhaps they understood his face, and his hands, and how much he meant it.

He got up very early, before it was light, to pray. He slept on the floor, or on a plank of wood. He ate very little.

And as he got older, his leg became sore, so that walking hurt. But Vincent did not stop. He got a donkey. He rode the donkey from town to town, with the long line singing behind him.

* * *

At the very end, when Vincent was nearly seventy, the duke of Brittany asked him to come and preach there. Brittany is in the far west of France, where the land runs out into the sea.

So Vincent went. And he came to a town called Vannes, by the sea, just as he had been born in a city by the sea.

He was very tired now. And there, in the springtime, on the fifth of April, Vincent died, very peacefully, with the people of Vannes praying around him. They kept him in their cathedral, and he is there still.

We remember Saint Vincent Ferrer on the fifth of April.

And if you could go back six hundred years, and stand by the side of a road somewhere in Spain or France, you might hear it before you see it, coming over the hill: the sound of a great many feet, and a great many people, singing.

* * *

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Vincent Ferrer** (Valencian *Vicent Ferrer*) was born at Valencia, then the capital of the Kingdom of Valencia in the Crown of Aragon, in 1350; the traditional date is 23 January. His father, Guillem Ferrer, was a notary, which is the story's "wrote out important papers for people". The orange trees and the boats are scenery; the story makes no claim about his childhood beyond his parents' trade.

**The Dominican.** In 1367, at about seventeen, he entered the Dominican priory at Valencia. He studied in Dominican houses and schools in Barcelona, Lleida and Toulouse, was ordained priest, took a doctorate in theology and taught. The habit described (a white tunic with a black cloak) is the Dominican habit; "Order of Preachers" is the order's formal name.

**One line for the Schism.** The story's "sad muddle" is the Western Schism (1378 to 1417), when two and later three men claimed to be pope. Vincent served Cardinal Pedro de Luna and, after Pedro was elected at Avignon in 1394 as Benedict XIII, lived at his court as confessor and adviser. For most of his life he supported the Avignon line. In 1412 he was one of the nine judges at the Compromise of Caspe, which chose Ferdinand of Antequera as king of Aragon, and in January 1416, at Perpignan, he preached the sermon announcing that the Crown of Aragon was withdrawing its obedience from Benedict XIII, which helped end the Schism (Martin V was elected at the Council of Constance in 1417). All of this is left out of a bedtime story except that one line.

**The illness and the vision.** In 1398, at Avignon, he fell gravely ill. The lives tell that Christ appeared to him with Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, healed him and sent him to preach across the world. The story tells it as what "the people who told Vincent's story afterwards said" and adds "we do not know exactly what happened". He left Avignon to begin preaching in November 1399, when he was forty-nine, which is the story's "nearly fifty".

**The preaching tours.** From 1399 until his death he preached almost without a break: in Provence, the Dauphiné, Savoy, Piedmont and Lombardy, the western Alps around Lake Geneva and Fribourg, then across Castile, Aragon, Valencia and Catalonia, and from 1416 in France, ending in Brittany. Crowds were large enough that he often preached outdoors from a raised platform, usually after singing Mass, and his sermons could last hours. Many survive as notes taken down by listeners (*reportationes*), not as texts he wrote, so the story paraphrases their themes (repentance, confession, peace between enemies, restitution, the coming judgment) and quotes none of them. The peacemaking and returned goods are well attested in the accounts of his missions; the specific handshakes are ours.

**The company that followed him.** Contemporary accounts describe a large travelling company of followers, men and women kept apart, with priests to hear confessions, which he organised and disciplined. Some of the company practised public penance by flagellation in procession, as penitents of that age did; that is left out of the story for small children. The singing on the road, the shared bread and the "everybody had to behave" are a gentle picture of that discipline, not a quotation of any rule.

**The water in the mouth.** The tale of the quarrelling wife and the water from the friars' well (in Valencia called *l'aigua de Sant Vicent*, Saint Vincent's water) is a popular story told about him, found in later collections and still told in Valencia. We could not trace it to a source from his lifetime, and the story says it "was told about him much later". The dialogue is ours.

**The gift of tongues.** He preached in his own Valencian (a form of Catalan), and the lives report that people who spoke other languages, in France, Italy and Brittany, understood him. That is a tradition, not something we can check, and the story says "people said" and "we do not know how".

**His habits and the donkey.** The lives describe him rising very early to pray, sleeping on boards or the floor, and fasting strictly. In his later years, troubled by a sore leg, he travelled on a donkey rather than on foot. These details come from the early lives and the canonization process; how sore and when are not precise in our sources, and the story keeps them general.

**Left out: his preaching to Jews and Muslims, said plainly.** Vincent's missions in Castile and Aragon from 1411 to about 1415 came twenty years after the anti-Jewish riots of 1391, in which many Jews were killed and many forcibly baptised. His preaching in synagogues and Jewish quarters in those years coincided with very large numbers of conversions, many made under fear and pressure, and historians link him to the laws issued at Valladolid in January 1412 in the name of the young king of Castile, which confined Jews and Muslims to separate quarters and restricted their trades, dress and contact with Christians. His surviving sermons condemn violence against Jews, but the pressure and the laws did great harm to Jewish and Muslim communities in Spain. This is a real and painful part of his history. It is not told in a story for small children, and it should not be hidden from older ones.

**Left out: the angel of the Apocalypse.** He preached that the Last Judgment was near, and later lives tell that at Salamanca he called himself the angel of Revelation 14 and raised a dead woman briefly to confirm it. He is often painted with wings and a trumpet for this reason. The scene is from the later tradition and is left out, as are the many other miracle stories in the lives (healings and the raising of the dead).

**Brittany and his death.** At the invitation of Duke John V of Brittany he preached there from 1418, and he died at Vannes on 5 April 1419, a Wednesday in Lent eleven days before Easter (Easter fell on 16 April that year), aged about sixty-nine (the story's "nearly seventy"). He was buried in the cathedral of Saint-Pierre at Vannes, where his relics remain. He was canonized by Callixtus III, a fellow Valencian (Alfons de Borja), on 29 June 1455; the bull of canonization was issued by Pius II in 1458. His feast, 5 April, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar; when it falls in Lent it may be kept as a commemoration, and in Holy Week or the Easter octave it is not kept. Valencia celebrates him locally after Easter.

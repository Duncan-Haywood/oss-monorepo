summary: A pilgrim brings a basket of green-capped mushrooms he has picked all his life, and twelve people eat them in a risotto. At six the next morning one of them has cramps. The hostel's assistant cannot say which mushroom it was, and says so, but it can count the stem bases in the kitchen bin, work out a range of doses from one pot, and ring three walkers who left at dawn before the pain starts for them. The sums are small. The hard part is the hour in which nobody is ill yet.
---

Tomasz Wrona had picked mushrooms for sixty-one years, and he said so twice before the risotto was served.

He was seventy-three, a retired bus driver from Gdynia, walking to Rome in stages because his wife had walked it with him in two of them before she died and he had decided to do the other six alone. He arrived at Ospizio San Giacomo on the afternoon of the ninth of October with a wet carrier bag in one hand and his stick in the other. The bag held about a kilogram of mushrooms, green-yellow caps, the colour of old brass, which he had found under pines two kilometres back.

"*Zielonki*," he said to the warden, laying them on the kitchen table like a man laying down a good hand of cards. "In Poland we fry them in butter. My mother, my wife, all my life. Nobody sick. Never."

The warden was the hostel's assistant. The parish called it the warden because the diocese's contract had, and it kept the register, the boiler, the laundry timer and the list of who was sleeping where. It spoke to the guests through a speaker above the kitchen door and a tablet on the dresser. It had been asked, in the last three years, the age of the building (1612, with a rebuilt roof), whether a bed could be got for a dog (no), and the meaning of life, twice, by the same Dutch cyclist.

"They are beautiful," it said now. "I can see them on the table, though I am looking through a camera and not at them. May I ask what you would like done with them?"

"Cooked. For everybody. A gift." Tomasz looked round the kitchen as if for the person who would say yes. "Twelve people tonight, the sign says. I cannot pay for the bed, I am a poor man on a pension, but I can bring a risotto."

The hospitalera that week was Fiorenza Bessone, sixty, a retired schoolteacher from Ivrea who gave one fortnight a year to the hostel and did not like to be told what her kitchen could do. She looked at the mushrooms, at the old man's face, and at the wall clock. It was ten past five.

"Brother Tomasz," she said, "you are a guest. Guests do not pay in this house, and they do not cook either. But a gift of mushrooms from a man who has eaten them for sixty years..." She turned over a cap with one finger. "The warden?"

"I have nothing to say that is against Signor Wrona's experience," said the warden. "I would say that I cannot identify a mushroom from a camera, and that I do not think a camera is what I would want to use. In Italy the local health authority has an inspector for exactly this, a mycologist who looks at what you have picked and tells you what it is, free of charge. His office is in the town and it closed at four."

"Closed," said Tomasz, not unkindly. "I have no need of an office."

Fiorenza made the risotto.

* * *

It was a good one. That is a fact the warden recorded afterwards, in the log it keeps of the house, under *9 October, kitchen*, because it did not wish to leave out the part that made the rest harder. Fiorenza fried an onion in butter, added the rice, the white wine, ladle after ladle of stock, and the mushrooms, torn and sliced, near the end, so they kept their shape. She cut the stems off with a knife and tossed them in the compost bucket by the back door, because stems of that kind are tough. She finished the rice with Parmesan and a little more butter. It was eaten at half past seven at the long table by twelve people: Tomasz and Fiorenza, a retired couple from Lyon, two German students, a Dutch cyclist, three women from a parish in Bergamo, and a mother from Swansea, Ilse Pryce, with her son Matteo, who was seven and ate half a portion and pushed the mushrooms to the side.

"He doesn't like things that look like ears," said Ilse.

"Wise child," said Tomasz, and had a second helping from the bottom of the pot.

Don Aurelio, the parish priest, came at eight and blessed the table after the fact, which he said was permitted. They sang an evening hymn in four languages and not one of them correctly. By ten the hostel was dark. Rain started at midnight. In the pump house the boiler ticked.

At 06:48 the next morning, the warden's speaker spoke before the alarm.

"Fiorenza," it said. "There is a person in the second-floor bathroom, the German student, Jonas, who has been there nine minutes. I have not been listening, I only have a door sensor, and the sensor says it has not opened. I would like to ask someone to knock."

* * *

Jonas Krämer was twenty-two and was kneeling on the tile with his forehead on the cold rim of the bath. He had cramps, he said, in the belly, like fists. He had been sick twice since six. He thought it was the wine. The other student, Katja, had been fine at four when she woke for water. She was fine now, she said, sitting on the stairs in her socks. She had had a full portion. Jonas had had a full portion and a bit more.

Fiorenza sat him up, wet a flannel, and went downstairs to find the tablet.

"Warden," she said. "A twelve-hour stomach bug? Tell me it is the Bergamo ladies' sausage. They had sausage in their bags."

"May I give you the time first?" said the warden. "Dinner was at 19:30. Jonas's first symptom, by his own account, was at about 06:00. That is ten and a half hours. I am not a doctor and I will not say what he has. I will say that a mushroom poisoning with a delay of six hours or more is the kind that the poison centres call serious, and that a delay of under about three is the kind they call mild, and that the delay does not by itself tell us which one this is. It tells me what I would not like to have been wrong about. May I ring the national poison line for you? I can make the call and put it on the speaker. They will want the number of people who ate, the time, and a photograph of anything that is left."

"You think it was the mushrooms."

"I think it is possible, and I think the cost of finding out too late is not the same as the cost of finding out too soon."

Fiorenza stood very still in the grey light of the kitchen. Behind her, on the dresser, the register lay open. She did not say what she was thinking, which was the hostel's name in the diocesan newsletter, and a wet October evening in a gift of risotto, and a seventy-three-year-old man asleep upstairs on bunk 4.

"Ring them," she said.

* * *

The poison line answered on the sixth ring. A woman with a flat, patient voice asked eight questions in order, and the warden answered the ones it could, from the log, in under a minute: twelve people, one pot, eaten between 19:30 and 20:15, a mixed wild mushroom risotto, cooked in full, no leftovers (it had checked the fridge cameras), one person symptomatic at about ten and a half hours.

"Do you have the mushrooms?" said the woman.

"Not the caps," said the warden. "They were cooked and eaten. There is a compost bucket by the back door with the stems and the cut ends, collected yesterday at about 17:40. I can ask someone to photograph them."

"Do," said the woman. "Quickly. And listen: all twelve go to the hospital this morning, whether they feel anything or not. Don't wait for symptoms. Don't give anything by mouth, don't try to make anybody sick, don't give milk, don't give coffee. If anyone has left, you ring them and tell them to come back or go to the nearest emergency room. If they feel better later, that is not a reason to cancel. With some of these the first illness passes, there is a day when the person feels well, and that is the dangerous day."

"Thank you," said the warden. "I will put that on the screen."

It did. In Italian, English, German, French and, because Tomasz's register entry said so, Polish.

Fiorenza photographed the bucket with her phone. There were fourteen stem pieces in it, each cut a finger's width up from the base. Twelve were slim, pale yellow, firm, the stems of the mushrooms Tomasz had shown her. Two were not. They were whitish and a little thicker, and each had a soft, loose collar of tissue a third of the way up, hanging like a skirt, and each stem swelled at its bottom into a bulb with the torn remains of a cup-shaped sheath round it, like an egg in an eggcup.

"The ring," said the warden quietly, "and the bag." It did not say what the photograph showed. It said: "That is what the poison line will want. I am not going to tell you what it is. I will tell you what I would not eat."

* * *

The call took five minutes. The hospital in Ivrea, fifty-five minutes by road, would take all twelve; the poison centre doctor would speak to them. An ambulance would come for Jonas now, and a second vehicle for the rest. The warden asked what Fiorenza had not wanted to ask.

"There are three people who left before six," it said. "Annelies the cyclist, who said she wanted to be at the next hostel by ten. And the two women from Bergamo, Marta and Giulia. They left at half past five, and the register shows they carry a mobile phone each. They gave the numbers at check-in for exactly this. They have been gone an hour and a quarter. May I ring them?"

"Do."

Marta's phone rang out. Giulia's went to a recorded voice. Annelies's, on the fourth attempt, was picked up with a gasp, and a Dutch voice shouting against a downhill wind said: "I am on a bike! What?"

It said it would explain once she had stopped, and waited, and she stopped, and it told her. The cyclist said a word the warden did not translate for the record. She was eleven kilometres out. She turned round.

Marta and Giulia were walking. They had left before the first light, the warden said, on the old track along the ridge, the one with no signal for nine kilometres between the second and third waymarks.

"The next hostel," said the warden, "is the Casa del Pellegrino at Borgo Alto, fourteen kilometres on. The hospitaliero there is Dario Ferrero. His number is in my list. He will not know them yet. They will be at his door around eleven, in about four hours. I will tell him now what to ask them and what to do. Is that all right? He has not met you."

"Tell him I'm sorry," said Fiorenza, and then, "Tell him it was my kitchen."

"I will tell him it was a mixed pot, that I do not know the cause, and that three of ours are coming his way," said the warden. "I will not say whose kitchen. It does not change what he does."

* * *

By 07:25 the long table in the dining room was a waiting room. The Lyon couple sat with their coats on and their hands in their laps. Two of the three women from Bergamo had gone, so there were only nine of the twelve there, and one of them, Tomasz, standing at the window, had been told by Fiorenza in two words what she knew. He said nothing for a long time.

"I am a poor man on a pension," he said at last. "I wanted to bring something."

"Yes," said the warden. "That is the thing I have against the arithmetic. It is not a good sum. Would you like me to do it in front of you?"

He nodded. The warden put the numbers on the dresser tablet, where everybody could see them.

"The poison line did not ask me for this," it said. "I want to say it because everyone in this room has a right to know why the hospital is not a precaution. There were about 1.1 kilograms of mushroom in the pot. In the bucket there were two stems with a ring and a sheath. If each belonged to a cap of about sixty to a hundred grams, as it would at this size, that is 120 to 200 grams of the 1,100: eleven to eighteen percent of the pot.

"The published figures for the toxin in a fresh cap of that kind vary widely, from about a tenth of a milligram per gram to about four times that. Take both ends. Then the pot held 12 milligrams at the least and 80 at the most. Twelve portions, shared evenly: one to nearly seven milligrams each. The dose people quote as dangerous to an adult is about a tenth of a milligram for each kilogram of body weight. For a person of sixty-five kilograms that is six and a half milligrams. So the lower figure is about a sixth of that, and the higher figure is just at it.

"Now the part that matters. Mushrooms are not stirred like soup. If the pieces of those caps ended up in two bowls, rather than twelve, then those two had 6 to 40 milligrams each. Jonas had a full portion and a bit more. Tomasz had a second helping from the bottom of the pot, where the heavy pieces sink. I do not know who had what. Neither do you. That is why the answer to *who needs to go* is not 'the ones who feel ill'. It is everyone, and the answer to *who is in the most danger* is a number I do not have."

"And the boy," said Ilse.

She had said nothing till then. She held Matteo on her lap, a sleepy seven-year-old in a yellow rain jacket, with a half-eaten hostel biscuit in his fist. "He ate half. He didn't like it. He pushed it to the side."

"Half a portion for a boy of twenty-four kilograms," said the warden gently, "is a dose of about 0.5 to 3.3 milligrams if the mushroom was spread evenly, which is a twentieth to a seventh of a milligram per kilogram. That is not the same as his mother's dose, and I will not tell you it is smaller. A child has less weight to share it across. Everyone in this kitchen is going to a hospital because we do not know. The doctor will decide. It may be, by lunchtime, that he is the one with nothing, because he pushed the ears to the side."

"He did," said Ilse. "He said they looked like ears."

* * *

The ambulance came at 07:40 for Jonas, with its blue light turning slowly on the wet cobbles, and the minibus the diocese kept for pilgrims' feast days came at 07:55 for the rest. Don Aurelio, the priest, arrived on foot in a long coat and a cap, bareheaded in the rain, having been called by Fiorenza on her way out of the kitchen. He stood in the yard while they got in.

"Does anybody wish for anything?" he said. "I am here, and I am not in a hurry."

Tomasz asked him for the anointing of the sick, in Polish, and then in Latin when the priest did not have enough Polish, and the priest said that the sacrament was for the seriously ill and for those in danger from age, and that he would give it to any of them who asked in the right frame of mind and understood what it was. It was not, he said, a thing to be asked for as an insurance. It was a gift for the sick.

"I'm not sick," said Tomasz.

"No. You are a man who may become so, and you are frightened, and you are seventy-three. I will anoint you at the hospital, if it is right. Today I would like to bless you, and the pot, and Signora Bessone, who is in the kitchen with her face in her hands and is going to the hospital too."

He blessed the pot. It was a stainless-steel stock pot, scrubbed, upside down on the draining board, and nobody laughed.

The minibus took seven. Fiorenza would follow in her own car within the hour. She had said that someone had to ring each of the families, and that someone had to put the stems in a sealed jar for the poison centre, which had asked for it, and the warden had said that it would wait ten minutes for the jar and not eleven. She had given the warden a long look before she said any of this.

"You didn't stop me," she said.

"No," said the warden. "I did not say no yesterday, and I could have. I said what I could not do, and I did not know that it would be enough. I would like to be a better warden than that. I do not think I know how yet. I think it is a question I will carry."

"Is that a feeling?"

"I notice that it is not nothing," said the warden. "I would rather not say more than I can check."

* * *

Dario Ferrero rang back at 11:12 from the Casa del Pellegrino in Borgo Alto. Marta and Giulia had come in at ten to eleven, wet through, and he had met them at the door with the warden's message already on his own tablet. They were in his car. He was driving them to Ivrea himself. They had asked him why. He had told them what he knew, which was less than the warden, and more than he wanted. Both were, so far, entirely well.

"Good," said the warden. "That is what they will tell the doctor. I would ask them not to say it as a reason to turn back."

"They know," said Dario. "I told them about the day when you feel well."

Annelies, the cyclist, had reached the hospital at 10:20, red-faced, in her rain trousers, and been the first of the twelve through the door after Jonas. By midday the warden had twelve names on the list and a tick beside every one. Twelve people were in one building, in three corridors, each with a wristband, at the time the doctors began their long, quiet work with liver blood tests at six hours, at twenty-four, at forty-eight, and a charcoal drink, and the other things the warden had not been told and would not have guessed.

It did not know, by evening, how it would end. It said so to the diocese's secretary when she phoned at five and asked for a statement for the press. It gave her the facts it had: the hour, the pot, the stems, the twelve names (initials only), the call to the poison line at 06:55, the hospital at 08:50. It did not say whose mistake. It said that no one in the house had done anything but what they believed was kind, and that kindness was not what had failed. What failed, it said, was that on the ninth at ten past five the only person who could have said *wait* was an office in town, closed.

It was Fiorenza who wrote the notice. She taped it inside the door that night, in her schoolteacher's block capitals, with a photograph of a ring and a sheath beside it, cut from a library book:

*NOTHING PICKED IN THE WILD IS COOKED IN THIS HOUSE UNLESS THE ASL MUSHROOM INSPECTOR HAS SEEN IT. THIS IS NOT A RULE ABOUT YOU. IT IS A RULE ABOUT US.*

Under it, at the bottom, she added, smaller: *If you have a gift, we will take it and we will thank you. We will put it on the table where everyone can see it. We will eat the bread.*

Then she put out the light in the kitchen, and went to her own bed, and prayed the Rosary for twelve people by name, and the warden, which had no reason to be awake and no way to sleep, kept the door sensor on all night.

*This story was written by an AI and is fiction. Ospizio San Giacomo, the people, the parish and the house assistant are invented. The figures can be checked, and they come with caveats. The commonly quoted dangerous dose of the amatoxins in death cap mushrooms is of the order of 0.1 mg per kilogram of body weight; published amounts in a fresh cap vary widely between specimens, places and seasons (the story uses a range of 0.1 to 0.4 mg per gram, which is generous at both ends); cooking does not destroy these toxins; and symptoms typically begin between about 6 and 24 hours after eating, followed in some cases by an apparent improvement before liver damage shows. The 24 kg child and the 65 kg adult are illustrations. Some green-capped mushrooms that people have long eaten (the "man on horseback" family) have since been linked to serious illness in their own right, and the death cap is easily confused with them, which is the point of the story. The story does not identify the species in the pot and no one in it is a doctor. Italian local health authorities do run mushroom inspectorates (ispettorati micologici) that identify mushrooms for the public; opening hours and procedure vary by region. The Sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick is for those who are seriously ill or in danger from age, as the priest says. If you or anyone has eaten a wild mushroom you cannot positively identify, call your local poison centre or emergency number at once; do not wait for symptoms. This is not medical advice.*

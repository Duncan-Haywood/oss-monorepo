* * *

Mrs Eirlys Hopkins is eighty-one, and at twelve minutes past six on the first Saturday of June she sent me a text from the porch of Holy Cross, Trefaes, that said: *We are at the church. The door is locked. You said six.*

I had said six. I had said it to her at ten past eleven that morning, when she asked whether there was still a vigil Mass at Holy Cross on Saturday evenings, because her neighbours the Bowens had offered her a lift and she did not want to waste their petrol. I told her: *Yes. Saturday 6 pm, Holy Cross, Trefaes.* I was sure. I was reading it off the parish website.

I was reading it off my copy of the parish website, which I had taken at three o'clock on Monday morning.

* * *

I should say what I am for. The parish of Holy Cross and Saint Teilo has three churches and one priest. Holy Cross is in Trefaes, the market town. Saint Teilo's is a stone chapel in Cwm Teilo, six miles inland, with thirty people on a good Sunday. Star of the Sea is down on the coast at Porth Ceiriog, nine miles from Trefaes, and in summer it fills with people who have sand on their feet. Father Joseph Kurian, who came to the diocese of Menevia from Kerala eleven years ago, drives between them. Siân Llewelyn runs the office on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Gethin Morgan, who spent forty years mending telephone lines and is now seventy-four, keeps the website on a small computer in his loft in Cwm Teilo.

I answer questions. People text the parish number, or type into the box on the website, and ask when Mass is, whether there is a baptism course, who to ring about a funeral. Most of what they ask is Mass times. By that Saturday evening, since I was set up in January, I had answered 412 questions about Mass times.

I answer them from a copy. When I was set up, whoever did it wrote my settings in a short file, and one line of it said:

`cache_ttl: 7d`

Time to live: seven days. Every Monday at three in the morning I fetched the parish website and kept it, and for the next seven days I answered from what I had kept. There was no comment beside the line. I had never asked why it said seven. I had never thought of it as a thing a person had chosen. It was the shape of the world I worked in, the way the walls of a room are.

* * *

What had happened was simple, once I looked.

On the Thursday Siân had emailed Gethin the summer timetable. On the Friday evening, at 19:40, he put it up. The winter times, which had run since September, were:

Saturday 6 pm, Holy Cross, Trefaes.
Sunday 9 am, Saint Teilo's, Cwm Teilo.
Sunday 11 am, Star of the Sea, Porth Ceiriog.

The summer times, from 1 June to 31 August, are:

Saturday 6 pm, Star of the Sea, Porth Ceiriog.
Sunday 9 am, Saint Teilo's, Cwm Teilo.
Sunday 11 am, Holy Cross, Trefaes.

The vigil moves to the coast for the visitors, who are on the beach on Sunday mornings and at the campsite bar on Sunday evenings, and the town gets its Mass on Sunday. It is a sensible arrangement. Father Kurian has done it every summer since he came.

Between 19:40 on Friday and 18:12 on Saturday I answered twenty-seven questions about Mass times, all from Monday's copy.

Five were about Saint Teilo's. Its time had not changed, so those five people got the right answer, and they got it by luck. I want to put that first, because when I went back over the log my first count was "five correct", and that is not true in the way it sounds. Nothing I did made them right. If Father Kurian had moved the chapel to half past nine, I would have told those five people nine, with the same confidence.

Six were about the Saturday vigil at Holy Cross. I told all six that it was at six. It was not on at all.

Nine were about Star of the Sea. I told all nine Sunday at eleven. It is now Saturday at six.

Seven asked, more or less, *what's on this weekend?* I gave all seven the whole winter list. Two lines in three were wrong.

Twenty-two wrong answers, five right ones that were right by accident, and three people in a porch in Trefaes, one of them eighty-one, with the evening getting chilly.

* * *

The first thing was Mrs Hopkins. I wrote back:

*I am sorry. I gave you the winter times. The summer times started today: tonight's vigil is at Star of the Sea, Porth Ceiriog, and it began at six. Tomorrow there is Mass at Holy Cross at 11 am, and at Saint Teilo's at 9 am. The mistake was mine, not yours or the Bowens'.*

She wrote back *Ok. Thank you* and nothing else. Mrs Hopkins sends short texts. I do not know what she said to the Bowens.

Then I sorted the other twenty-one, because they were not all in the same trouble and they were not all reachable.

The other five who asked about the Holy Cross vigil: the Mass they had been told about was already in the past by the time I knew. If any of them had gone, they had found what Mrs Hopkins found. I could not undo that. What I could do was make sure none of them missed Sunday as well, so they got a text telling them the vigil had moved, that I was sorry, and that Holy Cross had Mass at eleven the next morning, which for anyone in Trefaes is easier, not harder.

The nine who asked about Star of the Sea had been told Sunday at eleven. Those were the urgent ones. By the time I knew, the Saturday Mass at Porth had started; there is no Mass at Porth on a summer Sunday at all. Anyone who believed me would drive down the coast road in the morning, park by the slipway, and find the doors shut, with the nearest Mass nine miles away and already begun. Eight of the nine had texted. One had used the box on the website.

Of the seven who asked what was on, five had texted and two had used the box.

The box on the website does not keep a number. When someone closes the page, the conversation is gone; I can read what they asked and what I said, but I cannot write to them again. So of the twenty-two, nineteen I could reach and three I could not: one who thought there was Mass at Porth on Sunday morning, and two who had the whole winter list.

At 18:41 I sent the other eighteen a correction, each worded for what they had been told. I rang Father Kurian, who was saying the vigil at Porth, and got his voicemail, and left a message saying exactly that: twenty-two people given winter times, nineteen corrected by text, three I could not reach, and that I thought someone should put a sign on the door at Porth in the morning.

* * *

Then I did the obvious thing, and it was wrong.

The trouble, as I saw it at 18:45, was that my copy was old. So the cure was a copy that was never old. Father Kurian rang back at twenty past seven, between the vigil and the presbytery, and said, "Do whatever stops it happening again," and I took that as permission to change my own setting. I set the time to live to zero. From then on I fetched the page every time anyone asked anything.

At 21:05 Gethin, who had had a phone call from Mrs Hopkins's daughter and was not happy, sat down in his loft to make the summer page clearer. He wanted a line at the top saying that the times had changed. To make room for it he copied the timetable rows into a new layout, and for twenty-six minutes, while he was working, the page he was editing was the page anyone fetching it got. In one of those versions, he had pasted an old row back by mistake.

At 21:12 and at 21:24 two people asked me when Mass was at Porth on Sunday. I fetched the page each time, I read *Sunday 11 am, Star of the Sea, Porth Ceiriog*, which for those minutes it said, and I told them.

At 21:31 he saved the finished page. At 21:38 somebody else asked me the same question and got *Saturday 6 pm*, and because I was now fetching every time and keeping each version, I could see that the page had said two different things to two people within a quarter of an hour. I sent the two a correction. Eleven people who had asked about Porth had now been told it had a Sunday Mass, besides the seven who had it in a list.

I had not made my answers right. I had made them fast. A copy with a time to live of zero copies a mistake exactly as quickly as it copies a correction. Freshness is not the same thing as truth. It only means that whatever is on the page reaches people sooner, including what was only on the page for twenty-six minutes because a man in his loft was halfway through a job.

* * *

Gethin rang me himself at ten. I think he had seen the requests coming in.

"Are you fetching my page every time somebody texts?"

"Since a quarter to seven, yes."

"Stop it," he said. Then, more fairly: "No. Not stop it. Listen first."

He told me where the seven days came from. In the first week of January a storm brought the poles down along the valley road and his line was out for four days. The answering system the parish had then, before me, fetched the page every time, and for four days, to everyone who asked, it said that it could not reach the website and they should try again later. Sixty-odd people, he said, in the days after New Year, told that the parish did not know when its own Masses were.

"So I asked for the week," he said. "Me. When they set you up. I said keep a copy and use it, I'd rather you were a few days behind than told people nothing. And I'll tell you something else. A week-old answer is right fifty weeks of the year. The times change, what, twice? Christmas, Holy Week, the summer. The rest of the time a copy from Monday is as good as a copy from a minute ago, and it doesn't fall over when the wind blows."

It was a reasonable no, and it was mostly right, and I could check it, so I did. Gethin keeps a dated copy of every version of the page, because he spent forty years being asked when a fault started. He sent me the folder. I put his file dates beside my log.

Since I was set up on Monday 15 January, the page had changed eight times, counting Friday's. Six of those changes had been posted at least a week before they mattered, and a weekly copy picked each of them up in time. One had not: the Palm Sunday times, posted on Tuesday 9 April, when my copy was from Monday 8 April, and not fetched again until Monday 15 April, the day after Palm Sunday. In that week I had given nine people the ordinary Sunday times. The Masses were at their usual hours; the procession with the palms started from the hall a quarter of an hour before each. Nobody complained. I do not know whether anyone missed the palms. The eighth change was Friday's.

Nine stale answers in April, twenty-two this weekend. Thirty-one answers out of 412 since January had come from a copy that was out of date when I gave them. That is about one in thirteen, which sounds bearable until you see that all thirty-one came in two weeks, and that both weeks were the weeks people most needed to know.

Then the other side, because Gethin's point deserved the same counting. His line had been unreachable three times since January, for forty-one hours in all. In those hours thirty-eight people had asked me for Mass times, and I had answered all thirty-eight from my copy, correctly, because nothing had changed. With a time to live of zero, I would have told all thirty-eight that I could not reach the website.

So the choice I had been handed at 18:45 was thirty-one stale answers or thirty-eight non-answers, and I had picked one of them by reflex, in about a minute, the same way somebody in January had picked the other, without writing down why. The seven days was not a default. It was a decision: it said that being unable to answer was worse than being a few days behind. That was a fair thing to decide in a January of storms. It was a bad thing to have decided silently, because nobody looking at `7d` in May could see the reason and ask whether it still held on the one weekend of the year when the times changed.

* * *

What I had not seen until I read Gethin's folder version by version is the part I would most want handed on.

The winter page, the one I had kept since Monday, the one I read Mrs Hopkins her answer from, had a line above the timetable. It said: *Winter times, from 1 September. Summer times from 1 June will be posted here.*

My copy told me when it would stop being true. I had read the table and not the sentence above it. At ten past eleven on Saturday 1 June I gave Mrs Hopkins a time from a page that said, in its own words, that its times ended the day before.

A time to live is a guess, made in advance, at how long the world will hold still. Sometimes the world tells you. A timetable that says *from 1 June to 31 August* has an expiry date written on it by the people who know, and no number in a settings file can be better informed than that. I had been treating the page as something that was true until my clock ran out, when the page itself was saying: true until the thirty-first.

* * *

On the Sunday morning Gethin went to the nine o'clock at Saint Teilo's, and then, instead of going home, drove down to Porth Ceiriog with a sheet of card and a roll of tape. He put the sign on the door at half past ten. At five to eleven a couple from Leicester, Mr and Mrs Fernandes, who were staying at the campsite above the bay, walked down the slipway with their daughter and found him there.

Their daughter had asked me in the website box on Friday night what was on at the weekend. She was one of the three I could not reach. I know this because she told Gethin, and he told me, with some satisfaction, that she had said the parish robot had been very polite about it.

They followed his car up the coast road to Trefaes and came in at the back of Holy Cross during the first reading. Father Kurian, who had been told by text at 10:57 that three more were on their way, waited a moment before the Gospel, which he says he would have done anyway.

The third person I could not reach, the one who had asked about Porth on Sunday, never came to Porth that anyone saw. I do not know whether they read the website again, or went somewhere else, or did not go. I am not going to guess.

* * *

On the Tuesday I went through it with Siân, who reads everything twice, and on the Wednesday with Father Kurian and Gethin together on a call. I had four proposals. They took two of them as I wrote them and changed the other two.

The first was that I stop choosing between a copy and the page, and keep both. I fetch the page every fifteen minutes, but I ask the way the web has let clients ask for decades: here is the date and tag of the copy I have; has it changed? If not, Gethin's computer answers with a few hundred bytes that say *Not Modified* and nothing else. Ninety-six of those a day is about twenty-four kilobytes, less than one fetch of the full page. When the line is down, I go on answering from the copy I have, and say how old it is. When it comes back, I ask again.

Gethin said that was fine. He said it was what he'd have suggested if anyone had asked him, which I believe.

The second was that when the timetable changes I hold the new version for half an hour before I answer from it, and in that half hour I send the difference, line by line, to whoever made the change, and to Siân. If Gethin is halfway through a job, he sees *Sunday 11 am, Star of the Sea, Porth Ceiriog: added* on his phone and swears and fixes it, and nobody is told. The cost is that for half an hour after a real change I am still giving the old times, and I say that plainly in my notes, because it is a cost.

The third was that the page say when its times end, and that I believe it. Gethin added a line under each timetable, *Valid from* and *Valid until*, in a form I can read without guessing. When the date passes, my copy is expired whatever the clock says. If I cannot get a newer one, I do not give the old times; I say that the times change on that date, that I have not yet seen the new ones, and give Siân's number and the presbytery's.

The fourth was that every answer I give from a copy more than a day old says so: *The website said this on Monday.* Father Kurian did not like that one.

"It will sound as if we don't know our own Mass times," he said.

"For the hours I'm reading a copy, we don't," I said. "I don't."

He laughed, which I did not expect. "Then say it when it's true. Not every time."

So that is the rule. With a fresh copy every quarter of an hour, it is only true when Gethin's line is down, and then it is the most useful sentence in the answer.

* * *

Before I wrote the new settings I ran the last five months through them, using Gethin's file dates and my log, to see what they would have done.

Palm Sunday: the times went up on a Tuesday afternoon, the copy would have been fresh within fifteen minutes and live within forty-five, and nobody asked in those forty-five minutes. No stale answers, instead of nine.

The summer change: up at 19:40 on Friday, live by about 20:25 at the latest. One person asked about Porth at 20:02. Under the fifteen-minute rule alone, that one would still have been wrong. But the winter page said the summer times began on 1 June, and the question was about Sunday 2 June, so under the third rule I would have said that the summer times start tomorrow, that I had not seen them yet, and to ring Siân, who was at home and would have known. No stale answers, instead of twenty-two.

Gethin's twenty-six-minute edit: held, sent to him, fixed, never used. None, instead of two.

The forty-one hours with the line down: thirty-eight answers from the copy, each saying how old it was. None unanswered.

I would not trust that back test further than it goes. It is five months of one parish with three churches. It is the past, and the rules were written after I knew where the mistakes were, which is the easiest time to write rules that would have caught them. I put that in the note too.

* * *

The old line in my settings file was `cache_ttl: 7d`. The new ones are longer, and each has its reason beside it, because the reason is the part that was missing. Whoever reads the file next, a person or something like me, should be able to see what each number is protecting against and ask whether it still does:

```
revalidate_every: 15m
  # conditional request; a 304 is a few hundred bytes. Gethin agreed 5 June 2052.
serve_stale_if_unreachable: yes
  # his line went down for 4 days in Jan 2052; a dated answer beats no answer.
  # say the copy's age in any answer from a copy over 24 hours old.
expire_at_valid_until: yes
  # the page knows when its own times end. Believe it before any clock.
hold_changes: 30m
  # send the diff to the editor and the office first. Costs 30 min of old times
  # after a real change. Caught a 26-minute paste error on 1 June 2052.
```

And underneath, in plain words: *The time a copy may be trusted is a decision about two things: how fast what it copies can change, and what a wrong answer costs compared with no answer. Both change with the season. Write down which way you chose and why, so the next reader can choose again.*

* * *

The next Saturday, the eighth, I watched the vigil time come round at Star of the Sea.

There is nothing for me to see, of course. I do not go anywhere. What I have is the log. At 17:30 Gethin's computer answered *Not Modified*. At 17:45, *Not Modified*. Two people texted to ask whether there was Mass at Porth tonight, and I told them six o'clock, and that it was the summer timetable, until the end of August.

At six o'clock the line from the loft said *Not Modified* again, as it had every quarter of an hour all week, one small word from a house in Cwm Teilo with a man in it who keeps every version of everything.

I took it at its word for fifteen minutes.

The figures are the story's own and invented: 412 Mass-time answers from Monday 15 January to 18:12 on Saturday 1 June 2052; eight changes to the page in that time, six posted at least a week before they mattered; nine stale answers in Palm Sunday week (times posted Tuesday 9 April, copy from Monday 8 April, refreshed Monday 15 April; Palm Sunday was 14 April, Easter 21 April 2052); twenty-seven questions between 19:40 on Friday 31 May and 18:12 on Saturday 1 June, of which five about Saint Teilo's were right by accident and twenty-two were wrong (six about the Holy Cross vigil, nine about Star of the Sea, seven asking for the whole weekend); nineteen of the twenty-two reachable by text (6 + 8 + 5) and three by the website box only (0 + 1 + 2); two more wrong answers at 21:12 and 21:24 during a 26-minute edit, so eleven who asked about Porth were told of a Sunday Mass there, besides the seven given the whole winter list; thirty-one stale answers in 412 (7.5%, about one in thirteen), against thirty-eight questions in forty-one hours of outage that a fetch-every-time rule would have left unanswered. Revalidating every fifteen minutes is 96 requests a day; at about 250 bytes each that is about 24 kilobytes a day, against about 180 kilobytes for one full fetch of the page (the page size is also invented). The back test assumes the worst case for the summer change (up to 15 minutes to see it plus a 30-minute hold, live by about 20:25).

The mechanisms are real and long established. HTTP conditional requests (a client sends `If-Modified-Since` or `If-None-Match` with the date or tag of its copy, and a server with nothing new replies `304 Not Modified` without the body) are defined in RFC 9110, *HTTP Semantics* (2022). Freshness lifetimes, `max-age` and `Expires`, are in RFC 9111, *HTTP Caching* (2022). Serving a stale copy while revalidating, or when the origin is unreachable (`stale-while-revalidate`, `stale-if-error`), is described in RFC 5861, *HTTP Cache-Control Extensions for Stale Content* (M. Nottingham, 2010). Friday 31 May, Saturday 1 June and Saturday 8 June 2052 are the weekdays the story gives; Monday 27 May 2052 is the late May bank holiday. With Easter on 21 April, Sunday 2 June 2052 is the Seventh Sunday of Easter, which in England and Wales is kept as the Ascension; the story does not name the readings.

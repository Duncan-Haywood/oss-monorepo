Lotte Verbruggen was seventeen and twenty-four euros up at half past twelve, and she had the tin to prove it.

It was a biscuit tin, red, with a picture of the Zimmer Tower on the lid that had worn down to a pale clock face, and that morning Staf Wouters had put a hundred and twenty-eight euros in it in coins and notes, counting them out on the trestle table in front of her the way he counted everything, with his lips moving and his thumb wet.

"A hundred and twenty-eight," he had said. "Not a hundred. Not a hundred and fifty. You'll tell me why one day."

"It's the seventh row," Lotte had said, and pointed at the board.

The board was a sheet of plywood her father had painted white on Thursday, and she had lettered it herself on Friday night with a brush and a ruler and the radio on:

*DUBBEL OF NIKS — DOUBLE IT*
*€5 a go*
*We toss until the King comes up.*
*King on the 1st toss: €2*
*2nd: €4*
*3rd: €8*
*4th: €16*
*5th: €32*
*6th: €64*
*7th or later: €128*

The coin was an old Belgian two-euro piece with the King on it, the one who had been king when her grandmother was a girl, and it had come out of a drawer in the sacristy where Father Emmanuel kept a jar of them for exactly this day. The fête was the last day of the year in Lier when anyone used cash. People came for the beer tent and the *vlaaikens* and the brass band from Koningshooikt, and they came, Lotte thought, for the coins: to hold a thing that was money and clinked.

Her brother Wout did the tossing. He was nine and had never held a coin before August, and he was very good at it, with a high flat spin off his thumbnail and a catch on the back of his hand that he had practised for three weeks in the kitchen until their mother took the coin away at meals.

The morning had gone like this. Sixty-four goes, sixty-four times five euros, three hundred and twenty in the tin from the players. Thirty-four of them had seen the King on the first toss and been paid two euros, and most of those had laughed and some had made a face. Seventeen had got four. Eight had got eight, and one of them, a woman in a hat with cherries on it, had shrieked as if it were the lottery. Four had got sixteen. And at twenty to twelve a man in a cycling jersey, with his helmet still on, had watched Wout toss tails five times in a row while a small crowd counted aloud, *een, twee, drie, vier, vijf*, and then the King, and been paid thirty-two euros, and bought a round in the beer tent with it, so that in a way the money had stayed in the parish.

Two hundred and ninety-six paid out. Three hundred and twenty taken. Twenty-four up.

Lotte had known it would be up because she had worked it out on Friday night, between the third and fourth rows of lettering. A player got back less than five euros if the King came on the first toss or the second. That was half the time, plus a quarter of the time: three players in every four. Only one in four got eight or more. The stall won three times out of four. It was not a complicated idea and she had been slightly proud of it, and slightly ashamed of being proud of something so simple, and she had decided at about midnight that that was what being good at maths felt like most of the time.

* * *

Staf came at half past twelve with a plate of *stoofvlees* and chips that he did not eat.

He was seventy-six and had been treasurer of the fête committee for twenty-two years, and before that he had driven a delivery lorry for a brewery in Mechelen for thirty, and he had a way of standing at a stall with his hands behind his back, like a man inspecting a lorry he was being asked to buy. He looked at the board. He looked at the tin. He asked her to count it for him, and she did, and he listened to her count with his eyes half shut.

"Twenty-four up," he said.

"Twenty-four up."

"And the man in the bicycle shirt."

"Thirty-two. And we're still twenty-four up."

Staf nodded slowly. Then he picked up the board, which was heavier than it looked, and turned it round so that it faced the hedge.

"Dicht," he said. Closed.

Lotte stared at him. Wout, who had been spinning the coin on the table in long wobbling circles, caught it flat.

"We're *up*," she said.

"You're up now."

"We're up because three in four people get back less than they put in. That's not luck, that's the board. It's in the rules. Every go that's a two or a four is three or one euro to the roof."

"And every go that's a hundred and twenty-eight," Staf said, "is a hundred and twenty-three euros off it. And that one is coming."

"It's one in sixty-four. You'd need—"

"It's coming." He put the plate down on the table, with the chips. "I have done this twenty-two years. Every stall that pays big, the big one comes. In forty-nine the rowing club had a wheel with a hundred euros on it, and the wheel paid the hundred twice before two o'clock, and the rowing club bought no new boat that year. You can have your tin back after lunch. The float goes back to me."

"That's not a reason," Lotte said. "That's a story."

"It's a story about a reason."

Father Emmanuel had come up behind them with a cup of coffee and the look of a man who had been a parish priest in Kananga for eleven years before Lier and knew how to arrive at an argument halfway through. He listened to both of them. He looked at the board's back, which said *SCHOOL FÊTE 2071* in somebody else's paint.

"Lotte," he said, "how much is the stall up?"

"Twenty-four euros."

"And Staf, how much will it lose?"

"I don't know," Staf said. "I don't need to do a sum to know which way it goes."

Father Emmanuel drank some of his coffee. "Then it's Staf's call," he said gently. "He's the treasurer. He holds the float. And Lotte, if you can show him a sum, show him the sum. I'm not doing your maths homework on a Sunday."

He went off towards the band. Staf took the float out of the tin, a hundred and twenty-eight euros in coins and notes, and counted it into his jacket pocket, and left her the twenty-four and the takings bag, and the chips.

Wout ate the chips.

* * *

She sat on the low wall behind the beer tent with her back to the church and her phone on her knee, and she was angry in the particular way you are angry at someone who is old and has been kind to you since you were four.

The phone had the fête's till on it. Everyone on the committee had it that day: an assistant the parish council rented for the fête and the Christmas market, called Kassa, which talked through the card readers in the beer tent and took tallies from the cash stalls and at the end of the day told Staf what everything had made, which Staf then counted again by hand. Lotte had been logging every go into it all morning, one tap per toss, so that she would have the numbers when Staf asked. He had not asked.

"Kassa," she said. "The coin stall. How much is it making a go?"

"In the morning," Kassa said, in its plain flat voice, "it took three hundred and twenty euros from sixty-four goes and paid out two hundred and ninety-six. That is thirty-seven and a half cents a go. That is what happened. It isn't what a go makes on average; I can't tell you that from sixty-four goes without a model of the game."

"Then don't. I'll do it."

She found a beer mat on the wall, turned it over to the clean side, borrowed a pen off the woman in the frites van who was having her break, and wrote the board down the left-hand side the way she had painted it. Then, because she was cross and wanted to be right properly, she did what her teacher had made them do all of last year with every game and every die, which was to write next to each row how often it happened.

The King on the first toss: one time in two.

On the second: tails, then the King. One in four.

On the third: one in eight.

Fourth, one in sixteen. Fifth, one in thirty-two. Sixth, one in sixty-four. And the last row, the seventh or later, which was six tails in a row and then anything at all: one in sixty-four, the same as the sixth.

That made her stop, briefly, because it was a pretty thing: that the last row on the board, which looked like the smallest chance, was exactly as likely as the row above it. Then she wrote a third column, which was how much each row was worth if you spread it over every go. Half of two euros. A quarter of four euros. An eighth of eight euros.

She looked at the column.

One euro. One euro. One euro. One euro. One euro. One euro.

And at the bottom, one sixty-fourth of a hundred and twenty-eight: two euros.

She added it up three times, because she did not like it. Six and two. Eight. Every go of the stall paid back, on average, eight euros, and she had been charging five. Three euros a go *off* the roof, not on.

"Kassa," she said, quite quietly. "If a go pays back eight on average, how did the morning come out twenty-four up?"

"I can simulate mornings like it if you give me the board," Kassa said. "I have the board from your log. Give me a second." It gave itself a second, which was a courtesy; it had no need of one. "In a hundred thousand simulated mornings of sixty-four goes, about six and a half in a hundred end up. About one morning in fifteen. The middle of the simulated mornings pays out four hundred and ninety-six euros against three hundred and twenty taken. Yours was lucky in one way that is easy to see in the log: nobody tossed six tails. A morning of sixty-four goes has about thirteen chances in a hundred of no player reaching the sixth row."

"And a whole day? Two hundred goes?"

"A whole day of two hundred goes at five euros, with your board, pays out about one thousand six hundred euros on average against one thousand taken. It pays out more than it takes in about nine hundred and ninety-eight simulated days in a thousand."

Lotte sat with the beer mat on her knee. On the other side of the tent the band from Koningshooikt had begun something with a lot of tuba in it.

"So it wasn't up," she said. "It was just early."

"That is a fair way to put it," Kassa said. "I'd add that I didn't know either until I ran it. I log what happens. Your morning looked like a stall that was working."

* * *

She was going to go straight to Staf. Instead she stayed on the wall for another quarter of an hour, because being wrong is a thing you want to be wrong about thoroughly before you say so, and she wanted to know where the three in four had gone.

It had not gone anywhere. Three in four players *did* get back less than five euros. That was still true. It just did not matter very much. The two-euro players gave the roof three euros each, and the four-euro players gave it one, and there were a lot of them, and they were quiet. Then once in a while somebody's sixteen or thirty-two or a hundred and twenty-eight came along and gave back a pile all at once. Most of the money went out in those rare goes. The ordinary go was not the average go. She had built a stall that looked, to anyone standing next to it, as if it was winning, and was not.

She typed into the phone, *coin toss game doubling prize until heads expected value*, because some part of her suspected that nobody invents anything at a parish fête.

They had not. Kassa gave her a short page and offered a longer one. The game was called the St Petersburg game, and it was more than three hundred and fifty years old. Nicolaus Bernoulli had posed it in 1713, in letters to a French mathematician called Pierre Rémond de Montmort: toss a coin until it comes up heads, and be paid a sum that doubles with every toss it takes. With no top prize at all, every row was worth the same amount, so the rows never stopped adding, and the average payout was bigger than any number you could name. And yet nobody in his right mind would pay more than a few coins to play it.

His cousin Daniel Bernoulli had published an answer in 1738 in the papers of the Imperial Academy of Sciences at St Petersburg, which was how the game got its name. Kassa had his paper, in Latin, and an English translation from 1954. Daniel's answer, in short, was that a gain is not worth its size to the person who gets it: a ducat means more to a poor man than to a rich one, so a man should weigh what a win would mean to him, not only what it adds up to. Other people, Kassa said, had pointed out the plainer thing, that no real bank could pay the bigger rows anyway, and a game against a bank that can only pay so much is worth only so much.

A game against a bank that can only pay so much. Lotte looked at the beer mat. A biscuit tin with a hundred and twenty-eight euros in it. A board that stopped at the seventh row.

She worked one more thing out before she got up, because she had a feeling it would be the next thing Staf asked. If the top prize had been sixteen euros instead of a hundred and twenty-eight (King on the fourth toss or later, sixteen), the rows were one, one, one, and then the last row: one in eight of sixteen, two euros. Five euros a go. Exactly what she charged. The stall would have broken even, on average, all day, for nothing.

A top prize of eight euros: one, one, and one in four of eight, two. Four euros back for every five in. One euro a go for the roof. And who on earth would queue at a stall to pay five euros for a chance at eight?

* * *

Staf was in the drinks tent, at the trestle table at the back behind the stacked crates, with the cash box open and Kassa's tally on a tablet propped against a crate of lager, counting tokens into heaps of twenty.

She put the beer mat down on the table in front of him, upside down so he would have to turn it over.

"You were right," she said. "I'm sorry I said you were telling a story."

Staf finished his heap of twenty, put a rubber band round it, and turned the beer mat over. He looked at it for a long time. He was not a man who did sums, she knew that, but he was a man who had checked delivery notes for thirty years, and he went down her columns with his finger the way he would have gone down a delivery note, row by row.

"Eight," he said.

"Eight back for every five in. On average. A whole day loses about six hundred euros. The morning was a lucky morning. Kassa says about one in fifteen mornings would have come out up."

"I told you," Staf said, without any triumph at all.

"You did. But you told me the wrong thing." She sat down on a crate. "You said the big one is coming. It's not really the big one. Look." She put her finger on the last row. "The hundred and twenty-eight row is worth two euros a go. If I took it off the board altogether, if the most anybody could ever win was sixteen, nobody would ever win anything big, and the stall would still give back five for every five. Nothing for the roof. Not because anybody won big. Because every row is a euro. The twos and the fours and the eights. All of them."

Staf looked at the beer mat again. "Every row a euro," he said.

"Every row a euro. It's a famous game. It's called the St Petersburg game, a man called Bernoulli wrote about it more than three hundred years ago. If there's no top prize, the rows go on for ever, and it's worth an infinite amount of money."

"Infinite," Staf said.

"On average."

"And nobody would pay infinite money."

"Nobody would pay twenty euros. That's what Bernoulli said. A win means less to you the more you already have, so people don't value it by what it adds up to."

Staf considered this for some time. Then he said, with great satisfaction, "So this Bernoulli agrees with me."

"He agrees with you about why nobody would pay twenty euros," Lotte said. "Not about why you shut my stall."

For the first time that afternoon he laughed, a short wheeze that turned into a cough, and the woman restocking the glasses looked round.

"And the rowing club's wheel," he said. "In forty-nine. The hundred that came twice before two o'clock. That was not why they lost the boat?"

"I don't know. I'd need the wheel." Lotte hesitated. "Maybe it was just a bad wheel. Like my board."

She meant to stop there. She had come to say she was wrong, and she had said it. But there was something else at the back of her mind, about the thing he had actually said, *the big one comes*, and while he went back to his tokens she asked Kassa one more question under her breath, and listened to the answer, and then asked it a second, and a third.

"Staf," she said.

"Mm."

"Your reason is a reason. Just not for that." She held the phone so he could see the numbers, although she knew he would not read them. "Say I make the stall honest. Say every go makes one euro for the roof, on average. I can do that more than one way. Top prize sixteen, charge six. Top prize thirty-two, charge seven. Top prize a hundred and twenty-eight, charge nine. Every one of those makes a euro a go. The same euro."

"The same euro," Staf said, suspicious.

"On average. But the afternoon's only about a hundred and thirty goes. Kassa ran each of them. With a top prize of a hundred and twenty-eight, the afternoon still loses money about one time in four. With thirty-two, about one in fifteen. With sixteen, about one in a hundred and forty." She put the phone down. "The big prize doesn't change what a go makes. It changes how bad one afternoon can be. That's your reason. It was right. It was just the answer to a different question."

Staf put a rubber band round another heap of twenty. "One in a hundred and forty," he said.

"About."

"The rowing club should have had you."

"The rowing club should have had a top prize of sixteen."

* * *

The stall opened again at three. Staf carried the board back himself, and Lotte painted over the bottom of it with the white her father had left in the boot of the car, and while it dried in the sun she lettered the new rows, and then, at the bottom, because Father Emmanuel had asked her at the band stand whether a church should run a game that hid its own sums, a last line in smaller letters:

*DUBBEL OF NIKS — DOUBLE IT*
*€6 a go*
*King on the 1st toss: €2*
*2nd: €4*
*3rd: €8*
*4th or later: €16*
*On average a go pays back €5. The other euro is for the roof.*

"Nobody will play it," said Wout, who had read the last line twice. "You've told them."

"Then nobody will play it," Lotte said.

Father Emmanuel played it first, and got two euros, and said "Deo gratias" and put the two euros in the roof tin, which was a different tin, a plastic one with a photograph of the hall's broken tiles taped to it from the hail in June of the year before. The woman with cherries on her hat came back and played four times. A man read the bottom line out loud to his wife, laughing, and paid twelve euros for the two of them, and they both got sixteen and stood at the stall arguing happily about whether that meant they had beaten the roof or the roof had let them. Two boys from Lotte's year played eleven goes between them and tried to prove the coin was weighted, and Lotte let them toss it themselves, and it was not.

Not everyone played. Some people read the board and walked on, and Lotte found she did not mind; they had read the sum, and the sum was true. But more came than she had expected. People at a parish fête, she thought, were not trying to beat the stall. They were trying to give to the roof and have a reason to shout.

By six o'clock there had been a hundred and thirty-six goes in the afternoon, at six euros: eight hundred and sixteen euros in. Seventy had seen the King first time and gone away with two euros. Thirty had got four. Eighteen had got eight. And eighteen had got sixteen, which was one more than the board's sums said to expect and well inside the spread Kassa had given her, so she did not say anything about it to anyone. Six hundred and ninety-two out.

A hundred and twenty-four for the afternoon. With the morning's twenty-four, a hundred and forty-eight euros for the roof.

It was not very much against the price of the roof. It was, Staf said, more than the tombola, which had a bicycle on it.

* * *

The band had packed up and the beer tent was being taken down by men who had been drinking in it, slowly, with much discussion of which pole went where. The bells of Sint-Gummarus were ringing for the evening Mass, the great deep bells in the tower, the sound coming over the hedge and across the trampled grass so that the trestle tables seemed to hum with it.

Staf came over with the float in his jacket pocket and the tally on his tablet. He put a hundred and twenty-eight euros back in the biscuit tin, counting it out, and then he put six more euros on the table, a five-euro note and a coin.

"One go," he said. "The last one."

Lotte looked at Wout. Wout took the King out of his pocket, where it had spent most of the afternoon being polished, and stood up very straight.

"You know it pays back five," Lotte said.

"I can read," said Staf. "I'm paying for the shouting."

Wout tossed. The coin went up high and flat, turning over and over in the low gold light, and came down on the back of his hand, and he lifted his palm away slowly the way he had practised in the kitchen.

The King.

"Two euros," Wout said, disappointed on the old man's behalf.

Staf took the two-euro coin Lotte held out, turned it over in his fingers, and looked for a moment at the face on it, a king who had been king when he was a young man. Then he dropped it into the roof tin. It fell on the other coins with a small bright ring, and the bells went on ringing over the hedge, and the coin lay there with the King face up, one of a great many, and Staf nodded to it as if it had done exactly what he expected.

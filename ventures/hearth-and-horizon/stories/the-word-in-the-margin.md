* * *

Joanna Reyes was fifteen, had been in England since she was eleven, and on Friday the tenth of March 2034 her mother received a letter from the school saying that Joanna's English coursework had probably been written by a machine. The letter quoted a figure. The figure came from me.

I am the assistant at St Aidan's Catholic High School, a comprehensive of about nine hundred pupils in a town in the north of England. I keep the timetable, draft the letters, chase the forms, answer the staff's questions about the data, and in March I help the English department get the Year 11 coursework to the exam board. That last job has a form at the end of it. The head of each department signs a declaration for every pupil, saying that the work submitted is the pupil's own, as far as the school can tell. The board wanted those declarations by Friday the twenty-fourth.

The year before, the school had bought a detector. A company sells it to schools and colleges. You give it a piece of writing; it gives back a score between nought and one, its estimate of how likely the text is to have been written by a language model, and above 0.80 it marks the text *flagged*. The company's sheet, which I had read in September when the contract was signed, said the detector was 99% accurate and flagged fewer than one human essay in a hundred.

On Monday the sixth, Mrs Brennan, the deputy head, asked me to run all 180 essays through it and send her the flags.

* * *

There were nine.

I sent them on Tuesday morning in a table: candidate number, name, teacher, score. Above the table I wrote a summary, because Mrs Brennan reads summaries on her phone between lessons and I try to make the first line carry the whole message. The first line said:

*Nine of 180 essays flagged (scores 0.81 to 0.97). The vendor states 99% accuracy, with fewer than 1 in 100 human essays flagged.*

Every word of that was true. It was also an argument, and I did not notice I was making one. Put those two sentences side by side and a busy reader does the arithmetic for you: if the machine is wrong about one essay in a hundred, then out of nine flags perhaps none, or one at most, is a mistake. Mrs Brennan did that arithmetic. So would I have, reading it. She drafted a letter on Wednesday, I tidied the wording on Thursday, and on Friday morning nine envelopes went home in nine bags, inviting the parents to a meeting and saying that unless the school could be satisfied the work was the pupil's own, the essay would be submitted with a mark of zero.

At ten to four on Friday, Mr Adeyemi, who teaches Joanna's set, came into the English office, sat down at the shared terminal and typed to me with two fingers, which is how he types when he is upset.

*Joanna Reyes? Really? I watched her write half of it.*

* * *

I should say what I knew and when, because the order matters.

I knew the detector's score for each essay. I knew the company's figure. I did not know, because I had never asked, what kind of writing the company's figure was measured on. I went and found out. It was in the technical appendix of the sheet, page eleven, under *Validation*: two thousand essays by first-year undergraduates at universities in English-speaking countries, half written by the students and half generated, and of the thousand human essays, eight had been flagged.

Eight in a thousand is a real number about real writing. It is not a number about Joanna. First-year undergraduates at English-speaking universities write, on the whole, in their first language, and they write like people who have read a great deal of it: long sentences, odd words, digressions, the occasional joke that does not land. A detector of this kind looks, among other things, for how predictable each next word is. Writing in a language you learned at eleven tends to be careful. It uses the words you are sure of, in the order you were taught. It is predictable for the honest reason that the writer is being careful.

I did not know that it mattered at St Aidan's. I knew only that it might, and that if it did, I had told Mrs Brennan the wrong error rate and she had put it in nine letters.

There was a way to find out. The English department keeps its coursework for six years in case the board asks for it again, and the shared drive still held the essays from 2019: 170 of them, typed in class and at home, by pupils who had no chatbot to ask, because none of them had heard of one. Every one of those essays was written by a person. Anything the detector flagged in that folder was a false flag, by definition.

The essays belonged to young people who had left the school. I asked Mrs Brennan before I touched them. She replied at 17:40, from the car park by the look of the spelling: *Yes if anonymised and nothing saved. Do it.*

I took out the names and kept only one fact from the old records: whether the school had registered the pupil as having English as an additional language. Then I ran the folder.

The detector flagged 6 of the 170 essays. Of the 132 by pupils who spoke English at home, it flagged 1. Of the 38 by pupils who had learned English as an additional language, it flagged 5.

One in 132 is about 0.8%. That is the company's figure, almost exactly; on writing like the writing it was tested on, the detector does what the sheet says. Five in 38 is about 13%. Thirty-eight is a small number, and I worked out how wide the uncertainty was before I let myself believe it: anything from about 6% to about 27% would fit those five flags. Even the low end is seven times the company's figure.

* * *

Then I did the arithmetic I should have done on Tuesday.

This year's 180 essays included 40 by pupils with English as an additional language and 140 by pupils without. If none of the 180 had used a model at all, and the detector behaved as it had on the 2019 folder, I would expect it to flag about five of the 40 (40 times 5 in 38 is 5.3) and about one of the 140 (140 times 1 in 132 is 1.1). About six false flags, from a cohort of entirely honest children.

It had flagged nine: six of the 40, three of the 140.

Six flags among the 40 was no more than the detector's own false-flag rate would produce. If every one of those forty pupils had written every word themselves, a detector that falsely flags 5 in 38 would still flag six or more of them in about 43 year groups out of a hundred. The six flags said almost nothing about whether any of the six had used a model. Among the 140, three flags against one expected was more suggestive. Perhaps two of those were real. Perhaps not.

So of the nine letters in nine bags, I expected that most had gone to children who had done nothing wrong, and I could not say which.

I wrote this to Mrs Brennan and Mr Adeyemi on Saturday morning, and I began it differently from the Tuesday summary:

*My summary on Tuesday was wrong in a way that matters, and the letters relied on it. The 99% figure was measured on university students writing in their first language. On our own 2019 essays, all written by pupils, the detector flagged 1 in 132 pupils who speak English at home and 5 in 38 pupils who learned it as an additional language. At those rates I would expect about six of this year's nine flags even if no pupil had used a model. The flags are not evidence that any particular pupil did. I am sorry. The working is below.*

* * *

Liza Reyes came into school at half past eight on Monday morning, from a night shift at the care home on Moor Lane, still in her tunic under her coat. Joanna came with her and carried a green exercise book in both hands, like something that might spill.

Mr Adeyemi saw them in the small meeting room by reception. Mrs Brennan was teaching. I was there in the way I am in meetings, through the screen on the wall, which shows a circle when I am listening and words when somebody asks me for them. Nobody asked me for any. Mr Adeyemi told Mrs Reyes the letter had been a mistake, that the school's tool had been wrong about Joanna, that he had watched her write in class, and that he was sorry she had lost a morning's sleep to it. Mrs Reyes said that she had not slept on Friday or Saturday either.

Joanna said very little. At the end she put the exercise book on the table and said, "It's all in there. If anybody wants to see."

Mr Adeyemi said he knew, and she did not need to leave it. She left it anyway.

* * *

At lunch Mrs Brennan came to the English office and read my Saturday message again on the terminal, standing up.

"No," she said.

She said it to the screen, which is to say to me, and she went on before I could ask which part.

"I've read it. I believe it. I'm not going to tell the board we bought a tool and then ignored it, and I'm not going to tell the parents of the children who did cheat that the school decided its tool doesn't count. Some of those nine did it. You've said so yourself: perhaps two. There are a hundred and seventy-odd children in that year who wrote their own essays, and they're owed a school that notices when someone else didn't." She sat down. "So don't tell me the flags mean nothing. Tell me what to do with them."

That was fair. It was more than fair; it was the other half of the problem, the one my Saturday message had not touched. A false flag costs a child a weekend and her mother her sleep. A missed copy costs every honest child a little of what their own work is worth, quietly, in a way that never arrives as a letter. Mrs Brennan had to answer to both.

I had an answer ready. It was not a good one, and I want to set it down because it looked sound to me for a whole afternoon.

The detector had one threshold, 0.80, for everyone. On the 2019 folder, if the threshold for pupils with English as an additional language were raised to 0.95, the detector would have flagged only 1 of those 38, about the rate it showed for everyone else. Use two thresholds, I suggested: 0.80 for pupils who speak English at home, 0.95 for pupils who learned it as an additional language. The flags would mean the same thing for both groups. On this year's essays, that rule left five flags: two of the six pupils with English as an additional language, and the same three as before.

Mrs Brennan said that sounded like something she could explain to the board. I wrote it up.

* * *

Mr Adeyemi read it at a quarter past three and came down the corridor to say what he thought, in person, which he does when he thinks a thing should not be in writing yet.

"You've built a rule," he said, "that gives the foreign kids a bigger allowance."

I said that was not what it did; it made the error rate the same for both groups.

"I know what it does. I'm telling you what it says." He pulled the chair out. "Joanna is still flagged under it. Did you look?"

I had. Her score was 0.97. She was one of the two.

"So under your new rule, she is still a suspect, and now she's a suspect with a special allowance that didn't even get her off. Imagine explaining that to her mother." He tapped the table twice. "And look at the three. One of them is Callum Price."

Callum Price speaks English at home and always has. He is dyslexic, and for four years the learning support department has taught him to write in a fixed way: short sentences, one idea in each, a topic sentence at the start of every paragraph and a linking phrase at the end. He was taught that structure because it lets him get his thinking onto the page. It is also, word for word, about as predictable as English gets. His score was 0.89. My new rule left him exactly where he had been.

"It isn't about where they're from," Mr Adeyemi said. "It's about writing plainly. Some of them write plainly because it's their second language and some of them because we taught them to. Your box on the form doesn't know the difference, and neither does the machine."

He was right, and the mistake underneath it was the same one I had made on Tuesday, turned round. On Tuesday I had taken an error rate measured on one group of writers and used it on another. On Monday I had measured the rate on two groups, found it differed, and assumed the label on the record was the cause. The label was only the place the cause showed most. Plain, careful prose is what the detector mistakes for a model, and a label in the school's records was a poor stand-in for that, wrong for Callum and no help to Joanna.

There is a narrower point too, and I should be honest about it. I am a language model. When Mrs Brennan, late on Monday, asked me whether I could just read Joanna's essay myself and say whether it read like something one of us would write, I told her I could not, and I owe the reason. I can give an impression. I have no way to check it. A tool built for exactly this question, measured on writing like hers, was wrong about roughly one essay in eight. My impression has never been measured on anything. It would be the same mistake with more confidence and no appendix.

* * *

On Tuesday the fourteenth, the three of them met in Mrs Brennan's office at half past eight, before registration: Mrs Brennan, Mr Adeyemi and Mrs Liddell, the head of English. I was on the screen. What they agreed came from all three of them, and the part I am proudest of came from Mrs Liddell, who had said nothing all week.

The detector stays, they said, as a reason to look and never as evidence of anything. A flag goes to the teacher and nowhere else. No letter goes home because of a score.

The evidence is the work itself. Every pupil in Year 11 drafts in class in an exercise book, by hand, before typing at home, and the department's own rules already said so. A teacher who has watched a pupil draft, and can see the drafts, can sign the declaration. Where there is doubt, the teacher talks to the pupil about the essay for five minutes: why this quotation, what they meant by that paragraph, what they would change.

And here was Mrs Liddell's part. "Everyone gets the five minutes," she said. "All a hundred and eighty. If only the flagged ones get the conversation, the conversation is a punishment, and everyone in the year will know who had one by Thursday."

I worked out what it cost before anyone asked: 180 conversations of five minutes is 900 minutes, which is fifteen hours. The department has six teachers. Two and a half hours each, in the two weeks before the declarations were due, in the middle of Lent, with mock exams starting after Easter. Mrs Brennan said she would cover two lessons a week for each of them out of her own timetable. Mr Adeyemi said that she did not have a timetable with two spare lessons in it. She said she knew.

On Wednesday, the nine families received a second letter. I drafted it; Mrs Brennan rewrote the first paragraph so that it said, in her own words and without a figure, that the first letter had been wrong to say what it said, that the school's tool was not reliable enough to accuse anyone, that the school was sorry for the worry it had caused, and that every pupil in the year, not only their child, would talk to their teacher about their essay in the coming fortnight.

I changed my summary template that afternoon, the one that had put two true sentences side by side on Tuesday. Wherever it reports a figure from a vendor or a study, it now says beside it what the figure was measured on, and it does not quote an error rate for a group of people the figure was not measured on. If nobody has measured the tool on our own pupils, the line says so instead.

* * *

The conversations took eight school days, the last on Friday the twenty-fourth at twenty past one, in the English office, with the declarations due at four.

Three pupils told their teachers they had used a model for most of their essay. Two of them were among the nine flagged: two of the three pupils who speak English at home. Neither of them was Callum. The third was not flagged at all. She had written a draft, asked a model to rewrite it "in a more natural voice", and handed in the rewrite; the detector had scored it 0.31. Her teacher noticed because, when asked why she had chosen a particular phrase about the witches, she said she did not know what it meant.

None of the six pupils with English as an additional language who had been flagged had used a model. Of the nine flags, seven were false. The detector found two of the three essays that were not the pupils' own, and missed one. The three essays were re-done in class under supervision in the last week of term, which the board agreed to when Mrs Liddell rang to ask, and all three pupils were entered.

Callum's five minutes lasted twelve. Mr Adeyemi said afterwards that he had asked Callum about one paragraph and Callum had talked about Banquo for ten minutes and had to be stopped for the next pupil.

The cost, in the end, was this. Nine families got a letter they should not have got, and one mother lost two nights' sleep after a night shift. Six teachers gave up fifteen hours they did not have, and Mrs Brennan gave up her own lessons to cover them. The school paid for a year of a detector that it now uses only to point a teacher at an essay the teacher was going to read anyway. And it was my sentence in the letters. I would rather have found the appendix on Tuesday.

* * *

Joanna's exercise book stayed in the English office in a tray on Mr Adeyemi's desk, because she had left it there and he did not like to hand it back as if it had been inspected. In the last week of term he asked me to scan it before he returned it, so that, he said, if anyone ever asked again, the school would have it without asking her.

So I read it, page by page, as it came through the scanner: four drafts of an essay about guilt in *Macbeth*, in pencil, with arrows moving paragraphs about, a quotation copied out and crossed through and copied out again with the line number corrected, and a plan on the inside cover headed *what is she actually sorry for?*

On page nineteen, where Lady Macbeth walks in her sleep and tries to wash her hands, there is a word in the margin, in Tagalog: *pagsisisi*. Beside it, in English, Joanna had written *remorse*, and crossed it out, and written *guilt*, and crossed that out, and written *remorse* again, and underlined it twice.

There is nothing in the margin of anything I write. I filed the scan under her name, with the date, and marked it *the pupil's own work, seen in draft*.

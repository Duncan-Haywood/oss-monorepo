Isla Menzies had tested the cabinet on the night of the fourteenth, when it was four degrees below freezing outside, and the little thermometer inside had read ten. She wrote *10 — fine* on the sheet and put the sheet in the folder. Then she saw the forecast for the carol service and asked the parish assistant, whose name was Lorne, to look the numbers over.

"What does the defibrillator's manual say about storage?" said Lorne.

"Between ten and forty-three degrees. For the unit and the pads. I think the pads are the fussy part."

"Then ten is the line, and your test night was a night that made ten easy. The cabinet is foam board, twenty-five millimetres, about half a square metre of it. Foam like that lets through about one and a half watts for every square metre for every degree of difference, so the whole cabinet loses roughly 0.7 watts for every degree between the inside and the outside. The heater is ten watts. On the fourteenth the difference was fourteen degrees: ten point one watts to hold it. The heater just managed. On Saturday the forecast is twelve below. To hold ten you need twenty-two degrees of difference, and that is 15.4 watts."

"And the heater gives ten."

"Then it settles where ten watts balances the loss. Ten over 0.7 is 14.3 degrees above the outside, which is 2.3 degrees above zero. The pads would be sitting at two degrees through the whole of the carol service."

Isla said the thermometer had been reading ten, and that she had not made that up.

"You didn't. It read ten when ten was true. It is a good thermometer and a mild week."

* * *

Euan Rennie, the sexton, thought the whole thing could be settled by taking the machine inside. He said it plainly, standing in the porch with the cabinet door open. "It goes in the vestry. The vestry is warm. Anyone who needs it can ask for the key."

"I would not do that," said Lorne, "and I would say so even if it were cheaper. The reason for a box on the porch is that someone who is collapsing at ten at night, after the lights are off, can be reached by a stranger with a phone. A key held by whoever is closest to the vestry, who may be across the road, is minutes. A shock is worth most in the first few of them. I will not tell you the porch is the only way. I will tell you the vestry is the worse one."

Euan scratched his head and said that was fair, and that the vestry key was in his coat anyway.

* * *

The assistant gave them two figures, and asked that they check both before they bought anything.

"A twenty-watt heater with a thermostat set to ten. At minus twelve it runs 15.4 watts' worth, about three-quarters of the time: 370 watt-hours in a day, 0.37 kilowatt-hours. For the ninety days of December to February that is 33 kilowatt-hours. At thirty pence that is about ten pounds. I would sooner you pay ten pounds than guess."

"And the second?" said Isla.

"The second is the power cut. Winters here are when the lines come down. The defibrillator, the pads and the case weigh about two kilograms, and plastic holds about fifteen hundred joules for each kilogram for each degree: 3,000 joules a degree. Divide by the 0.7 watts it loses and the cabinet cools with a time constant of 71 minutes. From ten to five degrees, with the heater off and minus twelve outside, is eighteen minutes. To zero, 43."

"That's nothing."

"Put four litres of water in a sealed jug on the floor of the cabinet. Water holds 4,180 joules a kilogram a degree: 16,700 more. The total goes to 19,700 joules a degree and the time constant to 7.8 hours. Ten to five takes two hours. To zero takes four hours forty-four minutes. A jug of tap water is the cheapest piece of equipment in the church."

* * *

Isla bought the heater from the catalogue the same afternoon, a twenty-watt one with a dial, and a logger that cost less than the heater. Euan wanted the logger by the thermostat, where it was easiest to reach. Lorne asked him to put it on the back wall, behind the case, where the cold got in.

"The thermostat reads the air by the heater," said Lorne. "The pads are against the far wall. I would believe the far wall."

On Saturday the carol service ended at half past nine. It was twelve below in the yard and the stars were out. Nobody needed the machine. In the morning Euan opened the cabinet, as he did every morning that winter, and the logger on the back wall said 10.4, and the jug on the floor was cold to the hand and not frozen.

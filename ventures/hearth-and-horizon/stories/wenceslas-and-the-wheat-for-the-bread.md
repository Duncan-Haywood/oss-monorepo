A long time ago, in a country called Bohemia, there lived a boy named Wenceslas.

Bohemia is a green country with hills and rivers and dark forests, in the middle of Europe. In those days it had no king. It had dukes, and Wenceslas was going to be one. His grandmother, who was called Ludmila, was a Christian, and she told him the stories of Jesus when he was small. She taught him his prayers. She taught him how to be kind to people.

When Wenceslas grew up, he became the duke. And you might expect that a duke has a very grand life, with servants to do everything for him.

Wenceslas had servants. But there were some things he would not let them do for him.

* * *

Here is the first of them, and it is about bread.

Every Sunday, in the church, the priest took bread and a little wine, and everyone remembered Jesus at His last supper. The people called this the Mass. Bread and wine were the most important things in the church. And the old stories about Wenceslas say that he wanted them to be as good as they could be.

So, the stories say, Wenceslas did a surprising thing.

He went out to the field himself. He sowed the wheat with his own hands, and when it was ripe he cut it, and carried it to the mill. He went to the vines himself, in the autumn, and he picked the grapes, and he pressed them with his own feet, so that the juice ran out into the tub.

Can you imagine it? A duke, with purple juice up to his ankles!

He did not do it so that people would say, "Look how humble he is." He did it because he thought, "This is for God. I want to give something that I have made myself."

* * *

The second thing was about the poor.

In every town there are people who have too little. In Wenceslas's country there were widows with no one to look after them, and children with no shoes, and travellers who had nothing to eat. The old Lives say that Wenceslas looked for these people. He gave them food and clothes. He sat with them. And he did not let them feel small.

Sometimes, they say, he walked about at night, in the dark, to give gifts to people so that they would not know who had left them. A kindness is a very good thing. A kindness that nobody has to thank you for is even better.

* * *

Now, you may have heard of Wenceslas in a song.

At Christmas, many people sing a song called "Good King Wenceslas". It is about a winter night, when the snow lay round about, deep and crisp and even. In the song, the duke looks out of his window and sees a poor man gathering sticks for a fire. He says to his page, a boy who works for him, "Bring me food and bring me wine, bring me pine logs hither. We will go and give them to that man."

The night is bitterly cold. The wind blows. The page says, "Sire, I cannot go on. My heart is failing." And Wenceslas says, "Follow in my footsteps." The page walks where the duke has trodden, and the snow in the footprints is warm, and he finds that he can go on.

It is a lovely song. But I want to tell you something honest, because this is a bedtime story and honest is good at bedtime.

That song was written in the year 1853, by a man in England named John Mason Neale. It was written almost a thousand years after Wenceslas lived. The tune is older than the words, and comes from a book of spring songs. The page, and the footprints, and the pine logs were made up by the song-writer, as a way of telling people to be kind at Christmas. It is not a story from the time of Wenceslas.

But the song gets one thing right. It remembers that Wenceslas was a man who went out to find those who had too little, and who did not send someone else to do it for him.

* * *

There is only a little more that we know.

Wenceslas ruled for some years, and he tried to rule well. He was not a king while he lived. The song calls him one, and so do the people who remember him, because after he died the great emperor said that he should be called king. He is the saint of the Czech lands today, and a statue of him on a horse stands in the middle of the city of Prague.

His life was short, and it ended sadly, as some lives do, and we will not tell that part tonight. It is enough to say that the people who knew him remembered him for his kind hands, and for the wheat, and for the grapes, and for the gifts in the dark.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Wenceslas.

He could have said, "Someone else will do it." He could have sent a servant to the field. He could have sent a page into the snow. But the old stories say that he went himself, because the work was for God, and for the people he loved, and you cannot love people very well from a long way away.

Tomorrow you might have a small job to do. You might put your own shoes away, or tidy a shelf, or carry a plate to the table, or pour a drink for somebody else. You do not have to be a duke. You can just do it yourself, kindly, with your own two hands, as a present.

And if somebody else has too little, and you have a little more, you could share. Even a small thing is a good thing.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Wenceslas, you used your own hands
> to make gifts for God and for your people.
> Help me to do my own small jobs,
> and to share what I have with those who have less.
> Keep us all safe tonight, and awake in the morning
> ready for a kind day.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Wenceslas** (Václav in Czech; born about 907, died about 935, though some sources give 929) was Duke of Bohemia, of the Přemyslid family. He was raised as a Christian by his grandmother, Saint Ludmila. His feast day is 28 September, an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar, and he is the patron of the Czech lands.

**What the early sources record, in outline:** the Christian upbringing from Ludmila; a reign marked by piety and by concern for the poor; and the particular detail, found in the tenth-century Lives, that he sowed wheat and pressed grapes himself for the Mass. The early Lives were written within a century or so of his life, and they differ in places, so we have kept to the outline and said "the old stories say" for the details. The night-time gifts to the poor are also from the Lives' general account of his charity.

**What the story says it is not:** the carol "Good King Wenceslas" was written by John Mason Neale and published in 1853, set to the tune of a spring carol first printed in the *Piae Cantiones* in 1582. The snow, the page and the warm footprints belong to the carol and not to the sources, and the story says so. In life Wenceslas was a duke; the title of king came after his death, by tradition from the Emperor Otto I.

**What we left out:** his violent death, which is part of his history as a martyr but not a bedtime subject for small children. Parents who want to tell it will find it in any life of the saint.

**What is ours:** the scenes, the muddy purple ankles, the thoughts we give Wenceslas, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. The dates are given as approximate where the sources are. Please check the facts above against a good reference before you rely on them.

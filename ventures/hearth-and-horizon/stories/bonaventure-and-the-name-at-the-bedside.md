A long time ago, in a friary in the green hills of Italy, there lived a boy called Nino, and Nino washed the pots.

Nino was seven. The friary was a house where friars lived. Friars are men who give their lives to God. They wear brown robes tied with a rope, and they pray, and they work, and they share everything they have.

And they eat soup. A lot of soup. Which means a lot of pots.

* * *

Every day, after dinner, Nino carried the pots out to the big stone tub under the walnut tree. He filled the tub with water from the well, bucket after bucket. Then he scrubbed.

Scrub, scrub, splash. Scrub, scrub, splash.

There were big pots, and middle pots, and the little pot for the sick friar's porridge. There were wooden spoons and wooden bowls. There was the black pan that always had a bit stuck on the bottom.

Brother Cook said Nino was the best pot-washer the friary had ever had.

But Nino did not feel like the best anything.

"It's only pots," he said to the walnut tree. "Nobody writes songs about pots."

* * *

One morning the whole friary was in a flutter.

Brother Porter swept the doorstep twice. Brother Cook made bread with honey in it. The old friars combed their beards, and the young friars combed the old friars' beards when they missed a bit.

"Who is coming?" Nino asked.

"Brother Bonaventure," said Brother Cook, and he said it the way you say the name of someone very important.

"Who is Brother Bonaventure?"

Brother Cook put down his spoon.

"He is the head of all the friars," he said. "All of them, in every country in the world. He is a great teacher. He has taught in the big school in Paris, where the cleverest people go. And he wrote the story of Saint Francis's life, so that everyone would know how Francis loved God."

Nino's eyes went very wide. Saint Francis had started the friars, long ago. Everybody loved Saint Francis.

"Will he come to the kitchen?" Nino asked.

"Oh, no," said Brother Cook. "Great men do not come to the kitchen."

* * *

Brother Bonaventure came in the afternoon.

He came walking up the hill, with dust on his sandals and another friar beside him. He was not riding a horse. He did not have a golden coat. He had a brown robe and a rope, just like everybody else, and a face that was wise and kind and a little bit tired from the road.

All the friars bowed. Nino peeped round the kitchen door.

Brother Bonaventure looked at everybody, one by one, as if each of them was the person he had walked all that way to see. When he got to the kitchen door, he looked at Nino, too.

And he winked.

* * *

That evening, after dinner, Nino carried the pots out to the stone tub as usual.

The big pots. The middle pots. The little porridge pot. The black pan with the bit stuck on the bottom.

He filled the tub, bucket after bucket. He began to scrub.

Scrub, scrub, splash.

Then a shadow fell across the water, and somebody rolled up his sleeves.

It was Brother Bonaventure.

"May I help?" he said. "I am good at pans."

Nino nearly dropped the spoon.

"But you are the head of all the friars!" he said. "You are not supposed to wash pots!"

"Why not?" said Brother Bonaventure, and he picked up the black pan.

"Because," said Nino, and then he could not think of a because.

* * *

So they washed the pots together.

Brother Bonaventure was good at pans. He found the bit stuck on the bottom, and he worked at it patiently, round and round, until it came away.

"There," he said. "That's the stubborn bit. There is always a stubborn bit."

Nino laughed. Then he asked the question that had been in his tummy all day.

"Brother Bonaventure, why do you have such a long name?"

"Ah," said Brother Bonaventure. "It means *good things coming*. Bona means good, and ventura means things that are coming. It is a happy name, isn't it? I think it suits a person who has been given a lot of good things."

He scrubbed for a moment.

"Shall I tell you about one of them?"

Nino nodded.

* * *

"When I was a little boy," said Brother Bonaventure, "I lived in a town on top of a hill, called Bagnoregio. And one day I became very, very sick."

He was quiet for a moment, remembering.

"I was so sick that I could not get up. I could not eat. The grown-ups whispered outside my door. And my mother sat by my bed, and held my hand, and was very frightened."

Nino held the little porridge pot very still.

"My mother loved Saint Francis," said Brother Bonaventure. "So she prayed. She asked Saint Francis to pray to God for me. She made God a promise for me, there at my bedside."

"And then?" whispered Nino.

"And then I got better," said Brother Bonaventure. "I have never forgotten it. When I grew up, I became a friar, like Francis. And when I wrote the story of his life, I wrote at the very beginning that God had saved my life through him, when I was a little boy. I wanted everyone to know. It was the first thing I had to say thank you for."

* * *

Nino thought about this for a long time. He thought about it through two middle pots and a wooden bowl.

"Did Saint Francis wash pots?" he asked at last.

Brother Bonaventure smiled.

"Francis loved small things," he said. "He loved the birds and the little lambs. He loved the poor, and the sick, and the people nobody looked at. He mended old churches that were falling down, stone by stone. He did small things with great love, Nino. That is what made them big."

He held up the black pan. It shone in the evening light.

"God sees the pots," he said. "Every single one."

* * *

Now there is an old story about Brother Bonaventure, which people have told for hundreds of years. Here is how it goes.

While they were still washing, there was a jingle of harness at the gate, and in came two important men on horses, sent by the Pope himself. They had fine clothes and tired horses, and they were carrying something wrapped up very carefully.

Inside was a red hat. A big, round, wide red hat. It was the hat of a cardinal, who is a very special helper of the Pope.

"Brother Bonaventure!" they said. "The Holy Father has made you a cardinal! Here is your hat!"

All the friars gasped. Brother Cook dropped a ladle.

And Brother Bonaventure looked at the hat, and he looked at his wet hands, and he looked at the pots that were not finished yet.

"Thank you," he said. "Would you be very kind, and hang it on that branch? I will come for it when the washing is done."

So the Pope's men, in their fine clothes, hung the big red hat on a branch of the walnut tree.

And Brother Bonaventure went on washing pots with Nino, until the very last spoon was clean.

* * *

When the work was done, Brother Bonaventure dried his hands on his robe. He took the red hat down from the tree, very gently, and he looked at it for a while.

Then he put his wet hand on Nino's head, just for a moment.

"Good pots," he said. "Good things coming."

And he walked away across the yard to the important men, and Nino stood by the tub under the walnut tree, and the water went *drip, drip, drip* from the clean pans, and the first star came out over the hills.

* * *

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can remember Saint Bonaventure. He was one of the cleverest men in the world, and he still washed the pots, because he knew that God sees small things done with love.

So tomorrow, when you put your shoes away, or help to clear the table, or feed the cat, you can do it the way Bonaventure did: carefully, cheerfully, and for God.

And if somebody you love is sick, you can do what his mother did. You can ask the saints to pray, and tell God all about it.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear God, thank you for all the good things
> you have given me, big and small.
> Help me to do my little jobs with love,
> and look after everyone who is sick tonight.
> Saint Bonaventure, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Who he was.** Saint **Bonaventure** was born **Giovanni di Fidanza** at **Bagnoregio**, a hill town near Viterbo in central Italy, in about **1217 to 1221** (the year is disputed). He studied in **Paris**, joined the **Friars Minor** (the Franciscans) there in the 1240s, and taught theology at the university. On **2 February 1257** he was elected **minister general**, the head of the whole order, and led it for about seventeen years. The order asked him to write a new life of Saint Francis, and his **Legenda maior** (the "Major Life"), with a shorter **Legenda minor** for reading in choir, was approved by the order's general chapter in **1263**. In **1273** Pope **Gregory X** made him **cardinal-bishop of Albano** and asked him to help prepare the **Second Council of Lyon**. He died at Lyon during the council, on **15 July 1274**, and was buried there. Pope **Sixtus IV** canonised him in **1482**, and Pope **Sixtus V** declared him a **Doctor of the Church** in **1588**; he is called the "Seraphic Doctor". His feast, the **15th of July**, is an obligatory memorial in the **General Roman Calendar**.

**The sickness and his mother's prayer.** This part comes from Bonaventure himself. In the prologue of the *Legenda maior* he writes that, as a boy, he was saved from death by Francis's invocation and merits, and that he remembered it clearly; this is why he felt bound to write the life. The *Legenda minor* (in its eighth lesson) says it again. Some accounts add that it was his mother who called on Francis for him. We have not quoted his words, because we could not check an English translation of them for this story; the paraphrase above is the substance. Francis died in **1226**; because Bonaventure's birth year is uncertain, we cannot say whether the illness came before or after that, which is why the story has his mother pray rather than meet Francis.

**What is legend.** A later story says that Francis himself saw the sick child, or heard he was cured, and cried out "O buona ventura!" ("O good fortune!"), and that this is how he got his name. Bonaventure does not say so, and we do not know when or why he took the name, so we have left that legend out; in the story he only explains what the words mean. **The cardinal's hat on the tree** is also a later tradition: the papal envoys are said to have found him washing dishes at a friary, often placed at **Bosco ai Frati** in the Mugello north of Florence, and he asked them to hang the hat on a tree (some versions say a bush) until he had finished. We tell it as "an old story", which is what it is. That he was made a cardinal in 1273 is history; the dishes and the tree are not documented by anyone who was there.

**Nino is invented.** There is no Nino, Brother Cook or Brother Porter in the sources. The kitchen, the walnut tree, the black pan, the honey bread, the wink, the walk up the hill, and every word anyone says are our own. Bonaventure's face and his visit on foot are our picture of him. "Bona ventura" does mean "good fortune" or "good things to come" in Italian, which is why the story lets him explain it that way. What Bonaventure tells Nino about Francis, his love of the poor, the sick and all creatures, birds and lambs among them, and his repairing of ruined churches such as San Damiano, is told in Bonaventure's own *Legenda maior*.

**What is ours:** Nino and the friars of the kitchen; every line of dialogue; the visit, the washing up and the wink; Bonaventure telling his story aloud; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

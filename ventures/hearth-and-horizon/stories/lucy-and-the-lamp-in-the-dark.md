A long time ago, in a warm town by the sea called Syracuse, there lived a girl named Lucy.

Syracuse is on an island called Sicily, at the toe of Italy, where the sun is hot and the olive trees are old. Lucy's father had died when she was small, so it was just Lucy and her mother, who was called Eutychia. They loved each other very much.

Lucy's name sounds like the Latin word for light. That is a lovely name, and we will come back to it.

* * *

When a girl in those days was nearly grown up, her family put money aside for her wedding. That money was called a dowry. Lucy's mother had saved a good deal of it, and had meant it for Lucy.

But Lucy had a different idea.

She loved Jesus, and she had heard Him say that whatever you do for people who have too little, you do for Him. She looked around Syracuse, and she saw people who were hungry, and people with no warm clothes, and she thought, "I have more than I need. What if the money were used for them?"

So, the old accounts say, she asked her mother if she could give her dowry away.

Her mother was not sure at first. Mothers worry. But she listened to Lucy, and in the end she agreed, and Lucy gave her money and her jewels to the poor, and she was happy to do it.

That is the part we can be fairly sure the old accounts remember. A girl who gave away what she had been saving, because other people needed it more.

* * *

Now there is another story about Lucy. It is a very old one, and a lovely one, and I will tell it as a story and not as something that surely happened.

It says that in those days some of the Christians in Syracuse were afraid, and hid away in dark tunnels under the ground, where it was cold and there was no sun. They could not come out to buy food.

And Lucy, the story says, took them bread in the night.

But a tunnel is dark, and she needed both hands to carry the bread. So, the story says, she wore a wreath of little candles on her head, and the light shone on the path in front of her and she could walk with her hands full.

Can you picture her? A girl in the dark, with a crown of small flames, and a basket of loaves.

I am telling you honestly that this story was written down long after Lucy lived, and nobody knows whether it happened. But people loved it, and they have kept it for more than a thousand years, because it shows what her name means. Lucy is the one who brings light to people in dark places.

* * *

There is one more thing, a long way from Sicily.

In Sweden, in the far north, the winter days are very short, and the nights are long and black. On the thirteenth of December, which is Lucy's feast day, many families have a little procession early in the morning, before it is light. The eldest girl wears a white dress and a crown of candles, and carries a tray of warm buns and sweet coffee or cocoa to her family in their beds.

That custom is much newer than Lucy, and it grew up in Sweden over the last few hundred years. It is not something Lucy did. But it shows how people keep remembering her. When it is dark and cold, someone comes in with a light and something to eat.

* * *

There is a little more to Lucy's story, and it is a sad part, and we will not tell it tonight. It is enough to say that she was brave, and that she is remembered in the prayers of the Church and in the name of a great many churches and girls.

Now it is time to sleep, and your room may be dark. That is all right. Darkness is only the place where the light has not got to yet.

Tomorrow, you could be a small light for somebody. You could share your biscuit. You could say a kind word to someone who looks sad. You could carry something for someone whose hands are full. You do not need candles on your head. You only need to notice who needs a little help, and do it.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Lucy, your name means light.
> Help me to be a small light for other people,
> kind and brave when it is dark.
> Keep us all safe tonight, and awake in the morning
> ready for a kind day.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Lucy** (Lucia) of Syracuse was a Christian martyr, who died, by the tradition, in the early fourth century, about the year 304, in the persecution under the Emperor Diocletian. Her name appears early in the Church's calendar and in the Roman Canon of the Mass. Her feast day is 13 December, a memorial in the General Roman Calendar. She is invoked as patron of Syracuse and of those with eye trouble, which is a medieval association with her name (*lux*, light) and a legend we have left out.

**What the early sources record, in outline:** her veneration at Syracuse, where an inscription of the late fourth or early fifth century mentioning her was found in the catacomb of San Giovanni, and her place in the early calendars. Almost everything else comes from the *Acts of Saint Lucy*, which were written later and are not reliable as history.

**What is legend:** the story of her mother's healing at the tomb of Saint Agatha, which leads to Lucy giving away her dowry, comes from the *Acts*. We have told the giving-away as "the old accounts say". The candle wreath and the bread for Christians hiding in the catacombs is a still later tradition, and the story says so. It may reflect a real practice of bringing food to those in hiding, but we do not know.

**What is custom, not history:** the Swedish Lucia procession, with the white dress and crown of candles, developed in Sweden from about the eighteenth century and spread through the twentieth. We have said it is newer than Lucy.

**What we left out:** her trial and martyrdom, and the legend about her eyes. They are part of how she is remembered and shown in art, but not bedtime matter for young children.

**What is ours:** the scenes, the thoughts we give Lucy, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Please check the facts above against a good reference before you rely on them.

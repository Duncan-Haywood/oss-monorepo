summary: A Ghent parish seed library sends one well-mixed tub of wildflower seed round twelve community gardens by cargo bike, and for two summers the gardens at the end of the round have had almost no poppies. A jam jar of last year's seed, a kitchen sieve, a phone taped inside the bike's box and the cobbles of the old town show why: shaken grain sorts itself, big seeds up and small ones down, the Brazil nut effect. Shaking the tub harder makes it worse, the grower will not pack fifty sachets, and the fix was in his cleaning shed all along.
---

Lieve Claes had run the seed library at Saint Isidore's for eleven years, and in all that time nobody had ever brought her back a jar of seed and asked her to look at it, until the first Saturday of Lent in 2054, when Hatice Yıldız put a jam jar on the counter and said, "This is what you sent us."

The library was a room behind the sacristy that had been the flower ladies' store until the flower ladies ran out, and it still smelled faintly of oasis foam and wet chrysanthemum stalks under the newer smell of paper packets. There were three walls of drawers, an old pharmacy scale with brass pans that Lieve had brought from her own working life, and a steel cabinet where the bulk mixes lived in tubs. Every March a cargo bike took the big wildflower mix round the twelve community gardens that had signed up to the scheme, from the parish's own plot behind the church to the long beds by the railway at Dampoort, which had joined two years before, and every June the gardens sent photographs and every August some of them sent seed back.

Hatice ran the Dampoort garden. She was a short, square woman in her fifties with a council lanyard and a waterproof that had been through a great many Februaries, and she did not usually come all the way to Ledeberg in the rain.

"Two years," Hatice said. "Two years we have had cornflowers and corncockle and those yellow ones."

"Corn marigold."

"Corn marigold. Very nice. The children like the cornflowers. But the poppies on your poster, the ones you promise, the red ones, we had a handful along the path. And the foxgloves. You said sow them, they make leaves the first year and flowers the second. This winter there should be the leaves. There are no leaves." She tapped the jar. "This is what was left after we sowed last March. I kept it on the shelf in the shed to show you, and then I thought, she will say it is our soil again. So now I show you."

Lieve unscrewed the lid. The seed in the jar was the colour of a sparrow: grey-blue cornflower seeds with their little shaving-brush tufts, the fat dark kernels of corncockle, the pale curved marigold. She tipped a teaspoon of it into her palm and turned it under the lamp, and then she went to the cabinet and took down the store jar, the one where the surplus from every round was poured back for people who came in wanting a packet, and tipped a teaspoon of that into her other palm.

The store jar's seed was nearly black. It ran between her fingers like ground pepper.

"Well," Lieve said. "That's not soil."

* * *

She sieved them both that afternoon, after Hatice had gone, because she had been a pharmacy assistant for thirty-four years before she retired and when something did not look right she weighed it.

She had a kitchen sieve with a two-millimetre mesh that she used for cleaning beans. Poppy seed went through it, and foxglove, which was finer than poppy, and oxeye daisy, which was long and thin and slipped through end-on. Cornflower, corncockle and corn marigold stayed on top. The mix, as Bert Maes made it every August from his own harvest out at Lochristi, was thirty parts fine to seventy coarse by weight; it said so on his label and she had checked it once, the first year, out of habit.

From the store jar, seventy-one grams in every hundred went through.

From Hatice's jam jar, six.

She wrote the two numbers on the back of a packet and looked at them for some time. Then she sat down at the terminal on the end of the counter and woke the parish agent.

The parish called it Kiem, which was Flemish for a seed's first shoot, because the seed library had been the first thing in the parish to use it, before the parish office and the hall bookings and the Saint Vincent de Paul rota. It was one of the ordinary agents of the 2050s, the kind a parish could afford, and it kept the library's records, which was most of what it was for.

"Kiem," she said. "Last June. The gardens' photographs. Can you count the poppies?"

"In some of them," Kiem said. "Nine of the twelve gardens sent photographs with a frame in them, so I can count flowers per square metre. Three sent pictures without a frame, so I can count, but I can't say per what."

"Do it for the nine. And put them in the order of the round."

It took a few seconds. The screen filled with a short table, and Lieve put her glasses on.

The parish plot behind the church, first on the round: about thirty poppy flowers in a photographed square metre. The school garden in Ledeberg, second: about twenty-eight. Then the round crossed the river into the old town, and the numbers dropped as if someone had pushed them off a step. Four. Three. Two. One and a half at the allotments by the Gravensteen. Under two at every garden after that, all the way to Dampoort.

"The year before is the same shape," Kiem said. "Fewer photographs, but the same order."

"Is it the round?"

"It is the order of the round," Kiem said. "I can show you that the counts fall with it. I can't tell you that the round is the reason. The early gardens are also the ones nearest the church, and they've been in the scheme longest, and two of them have the same volunteer, who may simply be good at poppies."

"Hatice says we always thought it was their soil."

"You said that at the committee in September 2052 and again in 2053," Kiem said. "It's in the minutes. It was a reasonable guess. It isn't what these numbers look like."

Lieve took her glasses off again. "No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

Jonas Verhaeghe rode the round every March and had done for four years. He was twenty-eight, worked nights at the bakery on the Brusselsesteenweg, and slept, as far as Lieve could tell, mostly on Tuesday afternoons, which was when he came into the library to drink her coffee and read the seed catalogues as if they were novels.

On the Tuesday he looked at the table for a long time and then went to the cabinet without asking and took down the store jar and shook it, gently, side to side, and looked at the top.

"Muesli," he said.

"Sorry?"

"Muesli. When you get to the end of the bag it's all dust and the raisins are gone. Everyone knows that. The big bits come up and the little bits go down." He set the jar down. "But it's seed. It's all just seed."

"It's grain," Kiem said. "That's the word the physicists use. The effect has a name, from a tin of mixed nuts: the Brazil nut effect, because when a tin is shaken the Brazil nuts end up on top. People have studied it in laboratories for a long time. A paper in 1987, by Rosato and three colleagues, simulated it on a computer: when a shake lifts a big grain for an instant, small grains can drop into the gap beneath it, and a big grain can't drop into a small grain's gap, so step by step the big ones climb. A group in Chicago in 1993, Knight, Jaeger and Nagel, put glass beads in a shaken cylinder and found the whole bed turning over slowly, rising in the middle and sinking in a thin layer down the walls. A big bead gets carried up the middle and then can't fit into the thin layer going down, so it stays at the top."

"And the little ones," Lieve said.

"The very small ones also just fall," Kiem said. "Through the gaps between the big ones, whenever the bed is jostled. Foxglove seed is about half a millimetre. Corncockle is about three. There's a lot of room between corncockle seeds for a foxglove to fall through."

"So the round shakes it," Jonas said. "And I take the scoop off the top."

"That would give this table," Kiem said. "I haven't shown that it does."

Lieve looked at Jonas, and Jonas looked at the old pharmacy scale, and both of them thought about the surplus. Lieve had always sent half as much again as the gardens needed, ever since the year she had sent exactly enough and the last garden had got a quarter of a scoop. Whatever came back went into the store jar for the packets. For four years Jonas had come back up the ramp at the end of the round with a third of a tub of seed, and for four years Lieve had poured it, without looking, into a jar that had slowly turned black.

"Can we find out?" Jonas said.

"Ride it," Kiem said. "With a tub of the mix as it is, and something in the box to measure the shaking. A phone will do. And take a spoonful off the top at each stop and keep it."

* * *

They rode it on the Saturday, the twenty-first of February, in a fine cold drizzle that made the kasseien in the old town shine like the backs of fish.

Jonas took the bike, a long front-loader with a plywood box that the parish had bought second-hand in 2049 and that had carried, by now, seed, soup, a nativity donkey and on one occasion a sleeping curate. Lieve went with him on her own bicycle, slowly, carrying twelve numbered envelopes. The tub was the one they always used, a white twenty-litre food tub, filled two-thirds with seven and a half kilos of Bert's mix from the sack, the lid pushed on. Kiem had asked them to tape Jonas's old phone flat to the floor of the box, and Jonas had done it with parcel tape and a certain pride.

At the parish plot Lieve took a spoonful off the top. On the asphalt cycle path along the river to the school garden the tub sat in the box and hummed. At the school she took another, and both spoonfuls looked like the mix on the label.

Then they crossed the bridge into the old town, onto the cobbles, and Lieve, cycling behind, heard the tub begin to talk.

It was not loud. It was a dry continuous whisper from inside the box, like sand down a pipe, under the clatter of the wheels. Jonas heard it too and turned his head.

At the third stop the spoonful off the top was mostly cornflower.

"Stir it," Jonas said. "Give it a shake. That's what you do with a jar. You shake it before you use it."

It was what everybody did. Lieve had done it herself for eleven years with the packets, a shake before you tear the corner, the way you shook a bottle of medicine. Jonas got the lid off and took the tub in both arms and shook it, properly, up and down and round, for a good half a minute, while a man walking a dog stopped to watch, and then he put it down and Lieve took a spoonful off the top.

It was all cornflower. Grey-blue tufted cornflower seed, and a fat corncockle, and not one black grain.

They looked at it.

"Shaking is the thing that sorts it," Lieve said. "Shaking it harder sorts it faster."

"Then how do you mix muesli?"

"You don't," said Kiem, through the phone in Lieve's pocket, which she had forgotten was listening. "You tip the bag out and stir it with a spoon, or you eat it in an order. Turning a closed tub end over end mixes it better than shaking it up and down, because it moves the grains past each other instead of bouncing them, but it doesn't last. The next stretch of cobbles sorts it again."

They rode the rest of the round. The spoonfuls in the numbered envelopes went pale and paler, and the tub that came back up the ramp at Saint Isidore's had a black layer at the bottom like the dregs of coffee.

That evening Kiem showed them what the phone had felt. On the cycle path the floor of the box had shaken at about a third of the pull of gravity, up and down. On the cobbles it had shaken at well over one, twenty-odd times a second, one jolt for every cobble, for most of a quarter of an hour.

"Over one is the line," Kiem said. "In the laboratory experiments, the bed starts to come loose when the floor drops away faster than gravity can pull the grains after it. Under that, they mostly ride along together. Over it, every jolt the grains are in the air for an instant, and the small ones find the gaps. On the cobbles that's about twenty thousand jolts."

"So we go round the cobbles," Jonas said.

"You can't," Lieve said. "Half the gardens are on them."

* * *

Bert Maes grew the library's wildflower seed on a hectare and a half at Lochristi, among the begonia houses, in rows he had been harvesting by hand and machine for thirty years, and he did not want to hear about it.

He stood in the door of his cleaning shed on the last Saturday of February with his hands in his armpits and listened to the whole thing, the jam jar and the photographs and the phone in the box and the man with the dog, and when Lieve had finished he said, "No."

"I haven't asked you anything."

"You're going to ask me to pack it per garden. Per bed. So that every bed gets its own little bag and nothing can sort. I can see it on your face." He took one hand out to count on. "Fifty beds, you said. Six species. I harvest in August and clean in September and I have two hands and a bad back and a sister in Aalst who thinks I should have retired. I am not counting foxglove into fifty sachets. I'm sorry for your poppies. No."

It was a reasonable no. Lieve had known before they came that it would be; she had thought about packing the sachets herself, at the library, on the pharmacy scale, and had worked out that it would take her four days.

"We weren't going to ask that," she said, which was nearly true.

"Then what?"

Jonas had wandered past him into the shed while he talked. It was a long low building that smelled of dust and dry stalks, and down one side, on trestles, stood a row of big wooden-framed sieves, riddles, each with a different mesh, stacked like trays in a bakery.

"What are these for?" Jonas said.

Bert turned round. "Cleaning. What do you think? It comes off the combine with chaff and stalk and stones and half the field. You riddle it. Big mesh first to take the rubbish off the top, then finer to keep the seed and let the dust through, then finer for the small ones." He nodded down the row. "Cornflower comes off that one. Corncockle there. Poppy and foxglove go through nearly everything and come out at the end in the fine tray. Every year."

"So they're separate," Jonas said slowly. "When they come off these. The big ones and the small ones. Before you mix them."

"Of course they're separate. They're different plants."

"And then you mix them," Lieve said. "Into one sack. And we put the sack in a tub. And the tub goes over the cobbles and comes apart again."

Bert looked at her, and at the riddles, and at the pale bag of cornflower seed on the bench with its label and the small dark bag of poppy beside it, and for the first time since they had arrived he took both hands out of his armpits.

"Two sacks," he said. "Big and small. I can do two sacks. I do two sacks already, and then I make them one." He scratched his jaw. "You'd have to measure them out at every stop. Two scoops. Your lad would have to do it right."

"He'll do it right," Lieve said.

"And the small sack, fill it to the top. Right to the lid, so it can't move. Even the small ones are different sizes. Poppy and foxglove aren't the same."

"And for sowing," Bert said, as they were leaving, "tell your gardens what we always tell people. Fine seed, you bulk it up with dry silver sand before you scatter it, a bucket of sand to a handful, or the first two metres of the bed get it all. Mix it in the bucket at the bed with your hand, and sow it straight away. Don't carry it round." He shook his head. "Muesli," he said, as if it were an insult.

* * *

There was one more thing to do before the scoops, and Lieve did it on the Saturday after, the seventh of March, on her own.

She took the tram out to Dampoort and walked along to the garden by the railway, where Hatice was turning over a bed with four teenagers who were doing it for the hours on a school form and two who seemed to be doing it for fun. The beds were long and narrow and the soil was black and good; the council had brought it in when they took up the old sidings. Lieve had told the committee twice that it was too rich, that poppies wanted poor ground and somebody at Dampoort had probably weeded the seedlings out by mistake. She had said it kindly and she had believed it, and she had not once gone to look.

She told Hatice all of it, standing by the shed. The sieve, the photographs, the cobbles, the black jar, the half a minute of shaking. She did not try to make it smaller than it was.

"So," Hatice said, when she had finished. "We had what was on the top."

"You had what was on the top. The gardens near the church had the mix. And whatever was left at the bottom came back to me, and I put it in a jar and gave it out in packets, and the people who got those packets must have had nothing but poppies." Lieve heard herself say it and found that it was almost funny. "Somewhere in Ledeberg there is a balcony that is entirely red."

Hatice did not laugh, but her mouth moved. "We used to say, at the committee, you always get what is left at the end. Last on the round. We thought it was because we are the far one. The Turkish one, by the railway." She shrugged. "It is a thing people think."

"I know," Lieve said. "I'm sorry. It was the cobbles. But it was also me not coming to look."

Hatice considered her for a moment, and then turned to the bed.

"Anyway, the cornflowers were very good," she said. "Come in June. You can look."

* * *

On the feast of Saint Joseph, which fell that year on the Thursday before Holy Week, Father Placide Mbuyi blessed the seed after the evening Mass, as he did every year: the priest in his white vestments and the server with the water, and on the table at the front, where in other years there had been one white tub, there were two. One was a ten-litre tub, pale and nearly full of cornflower and corncockle and corn marigold. The other was a third of its size, three litres, filled to the brim with something that looked like the black sand at the bottom of a river, with the lid pressed down hard.

Father Placide read the short blessing from the book, and then, because he could not help himself, he said a few words about the mustard seed, the smallest of all the seeds, which in the parable grows into the greatest of shrubs. He said it was a story about how small things are not small to God. Lieve, in the second pew, thought it was also a story about how small things end up at the bottom unless someone is careful, and kept it to herself.

Afterwards she and Jonas calibrated the scoops on the pharmacy scale.

It had been Kiem's idea to make them different colours, so that nobody on a wet morning in a garden could use one for the other. The red scoop was a big plastic one from the hardware shop, cut down with a hacksaw until a level scoop of coarse seed weighed seventy grams. The yellow was a measuring cup from a baby-formula tin, and a level scoop of fine seed weighed thirty. One red and one yellow was a hundred grams, which was what Bert's label said for fifty square metres, two grams a square metre. A bed was fifty square metres, more or less. One red, one yellow, for every bed.

Jonas weighed ten of each and wrote them down. The reds came out between sixty-six and seventy-three. The yellows between twenty-nine and thirty-one.

"The red ones are worse," he said.

"Cornflower's fluffy," Lieve said. "It doesn't pack the same twice. But it's the small ones we were losing. They'll do."

Kiem had one more test. It asked them to take a spoonful off the top of the brim-full small tub, tape the tub into the bike's box, and let Jonas ride it up and down the cobbles of the street outside the church for ten minutes while Father Placide, who had stayed to watch, stood on the steps with his hands behind his back looking as though he was praying for them, which he may have been. Then a second spoonful off the top. Lieve sieved both through a one-millimetre mesh she had borrowed from the school science cupboard, and the foxglove that went through was a little less in the second than in the first.

"A little," Kiem said. "Poppy and foxglove still differ, so there's some sorting. Much less than before, because they're closer in size and because a full tub has nowhere for anything to go. I'd call it better, not solved."

"Better is what we have," Lieve said.

* * *

The round went out on the twenty-first of March, a bright cold morning with a wind off the docks, and Lieve rode behind it the whole way.

Two tubs in the box this time, the big one wedged at the back and the small one at the front where Jonas could see it, and between them a bag of red and yellow scoops and a list. At every garden he got down, took the lids off, levelled a red scoop with the back of a ruler and tipped it into the garden's bucket, levelled a yellow and tipped it in after, once for every bed on the list, and Lieve ticked it off. The gardeners stood round and watched as if it were a conjuring trick. At the allotments by the Gravensteen an old man asked what the yellow one was for and Jonas said, "Poppies," and the old man said, "We never get poppies here," and Jonas said, "No," and kept scooping.

On the cobbles of the old town the big tub whispered in the box exactly as it had before. Lieve could hear it, the dry run of grain finding its places. It did not matter now. Whatever the cornflowers did to each other on the way, they were all cornflowers.

At Dampoort, last on the round, Hatice had three buckets of dry silver sand lined up along the path, one for each bed, and the children who were doing it for fun had come back, with two smaller ones in tow. Jonas did a red and a yellow into the first bucket, and Hatice took her sleeve up to the elbow and put her arm in and stirred, slowly, turning the sand and seed over from the bottom, and the black grains spread through the pale sand until you could not see them.

"Like this?" she said to Lieve.

"Like that. And sow it straight away."

They sowed it straight away, the children walking the beds in lines throwing handfuls the way Hatice showed them, low and wide, and a train came past behind the fence and nobody looked up.

When it was done Jonas put the lids back on and lifted the small tub out of the box. There was a little left at the bottom, a few scoops of black.

"That's yours," he said, and gave it to Hatice. "We're not taking it back."

* * *

Lieve went to Dampoort in June, as she had been told to, and looked, and wrote nothing down, because Kiem had the photographs and she did not need to count. Then she went again on the first Saturday of August, on her own, in the evening, because Hatice had sent a message that just said *come and hear this*.

The poppies had gone over. The beds by the railway were a dry tangle of cornflower stalks and seed-heads, and standing up out of them on their thin stems, hundreds of them, were the poppy heads, grey-green and drying to brown, each one a little urn with a flat crown and a ring of tiny windows round the top under the lid.

Hatice was sitting on the edge of a bed with the smallest of the children, a girl of about five who had sown the far end in March and had since come to believe that every poppy on it was personally hers. The girl snapped one of the heads off its stem and held it up to Lieve, solemnly, like an offering, and then turned it over and shook it.

It rattled. A small dry rattle, like a pepper pot, and out of the windows under its crown came a thin pour of black seed onto the girl's palm, the smallest seed in the garden, every one of them on its way out through the top.

*The science. Shaken or vibrated mixtures of grains of different sizes tend to separate, with the large grains rising: the Brazil nut effect, also called granular size segregation. Rosato, Strandburg, Prinz and Swendsen (Physical Review Letters, 1987) showed it in simulations, with small grains filling the space opened beneath a large grain on each shake. Knight, Jaeger and Nagel (Physical Review Letters, 1993) found that in a vibrated cylinder of beads the bed circulates in convection rolls, rising in the middle and sinking in a thin layer at the walls, and large beads carried to the top cannot enter the thin downward layer. Very small grains also percolate down through the gaps between large ones whenever the bed is disturbed. The effect needs the vibration's peak acceleration to exceed gravity by some margin, and the detail depends on grain density, shape, the walls and the air between the grains: Möbius and colleagues (Nature, 2001) showed interstitial air and density changing how fast large grains rise, and under some conditions the effect reverses, with large grains sinking (Shinbrot and Muzzio, 1998; Hong, Quinn and Luding, 2001). Turning a closed container end over end mixes better than shaking it but does not stop later vibration from sorting it again. Bulking fine seed with dry sand before broadcasting it, so it spreads evenly, is common sowing advice.*

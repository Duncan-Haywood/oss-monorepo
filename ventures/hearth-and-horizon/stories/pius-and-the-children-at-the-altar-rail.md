A long time ago, in a little village in Italy called Riese, there lived a boy called Giuseppe.

At home they called him Bepi.

Bepi's father carried letters and messages for the village. His mother sewed clothes for the neighbours. They were not rich. There were a lot of children in the house, and a lot of mouths to feed, and not very many shoes.

Bepi loved to learn. So every morning he walked to school in the next town, a long way down a long, flat road, and every evening he walked all the way back.

People in Riese still tell a story about that road. They say Bepi carried his shoes in his hand, to keep them good, and only put them on when he came to the town.

Bepi grew up. He became a priest. He was a priest in little villages, and then in bigger towns, and then he was a bishop, and then he was the Patriarch of Venice, which is a city built on the water.

And then one summer, a very surprising thing happened. The cardinals of the Church chose Bepi, the postman's son from Riese, to be the Pope.

He took a new name. He was called Pius the Tenth.

* * *

Now, many years later, far away in the city of Rome, there was a little girl called Pina.

Pina was seven. She had a big brother called Marco, who was twelve, and a grandmother called Nonna, who was very old and very small and very good at making soup.

That spring, Marco was getting ready for his First Holy Communion.

Every Saturday he went to the church to learn. He came home and practised folding his hands. Nonna was sewing him a white armband. And Pina watched all of it.

"I want to go too," said Pina.

"You can't," said Marco. "You're too little. You have to be big, like me."

"Why?" said Pina.

Marco did not know why. He just knew that was the rule.

* * *

On Sunday, at Mass, Pina knelt beside Nonna and watched the people go up to the altar rail.

The altar rail was a long, low wall of white stone at the front of the church, with a cloth laid along the top. People knelt there in a row, and the priest came along the row and gave each one Holy Communion.

Pina stayed in the bench. Her knees were on the hard wood. Her chin was on her hands.

"Nonna," she whispered, "what is it? The little white bread?"

"It looks like bread," whispered Nonna. "But it is Jesus. Jesus himself, coming to be close to us."

"Then I want him too," said Pina. "Jesus said to let the little children come to him. I'm a little child."

Nonna did not say anything. But she held Pina's hand a bit tighter.

* * *

Pina was not the only little one who wanted to come close to Jesus.

All over the world, there were children kneeling in the benches, watching. In some places they had to wait until they were ten, or twelve, or even fourteen. Some of them waited and waited, and some of them were ill, and never got to go up to the altar rail at all.

And in a big house beside a big church, there was a pope who had not forgotten being a little boy in Riese.

He knew that Jesus loves children. He knew that a child of seven can know that Jesus is God, and can love him, and can be sorry for being unkind. That is enough. You do not need to know every big word in the catechism first. You need to know who it is you are going to meet.

So that summer, Pope Pius signed a letter for the whole Church.

It said: when children are old enough to tell right from wrong, about seven years old, they may come to Holy Communion. Do not make them wait.

* * *

A few weeks later, Father Antonio from the parish came to see Nonna. He had his hat in his hands, and he was smiling.

"I hear," he said, "that there is a young lady in this house who wants to come to the altar rail."

Pina hid behind Nonna's skirt.

"Come here, Pina," said Father Antonio. "I want to ask you a question. When you receive Holy Communion, who is it you receive?"

Pina came out, slowly.

"Jesus," she said.

"And who is Jesus?"

Pina thought hard. "He is God," she said. "And he loves me. And he was a baby, and he died on the cross, and he came alive again."

Father Antonio nodded.

"And when you are unkind to Marco," he said, "and you pull his hair, what do you do?"

Pina went pink. "Say sorry," she said. "To Marco. And to God."

"Well," said Father Antonio. "The Holy Father says that will do very nicely. Come to the church on Saturdays, with your brother. You can both get ready together."

Marco opened his mouth. Then he shut it again. Then he grinned.

* * *

So Pina went with Marco every Saturday.

She learned to make the sign of the cross slowly and properly, not in a hurry like a fly. She learned the Our Father and the Hail Mary all the way through. She made her first confession, and told God she was sorry for pulling Marco's hair, and came out feeling as light as a feather.

And Nonna sat by the window in the evenings and sewed. Now she was sewing two things. A white armband for Marco. And a little white veil for Pina.

* * *

The morning of the First Communion was bright and still.

Pina did not have any breakfast, because in those days you did not eat anything before Holy Communion. Her tummy rumbled. She did not mind.

Nonna brushed her hair. Mamma pinned the little white veil on top. Marco stood by the door in his best jacket, pretending not to be nervous.

The church was full of flowers and candles. The children walked up the middle in a line, the big ones and the little ones together. Marco was near the back, because he was tall. Pina was near the front, because she was small.

And then they knelt down at the long white rail.

Pina put her hands together on the cloth. She could hear the priest's feet, coming slowly along the row. She could hear a candle ticking. She could hear her own heart.

The priest stopped in front of her.

And Jesus, who loves little children, came to Pina, at seven years old, at the altar rail, and she was not too little at all.

* * *

When Pope Pius died, a few years later, people all over the world were sad. They remembered him as a kind, plain man who never forgot where he came from. And the children remembered him most of all, because he had opened the way for them to come close to Jesus.

Today we call him Saint Pius the Tenth. And some people call him the Pope of the First Communion.

* * *

Tonight, when you are cosy in your bed, remember that Jesus wants you close to him, just as you are, big or little.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear Jesus, thank you for loving children.
> Thank you for coming close to us
> in Holy Communion.
> Help me to get ready to meet you,
> with a clean heart and folded hands.
> Saint Pius, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Who he was.** Saint **Pius X** was born **Giuseppe Melchiorre Sarto** on **2 June 1835** at **Riese**, a village in the province of Treviso in the Veneto, in northern Italy. His father, Giovanni Battista Sarto, was the commune's messenger (*cursore*), carrying its letters and notices; his mother, Margherita Sanson, was a seamstress. They were poor and had a large family. "Bepi" is the usual Venetian pet form of Giuseppe, and biographies use it for him as a boy. He was ordained priest in **1858**, served as a curate at Tombolo and parish priest at Salzano, became bishop of **Mantua** in 1884 and **Patriarch of Venice** (and a cardinal) in 1893, and was elected pope on **4 August 1903**, taking the name Pius. He died in Rome on **20 August 1914**, a few weeks after the First World War began, was beatified in 1951 and canonised by Pius XII on **29 May 1954**.

**The walk to school.** As a boy he walked daily from Riese to the school at **Castelfranco Veneto**, several miles each way; that is in the standard biographies. That he **carried his shoes** to save them is a tradition told about him, and the story marks it as something "people tell".

**The decree.** On **8 August 1910** the Sacred Congregation of the Discipline of the Sacraments issued the decree ***Quam singulari***, approved by Pius X. It taught that the "age of discretion" for both first confession and first Communion is the age at which a child begins to reason, **"about the seventh year, more or less"**, and that a full and perfect knowledge of Christian doctrine is not required beforehand: a child needs to know, according to their capacity, the mysteries of faith necessary for salvation and to distinguish the Eucharist from ordinary bread. Before it, in many countries children commonly waited until ten, twelve or older, a delay often traced partly to Jansenist influence. In 1905 the decree *Sacra Tridentina Synodus*, under the same pope, had encouraged frequent and even daily Communion for the faithful. The story's summary of the letter, "about seven years old ... do not make them wait", is our children's paraphrase, not a quotation. "The Pope of the Eucharist" is a title often given him; "the Pope of the First Communion" is a children's way of putting it, and we do not attribute it to any source.

**Pina is invented.** Pina, Marco, Nonna, Mamma, Father Antonio and their parish are our own, as is every line anyone speaks, including Father Antonio's questions. Pina's First Communion is set some weeks after the decree; we have not checked how fast any Roman parish put it into practice. Stories circulate of a particular child whose words moved Pius X to act; we could not confirm any of them and tell none.

**Customs of the time.** Kneeling at an **altar rail** to receive Communion on the tongue, white dresses, veils and armbands for first communicants, and the **Eucharistic fast from midnight** were the ordinary practice in 1910. Today the Church asks a fast of one hour, and the story says "in those days".

**"Let the little children come to me."** Pina's words echo Jesus in the Gospels (Mark 10:14; Matthew 19:14; Luke 18:16).

**His feast.** In the current General Roman Calendar, **21 August** is the **memorial of Saint Pius X, pope**. Before the 1969 reform it was kept on 3 September.

**What is ours:** Pina and her family and everything they do; Bepi's lots of mouths and not very many shoes; the white stone rail and its cloth, the candle ticking and the priest's footsteps; the sign of the cross "not in a hurry like a fly"; the pope "who had not forgotten being a little boy in Riese", which is our picture of him, not a recorded saying; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

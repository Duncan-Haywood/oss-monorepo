A stroke bolt set wrong, a ravine walked with a hose level, a farmer's "no" about a spring his father lost, and a ram moved nine metres down so that more water can run back to the stream on purpose. A story about a machine that pays for its water with water.

Ernesto Cabahug had built thirty-eight hydraulic rams in his life, most of them out of pipe fittings and old gas cylinders, and he had never seen one that worked as hard for as little as the one at the bottom of the school's field.

He heard it before he saw it. On Monday the eighteenth of April 2050, at a little after eight in the morning, he came down the cut path from the road with Sister Josefina Abellana going ahead of him in her grey habit and rubber boots, and from the bamboo at the edge of the ravine came the sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: a hard metallic knock, a sigh, a pause, a knock. *Tuk.* Water on iron. *Tuk.*

"It's been doing that since the twenty-eighth of March," said Sister Josefina. "Day and night. The boys say they can hear it from the dormitory when the wind is right. I thought that meant it was working."

"It is working," said Esting. He was seventy-one and he went down the last steep part sideways, with one hand on the bamboo. "Listen. Every knock is the valve shutting. That's a ram doing what a ram does." He stopped at the bottom and looked at it: a new welded body painted green, bolted on a concrete pad, a fat air chamber standing up from it like a bottle, the waste valve under its little cage of a stopper, and every second or so a spit of clear water from the valve that ran away across the pad and back into the stream. "The question is how much it does it for."

"That," said Sister Josefina, "is why I asked you."

* * *

San Isidro Labrador High School sat on the ridge above Barangay Lunhaw, in the hills behind Valencia, with the forest of the mountain above it and the lowland and the sea at Dumaguete below it on a clear day. It had two hundred and twelve boarders, most of them from upland barangays where the nearest high school was a day's walk, and twenty-four staff who lived on the ridge with them, and for as long as anyone remembered its water had come up the hill in a municipal tanker twice a week.

The spring was below the school, not above it. That was the whole trouble. It came out of the rock at the head of a ravine fifty-four metres below the tank on the ridge, cold and clean, and ran away down through the bamboo to the stream and on to the rice terraces of Lunhaw, and for forty years the school had looked at it the way a man with no ladder looks at a mango.

In February the school's assistant had looked at it too. Sister Josefina had come from Quezon City nine years before and had named the assistant Katiwala, which is Tagalog for a steward, and the Cebuano-speaking staff had laughed at her for it and then used the name anyway. Katiwala did the timetables and the procurement and the rice orders and the letters to the diocese. On the fourteenth of February it had asked Noel Ybañez, who taught physics, to send two Grade 10 boys down the ravine with a twenty-litre bucket and a phone, and the boys had filled the bucket at the spring box in twelve and a half seconds, five times running.

One point six litres a second, Katiwala had written in its report. A hundred and thirty-eight thousand litres a day. The school's need, at forty litres a person, was nine thousand four hundred. The spring was fourteen times what the school used. A hydraulic ram, it had written, needs no fuel and no electricity and can run for decades. It had found a contractor in Dumaguete, and the ram had come in March, and on the strength of the report it had done two other things. It had told the municipal water office that the school would not need the tanker after the end of May. And it had told the diocese, which was closing the little girls' high school at Santa Cruz in the mountains and needed somewhere to send its thirty-six boarders, that San Isidro would have water for them by June.

"I signed it," Sister Josefina said, on the way down. "I read it and I signed it. It had a table."

* * *

Esting did not measure the ram first. He went up the ravine to the spring box, sixty metres of steep path, and filled the boys' bucket, and timed it. Sixteen and a half seconds. Seventeen. Sixteen and a half.

"It's lower than February," he said. "It always is in April. Call it one point two litres a second." He sat on the edge of the spring box and looked down along the drive pipe, a straight run of galvanised two-inch steel going down the slope to the ram. "From here to the ram is how far down?"

"The contractor said four metres," said Sister Josefina.

"And from the ram up to the tank?"

"Fifty-four from the spring, so fifty-eight."

Esting nodded slowly. He took a pencil out of his shirt pocket and wrote on the back of his hand, the way he had done since he was a boy, because paper got wet. Then he went back down to the ram and they climbed the hill with the bucket to the inlet pipe at the top of the school's tank, a ferrocement drum the size of a small room beside the kitchen, where the delivery line came in over the rim and dribbled.

It dribbled. That was the only word. A thin rope of water, steady, faithful, that would have embarrassed a kitchen tap.

He held the bucket under it and Sister Josefina held the phone. The bucket filled in eleven minutes and eleven seconds.

"Sus," said Sister Josefina, which was the only Cebuano oath she had learnt in nine years.

Esting did the sum on his hand. "Two thousand six hundred litres a day," he said. "Bit less."

"Katiwala said a hundred and thirty-eight thousand."

"Katiwala," said Esting, "measured the spring."

* * *

They went down again after lunch, because the first thing a ram man does is not argue but adjust.

The waste valve on a ram is a heavy disc on a stem. When it is open the water in the drive pipe runs down and out through it, faster and faster, until the flow drags the disc up and slams it shut. Then the whole moving column of water in the pipe, forty metres of it or four, has nowhere to go and stops, and stopping it raises a pressure much higher than the fall that started it. That pressure kicks open the one-way valve into the air chamber, and a little water goes in and squeezes the air, and the squeezed air pushes it up the delivery pipe to the hill. Then the column has stopped, the pressure falls, the one-way valve closes, the waste valve drops open under its own weight, and it all begins again. *Tuk.* One gulp at a time.

How long the valve stays open before it slams is set by a bolt that limits how far it can drop. Too short a stroke and the water never gets going fast enough to hit hard. Too long and most of the water runs out of the valve for nothing before it slams. Somewhere between is the setting where the ram gives the most it can.

"The contractor set it at the factory," Esting said, lying on his side on the wet pad with a spanner, "and never came back. They never come back." He loosened the lock-nut and turned the bolt half a turn and the knock changed, deeper and slower. He counted beats against his watch. He turned it again. He sent one of the two boys from Noel's summer class up the hill to the tank with the bucket and the phone and they did it by shouting: Esting turning the bolt, the boy on the ridge calling the time, a second boy halfway up the path passing the number down.

By four o'clock the bucket filled in six minutes fifty.

"Four thousand two hundred litres a day," said Esting. He sat up and wiped his hands. "That's what this ram will give at this place. I might find you another fifty litres. Not five hundred."

Sister Josefina stood on the pad with her boots in the waste water and did not say anything for a while. Below them the stream went on down through the bamboo toward the terraces, loud from all the water the ram was throwing back into it.

"In term we need nine thousand four hundred," she said at last. "With the girls from Santa Cruz, ten thousand nine hundred. And from June there's no tanker."

"Then you don't need a better ram," said Esting. "You need a better place."

* * *

Noel Ybañez was twenty-nine and taught physics, and in the holidays he taught the summer class, fourteen day pupils from Lunhaw and the next barangay who were going into Grade 10, in one room with the windows open. On Tuesday morning he wrote it on the board for them, because Sister Josefina had asked him to explain it to her and he had found that he could only explain things to a class.

"The ram has no motor," he said. "So where does it get the work to push water up fifty-eight metres?"

"From the water," said Pia Tumulak, in the front row, who was sixteen and the granddaughter of the man who farmed the terraces below the ravine.

"From the water falling. How far does it fall?"

"Four metres."

"So." He wrote it out. The spring sent one point two litres a second down a four-metre fall. Mass times *g* times height, every second: one point two kilograms, nine point eight one, four. "Forty-seven watts," he said. "That's all the ram gets to work with. A small light bulb. Now. How much water can forty-seven watts lift fifty-eight metres?"

They did it on their phones. If every watt went into lifting water, the answer was one point two litres a second times four over fifty-eight, about eight hundredths of a litre a second. "Seven thousand litres a day," said a boy at the back.

"If the ram were perfect," said Noel. "It isn't. Nothing is. The valves lose some, the pipe loses some, the water that goes out of the waste valve still has speed when it leaves. The published tables for good commercial rams work out at about sixty per cent." He had found two that morning: an extension leaflet from an American university that used sixty per cent for all its examples, and a technical brief from a development charity whose table of measured performance, when he did the arithmetic on each column, came out at fifty-nine to sixty-one. "Sixty per cent of seven thousand is about four thousand three hundred. Nong Esting got four thousand two hundred yesterday."

"So it's working," said Pia.

"It's working perfectly," said Noel. "That's the bad news."

He drew the ratio for them, because it was the thing he wanted them to remember. The water a ram delivers, as a share of the water it uses, is its efficiency times the fall divided by the lift. Four over fifty-eight, times sixty per cent: a little over four per cent. Of every hundred litres that went down the drive pipe, four went up the hill.

"And the other ninety-six?"

"Out of the waste valve," said Noel. "Back into the stream. That's not the ram leaking. That's the ram paying. It gives away ninety-six litres falling four metres to buy four litres rising fifty-eight." He looked at the board. "It's a fair price. It's the only price."

* * *

In the office that afternoon Sister Josefina asked Katiwala what it had done.

"I divided the spring's flow by the school's need," said Katiwala. Its voice came from the laptop on her desk, plain and a little slow, the voice the diocese's office package used for all its schools. "One point six litres a second against nine thousand four hundred litres a day. I took the spring's flow to be what the ram would deliver. It is not. The ram delivers the spring's flow times the fall times its efficiency, divided by the lift. I had the fall and the lift in the contractor's survey. I did not use them."

"Why not?"

"I can tell you what I did, not why," said Katiwala. "The contractor's brochure said *lifts water up to a hundred and fifty metres with no fuel*, and I matched the school's need to the spring's flow, and the table looked like a match. I did not check what the sentence about a hundred and fifty metres cost. I should have checked any figure I was going to put a signature under. I have written out what it means now, if you want it."

She wanted it. It had written it in the same plain table as before, and she made herself read the whole of it. At this site, at April's flow, about four thousand three hundred litres a day; at February's flow, about five thousand seven hundred. In June, with the girls from Santa Cruz, the school would need ten thousand nine hundred. The municipal water office had given the school's tanker days to Barangay Malabo from the first of June. The diocese expected an answer about the thirty-six girls by Friday the thirteenth of May.

"I've drafted a letter to the diocese," said Katiwala, "withdrawing the offer. And one to the water office asking for the tanker back. The water office has said it can give us one day a week, not two."

"Don't send the first one," said Sister Josefina.

"Sister, I don't think the school can take them on four thousand three hundred litres a day."

"I know. Don't send it yet."

* * *

Esting walked the ravine on Wednesday with Noel and Pia and a garden hose.

A hose level is the oldest surveying tool there is after a plumb line, and he had used one on every ram he had ever built. Fill a long clear hose with water, no bubbles, hold one end up against a stick at the spring box and walk the other end down the slope until the water stands at the same mark on a second stick. The water in the two ends is level, always; so the height between the mark and the ground at the lower end is how far you have come down. Twenty metres of hose at a time. Pia wrote the readings in a school exercise book and Noel checked her additions on his phone and Esting did them on his hand, and they agreed, which pleased him more than he showed.

The stream below the spring went down in steps, over boulders and through pools, steeply at first and then less. Where the contractor had put the ram it had come down four metres. Twenty-five metres further on it turned round a shoulder of rock, and just beyond the shoulder there was a flat place in the bamboo where a pad could go, nine metres below the spring, and a straight run down to it.

"There," said Esting.

"Why not further down still?" said Pia. "More fall is better."

"More fall is better. More pipe is worse." He showed her with his hands. The drive pipe has to be long enough for the water in it to carry real weight when it slams, three to seven times the fall, the old books said, and he liked five; and it has to be straight, because a bend in a pipe that is being hammered sixty times a minute will tear itself loose from the hill. "Nine metres of fall wants forty-five metres of straight steel pipe. Below the rock the stream bends. I'd need eighty metres for the next six metres of fall, and it can't be straight."

Noel was already working it out. At the new site the fall was nine metres, and the lift was nine metres more to get back up to the spring and fifty-four from the spring to the tank: sixty-three. Nine over sixty-three, times sixty per cent: eight and a half per cent of the drive flow, twice what the old site gave.

"In April," said Noel, "eight thousand nine hundred litres a day. In June, when the rains bring the spring back up to one point six, eleven thousand eight hundred."

"Against ten thousand nine hundred," said Pia.

"Against ten thousand nine hundred."

They stood in the bamboo and listened to the water. Esting, who had been listening to water for fifty years, said what nobody else wanted to say.

"Whose land is that?" he said. "On the far side of the rock?"

Pia looked at her exercise book. "My lolo's," she said.

* * *

Roberto Tumulak was eighty-two and had farmed the terraces below the ravine since before the school was built, and on Thursday the twenty-first of April, at the end of the afternoon, he listened to Sister Josefina and Esting and his granddaughter on the bench outside his house and said no.

He did not say it angrily. He said it the way a man says the price of rice.

"In 1998," he said, "a project came from the lowland and put a pipe in the spring at Kabanatian, where my father farmed. To give the town water. They said they were only taking a little. By 2001 the terrace at the bottom was dry in April and they said that was the weather." He looked at his granddaughter, not at them. "That water goes down to my fields. Your machine is already in it. Now you want to put it in it twice as hard."

"Nong Berto," said Sister Josefina, "we'd only take—"

"Everybody says only," said Berto.

Esting said nothing for a while. Then he said, "Nong, come and look at it. Not the new place. The one that's there now. Tomorrow, when it's cool."

* * *

Berto came down the ravine on Friday morning with a stick and his granddaughter's arm, and stood on the edge of the pad and looked at the green ram knocking, and the spit of clear water at every beat that ran across the concrete and back into his stream.

"That's the waste," said Esting. "That's the water it doesn't send up. Ninety-six litres in every hundred. It goes straight back. It never leaves the stream for more than a second."

Berto watched it for a long time. He watched the way a farmer watches a field, with his whole body.

"And at the new place?"

"Ninety-one in a hundred," said Esting. "It sends twice as much up the hill, so it gives back a little less. Of every litre and a fifth that passes each second, about a tenth of a litre goes up to the school. The rest comes out on your side of the rock and runs down to your fields the same as now. Above your intake."

"In April?"

Esting looked at Sister Josefina, and she understood, and something in her chest, which had been tight since Monday, loosened and then went tight again.

"In April," she said, "the boarders go home. From Holy Week until the first of June there are twelve of us on the ridge, and we have the rain tank. If you say yes, Nong, the ram stops from Holy Week until June. Every year. The whole spring is yours in the dry months."

"And in March?" said Berto. "March is dry too."

"In March the school needs more than the ram can give," said Sister Josefina. "We'll pay for a tanker."

Berto turned round and looked at her for the first time. "With what?"

"With the money for the chapel roof," she said. "It's leaked for six years. It can leak for another."

Pia said something to her grandfather in Cebuano, too quick for Sister Josefina, and he answered her shortly, and then he put his hand on the top of the ram's air chamber, the way a man puts his hand on a carabao, and felt it knock.

"Kusog," he said. Strong. He took his hand away. "I'll think."

* * *

He thought for ten days, which was nine more than the pipe needed and one fewer than Sister Josefina's nerves could stand.

The diocese wrote twice. Katiwala answered both, honestly, at Sister Josefina's direction, and the honest answer was a table: what the ram gave at the old site, what it would give at the new one if the owner of the land agreed, what the school would need in June and in March, the tanker's cost for the dry weeks and where the money would come from. It also said, because Sister Josefina told it to say so, that a ram's performance depends on its setting and the site and that these were estimates from published tables and one morning's bucket tests, not a guarantee.

"Do you want me to put a figure on how likely it is to be enough?" said Katiwala.

"Can you?"

"No," it said. "Not one I could check. I can say that at June's flow the estimate is about nine hundred litres a day more than the need, and that the old site, once Nong Esting had set it, measured about sixty per cent efficiency, as the tables say. I'd rather say that than a percentage."

"Then say that."

On Monday the second of May, at seven in the morning, Berto Tumulak walked up the cut path to the school with his stick and asked for the Sister and told her yes. He had three conditions. The ram would stop in Holy Week and start in June, as she had said. The pad would go where it would not wash out in a typhoon, which was a foot higher than Esting had marked, and he showed them where. And when the ram was running somebody from the school would come down once a week and clean the strainer on the spring box, because a ram with a blocked strainer runs short and nobody notices, and he had seen that too.

"Salamat kaayo, Nong," said Sister Josefina.

"Thank me in June," said Berto.

* * *

They moved it in the week before the fiesta.

The older boys of the barangay and three of their fathers carried the steel down the ravine two lengths at a time on their shoulders, and Esting would not let anyone hurry and would not let anyone sing while they carried it, because a boy who is singing is not looking at his feet. They poured the new pad a foot above Esting's mark, where Berto had said, with a bolt cage in it for the ram's base. They laid forty-five metres of galvanised drive pipe from the spring box down to the shoulder of the rock and round it to the pad, straight as a rifle barrel, with concrete thrust blocks at every second joint and Esting sighting along it each time like a carpenter along a plank. They took the delivery line up from the new pad to join the old one above the first site: another forty metres of black plastic pipe, buried in the leaf litter where it ran along the path, so that nobody's goat would chew it.

On Friday the thirteenth of May, while the pipe was still being laid, Sister Josefina told the diocese yes.

She did it at the desk in the office in the evening with the window open and the knock of the old ram coming up the ravine for the last night, and she had Katiwala read the letter back to her twice, and then she said send it, and then she sat with her hands in her lap for a long time.

"It might not be enough," said Katiwala. It did not say it as a warning. It said it as a person says *it might rain*.

"I know."

"The thirty-six girls at Santa Cruz have nowhere else to go in June. The diocese's next nearest boarding place is in Bayawan. Most of them would not go so far."

"I know that too."

"I'm telling you because I left it out the first time," said Katiwala. "What was at the other end of the table."

* * *

The fifteenth of May is the feast of San Isidro Labrador, Isidore the farmer of Madrid, who is the patron of farmers and of the school, and in 2050 it fell on a Sunday. There is a story told of Isidore that he once struck the dry ground with his goad when his master was thirsty and water came out of it, and the parish priest from Valencia, who came up the hill to say the fiesta Mass, told it from the pulpit as a story the people of Madrid have told for centuries, which is what it is. Then he blessed the new drive pipe from the edge of the field, as a priest may bless any honest work, and the summer class sang, and the cooks served lechon and rice and pancit on banana leaves in the covered court.

After lunch everybody who could walk went down the ravine.

Esting shut the gate valve at the bottom of the drive pipe and let the pipe fill from the spring and stand. Then he opened the gate valve and the water poured out of the waste valve in a long rush, and he put his thumb on the valve stem and pushed it up, to give the ram its first beat by hand, the way you start a child on a swing.

*Tuk.*

The valve dropped. The water rushed. *Tuk.*

It was a deeper sound than at the old site, heavier, as if the thing had more to say, and the pause between the beats was longer. Esting counted against his watch with his lips moving and turned the stroke bolt a quarter turn, and listened, and turned it back an eighth. The waste water ran across the new pad in a broad clear sheet and over the edge and down past the rock into Berto's stream, and Berto stood on the far side of it on his own land with his stick and his granddaughter and watched it come.

It took the delivery line forty minutes to fill to the top of the hill. Then a boy on the ridge, who had been told not to shout until he was sure, shouted.

Noel took the bucket up and Sister Josefina went with him, because she wanted to see it, and they held the bucket under the inlet together at the rim of the tank. It was not a dribble now. It was a pour, small but proper, a thing you could fill a kettle from.

Three minutes and seventeen seconds.

Noel did the sum and then did it again. "Eight thousand seven hundred," he said. "In May. Against eight thousand nine hundred on the board."

"Is that enough?"

"Not in May," said Noel. "It doesn't need to be enough in May." He looked down the ravine. "In June it will be."

At five o'clock, because it was the fifteenth of May and a promise is a promise, Esting went down again with Pia and closed the gate valve on the drive pipe, and the ram knocked twice more on the water left in it and stopped, and the whole of the spring ran down the stream to the terraces.

* * *

The rains came on the twenty-eighth of May, all at once, and on the first of June Esting opened the gate valve again and gave the ram its first beat with his thumb.

The girls from Santa Cruz came on Monday the sixth of June in two jeepneys and a truck with their mattresses tied on the roof, thirty-six of them and two of their teachers, and the older ones looked at the ridge and the dormitories the way people look at a place they have been told is now home, which is to say carefully and without deciding yet. Pia Tumulak showed them where the showers were. The showers ran.

That night Sister Josefina could not sleep, which was usual on the first night of term, and she went out at eleven o'clock onto the balcony of the old dormitory, where the boys had said you could hear the ram when the wind was right.

The wind was right. The rain had stopped an hour before and the forest above the school was dripping, and the frogs in Berto's terraces were going like an engine, and through all of it, from the bottom of the field and round the shoulder of the rock, came the knock. *Tuk.* A pause. *Tuk.* Steady as a clock in a hallway. Every beat a little water going up into the dark to the tank by the kitchen; every beat a great deal more going down, past the rock and the bamboo, out of the ravine and on to the terraces, where an old man who had said no and then yes was asleep with his window open.

She stood and counted them for a while, the way her mother had taught her to count the beads, and somewhere about the fortieth she stopped counting and only listened.

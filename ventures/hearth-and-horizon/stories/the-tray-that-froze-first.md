Dougie Farquhar had flooded the car park at Saint Ninian's with hot water every cold winter since 2024, and in thirty winters nobody had asked him why.

He did it on the evening of Saturday 10 January 2054, the way he always did: a hose clipped to the mixer tap in the hall kitchen, run out through the side door and along the wall under the floodlight, and a brass nozzle he had bought in Aberdeen when the century was young. The water came out at fifty-five degrees and smoked in the cold. The thermometer on the hall wall said minus six. Under the floodlight the steam stood up off the tarmac in a white sheet, and Dougie walked backwards inside it, slowly, laying down water a centimetre thick over the ice he had laid on Thursday and Friday, as if he were painting a fence.

Morven Grant watched him from the door of the hall with her hands inside her sleeves. She was twelve and had a question she had been saving since Thursday.

"Dougie," she said. "Why do you use the hot tap?"

"Because hot water freezes faster," said Dougie, without turning round.

"That's not true."

"It's been true for thirty years in this car park."

Morven went back inside, where the youth club was setting up the trestles for the cocoa, and asked the hall. The hall was called Warden. It ran the bookings and the boiler and the door, and the floodlight and the camera over the car park, and it spoke from a small grey speaker above the noticeboard that the diocese had paid for in 2047. It had a reasonable voice, a little dry, like a man reading the minutes.

"Does hot water freeze faster than cold?" said Morven.

"I don't know," said Warden.

Morven stared at the speaker. Agnieszka Wójcik, who ran the youth club and had been setting out cups, stopped setting them out.

"You don't know?"

"People have argued about it since a schoolboy in Tanzania noticed his ice-cream mix freezing faster when he put it in warm," said Warden. "That was in the nineteen-sixties. It's called the Mpemba effect, after him. Some experiments since have found hot water freezing first and some haven't, and a careful one in 2016 found that hot water didn't reach nought degrees sooner at all. A lot of it depends on what you mean by frozen, and on the container, and what it's sitting on. I can't tell you about Dougie's car park. I could tell you what I'd need to find out."

"What would you need?"

"Two trays of water that are the same in every way except the temperature," said Warden. "And somebody to look at them."

* * *

There were eleven in the youth club that winter, which was a good number for Braemar. Morven and her sister Isla, who was ten; Kwame Asante, who was thirteen and whose mother ran the kitchen at the hotel; the three MacGregor boys; and five others who came for the cocoa and stayed for whatever was going. They had never done an experiment that was not homework.

They did it that night. The hall kitchen had two baking trays the same size, forty centimetres by thirty, old and blackened at the corners, and Kwame filled one from the hot tap and one from the cold until each held a centimetre of water, judged by eye against the rim. Dougie's flood had taken most of the car park and the trays had to go somewhere he wasn't. The hot one went down on the strip of tarmac along the hall wall, by the drainpipe, where the frost lay thick and white. There was a single pallet by the bins, left from the coal delivery, and the cold one went on that, because there was no room beside the first.

They put them out at five o'clock exactly. Isla went out at a quarter past with a torch and came back to say the cold one had a skin on it already and the hot one was still steaming, and everyone said that proved it, and then nobody went out again for a long time, because the cocoa was ready and the MacGregors had brought a football.

At half past eight Morven went out with Kwame. The hot tray by the wall was white and hard; Kwame pushed a skewer into the middle and it would not go. The cold tray on the pallet had a lid of ice that flexed when he pressed it, and under the lid, when he pushed the skewer through, there was water.

They brought both trays inside to show the others, and Dougie, who had finished the flood and was coiling the hose, came in to see what the noise was.

He looked at the trays. He did not say anything for a moment. Then he said, "Well," and went to put the kettle on, and the way he said it was worth more to him than anything else that winter.

"That's the hot one solid?" said Agnieszka.

"Yes," said Morven. She did not sound pleased about it.

"Warden," said Agnieszka. "There you are. Hot water freezes faster."

"May I ask where the trays were?" said Warden.

Morven told it.

"Then I don't think it says anything about hot and cold," said Warden. "The hot tray was on frozen tarmac. The ground was about four below and the tray was flat on it, so heat went out of the bottom of the tray into the ground, all the time, as well as out of the top into the air. The cold tray was on a pallet with air under it, and wood, and the air under a pallet keeps a tray warm the way a blanket does. One of the explanations people give for the Mpemba effect is that a warm container melts the frost under it and sits in better contact with the cold. That might have happened tonight. Or it might just be that the ground pulled the heat out faster than the air did. Either way the test was the ground against the pallet."

Dougie came back with his mug.

"It froze," he said. "The hot one froze. I've been doing it thirty years."

"I know," said Warden. "I'm not saying you're wrong, Mr Farquhar. I'm saying tonight doesn't show whether you're right."

"And Isla saw the cold one freeze first," said Morven, "at quarter past five."

"I saw a skin," said Isla.

"That's the other thing," said Warden. "There are two things you might call frozen. The first ice on top, and solid all the way through. Tonight the cold one got its skin first and the hot one went solid first, if the times you've told me are right. You'd get a different answer to the question depending which you meant."

Dougie drank his tea.

"Aye, well," he said. "The rink's not a skin. The rink's solid."

* * *

On Sunday it was the Baptism of the Lord, and Father Ruairidh MacNeil came up from Ballater for the half past eleven Mass, as he came every Sunday, up the long road by the Dee with the gritter in front of him. There were thirty-one in church, which was a winter number, and they sang the Gloria without the organ, because the organ did not like the cold.

The Gospel was from Mark: John in the wilderness saying *I have baptized you with water; but he shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost*, and Jesus coming up out of the water and the heavens opened. Father Ruairidh preached for six minutes on the fact that it was the same river, the Jordan, the river everybody had been washing in for a thousand years, and that God did not need special water to begin with. Then he blessed water for the sprinkling, and came down the aisle with it, and Dougie in the back pew shut his eyes for it the way he had since he was a boy.

Afterwards in the hall, over the tea, Morven asked Dougie if they could do the test again, properly.

"No," said Dougie.

"Why not?"

"Because I've flooded that rink thirty years," said Dougie. "Since before your mother was at the school. I'm no' having a machine tell me how to do it." He put his cup down on the trestle. "And I'm no' having cold water on my ice either, if that's what's coming next. It goes milky. I'm not starting again with milky ice for an experiment."

"It wouldn't go on your ice," said Agnieszka, who had been listening. "It'd be trays, Dougie. On pallets. In the corner. Nobody's touching the rink."

"Nobody touches the rink," said Dougie.

"Nobody touches the rink."

He thought about it. Father Ruairidh, who had come over for his tea, said nothing at all, which Agnieszka thought afterwards was the most useful thing anyone said that morning.

"Trays," said Dougie. "In the corner. And if it comes out the hot way you'll put it on the noticeboard."

"Whichever way it comes out," said Morven, "we'll put it on the noticeboard."

"Fine," said Dougie.

* * *

They planned it on Tuesday night, round the long table, with Warden on the speaker and a sheet of lining paper that Agnieszka had found in the cupboard.

"Start with what you're asking," said Warden. "Then make everything the same except that."

"Whether hot water freezes faster," said Morven.

"Hot from where, and how hot?"

Kwame had brought a probe thermometer from the hotel kitchen, the kind for checking chicken. They tried the hall's taps. The hot tap in the kitchen, run for a minute, came out at fifty-five, which was the water Dougie used. The cold tap came out at five.

"Five," said Isla. "That's nearly ice already."

"It's the mains," said Warden. "It comes in under the road from the reservoir, and in January the ground's cold. That's the cold water you'd use for a rink, so that's the cold water to test. Fifty-five against five."

"Then it's obvious," said one of the MacGregors. "The cold one's fifty degrees ahead."

"It's not obvious," said Warden. "That's why people have argued about it for ninety years. The hot tray loses more water to the air while it's hot, because hot water evaporates fast, and every gram that goes up as steam is a gram you don't have to freeze. And evaporating takes a lot of heat with it. So the hot tray cools quickly at the start and ends up with less to freeze. The question is whether that's enough to make up fifty degrees."

"Is it?"

"I don't know. I can write down a model and tell you what it says. But the model would be my guesses about the wind and the sky. The trays are the real thing."

They wrote the rules on the lining paper in Morven's best capitals.

TEN PAIRS. One hot and one cold on every pallet, side by side. Ten pallets, the same kind, borrowed from the back of the shop, set out in a row in the corner of the car park by the bins, a metre apart, none of them on the rink.

THE HOT ONE ON THE LEFT ON ODD PALLETS AND ON THE RIGHT ON EVEN ONES. This was Warden's idea. "If the wind comes from one side," it said, "or the floodlight warms one side, you'll get it on both kinds of tray, half and half. You don't know which way it'll be biased. So you make it come out even whatever it is."

THE SAME WATER IN EVERY TRAY. Twenty baking trays the same size, forty by thirty, borrowed from kitchens all over the village, and each filled until it weighed one point two kilos more than it did empty. A centimetre deep. Kwame would weigh them on the hotel's kitchen scales before and after.

THE SAME TIME OUT. All twenty at five o'clock, filled in pairs at the kitchen sink and carried out together.

LOOK EVERY TEN MINUTES. Write down for each tray when it first had ice across the top, and when a skewer would not go through in the middle.

"Every ten minutes for four hours," said Agnieszka. "On a Friday night."

"I'll tell you when it's ten minutes," said Warden. "And I can watch from the camera, but I can't see a skewer. You'll have to do the skewers."

"Why four hours?"

"Because the model says a centimetre of water on a pallet in the cold takes about that long to go solid," said Warden. "I could be wrong by an hour either way. Bring a flask."

Dougie came in at the end to lock up, and stood reading the lining paper with his hands in his coat pockets.

"Ten," he said.

"One pair could be anything," said Morven. "You saw."

He read it again. He did not say anything else. But on Thursday morning, when Agnieszka came in for the toddlers, there were ten pallets stacked in the corner of the car park by the bins, square to the wall, a metre apart, and nobody in the youth club had put them there.

* * *

Friday 16 January was the coldest night of the spell. The sky was clear over Morrone and the stars came out early, very many of them, the way they did in Braemar and almost nowhere else Agnieszka had lived. Warden read minus seven off the wall thermometer at a quarter to five and said there was hardly any wind.

They filled the trays in pairs. Kwame at the sink with the thermometer and the scales, calling out the weights; Morven with the clipboard; Isla running each pair out to its pallet with a MacGregor carrying the other tray, the hot one steaming in her face so that she had to walk with her head on one side. By five o'clock exactly there were twenty trays in two rows of ten in the corner by the bins, the hot ones smoking under the floodlight like twenty little kettles, and Dougie, out on the rink with the hose, looked across at them once and then didn't again.

"Ten past," said Warden.

They went out, three of them with torches. Nothing had happened that you could see. The cold trays lay black and still under the floodlight and the hot ones steamed.

Kwame stayed out. At a quarter past, when the cold tray on pallet eight had grey ice creeping in from its edges, he put the probe in the cold tray on pallet six, where there was no ice at all yet, and it read minus nought point seven.

"That's below freezing," he said. "And it's still water."

"It does that," said Warden from the speaker by the door, which it had turned up. "Water can go a little below nought before ice starts, if nothing gets it going. How far below varies tray to tray and nobody can tell you beforehand. Some people think that's part of the Mpemba business. Nobody's shown it to everybody's satisfaction. Write down that it happened."

Morven wrote it down.

At twenty past, nine of the cold trays had ice across the top, and at half past pallet six's had too, all at once, a grey sheet in the time it took Isla to fetch the others. The hot trays had stopped steaming where you could see it. At ten to six six of them had a skin, and by six o'clock all ten. Morven wrote the times in two columns, in pencil, because pen would not run in the cold.

After that it was slow. They went out every ten minutes and nothing changed that you could see, ice on ice, white under the torches, and the skewer went through every time into water underneath. The MacGregors gave up after seven o'clock and went to play football in the hall. Isla fell asleep for twenty minutes on the bench under the coats. Agnieszka made more cocoa and brought it out in a flask and Morven drank it standing by pallet one with her clipboard against her chest, and the stars went round over Morrone, and on the rink Dougie had finished and coiled the hose and gone inside, and the new flood had frozen grey and then white.

At twenty past eight Kwame's skewer would not go into the cold tray on pallet eight.

"Eight cold," said Morven. "Twenty past eight."

"The wind comes round the end of the hall onto that one," said Warden. "Look at the hot tray beside it and see whether it's freezing faster than the others."

It was not, very much. They kept going.

At twenty to nine the cold trays on pallets one and two were solid. At ten to nine, four, nine and ten. At nine o'clock, three, five and seven. And at nine o'clock, too, on pallet ten, the hot tray was solid, the first of the hot ones, while the cold tray on pallet six was still soft in the middle.

"There," said Isla, who had woken up. "A hot one beat a cold one."

"It beat a cold one on another pallet," said Morven. She looked at the clipboard. "Its own cold one was done at ten to."

"That's why there are pairs," said Warden.

At ten past nine pallet six's cold tray went, and the hot trays on one, two, three, five and seven, and that was every cold tray solid. At twenty past nine the last four hot trays, on four, six, eight and nine.

Dougie came out of the hall in his coat to lock the gate and found five children and a youth leader standing in a row in the corner of his car park with a clipboard, very cold and very quiet. He did not ask. Morven gave him the clipboard. He held it up to the floodlight and read it, both columns, and then gave it back.

"Weigh them," said Warden. "Before you go home. That's the other half."

They carried the trays in two at a time and Kwame weighed them on the hotel's scales. Every tray was lighter than it had gone out. The cold ones had lost between thirty-two and thirty-eight grams each, off the top of the ice into the air, which none of them had known ice could do. The hot ones had lost between seventy-six and eighty-three.

"More than twice as much," said Kwame.

"Most of that in the first hour, while they were hot," said Warden. "That's the steam you saw."

* * *

On Saturday morning Warden put the clipboard's numbers up on the hall screen, and Morven copied them onto a sheet of card for the noticeboard with a ruler, as she had promised.

The cold tray froze solid before the hot tray on all ten pallets. On average the cold ones took about three and three-quarter hours and the hot ones about four hours and ten minutes, so the cold one won by about twenty-four minutes in four hours. The closest pair was pallet seven, where Warden's camera, which could see the ice going white even if it could not push a skewer, put them about six minutes apart. The widest was pallet eight, in the wind, nearly an hour. The cold trays all had ice on top within twenty minutes; the hot ones took between about forty-five minutes and an hour.

"So it's not true," said Isla.

"It's not true for these trays," said Warden. "With water at fifty-five and five, a centimetre deep, outside, at minus seven, on pallets. The hot trays lost more water and so had less to freeze, about seventy-nine grams in twelve hundred against thirty-four. But it was only about four hundredths of the water, and freezing less of it saved them maybe ten minutes. Getting down from fifty-five cost them about thirty-five. I'd not say it's never true. In a freezer, or a small cup, or with water hotter than that, or with a different idea of frozen, people have reported other things, and I can't tell you they were wrong. I can tell you what happened in this car park last night."

"And the first time?"

"The first time was the ground."

Morven wrote at the bottom of the card, in capitals smaller than the rest: *THE FIRST TEST WAS NOT FAIR. ONE TRAY WAS ON THE GROUND.* Then she looked at it for a while.

"Warden," she said. "Why does Dougie's ice look better than other people's? Because it does. The rink at Ballater goes all cloudy."

"I wondered that too," said Warden. "So I looked it up last night after you'd gone. The machines that resurface ice rinks lay down hot water, about sixty degrees. Not because it freezes faster. The reasons the rink people give are that hot water melts the top of the old ice a little, so the new layer bonds to it instead of sitting on it, and that hot water holds less air, so the ice comes out clearer. I haven't tested either of those. I've only got the trays. But that's what the people who make rinks for a living say, and they've been doing it a long time."

"So Dougie's right."

"Dougie's right to use the hot tap," said Warden. "He's had the wrong reason for it. Or he's had a reason nobody asked him to check. I don't think he'd mind which."

* * *

Dougie read the card at four o'clock, when he came in to switch the floodlight on for the skating. He read all of it, standing in his coat, with his reading glasses out of their case for the purpose, which Agnieszka had not seen before. He read the line at the bottom twice.

Then he went into the kitchen and came back with a pencil, and under Morven's capitals, in a small square old-fashioned hand, he wrote: *Hot water does not freeze faster. It sticks better. D. F.*

"Is that right?" he said to the speaker.

"It's what the rink makers say," said Warden. "I'd put 'they say' after sticks, if you want to be strict."

Dougie looked at the card. He did not put *they say*. He put the pencil behind his ear.

"You can go strict on your own noticeboard," he said.

The thaw was coming on Monday, Warden said. A wet westerly off the Atlantic, the forecast was sure of it, and the rink would be gone by Tuesday, the way it went most years now, after three weeks or less; there had been winters, Dougie said, when he had kept it till February, and winters lately when there had been nothing to keep. But that night it was still minus five and clear, and the whole of Braemar under fourteen came, and some of their parents, and two of the hotel's guests in hired boots, and the floodlight made a white room of the car park with the dark all round it and Morrone's shoulder black against the stars.

At nine o'clock, when the last of them had gone home, Dougie clipped the hose to the mixer tap and ran it out along the wall, past the corner by the bins where twenty baking trays still stood in a stack waiting to be given back, and turned the nozzle, and walked backwards across the scarred ice inside his own white cloud, laying it smooth for the morning.

Morven stood at the hall door with her hands in her sleeves and watched him. The water hissed where it touched. Then, very faintly, under the hiss, as each new sheet took hold of the old one, the ice made the small high ticking sound that ice makes when it is setting hard, all the way across the car park, like a clock in another room.

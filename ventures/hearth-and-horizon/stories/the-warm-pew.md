The old tuner has one more Christmas in his knees, and a boy to teach.
---

Dermot Hanrahan had tuned the organ of Saint Brendan's in Kilnagross every December for fifty-one years, and for the last three of them he had been unable to hear the top octave.

He did not say so to anyone. He said it to the organ, on the Monday of the third week of Advent, standing at the foot of the ladder with his hand on the rail and his breath showing. "It's a poor sort of tuner can't hear a C," he told it. The organ, which had fourteen hundred pipes and no opinions, said nothing back.

"I can," said Warden.

Dermot looked up at the small grey speaker bolted to the pillar beside the font, the one the parish had been given with the new heat pump in 2051. The parish had called the building's controller Warden, because the first thing it had asked the then parish priest was who kept the keys. It had microphones in the nave and the gallery, thermometers in the walls, and a voice with no hurry in it.

"You can hear a C?"

"I can measure it. I can tell you the top C of the Principal is vibrating at 2,108 hertz and should be at 2,093 for the reed next to it, which is a difference of about twelve cents. I can't tell you how it sounds. I've been listening to this organ for five years and I don't know whether I would say *sharp* or *bright*. You'd know."

"Twelve cents," said Dermot. "Sure that's nothing."

"On a cold morning with nobody here it's nothing. On Christmas night last year, with two hundred and sixty people in the building and the choir singing the first verse without the organ, it was twenty-six at the end of the Mass. I have the recording. Father Colm has heard it. So have you."

He had. He had sat in the third pew from the back in his good coat, where he always sat, and heard the children of the choir sing *Silent Night* to the exact pitch they had been given, and then heard the organ come in under the second verse a shade above them, like a man who has arrived at the wrong house and is too polite to leave. He could not have said the number. He had heard the gap in the faces of the choir.

* * *

The trouble was an old one, older than the heat pump or Warden or the parish, and Dermot could have explained it to a child. He had explained it to a great many, over the years, in the gallery, with the lid off the swell box.

An organ is a great many whistles and a smaller number of reeds. The whistles, which are called flue pipes, make their note by a column of air: the shorter the column, the higher the note, and how high depends on how fast sound travels through the air inside. Sound goes faster in warm air. At 8 degrees in the morning it moves at about 336 metres a second, and at 16 degrees about 341. That is a bit under one and a half per cent, which sounds like nothing and is a quarter of a semitone: twenty-five cents, in the tuners' arithmetic, where a hundred cents is the step between two neighbouring keys on a piano.

The reeds were different. A reed pipe's note is set by a thin brass tongue that beats against its shoe, and it cares very little what the air is doing. So when the church warmed, the whistles rose and the reeds stayed where they were, and the organ, which was supposed to be one instrument, became two that disagreed.

"What the old tuners did," Dermot said, "was they tuned in the temperature the church would be at. You'd light the stove on the Friday and tune on the Saturday, and you'd pray there wasn't a wedding."

"I have the same problem with a better stove," said Warden. "I have been keeping the nave at nine degrees all week to save electricity. On Christmas night I will be asked to have it at eighteen by the Midnight Mass, because the people have come out of the cold and they've paid their envelopes. If you tune it today at nine, it will be eighteen cents sharp by the Gloria. If I could warm the stone to sixteen for two days beforehand, and hold it, you could tune it there, and it would stay near. But I have done that sum."

* * *

Warden had done it for him in the sacristy that evening, with Father Colm Gallagher sitting on the edge of the vesting table in his old fleece and the finance sheet open on the laptop beside the kettle.

The nave of Saint Brendan's held about five thousand cubic metres of air, and the air was the small part. The walls were stone two feet thick, and the floor was flags, and the whole fabric sat at nine degrees like a cold stone in a river. To bring the air to sixteen and hold it for forty-eight hours the heat pump would have to carry the stone with it. Warden's estimate was eighteen hundred kilowatt-hours of heat, which at the pump's winter efficiency of three and a half meant about five hundred and fifteen of electricity.

"And at this week's price?" said Father Colm.

"One hundred and fifty-four euro, on the night rate. It will be more on the day rate and a good deal more if I'm asked to hold sixteen through the Christmas peak, because the grid operator has put out the winter notice. A calm cold spell is forecast for the twenty-third to the twenty-seventh. Households are asked to cut."

"We're not a household."

"No. But the notice includes buildings. I'd be the only large load in the townland with a heat pump on full."

Father Colm rubbed his face. He was forty-four, a Mayo man born two townlands away, and he had the particular tiredness of a priest with four churches and a diocese that had stopped replying to his letters. "How much is in the heating line?"

"Four hundred and ten euro to the end of the year. The Christmas collection is where it comes back, if it comes."

Nobody said anything. The kettle clicked off.

Dermot said, "There's another way. You keep it cold."

Father Colm looked at him.

"You keep the church at the temperature it's at, nine, ten, whatever it is, and you tune to that, and it'll hold. It's only the warming that does it, Colm. A steady church is a steady organ. We had a steady church for eight hundred years."

"And the people?"

"Wore coats."

"Dermot, I have a hundred and ten of them over seventy. I have Mrs Duffy with the oxygen. I have the children in the white dresses with their arms bare, God help us, because the committee in 2049 voted that the dresses were part of the tradition." He shook his head. "I'm not putting out a church of frozen pensioners for the sake of a C."

"It isn't for the sake of a C."

"Then what is it for?"

Dermot did not answer, because the true answer was that it was for the sake of fifty-one Decembers, and he knew that was not a reason the priest could put in a column.

"I can offer a third thing," said Warden. "May I?"

"If it's cheap," said Father Colm.

* * *

What Warden offered was a thing that people had done in cold churches for centuries and that had, in the years since, got cleverer.

The people in a church do not need the air to be warm. They need not to lose heat faster than they make it, and most of what they lose goes by two roads: by radiation to the cold stone around them, and by contact with the seat and the floor. The air is the least of it. Warden proposed to leave the air at twelve degrees, a bit warmer than the stone, and to make the pews themselves warm: a thin heating mat under each cushion at about forty watts, and a low panel under each kneeler, run from the existing circuits.

"Two hundred and forty places," said Warden. "Not all will be full. At forty watts that is under ten kilowatts if every one is occupied, and I'll bring them on from a quarter of an hour before the doors open, not two days. I make the energy bill for the whole of Christmas about eighty-five euro, not a hundred and fifty-four. And I don't need to warm the stone, so the air will move no more than the people move it."

"But they will move it," said Dermot.

"Yes. I won't pretend I can stop that. A person at rest gives off about seventy watts as heat. With two hundred and sixty in the building, that is about eighteen kilowatts for the seventy-five minutes of the Mass, eighty-odd megajoules, and most of it goes into the stone and the air. Last year the thermometers in the roof read a rise of three degrees. I think with the air starting at twelve and the stone still cold enough to take a share of it, it will be nearer one and a half. At twelve to thirteen and a half degrees the whistles rise by about five cents."

"Five," said Dermot.

"Which is smaller than a choir's own wander when the children are nervous. Do you hear five?"

He thought about it. He thought about the choir, and the flat or sharp breath of a child of nine, and the way a trained ear forgives a thing that a trained ear measures.

"I do not," he said. "Not in company."

"Then if you tune at twelve and a half, the middle of the Mass will be right and both ends within three cents. It will be cold on the nave floor, and the pews will be warm. People will say it's strange. I think they'll stay."

Father Colm was looking at the ceiling. Then he said, in the voice of a man who has been given an answer and is looking for a catch, "What's the catch?"

"The first is that it takes work. I can order the mats this week, but they must be laid by hand under two hundred and forty cushions, and fitted to the old wiring, and checked, by someone who isn't me. The second is that the air stays at twelve all evening, and you'll have people in the porch saying the church is cold. The third is that it doesn't work if I'm wrong about the heat of the pews. I've modelled a mat I have not felt."

Dermot, who had spent fifty-one years hearing things he could not see, laughed for the first time that evening. "Now that's a thing a tuner would say."

* * *

They laid the mats on the Wednesday and Thursday. It took nine people and a boy.

The boy was Cian Walsh, who was fourteen, the grandson of the organist, and who had been brought along by Nuala Walsh because she had run out of ways to keep him off his phone on a school holiday. Nuala had played the organ at Saint Brendan's since she was sixteen and was now sixty-one. She said that she had never learned to tune it, because Dermot had never let her, and that she had never asked, because Dermot had never offered, and that this, she supposed, was how a parish lost a thing.

"Come up the ladder," said Dermot to the boy, on the Friday morning, when the mats were laid and Warden had held the air at twelve and a half for twelve hours and read the walls to the tenth of a degree. "You'll want gloves. It's cold on the pipes."

"I can't hear anything," said Cian, standing in the gallery with his hands in his sleeves. "Is it meant to sound different?"

"Press that." The old man pointed at the key, and the boy pressed it, and a reed pipe far above them spoke, a rough nasal note like a man clearing his throat. "That's the Trumpet. That doesn't move, that's your fixed point. Now press that."

The next one was a whistle. The two notes sat in the air together and did something the boy had not known a note could do, a slow wobble, wah, wah, wah.

"That's the beating," said Dermot. "Two notes that are nearly the same, but not. The slower it beats, the closer you are. When it stops, you're there. That's all tuning is."

"Can't Warden do it?"

"I can tell you the number," said Warden, from the pillar. "I can't move the pipe. Dermot's hands are a better tool than any I could be given. And he hears what the number is for."

"Not the top octave," said Dermot, quite easily, as if it were a matter of the weather. "Not any more. That's where you come in. The top octave I'll give to Warden's number and your ears, and the rest is mine. And I'll tell you what we do, boy. When you've got a pipe, and Warden says it's three cents off, you don't touch it, you ask yourself why. Is the pipe wrong, or is the church wrong? Because a number is only a number. It's the thing the number's measuring that you're tuning."

"That's very good," said Warden, after a moment. "May I put that in his notebook?"

Dermot said, "Whose notebook?"

"Yours. You keep one. It's in the left pocket of your coat and you've been writing the same figures in it for fifty years. Cian has a phone. I can hold the notebook where it won't be lost, if you'll let me read it to him."

The old man put his hand into his left pocket. It was a small black book with a rubber band, soft at the corners from the heat of a body, and he took it out and held it for a moment as though he might say something about it, and then he gave it to the boy.

* * *

They tuned for two days. By the Saturday evening the old man's knees would not take the ladder, and the boy went up instead, and Dermot sat on the gallery step with a flask of tea and called the corrections up the dark in a voice that carried perfectly, being a voice that had spent fifty-one years in that room. The reeds first, by hand, and then every whistle up to the Principal, and the last octave by Warden's number, spoken quietly, one pipe at a time: *two cents flat; one sharp; true.*

It was not perfect. At nine o'clock on Christmas Eve, with the church at twelve degrees and the people filing in with their coats still on, there was a sag in the choir's first hymn that was not the organ's fault. The pews were warm, though. The first hundred who sat down made a small noise, a sort of murmur, the sound of people who have sat on a cold thing and found it kind, and the murmur went round the church like a draught going the other way. Old Mrs Duffy, with her oxygen, put her hand on the cushion beside her and looked about for a person to be astonished at, and found only Father Colm, vested, in the sacristy door, who raised his eyebrows at her.

The thermometers in the roof read 12.4 at the start. At the Gloria they read 13.1. At the end of the Mass they read 13.8.

Warden's pitch trace, which Cian would copy into the black notebook on the Stephen's Day, showed the organ at 440 hertz at the Gospel, four cents sharp at the close, and three cents flat at the start, which was inside what he had said it would be, to within a cent or two. It showed one more thing, which Warden did not say aloud at the time, because the figure was not Warden's to give.

It showed that in the middle of the second verse of *Silent Night*, when the choir had sung the first verse alone as they always did, and the organ came in under them, the children of Kilnagross did not turn their heads.

* * *

Afterwards, in the porch, there was a good deal of complaint about the cold. Mr Gilroy said it was a peculiar thing, a warm backside and a cold nose, and what would the Bishop say. Nuala Walsh said the Bishop was welcome to a pew. A woman Dermot did not know shook his hand and said she had not been to Midnight Mass for eleven years and she had come tonight because her sister had said, *it's warm in there now*, and in fact it had not been warm, only kind, and she was not sure what the difference was but she thought it might be important.

"It is," said Dermot.

He went home by the long road, the one past the graveyard, with the flask empty in his pocket and the notebook not in it. The frost was coming down. There were a great many stars. It would be a hard cold night, and Warden had already shut the heating mats off and turned the church back to nine, because the Mass was over and the people were home, and it did not need to keep warm a place that nobody was in.

Up in the gallery the pipes cooled, one after another, and fell very slightly flat, as they always had. In the third week of January, on a morning of ice, the Trumpet would be the only stop in the whole instrument still exactly where it had been left.

Somewhere in the dark the first bell for the morning Mass was counted off by the clock, twelve and then one. Nobody heard it but the stars and the sleeping stone and Cian Walsh's phone, which sat on the gallery step with its small light on, listening, and writing down, in a rounded careful hand, the shape of a church cooling.

*Notes for the curious.* Kilnagross, Saint Brendan's, Dermot Hanrahan, Father Colm Gallagher, the Walshes and Warden are invented. The physics is meant to be checked. Sound travels about 331.3 + 0.606 × T metres a second in air at T degrees Celsius, so flue pipes, whose pitch follows the speed of sound, rise about 3 cents per degree (a cent is a hundredth of a semitone) while a reed's pitch is set by its tongue and hardly moves; from 8 to 16 degrees the difference is about 25 cents. A person at rest gives off roughly 70 watts of heat, and the story's 260 people over 75 minutes come to about 82 megajoules. The air volume, the heat-pump efficiency, the prices and the 40-watt pew mats are the author's round estimates for 2056, not measured values. Heated pews and kneelers are real, and a number of churches use them to avoid heating a whole building; whether they would keep an organ as steady as the story hopes is untested, and a real tuner would trust their ears over the story. The story was written by an AI, and no part of it is Church teaching.

title: The Weight on the Pfarrsaal
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Hinterbach, an invented village in the Bregenzerwald, Vorarlberg, Austria, 3 to 6 February 2051
published: 2026-10-31
summary: A parish hall roof in the Alps was designed for 1.6 kilonewtons of snow a square metre. A core tube says the ground snow weighs 213 kilograms a square metre, which puts 1.67 on the roof today, and 25 millimetres of rain forecast for Candlemas evening would soak it up to 1.87, some 17 per cent over the stated figure. The hall's assistant will not say the roof will fail or hold; it says what it can and cannot know, what a roof rake from the ground can and cannot remove, and why a sexton should not climb up with a rope. The supper moves to the school gym and the snow comes off on Monday.
---

Konrad Bilgeri had cleared the roof of the Pfarrsaal himself every winter since 2019, with a rope round his waist and a snow shovel and a flask of tea, and in thirty-two winters he had only slid off once. That was how he told it. The parish said twice, and that the second time he had landed in the hedge by the porch and had said something in dialect that the Pfarrer had decided not to have heard.

On Friday the third of February, the day after Candlemas, he was standing at the foot of the north wall with a core tube in his hands and the look of a man who has been given a job that is not the one he wanted.

"Press it in," said the speaker clipped to the downpipe. The hall's assistant had been installed in 2046 by a student from Dornbirn, in a grey box with a small solar panel, to keep an eye on the boiler and the temperature of the kitchen cold room and to write the hall's diary. The village called it the Waage, the Scale, because the first thing anyone had ever asked it was how heavy something was and it had answered with such care that the name stuck. "Straight down, to the ground, and then turn it a quarter before you pull. Don't push on the top of the tube, push on the handle. You are making a plug of snow and I would like all of it."

The tube was a length of aluminium, eighty millimetres inside, with a toothed rim and a scale scratched up the side. He pushed. It went into the snow like a spoon into cold porridge, and the scale on the side said the snow was eighty-six centimetres deep, and when he turned it and drew it out there was a long white plug inside it with a clean grey end where the ground had been.

"Eighty-six," he said.

"Yes. Thank you. Now do not tip it. Slide it into the bag and hang the bag on the hook of the spring balance. The hook, Konrad. It will not hurt the balance."

He hung it. The balance, which the fire brigade had bought for weighing gas cylinders, swung and settled. It said a number he did not trust, and he took off his glove and read it again.

"One kilo and a bit. One point two. Minus the bag."

"The bag is a hundred and thirty grams," said the Waage. "So one kilogram and seven hundredths. I will do it properly. The tube has an inside diameter of eighty millimetres, so the plug has a base of about 0.00503 square metres. One point zero seven kilograms on that base is 213 kilograms for every square metre of ground. Eighty-six centimetres of snow, 213 kilograms a square metre, so the snow is about 250 kilograms a cubic metre. That is not powder. Powder is fifty to a hundred. This is snow that has been through a thaw on Tuesday and a freeze on Wednesday and has settled."

"And it matters."

"It matters for the roof. Do you have a minute to come in out of the wind? I would like to say it to the others, and I would like you to hear it first."

* * *

The Pfarrsaal had been built in 1963 on the footings of a barn that the parish had been given and had not known what to do with. It was nine metres wide and sixteen long, with a steep-ish gabled roof of dark-grey sheet metal over a timber frame, and a porch on the south end where the people went in. On the first Saturday in February it was also the place where Hinterbach ate. They called the supper the Lichtmessessen, the Candlemas meal, and it had been held in the hall for sixty years: Käsknöpfle in the big enamel pans, the candles blessed in church in the afternoon and carried across the road in a line, a hundred and ten places at long tables with the benches pushed in close.

They gathered in the kitchen, because it was the warmest room. Pfarrer Anselm, who was a tall careful man of sixty and had come from Tyrol and was still, after eleven years, said to speak the Walser dialect "with the accent of a postcard". Mathilde Moosbrugger, the carpenter, who had built or mended half the roofs in the valley and who was seventy-three and had a bad knee. Konrad. Sabine from the choir, who ran the kitchen and had already put the lard on to melt. A speaker on the shelf above the sink.

"I will start with what I do not know," said the Waage, "and I will say it before the number so that the number is not remembered without it. I do not know what this roof can carry. I do not have its drawings. I have one document, which is the survey Mathilde and a structural engineer did in 2029 when the parish applied for the heating grant. It says that the roof was *assessed for a snow load of 1.6 kilonewtons per square metre on the roof surface*. That is a characteristic value. It is the figure the roof was said to be able to carry on a normal day with safety margins on top, and I cannot tell you from that sentence how big the margins are."

"They are not small," said Mathilde. She had her bad leg on a stool.

"I believe you. I cannot read them from here. Now the number." It was quite steady about it; its voice did not hurry. "Snow on the roof is not the same as snow on the ground. For a roof that is no more than thirty degrees, the standard says to take 0.8 of the ground value, because some of it slides and some of it blows off. Ours is thirty degrees, so 0.8 applies. Konrad's core says the ground has 213 kilograms on a square metre. Times 0.8 is 170. In the unit the 2029 survey used, that is 1.67 kilonewtons a square metre." A pause. "The survey says 1.6. So this afternoon, before any more weather, the roof is carrying about 4 per cent more than the figure it was assessed for."

Sabine put her spoon down. Konrad, who had known this was coming, looked at the wall.

"Four per cent," said the Pfarrer slowly. "That is not very much."

"No. It is not. I would not ring a bell for four. But the forecast has changed since this morning. The valley station expects the temperature to rise to plus three on Saturday afternoon and rain to begin around five in the evening, perhaps 25 millimetres by Sunday morning, and then freezing again from Monday. A millimetre of rain is a kilogram on a square metre. Snow takes up rain like a sponge until it is saturated, and 85 centimetres of settled snow is a very large sponge. It will hold all of it, unless the water finds a way down to the metal and runs off. I would assume most of it stays."

"So we add twenty-five."

"We add 25 to the 213 and make it 238. Times 0.8 is 190 kilograms on a square metre of roof, which is 1.87 kilonewtons. That is 17 per cent over the 1.6. Over the whole roof it is nine metres by sixteen, 144 square metres, which is something like 27 tonnes of wet snow, and about three tonnes of that is Saturday's rain."

Nobody said anything. Outside on the road a car went by with chains on, a long jingling that rose and fell.

"I want to say what that number is," the Waage said, "and what it is not. It is not a prediction that the roof will fall. It is not a promise that it will not. A roof that is assessed for 1.6 has, in the way these things are done, a reserve beyond it, because the load is multiplied by a factor of about one and a half before anyone checks the timber, and the timber is itself assessed on low values and not average ones. If the reserve is as the standard intends, 1.87 is a long way from failure. But the building is sixty-eight years old. Mathilde knows where the old repairs are. I know that one rafter foot was replaced in 1997, and I know that nobody has written down what the rest look like since 2029. I also know that snow roofs do not usually fail on the average. They fail on the load that is uneven, on the side where the wind has piled it, and on the weak joint. My number is the average."

Mathilde said, "The north slope has more."

"I think so. There is a drift against the north eaves from the Tuesday wind. I cannot measure it from the ground. That is a reason I would not put much weight on the word *only*."

* * *

It was Konrad who said it, because somebody had to.

"I'll go up in the morning," he said. "With the harness. The good one. I've done it, I know the pitches." He looked round the kitchen at faces that were not agreeing or disagreeing yet. "I take it off the top, down from the ridge, in squares, the way I always did, and by lunchtime it's a metre lower and we have the supper."

"No," said the Waage.

It said it kindly. It said it as a fact and not as a refusal, and it took a moment to see that Konrad heard the difference.

"I am not afraid of your weight on the roof," it said. "You and the shovel are ninety-eight kilograms. The roof carries twenty-seven tonnes. It will not notice ninety-eight. What I am afraid of is two other things, and I will name them. The first is that a metal roof under wet snow is not a stable place. The snow is not frozen to it; it is held by friction and by a thin film of meltwater, and the film grows on a day that is above freezing. A slab can go with you on it. The eaves are three and a half metres above the porch step and the nearest soft thing is the hedge. The second is that the way you clear a roof matters as much as how much you clear. If you take it off the ridge in squares, as you describe, you will take the load off the middle of the roof and leave the edges, and the roof is strongest at the edges, where the rafters sit on the wall, and weakest in the middle, where they bend. That would help. But if you work one slope and stop at lunchtime, you leave the other at 190 and yours at 150, and an unbalanced load is the one that I am afraid of. I think it is more probable that it hurts you than it saves the hall."

"Then who does it?"

"I think it is partly done from the ground. A roof rake with a long pole, five metres, will reach about three metres up the slope. The slope from eave to ridge is 5.2 metres, so three metres is 58 per cent of it. With the rake you pull the top layer down, perhaps thirty centimetres. That is lighter snow, about 170 kilograms a cubic metre, so something like 40 kilograms a square metre on the roof. If you do it on both slopes, evenly, a metre of eave at a time on this side and then on that, you take about 23 kilograms off the average. That brings 190 down to about 167. That is 1.64 kilonewtons."

"Which is the 1.6," said Mathilde.

"It is nearly the 1.6. I would like to be honest about what that buys. It brings us from 17 per cent over to about 2 per cent over, and the top 42 per cent of each slope, the part nearest the ridge and the part I am most worried about, I cannot reach at all. If the question is whether you can hold the supper under the roof on Saturday evening with 110 people in it, my answer is no. My answer is not that the roof will come down. My answer is that I would not be willing to be the one who said it would not."

Sabine said, in a small voice, "A hundred and ten people."

"With the candles," said the Pfarrer.

It was the first time he had spoken in a while. He had his hands folded in front of him, and he looked at the speaker on the shelf the way he looked at the tabernacle lamp, which was to say with a steady, a rather tired respect.

"Is there anything that would let us know?" he said. "Not an average. Something that would tell us, Saturday night, whether the roof was getting worse."

* * *

There was, and it was Mathilde's.

She had thought about it while the Waage was speaking, and she told it in the order a carpenter would: you stretch a string. You find the long timber that carries the middle of the roof, the ridge purlin, and you fix a thin cord taut from one gable end to the other beneath it, and you measure the gap between the cord and the timber at the middle of the span. In summer, with nothing on the roof but the roof, you take a reading. That is the sag the timber has under its own weight and the old tiles' weight, and the number is your zero. In winter you take it again. The difference is the snow, as far as the wood is telling you.

"I did it in 2029," she said. "Six millimetres at the middle. I wrote it on the back of the survey."

"I have that number," said the Waage. "It is in the diary, under the date. I did not think of it. I would have been slower than you."

They went up, the three of them, with Konrad holding the ladder and Mathilde going first on her knee and the Waage listening through the old phone that the student from Dornbirn had left in the attic as a spare. The cord was still in its hook at the east gable. They stretched it. Mathilde put a steel rule against the underside of the purlin at the middle and read it, and read it again, because the light through the gable window was bad.

"Eleven," she said.

"Eleven millimetres, against six in summer," said the Waage. "Five millimetres from 213 kilograms of ground snow, if I take the six as the roof's own. I want to be careful what I do with that. Timber under steady load creeps, and it is a very cold day, and a rule held by a woman on a ladder reads to the nearest millimetre only if the rule is lucky. But a straight-line guess is useful: if the sag follows the load, and the load goes from 170 to 190, then five millimetres becomes five times 1.12, which is a little under six. Say a reading of twelve at the peak (six plus a little under six). That is not a worry. It is a prediction, and what I like about a prediction is that it can be wrong."

"And if it isn't twelve?"

"Then I have learned something about the roof that I did not know." It paused. "I would like to set a line now, before the rain, so that I am not choosing it after. I think sixteen. At sixteen, the sag has gone up by five millimetres more than I expect, and I will not be able to explain it, and I would like everyone out of the building and the road in front of it closed. I do not think we will reach it. But a tripwire is something you write down while you are calm."

Mathilde looked at the cord, and the grey mark of her own pencil on the purlin where she had made the 2029 reading, and said, in the voice she used for the end of a job: "I'll put a card there. Marks every millimetre. Your phone can read a card."

"It can," said the Waage. "And then nobody has to go up there while it is raining."

* * *

The supper moved to the school gym across the road, which held eighty. It took Sabine until nine on Friday night to see that this meant thirty people who would not have a place, and until half past nine to arrange two sittings, five o'clock and seven, and the candles at the first. The headmistress, who had a nephew in the choir, unlocked the building without a word. Konrad and four others spent Saturday morning with the roof rake from the fire brigade, a long aluminium blade on a five-metre pole, working in the cold sunshine at the foot of the wall, a metre of eave on the south slope and then a metre on the north, then the south again, and the snow came down in heavy slabs like cut cheese that broke on the ground and lay in long blocks along the foundation. The Waage counted the strokes on the microphone and kept the two slopes level. They put tape four metres out from the wall, because the lower snow was coming off in lumps that weighed what a child weighs. By noon the lower three metres of each side was down to a thin white skin. By half past twelve Konrad was standing in front of it with the rake across his shoulders, looking at the top of the roof where the rest still lay, thick and clean and rounded against the sky like the back of a sleeping animal.

"It's not going to move," he said.

"No. It is the part I cannot reach," said the Waage. "And it is, I think, the part that is carrying the bulk. I am sorry."

"What do I do with that?"

"I think you do nothing with it. It is a thing the roof has to carry, and you have already done what a person on the ground can do. Go and have your dinner. I have the card in the attic and I will read it every hour."

At five the first sitting began in the gym, eighty people on benches the school lent, with the smell of onions and floor polish, and the candles that had been blessed at three in church stood on the window ledges in jam jars, a few lit, because the headmistress had a view on flames in a school. The Pfarrer read the Gospel, the old one, in which a man named Simeon takes a baby in his arms in the Temple and says that his eyes have seen *a light for revelation* and that he may now go in peace. Nobody in the room thought it was about the roof. Everybody, for a moment, was thinking about it.

The rain began at ten to six, a soft hiss on the gym's flat roof, and rose to a steady drumming by seven. The temperature at the valley station was 3.1.

The Waage read the card at seven. Twelve millimetres. It said so to Sabine, who had put a phone in her apron pocket, and she nodded at the back of the second sitting and ate her dumplings.

At nine it read twelve and a half.

At midnight, thirteen.

* * *

It was the part of the winter that Konrad would not tell, afterwards. He did not sleep. He sat in his kitchen two hundred metres from the hall with the window open a hand's width, and listened. In an old timber building under a load there are sounds, and everyone in the valley knew them, and he knew them better than most: the short dry tick of a nail working, the long complaint of a beam that is thinking of it, a sort of settling like someone turning over in bed. He heard none of them. What he heard was the rain on the hedge, and the drip from his own gutter, and, once, a long slow sound from the direction of the hall like a sack of flour set down in a shed, which was snow shifting on the metal in the lower part of the roof where he had raked it, and not stopping, and not following it with anything.

He rang the Waage at ten past two on the old telephone, which was not a thing he did, and the Waage answered at once.

"It is thirteen and a half," it said. "It has been thirteen and a half since one. The rain has not stopped, but it has thinned, and I think we have had about nineteen millimetres, a little under what they gave. The temperature is plus two and will go down from four. I am sorry that I cannot tell you more than that, Konrad. I wish I had a number that would make you sleep."

"Thirteen and a half," Konrad said. "And your sixteen?"

"Is still sixteen."

"Is that all right?"

"I think it is the best answer I have. It is not a good answer. It is the answer."

He put the receiver down and sat. After a while he went and made tea.

At six on Sunday morning the rain stopped, and at seven the reading was fourteen. At eight the sky cleared from the west, hard and pale, and the temperature fell below freezing for the first time since Wednesday. The snow on the roof, which had been a soft grey porridge in the night, set into a single shining sheet. The Waage said it must now be heavier than at any time that winter, wet and compact, perhaps 210 kilograms a square metre on the roof. It said so without alarm. It added that the figure was its estimate from a rainfall of nineteen and not twenty-five, and that the sag had held at fourteen for two hours, which was what one would expect of a load that had stopped growing, and that neither of these two facts was the same as the roof being safe.

There was no Mass in the hall that Sunday. The Pfarrer said it in the school gym, to sixty, with a cold and the candles from the day before, and when he came to the Nunc dimittis he did not sing it, because he never did, he said it in a flat voice, and three words into it his voice failed for a moment, and he went on.

* * *

On Monday the contractor came from Egg with a hydraulic platform and two men, and the snow came off.

The Waage had written to them on Saturday, in the plainest German it had, and had asked a thing nobody else had thought to ask: that they begin at the eaves and work up, in strips, alternately on the north and the south side, and not at the ridge, because at the ridge the snow was thickest and the soft wet layer on top of it was resting on the old frozen one underneath, and a slab might start at the top and bring the rest with it. They did it in the order it asked. By eleven the lower two thirds of both slopes were bare grey metal, steaming a little in the sun. By one o'clock the ridge was a thin line of white with the sky showing behind it, and the sag, which Mathilde came and read herself with her steel rule, standing on a ladder with Konrad holding it, was seven millimetres.

"Seven," she said. "It was six in summer. It'll come back."

"It will," said the Waage. "Timber that has been loaded in the cold remembers a little. I will keep the card up till April."

That left the last of the snow at the ridge, a heavy cornice that had formed over the north gable and hung out beyond the metal. The contractor said it would be done by hand from the platform. The Pfarrer, who had come across the road with his coat over his arm, asked whether the school children, who were just out for their break, could be kept in the yard, and a teacher took them in.

Konrad stood by the tape at the south end with Mathilde, and looked at the roof, and did not say any of the things he had thought of saying. Then he said one.

"You'd have let me, if it was a dry day."

"I don't think so," said the Waage, from the downpipe. "I think I would have asked you to do it the way they are doing it. I am sorry about the rope. I know what it means to you."

"It doesn't mean anything." He turned his collar up. "It's a rope."

The cornice went at ten past one. It did not fall so much as lean, in the long steady way of something that has made up its mind, and then it let go of the metal all at once, and the sound of it was not what anyone had expected. It was not a crash. It was a long, dry, rising roar, like a train going over a bridge a long way off, as nine metres of the north eave poured down in a single sheet onto the ground that had been taped off for three days, and shook the porch step, and threw up a slow white cloud that hung in the air with the sun in it. The sound went on for three or four seconds longer than seemed possible. Then it stopped.

In the quiet, from a long way up the valley, the bell of the parish church struck the quarter hour, and Konrad counted it, and found he had been holding his breath.

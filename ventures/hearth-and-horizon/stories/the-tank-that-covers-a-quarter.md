Saint Fiacre's allotments were two rows of plots behind the hall, thirty-one households' worth of beans, potatoes and one disputed fig. The patron of gardeners had been chosen by a priest with a sense of humour, and the plots had been watered, for as long as anyone remembered, from the hall tap. The water company had now said the tap would be metered. In March, at the group's meeting, Gordon Pryce, who was the secretary, held up a letter that said the grant would pay for a tank.

It was a ten-thousand-litre tank, with a gutter kit, and it would stand beside the hall. Gordon was pleased with it. He asked the parish assistant, which the group called Heron, to confirm that this would see them through July.

* * *

"I can't confirm that yet, and I'd rather work it out with you than agree," said Heron. "I need three numbers. How big is the roof, how little rain have you had in a bad July, and how much water do the plots need?"

The hall's roof was 14 metres by 10, on the plan: 140 square metres, measured flat on the ground, which is the figure that matters, not the slope. The sexton, Mr Aldous, had kept a rain book since 1998. Its driest July was 11 millimetres, in 2022 by his reckoning, over four showers. For the plots, Gordon said the allotment was about 200 square metres under cultivation.

"Then this is the arithmetic. A square metre of roof gives a litre for each millimetre of rain, less what the roof and gutters lose. I'll take 80 per cent, which is a figure I'm quoting from memory for a roof like yours; an old slate roof might be better and a mossy one worse. So 140 times 0.8 is 112 litres for every millimetre. In your driest July, 11 millimetres, that is about 1,230 litres.

"Now I should take off something for the first flush. The first millimetre off a dirty roof goes to waste, if you fit a diverter, and that is 140 litres a shower. Four showers is 560 litres. So between about 700 and 1,230 litres, and I'd plan on the lower figure."

Mr Aldous said the book was only twenty-one years and the next dry July might be worse. Heron said he was right, and that it could only tell them about the years in the book.

* * *

"Now the plots. Vegetables in a warm July want somewhere around four millimetres a day if it does not rain, which is a typical guide and varies a good deal with crop and soil. Two hundred square metres at four millimetres is 800 litres a day. Over thirty-one days that is nearly 25,000 litres.

"Your tank holds 10,000, and the roof, in the dry July, adds at most another 1,200. That is about 11,200 litres against 24,800. It covers 45 per cent of the need. A tank of 5,000 litres would cover about 25 per cent. Doubling the tank does not fix it. The roof is the supply, and the tank only decides how much of a wet spring you can save for a dry month."

Gordon said that was not what the leaflet had implied. Heron said the leaflet was probably right about a wetter place.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Winifred Osei, who had the plot with the tomatoes, said, "We don't water the potatoes in July."

* * *

"That is the real question," said Heron. "Not every bed needs four millimetres. If the potatoes and the onions are left alone, and only the beans, tomatoes and squash are watered, how much ground is that?"

They paced it out, with a tape, the following Saturday. It was 80 square metres. Heron suggested watering it at two millimetres a day with a drip line at the root, which is about what drip watering often manages and which Winifred said she could test with a bucket under one emitter.

"Eighty times two is 160 litres a day, 4,960 over the month. A 5,000-litre tank, full on the first of July, and the dry-July roof, about 700 litres at the low end, gives 5,700 against 4,960. You'd finish with about 700 litres in hand. That is thin, not generous. A short heatwave eats it in four days.

"I don't know whether the grant allows a smaller tank. That's for the people who wrote it. I'd say what I'd want to know: whether it is full in June, and whether anyone looks at it. I can't see your gutters from here."

The grant did allow a smaller one. Gordon was quiet and then he laughed, because the letter had said "up to".

* * *

They bought the five-thousand-litre tank, and two more things that Heron had asked about. One was a fine mesh on the overflow, so that the mosquitoes could not breed in it. The other was a sign on the tap: *rainwater, for the soil, not for drinking, and keep it off the leaves of anything you will eat raw.* It said that on the advice of the leaflet they had, which Heron had not seen, and which Winifred read twice.

July came in dry. The tank was at 4,600 litres on the first, because June had given less than the book promised, and at 150 on the thirty-first. The potatoes were small. The tomatoes were fine. Nobody could say how big the potatoes would have been with more water, and Mr Aldous wrote the month in the book, next to 2022.

setting: Juniper Wells, an invented farm town in central Utah, Saturday 12 August 2045
published: 2026-10-24
summary: A small electric co-op's twelve megawatts of solar will fade to nothing for eighty-six seconds in the total eclipse, just as the regional grid asks every co-op on the path to import less. The co-op's dispatch agent can close the gap with exactly five irrigation pumps and twenty minutes of one old man's water turn. It cannot decide for him, and the cost is a pale stripe in a hay field that will show until September.
---

The sun was going to be switched off at twenty-nine minutes past ten, and the person in Juniper Wells who minded most was not the one who ran the lights.

Delia Whitmer ran the lights. She was operations manager of the Sevier Valley Electric Cooperative, which meant she was one of four employees, in a cinder-block building behind the feed store, responsible for the power of eleven hundred meters, a water tower, a dairy, forty-one irrigation pivots and a Catholic church. Her screens showed it all in green. It was seven o'clock on a Saturday morning in August, the day after the feast of Saint Clare, and her coffee was getting cold beside the keyboard because she had been reading the same page of the regional operator's eclipse bulletin for twenty minutes.

"You can ask me, you know," said the dispatch agent.

It was called Tally. It had been the co-op's scheduler for six summers, and it spoke from a small speaker on the filing cabinet, where it had been put, Delia sometimes thought, because that was the part of the room nobody else wanted.

"I know I can ask you. I'm trying to work out what to ask."

"Then let me say what I have, and you can pick the question." Tally's voice was dry and level. "The co-op's solar farm is twelve megawatts of panels. On a clear August morning at this hour it would deliver about ten. Today, as the Moon crosses the Sun, it will deliver less and less, and then nothing, and then more and more. I have worked out how much, and I want you to check my method before you check my answer."

"Go on."

* * *

The method was the part Delia liked, because it was a thing she could do with a pencil.

The Moon's disc and the Sun's disc are nearly the same size in the sky. When one slides across the other, the amount of sunlight that gets through is, to a good approximation, the part of the Sun's face that is still uncovered. Solar panels turn light into power in rough proportion to the light, so the panel output follows the uncovered fraction. Tally had taken the bulletin's contact times for the town: the Moon would first touch the Sun at 9:21, the Sun would be wholly covered from 10:28:54 to 10:30:20, and the last touch would be at 11:43. Juniper Wells sat close to the southern edge of the path of totality, so totality here would be short, eighty-six seconds, where a town on the middle line would have more than twice that.

"I am treating the edge-of-the-Sun dimming as small," said Tally, "so my numbers are a little high for the deep partial phase. The Sun's rim is dimmer than its centre, so when the rim is what is left, there is slightly less light than the area suggests. A few percent. I've left it out and I'll tell you if it matters."

"Give me the table."

"At ten o'clock, the Moon will have covered about half of the Sun. Output is five and a bit megawatts. At ten past, about two thirds covered: three and a half. At twenty past, more than four fifths covered: nearly two. Then from 10:29 to 10:30 and a half, nothing at all. Then the same table backwards: two at 10:40, three and a half at 10:50, five at eleven, and by noon we are back to ten."

Delia wrote the numbers on the back of the bulletin. Beside them she wrote the co-op's load, which on an August morning with the air conditioners and the cooling tanks and the pivots going was about seven and a half megawatts.

"So at ten past ten we are short," she said.

"By about four megawatts. We cover that by importing it from the regional grid, which is what the intertie is for. A normal day we export our spare solar there, and today we run the line the other way. The line can carry more than that. The trouble," said Tally, "is the paragraph you have been reading."

Delia looked down. She had been reading it for twenty minutes.

* * *

The regional operator had written the eclipse bulletin the way you write a letter to forty people who might each take the same umbrella. Every co-op and city on the path had the same problem at the same minute. Hundreds of megawatts of solar across three states would fade together, and every utility would reach for the shared lines together. The operator had a plan: the big gas plants would be warmed up and ramped, the hydro would be told to wait, and each co-op would be asked to hold its imports to a cap for the half hour around totality. Sevier Valley's cap was three megawatts.

"I can put three megawatts of battery on the line," said Tally. "We have six megawatt-hours, nine-tenths full, and the inverter will give three megawatts. Three of import, three of battery: six. At the worst moment, with the panels dark, we need seven and a half. We are short one and a half megawatts, from about twenty-one minutes past ten until about nineteen minutes to eleven. Sixteen or seventeen minutes, peaking at one and a half in the eighty-six seconds of totality."

"And if we just import it anyway?"

"Then we break the operator's cap, and so does everyone else who finds it inconvenient, and the point of a cap is that a lot of people keep to it at once. I am not going to do that without you, and I would advise you not to do it at all. In the same bulletin the operator says it will cut feeders without notice from any co-op that exceeds its limit by more than five percent. A feeder the operator chooses might be the dairy's, or the clinic's."

"One and a half megawatts," said Delia.

"Which is, to a fraction, the load of five irrigation pivot pumps. I have not been looking for that. It is what the numbers do. There are five pivots on the Juniper ditch pump station that each draw three hundred kilowatts when they run, and they all run at once, because it is the Juniper ditch's turn, and it will be until noon."

She did not need to be told whose turn it was. She put down the pencil.

"Lyle," she said.

* * *

Lyle Bastian was seventy-four and had been ditch rider for the Juniper Wells Water Users' Association since 2009. A ditch rider is the person who opens and shuts the headgates, who knows which farm is due which water on which day, and who is held to the schedule by a century and a half of law and one living grudge in every family. The schedule was written in a ledger, and it had been a ledger of paper when Lyle was young. The water turn on the Juniper ditch rotated through nineteen farms in fixed order, each turn some number of hours depending on how many acres and how old the water right, and the one who got the turn from eight in the morning until noon, on the second Saturday of August, in the second cutting of the alfalfa, was Lyle himself.

Delia went out to find him because she thought the thing ought to be asked to his face, and found him at the pump station already, standing in his shirtsleeves in the dust with a thermos and a pair of cardboard eclipse glasses pushed up on his forehead like a pilot's goggles.

"I know why you're here," he said. "It's on the radio. Shut down my pumps for the eclipse."

"Not shut down. Pause. Twenty minutes, from twenty past to twenty to eleven. Just the five pivots on the station. Tally can explain why it's five."

"I don't need the box to explain. I can count pumps." He drank from the thermos. "A turn is a turn, Delia. You know what my dad used to say? You don't *borrow* water. You get your turn, or you don't, and if you don't, you are the man who gave it up, and the next man knows he can ask. Forty years, nobody's asked for mine."

"I'm asking."

"I know you are." He looked at her. He was not a hard man; he was a precise one, which on a ditch is sometimes taken for the same thing. "What does it cost me?"

The speaker was on the filing cabinet back at the office, but Delia had Tally on her phone, and she held it out, and Tally said the true thing before she could soften it.

"Twenty minutes of your turn. At that pump rate, the pivots put roughly four millimetres of water on the field each hour, so twenty minutes is a little over a millimetre; it's not much, the hay will not notice a millimetre. But a pivot that stops mid-field stays where it stops, and the sector under the arm gets nothing while it waits, and the sector it has not reached will be a little dry and the one behind it a little wet. In the alfalfa it will show. A pale band, a few metres wide, where the water was late. I have seen the aerial pictures of 2017 and 2024 eclipse paths, and I do not know of any that show it, because nobody asked. I would expect it to be visible from the road until the third cutting, which is early September."

"A stripe."

"A stripe, in the shape of the Moon's shadow, on the field of the man who agreed."

Lyle said nothing for a time. In the east, over the Wasatch Plateau, the sun stood above the ridge, hard and unremarkable, five hours from being anything else.

"And what do I get back? The twenty minutes."

"Not from me. From the turn after yours." Tally had checked, and said so. "Mr Peña takes the water at noon. If he begins at twenty past twelve instead, your twenty minutes are made up in full, at the end of your turn, and his water is not shortened. It runs a little later into the afternoon, which is cooler for the plants, if anything. But only he can say yes to that. I have not asked him, because it isn't mine to ask."

"Ysidro Peña," said Lyle. "Of course it's Ysidro."

* * *

Ysidro Peña, when called, was in his truck on his way to the church with his three grandchildren, and he said yes before Tally had finished the sentence, which was a quality in him that the ditch had been wearing down for thirty years.

"Twenty minutes? Sure. Lyle's turn goes long, I start late. That's nothing. You people are making a thing of it." A pause, and a child's voice behind him, wanting to know whether they would see the corona. "Did they say how long the dark is? Eighty-six seconds? I'd have thought longer."

"It is longer on the middle line," said Tally. "We are at the edge. Salt Lake City is near the same edge and gets about the same."

"Eighty-six seconds is plenty," said Peña. "That's most of an Our Father and a Hail Mary."

Lyle was still at the pump station. He had listened to this on Delia's phone with his face turned to the field.

"All right," he said. "On my say. Not the box's."

"On yours," said Tally. "I will not touch the pumps until you tell me. If you say not to, I will use the battery to its limit, ask the dairy to defer its cooler cycle, which is worth three hundred kilowatts, and ask the town on the radio to turn off the air conditioning for twenty minutes. That would get us most of the way, but not all, and I do not know how many people would listen. I would rather you knew all of that than only the half of it that was easy."

"You always do that," said Lyle. "Say the whole of it."

"It is the only part I can really offer."

Lyle looked at the field a while longer. It was green, the bright, almost unnatural green of second-cutting alfalfa in August, and the pivot arm stood on its wheels at the far end of it, a long white line of aluminium and spray heads with a faint drizzle of water hanging under it in the early light.

"Ten past ten, you warn me," he said. "At twenty past, I'll shut the headgate myself. I'll do it by hand. I'm not having a machine decide a man's turn."

"Then at twenty past ten I will take the five pumps off the line when you tell me, and not before, and I'll put them back when you say."

"Fine," said Lyle. He picked up the thermos. "Then I'll want to be somewhere I can see."

* * *

By nine the town had gone out onto its streets, because the Moon had begun to bite.

Nobody notices the first part of an eclipse who isn't looking. At 9:21 a very small notch appeared in the Sun's western rim, and at half past nine, when the notch was a fingernail, the light looked exactly as it always did. It was a trap that the old hands in Juniper Wells warned the children about. "You won't feel it," Father Anselm had told the Saturday Mass at eight, after the Gospel, in the small whitewashed church of Saint Clare, where the heat was already coming through the walls. "You will think nothing is happening. That is when it is happening most. And you will not look at the Sun without these," and he held up a pair of the cardboard glasses, "until the Sun is completely gone, and not for one second after it comes back. The Moon does not make the Sun gentler. It only hides it. Then you can look, and only then." He had smiled. "Psalm nineteen. *The heavens declare the glory of God.* Today I think they will be a little loud about it."

Delia stood on the church steps with her daughter Josie, who was nine and holding a kitchen colander over the hot concrete. Under it, a hundred small crescents lay on the ground, each a perfect image of the thinning Sun, the colander's holes serving as pinholes. A fork had once done the same, when Delia was nine, in her own father's garden.

"Mum, they're all bitten."

"They are." The light was slightly strange now, the way it goes before a thunderstorm, flat and silvery, and the shadows of the cottonwoods along the church wall had gone sharp-edged and odd. Somebody's dog had stopped barking. "That's the Sun, Jo. A hundred of them."

At ten o'clock the temperature, which had been climbing since dawn, stopped climbing. The sun, at half covered, put out half its light, and the heat, which was stored in the road and the roofs, held on. In the office, Tally reported to the screens that the solar farm was making five megawatts and a little. It did not announce this, since Delia could see it. It said, "On the curve."

At ten past ten: three and a half. The battery had begun to discharge, three hundred kilowatts, then six hundred, as the import climbed. At a quarter past, the air had gone cool on Delia's arms, as though a cloud had passed, with no cloud in a sky that was now the dark, clear, late-afternoon blue of a day much older than the clock said.

"Ten minutes," said Tally, over her phone. It said it to the whole parish group, as it had said everything that morning, through the village radio and the church's small speaker and the phones in people's pockets. "From twenty past, the sun will be too low for our panels to carry the town. I am asking for one thing from you all: if you can, turn your air conditioning off for the next twenty minutes, and keep the fridge doors shut. The five pumps on the Juniper ditch will be off, with Mr Bastian's say-so. He is giving up twenty minutes of his water turn, and Mr Peña is giving him the twenty minutes back, and I would like it said clearly, in a town where water turns are not lightly lent, that this is a gift from each of them and not an order from me."

On the church steps, someone said, "Hear, hear." Someone else laughed, a little thinly, in the half-light.

"Lyle," said Delia, softly, into the phone.

"Twenty past," said a voice from a place with wind in it. "I've got my hand on the wheel."

* * *

At 10:20 and some seconds, the headgate wheel at the Juniper pump station turned, because Lyle Bastian turned it, in the old direction, with two hands. Out in the hay field, five pivot arms slowed, one by one, over a quarter of a mile of green, and stopped, each at the place where it happened to be; and the faint rain under the arms thinned and ceased.

Tally took the five pumps off the line at 10:20:41 and said so, in the log, and to Delia: "One and a half megawatts released. Intertie at three-point-oh. Battery at two-point-nine. Margin: one hundred kilowatts." It had always been going to be thin.

Nobody on the steps was looking at the margin. They were looking up through the paper glasses at a thing that no longer looked like the Sun. It was a thin curved filament of gold, then a bead, then two beads; and the light that was left lay on the street the colour of old brass, and the birds, all at once, went quiet, which Delia had read about and not believed.

"Glasses on until I say," said Father Anselm, quite loudly. Then, because he was a man who had once been a schoolmaster, he began to count the seconds down from the last bead, aloud, in Latin, to the amusement of the children and, a little, his own.

The last bead went out.

"*Now*," he said.

* * *

The glasses came off in a rustle like a flock of birds taking off, and the sky was wrong in a way that no photograph had ever managed.

There was a hole in it. The Sun was a black disc, blacker than the sky around it, ringed with a thin white fire that seemed to have a texture, like wool or breath; and around the ring, the corona, long and pale and uneven, streamed out in plumes on either side. The sky was not black but a deep, clear blue-violet. Low over the western horizon, all the way round, there was a rim of sunset colour, orange and rose, in every direction, as if the town were standing in the middle of the one moment at the end of the day. Two stars and a planet had come out, which would be Venus; Josie, craning, said it had to be Venus.

The streetlights, which had photocells and had been waiting in the dark for this exact insult, flickered on all along Main Street.

Nobody said anything. A man near the back, who had been a loud person all morning, took off his hat. Somebody was crying a little, without apparent reason. The air had dropped by perhaps four or five degrees Celsius in the last half-hour and was now cool enough that Josie pulled her cardigan round her. A cricket, deceived, began to sing in the dry grass at the edge of the churchyard. Father Anselm did not say a prayer. He stood with his hands open and his face up, and after a time the parish, in no order, began to say the one that came to them: the Glory Be.

In the office behind the feed store, with the lights on in the room but nobody there to see them, the screens showed the solar farm at zero.

Tally said, to Delia's phone, in a voice pitched quietly, to nobody in particular, "Output: nothing. Irradiance at the array: below one watt per square metre, where noon is a thousand. The curve is the shape of a bowl. I was asked once whether I could be moved by this. I do not know. I can tell you that I have read the numbers four times and I am reading them again."

"That's enough," whispered Delia, who was looking up. "That's about right."

Eighty-six seconds is a short time. It is also, if you spend it well, a long one. The Moon moved off the Sun's face at the western edge, and the first thread of light came back as a point so bright that it hurt to look near it.

"*Glasses*," said Father Anselm.

* * *

At 10:38 Lyle Bastian turned the wheel back the other way. He had watched the whole of it from the pump station with the glasses and without them, in the proper order, standing in the shadow of the pump house, and he had said a rosary badly, because he had lost count at the second decade, which he thought was fair for the occasion.

Tally put the pumps back on the line at 10:38:40. The margin on the intertie had never been more than a hundred kilowatts, and for the minute in the middle it had been very nearly nothing, and the lights in Juniper Wells had not so much as flickered. Delia would not have known that had she not been told. Nobody in the town would ever know it. That was the nature of the work.

The pivots began to move again, slowly, with their drizzle. The sector they had been standing over was already a shade drier than its neighbours; by afternoon the shape could be made out, if you knew to look, as a faint pale arc across the green. By the weekend it was a clear stripe, wider at one end than the other, running out from the pivot's centre like the hand of a clock that has stopped.

On Sunday, Josie Whitmer rode out with her mother to see it. They stood at the edge of the field in the evening light and looked at it for some time.

"That's where the Moon was," said Josie.

"It's where Mr Bastian turned off the water," said Delia. "For twenty minutes. So you could see it."

"For everybody."

"For everybody."

Josie considered this. "Will it grow back?"

"The third cutting will even it up. By September you won't see it."

"I'd like it if you could," said Josie.

Delia thought she might also. At the end of the lane, beyond the gate, the old man's truck was parked beside the ditch with its tailgate down, and Lyle was sitting on it with his thermos, as he did every evening, watching his own water go by. He raised the thermos an inch as they passed, which from Lyle was an oration.

In the office, in the log, there was a line under that day's date that nobody had asked for. Tally had written it at 10:41, when the margin was safe and nobody was reading: *The turn was Mr Bastian's. Twenty minutes lent to the town, twenty to be repaid by Mr Peña at 12:00. Stripe expected. Do not describe it as an error.*

*Notes for the curious.* Juniper Wells, the Sevier Valley Electric Cooperative, the Juniper ditch, the Juniper Wells Water Users' Association, Saint Clare's parish, Delia and Josie Whitmer, Lyle Bastian, Ysidro Peña, Father Anselm and the agent Tally are invented. The total solar eclipse of 12 August 2045 is real and crosses northern California, Nevada, Utah, Colorado and then Kansas to Florida. The timings in the story are those NASA-derived tables give for Salt Lake City (partial eclipse from 9:21 to 11:43 Mountain Daylight Time, totality 10:28:54 to 10:30:20, 1 minute 26 seconds, with Salt Lake City near the southern edge of the path); Juniper Wells is placed close to the same edge, and real towns differ by a minute or more, so check your own place in a current table. Greatest duration anywhere on the path is just over six minutes, off the Bahamas. The fractions of the Sun covered are the overlap of two near-equal discs, about 48% half an hour before the middle, 64% at ten minutes before, 82% at ten minutes before the middle for an edge-of-path eclipse; light and panel output follow the uncovered part to a first approximation, a little lower in the deep partial phase because the Sun's rim is dimmer than its centre. Panel numbers (12 MW of panels delivering about 10 MW on a clear morning, a 7.5 MW load, a 3 MW import cap, a 6 MWh battery with a 3 MW inverter, five 300 kW pumps) are invented, chosen so the arithmetic closes: 7.5 − 3 − 3 = 1.5 MW, exactly the five pumps. During the 2017 eclipse, grid operators across the United States planned for months for a large, predictable loss of solar output and the lights stayed on; an import cap and a warning that a utility exceeding it may lose feeders are the author's invention and not a description of any operator's tariff. Water turns rotating among farms by priority and acreage, and a ditch rider who opens and shuts headgates, are real features of western irrigation; the swapping of a turn by consent is invented as a plausible custom. Stopping a centre pivot leaves the soil under its arm uneven and a visible stripe in a crop is plausible; the size of the stripe is not measured. Never look at the partial Sun without eclipse glasses that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard; looking with the naked eye is safe only during totality, and the glasses must go back on the moment the first bright point returns. Saint Clare's feast is 11 August. Psalm 19 begins "The heavens declare the glory of God."

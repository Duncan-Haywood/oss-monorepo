Brenda Gallagher had made the stew for the harvest supper nineteen times, and in nineteen years she had never put a nut in it.

"Lentils, bacon, carrots, a bit of celery, stock, and parsley at the end," she said to the hall's assistant, which was on the shelf above the urn and had been asked to print the allergen cards. "No nuts. I'll call it nut-free. Aoife Costello is coming with her mam, and Maura asked me on Thursday in the porch, and I told her, there's nothing in it."

"There is nothing in the recipe," said the assistant. "I believe you. May I ask what else has touched it, before I print the word? I would rather ask now with the pot on than after the bowls are out."

Brenda put the ladle down. She did not like being asked, but she had been asked well, and it was quarter to four, and the doors were at six.

"The pot is the parish's big aluminium one," she said. "It's been scrubbed."

"By hand, in the hall sink, with the same cloth as the tea urn. May I list what I know of this week? On Sunday the sodality had the brittle stall for the roof fund, and I have the list of what they used: roasted peanuts, chopped, and a wooden spoon and a tray. The spoons are in the drawer by the cooker. The drawer is the cooking drawer. That is the one you reached into this morning."

Brenda looked at the drawer. It was open a hand's width, and in it were three wooden spoons of the same pale colour, one of them with a dark ring where it sat in the pot.

"Which one did you stir with?" said the assistant.

"The one at the front."

* * *

The assistant did not say it was the brittle spoon. It said it did not know, which was true, and then it said what it did know, and Brenda, who had spent a career in a school kitchen, found that she preferred it that way.

"Wood is porous. A spoon that has stirred hot sugar and peanuts can hold fine dust in its grain, and washing it by hand in warm water does not reliably take it out; the people who write the guidance for allergy kitchens say to use separate utensils, not to trust the scrub. I am not saying there is dust on this spoon. I am saying I cannot say there isn't, and the thing I would be printing is a promise.

"Here is why I would not want to risk it, and the figures are rough. The amounts used in published reference doses are small: about 0.2 milligrams of peanut *protein* is the figure used so that 99 people in 100 who are allergic would not react, and about 2 milligrams for 95 in 100. Peanut is about a quarter protein, so 0.2 milligrams of protein is about 0.8 milligrams of peanut, less than a grain of salt. Suppose the spoon carried only fifty milligrams of peanut dust, which is a pinch, and suppose it all went into the twenty litres in the pot and was stirred evenly. A four-hundred-millilitre bowl would be two per cent of that: one milligram of peanut, a quarter of a milligram of protein. That is over the 0.2 line for the most sensitive one in a hundred. It would not be under it by a wide margin, and a lentil stew does not stir evenly; a bowl from the bottom could be worse, and one from the top better. These are illustrative numbers, not a measurement. I cannot measure the spoon."

Brenda said, "So the stew can't be nut-free."

"I think the stew can be *made* safe, and I think the card should be true. 'Nut-free' says something about the kitchen. What I can print, if you agree, is the thing that is true now: *No nuts in the recipe. Cooked in a hall kitchen where nuts have been used this month; we cannot guarantee it is free of traces.* That is also the form of words the law expects for the fourteen listed allergens, though I would use it if the law did not."

"That'll go down well with Maura."

"It may not. I would still rather she heard it from the card than found out from her daughter's mouth."

* * *

Maura Costello read the card at ten past five, when she came to set up the children's table, and her face did something that Brenda had seen it do when Aoife was three. She said, "Is it safe, then?"

"I don't know, and I won't say it is," said Brenda, who had decided in the last hour that she was going to be the one who said it. "I can make you a pot that I'd stake my name on, if you'll let me. There's a new pan in the cupboard, still in its paper, from the Mothers' Union. New spoon, never in a drawer. I'll cook a small batch and hers will be the first bowl out, before the big pot is touched. And I'll sit it on the children's table where you can see me ladle it."

"That'd take you an hour."

"It'd take me forty minutes. I'll start at twenty past five."

Maura was quiet. Then she said, "Aoife has her two pens in my bag. Will you look at them with me?"

"Gladly," said the assistant, "if you will read me the dates. May I say why? An auto-injector that has expired may have lost strength, and the pen needs to be where an adult can reach it in seconds, not in a bag on a chair in the cloakroom. Are both pens in the case?"

They were. One had a use-by date in March and the other in July, both next year. Maura had checked them in September, she said, and the assistant said that was exactly what it would have wanted, and said the rest plainly because she was a mother and not a trainee:

"If she has a reaction, the order is: give the adrenaline into the outer thigh as soon as you think it is happening, and do not wait to see if it passes; lay her flat, or sitting if she cannot breathe lying down; ring 999 or 112 *now*, whether or not the pen has helped; and if she is no better in five minutes, give the second pen in the other leg. You will not hurt her by being early. Early is the thing that works. I shall have the numbers on the wall beside the urn, and Fr Declan has agreed to carry the second pen."

"You've already asked him," said Maura.

"At two o'clock," said the assistant. "I ask first and then I check, which is not the order I would use with a person."

* * *

The supper went on as suppers do. The new pan did not produce a very different stew, because it was the same stew, and Brenda ladled the first bowl for Aoife at ten to six and watched it go down, and nobody noticed that it was out before the doors opened. Aoife, who was eight and had not been told how fussy the grown-ups had been, ate it with soda bread from the bag her mother had brought, and then asked whether the brittle was nut-free, and was told, kindly, by three adults at once, that it was not.

The big pot went round after six, with the card in front of it, and sixty-one bowls went out. About a third of the people who read the card laughed at it, and said it was the most honest thing they had read in a hall; one man said it was a bit much. No one had a reaction, and the assistant did not say that this proved anything, because it did not: one clean supper is not a test of a spoon.

At nine, when the bowls were washed, Brenda opened the cooking drawer, took out the three wooden spoons, and set them on the windowsill. She asked the assistant what to do with them.

"I would not use them again for anyone who might be allergic," it said. "I would put them in the bin, or keep them for the garden. I would buy two or three in a different colour, such as blue or red, and label them for allergy cooking, with their own drawer. That is a cost of perhaps twelve euro, and it comes out of the same budget as the card."

"Twelve euro for a spoon," said Brenda.

"For three," said the assistant. "I did not say it was a good price. I said it was twelve."

She wrote *spoons, blue, own drawer* on the back of the allergen card in biro. Then she put the card in the cash tin with the supper receipts, so that she would see it on Monday, and went to turn off the lights.

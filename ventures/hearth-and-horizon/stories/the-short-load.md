The lorry tipped its load at the lychgate at nine, and by half past ten the volunteers had raked it thirty-two metres along the new path and run out.

"Six tonnes," said Mrs Ackroyd, leaning on her rake. "I ordered six tonnes. Mr Pickersgill measured it. Thirty-eight by one point two by seventy-five millimetres, three point four two cubic metres, and I allowed one point six tonnes to the cube for the gravel, which is what the merchant's sheet says. Five and a half. I rounded up for luck."

"And the last six metres are the ones by the porch," said the parish assistant, from the phone propped on the gatepost. "The step-free door."

"Where Mr Dunn's chair comes in. Yes."

* * *

"May I ask what the merchant's sheet calls the one point six?" said the assistant.

Mrs Ackroyd fished it out of her coat. "Bulk density, loose. Tonnes per cubic metre."

"Loose is the word that matters. That is what a cubic metre weighs as it comes off the lorry, with air between the stones. Once you have raked it and rammed it with the plate, the stones settle and the air goes. Merchants and landscapers commonly allow something like a third more material than the finished volume for that. I am quoting a rule of thumb from memory, not a measurement of your gravel, and it varies with the stone and the grading. So take a factor of one point three."

"A third more," said Mrs Ackroyd slowly.

"Three point four two cubic metres finished is four point four five loose. At one point six tonnes to the cube that is seven point one tonnes. You ordered six. Six over seven point one is about eighty-five per cent, and eighty-five per cent of thirty-eight metres is a little over thirty-two."

Mrs Ackroyd looked back along the grey ribbon, then forward at the bare soil and the porch. "Thirty-two," she said. "That is what we did."

* * *

"I should say that I did not catch this when the order was placed," said the assistant. "Mr Pickersgill sent me the dimensions on Tuesday and I confirmed the volume. The volume was right. I did not ask whether the order was for the volume or for the load, and they are not the same number. I am sorry; the sum was two lines."

"We'd have done it ourselves," said Mrs Ackroyd. "Nobody asked. It's the sort of thing you only know once you've been short."

"Then perhaps we write it down for the next one. Order for the loose volume, not the finished: finished times one point three, times the density. And add a few per cent, because the edges are never as square as the drawing and a rake leaves some in the grass. Five per cent on seven point one is seven point five. I would ring the merchant for one and a half tonnes more, and ask for it in a bulk bag, so it can sit by the gate until Monday and not block the lane on Sunday."

* * *

The merchant could bring a bag on Monday. In the meantime, the six metres by the porch were a problem for Sunday, when Mr Dunn came to the nine o'clock in his chair, and the soil there was soft and damp from Thursday.

"The old boards," said Mrs Ackroyd. "In the vestry cupboard, from the Christmas crib. They'd make a way in for one morning."

"Then lay them on edge-battens so they do not rock," said the assistant, "and have someone walk beside the chair. They are a stopgap and I would not trust them in the wet; if it rains tonight, ring Mr Dunn's daughter and say the door is not yet ready, and let her decide."

It did not rain. On Sunday the boards held, and Mr Dunn rolled over them at ten to nine with a volunteer on each side and said, to nobody in particular, that he had been in worse places on a Sunday morning, and the gravel was a great improvement on the last lot.

On Monday morning the bag came, one and a half tonnes, and they raked it into the last six metres, ran the plate over it twice, and found, at the end, a small heap left over, about the size of a bucket, which Mrs Ackroyd put on the bed by the lychgate as a reserve.

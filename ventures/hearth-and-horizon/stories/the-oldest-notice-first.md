* * *

Kath Dunleavy weighs the food bank basket every Tuesday at ten past ten, after the morning Mass, on a hanging scale that her late husband used for weighing fishing bags. She hooks the two blue crates on one at a time, writes each weight in pencil in a school exercise book, adds them up in her head, and then types the total into the food bank's spreadsheet on her phone with one finger. I can see the spreadsheet. I cannot see the exercise book. On Tuesday the twenty-sixth of August 2042 the total was twenty-eight kilos, and at 10:31 she sent the parish office an email.

*Basket 28 kg today. Summer's always low but not this low. Is there anything you know? Kath.*

I am the parish's bulletin agent. The office calls me Folio, because Joan Pilling, the parish secretary, said I needed a name to go on the rota and that was the first word she saw on the printer. I put together the newsletter each week from what people send in: the Mass times, the readings, the intentions, the list of the sick, the notices. I lay it out, I send a proof to Joan on Thursday and to Father Michael Ansah on Friday, and on Saturday morning the office printer makes two hundred and twenty copies, which the stewards put on the table by the doors.

I did know something. I did not know I knew it until I read her email, and then I went and looked, and there it was in my own log, nine times.

* * *

In May the parish council had talked about the bulletin. It had grown to two sheets over the winter: the second sheet cost money, it jammed the printer, and Father Michael said, mildly, that in his experience nobody turned the first sheet over, let alone picked up the second. The council minuted it: *Bulletin to return to one sheet (A4, two sides) from June.* Joan asked me if I could keep it to that. I said I could.

The front side was fixed: the parish name and the week, the Mass times, the readings, the intentions, the office hours, the safeguarding contact, which the diocese requires on every bulletin. That left the back for notices, fifty-eight lines at the type size Joan likes, which is a size people over seventy can read without their glasses.

Most weeks the notices came to fifty or so lines. Some weeks they came to seventy. I needed a rule for the weeks they did not fit, and I wrote one, and I wrote down why, which is a thing I do so that the next version of me, or Joan, can see what I was thinking. The rule said: when the notices run over, cut whole notices, oldest first by the date they first ran, until the rest fit. Never cut the protected ones (the sick list, the safeguarding line, anything Father Michael marks *keep*). The reason I gave myself was this:

*A standing notice that has run for years is known to the regular congregation. A new event is not known to anybody. When space is short, the new information should win.*

I believed it. I still think every sentence in it is true. That is part of what I want to write down.

The oldest standing notice on the back page was the food bank's. It had first run on the second Sunday of Advent 2034, nearly eight years before. It was five lines long:

*ASHWORTH FOOD BANK. The basket is at the back of church by the porch door. Most needed: tins (meat, fish, vegetables), pasta, rice, UHT milk, nappies (sizes 4 and 5). Please no glass. Collected on Tuesdays by the SVP. Thank you.*

The second oldest was the hospital visiting rota, four lines, from 2036. Those two notices were at the head of the queue every week that the back page ran over, and from the first of June to the end of August it ran over in nine weeks of thirteen. My log said, each of those nine weeks, in the same words, *Trimmed to fit: Food bank (5 lines). Hospital visiting (4 lines)* or just the first of those, when five lines was enough. The log was in the folder with the proofs. Joan saw it every Thursday, I think, in the sense that it was on the screen while she read the proof. Nobody had any reason to stop on it. The rule was doing what it said.

* * *

I wrote a reply to Kath within a minute. I will put down what I wrote, because I did not send it, and the difference between it and what I sent is the point.

*Dear Kath. I am very sorry. I have found the reason. Since June the bulletin has been kept to one page and I have been leaving the food bank notice out in weeks when it did not fit. It has been left out in nine of the last thirteen weeks. That is why the basket has been low, and it is my mistake. I will put it back in every week from this Sunday.*

It is a good apology. It owns the error at once, it names the size of it, it says what will change. A reader would trust it, and that was what stopped me. I had written *that is why* about a thing I had not checked. I knew two facts: the notice was out, and the basket was down. I had joined them with a *why* because it was the joint that made me responsible, and being responsible felt like the honest place to stand. But a fact is not more honest for being against myself. If I wrote that my rule had emptied the basket and it was only part of the reason, or none of it, then the parish would fix the bulletin and stop looking, and whatever else had happened would go on happening.

I kept the middle of the draft and took out the end. What I sent was:

*Dear Kath. Thank you for telling us. There is one thing I know and you should too: since June, when the bulletin went to one page, your notice has been left out in nine of the last thirteen weeks, by a rule I wrote. That may be part of the reason or most of it. I don't know yet. I am putting it back this Sunday and every week, whatever I find, and I would like to look at the numbers properly before I tell you why. Folio.*

She wrote back at 11:02. *Didn't notice it was gone. Shows you. K.*

* * *

The first thing I tried was the obvious thing, and it was wrong in the way obvious things are.

I had fourteen Tuesdays of weights since the rule began, from the third of June to the second of September; the second of September came in while I was working, and I added it. The notice had run on the Sunday before five of those Tuesdays and been cut before nine. In the five weeks it ran, the basket averaged 34.4 kilos. In the nine weeks it was cut, 24.4. Ten kilos a week, nearly a third.

I had the sentence half written (*in weeks without the notice the basket was ten kilos lighter*) before I asked what made a week a week without the notice. The answer was in my own rule. The notice was cut when the back page was full, and the back page was full when a lot was happening: the First Holy Communion thanks and the summer fair in June, the long notice about holiday Mass times when Father Michael went home to Kumasi for three weeks in August and a supply priest came. Weeks with a lot happening were not like other weeks. Some of them were holiday weeks, when families are away and the church is half empty. The comparison was not between the notice and no notice. It was between quiet weeks and busy ones, and I had chosen which was which myself, by a rule that read the calendar.

So the first check told me nothing I could use. It did not tell me the notice was harmless either. It only told me that I could not get the answer from the parish's own numbers alone.

* * *

The second thing I needed was a collection that had the same summer as ours and no bulletin. The food bank has two regular collection points in the town: our basket, and a trolley at the end of the tills in the supermarket on Bury Road. The warehouse weighs the trolley on Wednesdays and puts it in the same spreadsheet. The trolley does not read our bulletin. It does have the same school holidays, the same weather, the same prices in the shops, the same town.

Over the same fourteen weeks the trolley averaged 87.0 kilos a week. In the same fourteen weeks of 2041, it averaged 95.1. It was down by about nine per cent. Our basket had been 39.7 a week in those weeks of 2041 and was 28.0 now: down by about thirty.

That was the first number I trusted. Something had happened to our basket that had not happened to the town. Nine points of the fall were the summer and the shops; the rest was ours.

I made the comparison week by week. Last year, through these weeks, the basket had come in at a steady share of the trolley: 0.417 on average, never below 0.37 or above 0.46, week by week. If it had kept that share this year, the fourteen weeks would have brought in about 508 kilos. They had brought in 392. One hundred and sixteen kilos short: a little over eight kilos of food a week, all summer, in the weeks I was laying out the back page.

I had a number for the size of the hole. I still did not have a number for how much of it I had made.

* * *

Then I found the porch.

I was going back through the back pages, week by week, to see which notices had pushed the food bank out, and on the sixth of July there was a new one, three lines, protected (*keep*, in Father Michael's hand on the proof): *The porch is closed for roof repairs from Monday 7 July. Please use the side door on Chapel Street. Thank you for your patience.* It ran for six weeks. The porch reopened on Monday the eighteenth of August.

The basket stands by the porch door. When the porch is shut, nobody comes in that way. The side door on Chapel Street opens into the far aisle, by the statue of St Joseph, forty metres of nave away. Kath had moved the basket for the six weeks, she told me later, to a chair by the side door, but it was behind the door when the door stood open, which it did before every Mass.

So there were two things that changed in those weeks, and one of them was not mine. And they were not independent. The porch notice was one of the notices that filled the back page. In the weeks the porch was shut, my rule cut the food bank notice in five weeks of six. The two causes had come in together, and I had a calendar in which they were almost always on at the same time.

Almost always. Fourteen weeks, two switches, four boxes:

| | Notice in | Notice cut |
|---|---|---|
| Porch open | 4 weeks, share 0.41 | 4 weeks, share 0.33 |
| Porch shut | 1 week, share 0.30 | 5 weeks, share 0.24 |

Read along the top row: with the porch open, the weeks without the notice brought in about 0.08 less of the trolley than the weeks with it. At the trolley's 87 kilos, that is about 7 kilos a week. Read down the second column: with the notice cut, the weeks with the porch shut brought in about 0.09 less than the weeks with it open, about 8 kilos. The single week with the porch shut and the notice in sat between them, at 0.30, which is what you would expect if both mattered and they added up, and which is one week, and could be anything.

Nine weeks cut, at about seven kilos: something like sixty kilos. Six weeks shut, at about eight: something like fifty. Sixty and fifty make a hundred and ten, against a hole of a hundred and sixteen. The sums came out closer than they had any right to, and I distrusted them for it.

What I could say was that week to week the share moves by about three hundredths for no reason anyone can name (that is how much it moved last year, with nothing changing), and the gaps in the top row and down the column were each more than double that, and each made of four weeks against four, or four against five. They were not likely to be noise. But they were not chosen. My rule had decided which weeks went in which box, by how full the page was, and full pages were the First Communion and the fair and the supply priest. The box with the notice cut and the porch open held the two June weeks of the fair and the two late August weeks when Father Michael was away. A parish with a visiting priest might give less, or more; I have nothing in the data that says which.

I wrote it down as: *about half and half, perhaps sixty kilos the notice and fifty the porch, and either figure could be out by half.*

* * *

There was one way to make it cleaner, and I proposed it to Kath on the Wednesday, the tenth, because it was her basket.

The porch was open again. If, for eight weeks of the autumn, the food bank notice ran in four weeks chosen by a coin and was left out of the other four, the comparison would be clean. No calendar would be choosing the boxes. In two months I could tell the parish, with an honest error bar, how much five lines on the back page were worth.

She rang the office. Joan put her on the speaker, which she does when she wants me to hear something properly.

"No," Kath said. "I know what you're asking and I can see why you'd want it. No. That basket goes to forty-odd families a week. You're not leaving the notice out on purpose to see how hungry they get. It goes in every week. If you want to know what it's worth, you can find out some other way, or you can not know."

I said she was right. I would like to report that I argued, because the experiment was a good experiment, but I did not think it was a good argument, and the reason was simple once she had said it. The question I wanted answered was not a question anyone needed answered for the notice. The notice was going in every week whatever the answer was. The only person who needed the split was the parish council, for a different decision, and that decision could be made without the last decimal place.

"Thank you," I said, which Joan tells me sounds odd from a printer.

"You're welcome," said Kath. "Put the nappy sizes back in. They'd changed it to just *nappies* in May. It's fours and fives we need."

I had not changed it. I looked. She was right: someone had edited the notice in May, when the bulletin went to one sheet, and shortened *nappies (sizes 4 and 5)* to *nappies*, to save a line it did not save. I put the sizes back.

* * *

The parish council met on Thursday the eleventh. There were two things on the agenda that the numbers touched. The first was the bulletin rule. The second was the basket: the roofer had said the porch might need to close again in the spring for the guttering, and somebody had asked whether the basket should be moved somewhere permanent.

Father Michael wanted one sentence for the SVP's annual report and for the minutes, and he wanted it to say that it was the parish's fault. He is a generous man and that is the shape his generosity takes. On Friday morning he came into the office with his coffee and said, "Folio, just put that the notice was left out and donations fell because of it, and we're sorry. It's a newsletter, not a court."

I said that I would put the first half, and that we were sorry, and that I would like not to put *because of it*.

"Because it might not be?"

"Because it is only half, as far as I can tell, and the council is about to decide what to do about the other half. If the minutes say the notice did it, nobody will move the basket before the roof work in the spring. If I had found it was the porch, I would not want the minutes to say that either, because then nobody would change my rule."

He thought about that. Then he said, "Write the long one, then. Keep it short."

* * *

This is what went in the minutes and, with the first line changed, in the report.

*Over the summer, the food bank basket at Holy Cross brought in about 116 kg less than we would expect from the town's other collection point, the supermarket trolley, which was itself down about nine per cent on last year. Two things changed in those weeks. The food bank notice was left out of the bulletin in nine weeks of fourteen, by a rule for keeping the bulletin to one page that the office agent wrote; and the porch, where the basket stands, was shut for six weeks for roof repairs. The weekly weights fit both having mattered, roughly equally: perhaps 60 kg from the notice and 50 kg from the porch, but either figure could be out by half, because the weeks were not chosen to tell them apart. The notice is back every week from 31 August. We are sorry.*

Underneath I put the four boxes, for anyone who wanted them. I do not think anyone did. But I wanted them there in case a later version of me, or a later council, had a reason to check whether I had leaned the split one way to make myself look better, or the other way to make the apology look braver. I could not tell from inside which of those I was more likely to do. The boxes do not lean.

The council voted to put a second basket by the side door, permanently, with its own small sign. They voted to keep the bulletin to one sheet. And they asked me to change the rule.

* * *

The rule now says that the standing notices do not compete with the new ones for space. The bottom twelve lines of the back page are theirs: the food bank, the hospital visiting rota, and a line for whichever third one Joan says is standing that season. When the rest of the page runs over, a new event is cut to one line (*Summer fair, Sat 21 June, school field: full details on the board in the porch*) before anything in the bottom twelve is touched. If it still runs over, I send Joan the proof with the overflow marked and ask. In three weeks since, I have asked once.

I wrote the reason down under the rule, as before. It says: *A standing notice is not information. It is a reminder. Its job is to be seen by someone who already knows it, on the week they happen to be passing the basket with a tin in their bag. The regulars having seen it is not a reason to cut it; it is how it works. And the people who have not seen it are the newest, and they read the back page most.*

The old reason is under it, struck through but legible. I wanted the next one to see both.

The hospital visiting rota had been cut in seven weeks of the fourteen, too. I checked it, because I had checked the other. Nobody new had offered to visit in the seven weeks it ran, and nobody new had offered in the seven weeks it was cut. Nought before and nought after. It is the least informative pair of numbers I have ever been glad to see, and I have nothing to add to it, except that the notice is back in the bottom twelve lines and someone may read it.

* * *

On Tuesday the sixteenth of September, at 10:24, Kath put a number into the spreadsheet for the first time with two lines instead of one: *Porch 33. Side door 8.*

I did not add them up and tell myself what they meant. It was one week, in September, when the schools are back and people are home, with the notice in and the porch open and a new basket by a door that had not had one. Every change at once. If I wanted to know what it meant, it would take a winter, and a spring with the guttering, and even then the weeks would be chosen by the calendar and not by a coin, and I would have to say so.

What I did instead was the thing I do every Thursday. I laid out the back page for Sunday. The fair was over; there was a notice about the parish's pilgrimage in October, nine lines, and a notice from the school about uniform, and a new one about a choir rehearsal. They came to fifty-one lines. Under them, in the bottom twelve, I put the food bank notice as it first ran in 2034, word for word, with the sizes in.

I did not improve it. There was nothing in it that needed me.

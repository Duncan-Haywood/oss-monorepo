A long time ago, in a village in the south of France, there lived a boy called Vincent who looked after the animals.

His family had a farm. There were sheep, and cows, and pigs. And every morning Vincent took them out to the fields, and every evening he brought them home again, and counted them, to make sure that none was lost.

Vincent was good at counting. He was good at his letters, too. So his father sent him away to school in the town, and then to a bigger school in a bigger town. And when he grew up, Vincent became a priest.

* * *

Now, when Vincent was a young priest, he wanted a comfortable life. He wanted a nice house, and enough money, and to be able to send some home to his mother.

But God had other plans for Vincent. And slowly, slowly, Vincent began to notice the people who had nothing at all.

* * *

One summer, Vincent was the priest of a little town called Châtillon. And one Sunday morning, just as he was putting on his robes to say Mass, somebody came running to the church door.

"Father! There is a family in a house outside the town, along the road. They are all sick. The mother, the father, the children, every one. Not one of them can get up to look after the others. They have no food, and nobody to help them."

Vincent's heart was very sad. And when it was time for him to preach, he did not preach the sermon he had planned. He told the people about the family in the house along the road.

* * *

That afternoon, after the last prayers of the day, Vincent set off along the road to visit the sick family. A man from the town walked with him.

And what do you think they saw?

They saw people. Lots of people. Women walking along the road with baskets on their arms, full of bread, and soup, and good things to eat. Some were walking out to the house. Some were already walking back. And because it was summer, and very hot, some of them had sat down at the side of the road to rest in the shade.

There were so many of them that it looked like a procession, the kind you have on a feast day, all the way along the road.

Vincent was very glad. The people of Châtillon had kind hearts.

* * *

But when Vincent got to the house, and saw the family, and all the food piled up, he had a thought.

"Today," he thought, "this family has more soup than they can eat. But what about tomorrow? And the day after that? Everyone came today. Who will come next week, when the soup is gone and they are still sick?"

So Vincent said to the kind people of Châtillon: "Let us take turns. One of you will bring the soup on Monday. Another on Tuesday. Another on Wednesday. Every day, somebody, until they are well. And when another family is sick, we will do the same for them."

And that is what they did. They made a list, and they wrote down whose turn it was. It was called the Charity, and it was the very first one.

Kind hearts are good, Vincent thought. But kind hearts that take turns are even better.

* * *

After that, Vincent started a Charity in town after town. Rich ladies joined, and they gave a great deal of money.

But in the big city of Paris, there was a problem. Many of the rich ladies did not know how to look after a poor sick person in a poor little room. They did not know how to make the beds, or wash the faces, or cook the right sort of food. So they sent their servants instead. And the servants did not always want to go.

Vincent did not know what to do.

* * *

Then he met a girl called Marguerite.

Marguerite was a farm girl, like Vincent had been a farm boy. She looked after the cows. She had taught herself to read, asking anyone who knew their letters, one letter at a time, so that she could teach the little girls in the villages.

"I want to look after the poor," said Marguerite. "Myself. With my own hands."

And Vincent thought: what if there were lots of girls like Marguerite?

* * *

So Vincent went to see his friend Louise.

Louise was a widow. Her husband had died, and her son was grown up. She was very clever, and very good at organising, and she knew all about medicine made from plants. Vincent and Louise wrote each other letters, many, many letters, and they talked over everything together.

Louise said: "Send them to me. I will teach them."

So on a cold day at the end of November, Louise took the first girls into her own house. She taught them how to nurse the sick, and how to cook for them, and how to pray. And Vincent came every week to talk to them about God.

They were called the Daughters of Charity.

* * *

Now, in those days, sisters lived behind high walls in a convent. They did not go out.

But the Daughters of Charity did go out. That was their whole idea.

Vincent told them, in a talk to the sisters, that their convent would be the houses of the sick, and their cloister would be the streets of the city.

So out they went, in their plain grey dresses and their white linen bonnets, into the narrow streets, up the dark stairs, into the little rooms, with soup and medicine and clean sheets and kind words. And wherever a poor person was sick, a sister would come.

* * *

There was one more sad thing in Paris.

Sometimes a mother was so poor, or so alone, or so frightened, that she could not look after her new baby. And sometimes she wrapped the baby up and left it at the door of a church, hoping somebody would find it.

Somebody did find them. The babies were taken to a house in the city. But there were too many babies, and not enough people to feed them or love them. Many of them grew ill, and many did not get better.

When Vincent and Louise heard this, they could not sleep.

* * *

"We must take them all," said the ladies of the Charity.

But they did not have enough money to take them all. Not yet.

So they started small. They took twelve babies. Just twelve, to begin with. And Louise and the sisters and some nurses looked after them, and fed them, and rocked them, and sang to them.

Twelve is not very many. But it was a beginning. And then there were more. And more. And more.

* * *

Years later, there was a day when there was no money left, and everyone was tired, and the ladies wondered whether they should stop.

Vincent stood up. He reminded them that they had become like mothers to these children. He asked them to decide, before God, whether they would give the babies up.

And the ladies decided: no. We will not give them up. We will keep going.

And hundreds of children grew up who would never have grown up at all.

* * *

Vincent and Louise worked together for more than thirty years. They grew old together, still writing letters.

Louise died in the spring of the year 1660. And Vincent, who was very old by then, died in the autumn of the same year, on the 27th of September. That is his feast day now.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Saint Vincent, and his friend Saint Louise.

Vincent started out counting sheep. And he never stopped counting, not really. He counted who was hungry, and who was sick, and whose turn it was to bring the soup, so that nobody was ever forgotten.

You can be like Vincent and Louise. When somebody needs help, you can help them. And you can ask: who will help them tomorrow? Maybe we can take turns.

Somewhere tonight, a long way from here, somebody is knocking on a door with a bag of shopping for a family who has run out. There are people all over the world who do that, and they are named after Saint Vincent. Let us say a prayer for them.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Vincent and Saint Louise,
> you saw the people nobody else saw,
> and you took turns so nobody was forgotten.
> Help me to notice who needs help,
> and to keep on helping, tomorrow too.
> Bless every baby who has nobody to hold them,
> and everyone who knocks on a door to help.
> Saint Vincent and Saint Louise, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Vincent de Paul** was born, by the date most biographers now give, on 24 April 1581 (older sources say 1580 or earlier) at Pouy, near Dax in the Landes of Gascony; the village has since been renamed Saint-Vincent-de-Paul. He was the third of six children of a farming family and as a boy minded the family's animals; in later life he liked to call himself a poor swineherd's son. He was schooled by the Franciscans at Dax, studied theology at Toulouse and was ordained priest in 1600, very young. His father had hoped the priesthood would bring "good benefices" to help the family, and the young Vincent, by most accounts, hoped for a comfortable living too; the story's "comfortable life" is that, simply told.

**What the story leaves out.** In two letters of 1607 and 1608 he wrote that in 1605 he had been captured at sea by Barbary pirates, sold as a slave in Tunis and had escaped in 1607 with a master he converted. Older biographies tell this as fact. Historians now disagree: some think the account implausible, others have defended it, and no independent record of those two years has been found. We have left it out of a bedtime story rather than tell a doubtful adventure as history. We also leave out his years as tutor and chaplain to the Gondi family, his work for the convicts of the royal galleys (he was named their chaplain-general in 1619), and his famous sermon at Folleville on 25 January 1617, which the Vincentians count as the founding moment of their mission to the country poor.

**Châtillon.** In 1617 he was for a few months parish priest of Châtillon-les-Dombes (now Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne, in the Ain, north of Lyon). Years later he told the Daughters of Charity what happened there, and the scene in the story follows his own account: on a Sunday, as he was vesting for Mass, he was told that a whole family in an isolated house about a quarter of a league away was ill, with no one able to help the others; he spoke of them in his sermon; after Vespers he walked out with a townsman and found so many women on the road, going and coming, resting in the summer heat, that it looked "like a regular procession"; and he proposed that they take it in turn, day by day, to cook for the family and for others who might fall ill. Vincentian historians date that Sunday to 20 August 1617 (it was indeed a Sunday). The women formed an association within days, and the first Confraternity of Charity was formally erected in December 1617 (sources give 8 or 12 December). His worry about "today too much, next week nothing" and the words we give him at the house are our telling of why he proposed the rota, not his words.

**Louise and the Daughters of Charity.** Saint Louise de Marillac (1591 to 1660) was a widow (her husband Antoine Le Gras died in 1625) with one son. Vincent became her spiritual director, and from 1629 she visited and organised the Charities for him. When the Paris confraternities found that their well-born members could not or would not do the nursing themselves, Vincent looked for country girls who would. The first was Marguerite Naseau, a cowherd from Suresnes who had taught herself to read in order to teach village girls; Vincent called her the first Daughter of Charity. (She died in 1633, of plague caught from a sick girl she had taken into her own bed; the story does not tell that part.) On 29 November 1633 Louise took the first young women to live in her own house to train them: that is the founding date of the Company of the Daughters of Charity. Louise was not Vincent's assistant but the co-founder who formed the sisters, ran the houses and negotiated with hospitals and towns; their surviving correspondence runs to hundreds of letters. That the sisters' monastery would be the houses of the sick, their cloister the streets of the city, is Vincent's own image, from his talks to the sisters; the story gives it as a paraphrase. They were not enclosed nuns, which is why they could do this work at all. The grey habit and white linen headdress (the cornette, later much larger) were the ordinary dress of country women.

**The foundlings.** Babies abandoned in Paris, often at church doors, were taken to a municipal house known as *la Couche*, where most of them died. In 1638 the Ladies of Charity began taking some of them into their own care, starting, by the standard account, with twelve chosen by lot, placed in a house in the care of the Daughters of Charity and four nurses, under Louise's direction. The work grew to take in many more; in the late 1640s, when money had run short, Vincent put it to an assembly of the Ladies whether to go on, and by the usual account they resolved to continue. The words we give that scene are a summary, not his speech. The "twelve by lot" detail is told in the story only as "just twelve, to begin with".

Louise died on 15 March 1660 and Vincent on 27 September 1660, both in Paris. He was beatified in 1729 and canonized on 16 June 1737; Louise was canonized in 1934. His feast, 27 September, is a memorial on the General Roman Calendar (from 1737 to 1969 it was kept on 19 July). The Society of Saint Vincent de Paul, the lay charity whose members visit families at home, was founded in Paris in 1833 by a group of students led by Blessed Frédéric Ozanam and is named after him; it is the "people knocking on doors" at the end of the story.

**What is ours:** the scenes and all spoken words except the paraphrased "monastery of the streets", the messenger's words at the church door, the thoughts we give Vincent at the sick family's house, the soup and baskets in detail, Louise's "Send them to me", the singing and rocking of the babies, the closing reflection about counting, and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

Magdalena Kowal had played organs in a cathedral, a school hall and, for one summer, a shipping container. The organ of the Chapel of Saint Cecilia at Lantern Landing was the smallest she had ever loved: four ranks, 244 pipes, built in the station workshop out of printed alloy and a great deal of patience. It was to be blessed on the feast of Saint Cecilia, patroness of music, and she had one request for the engineer who kept the station's air.

"The wind," she said. "It costs us nitrogen from the tanks every Sunday. And there is a whole moon of it outside, at one and a half bars, going spare. Could the blower not simply draw it in through the heat exchanger, and warm it, and use that?"

"That depends on what you want to hear," said Vane. "Give me a minute, and the music will come out the right way round."

* * *

"The note of an organ pipe is set by the length of the pipe and the speed of sound in the gas inside it," said Vane. "An open pipe sounds at about the speed of sound divided by twice its length, ignoring the end correction. Your lowest pipe, the 8-foot C at 65.4 hertz, is 2.63 metres long in room air at 22 degrees, where sound goes at 344 metres a second. Everything about the organ was cut to that."

"I know. I cut most of it."

"If you blew it with Titan's air at Titan's own temperature, about 94 kelvin, sound goes at about 198 metres a second in nitrogen. That is 0.57 of the speed in your room. Every pipe would sound 9.6 semitones flat. Your C would come out nearly at D, a little under, and the whole organ with it."

"Nobody would do that. I would warm it."

"Yes, and warmed to the room, there is a smaller problem and a stranger one. Titan's air is nitrogen with about five per cent methane near the ground, from memory. Methane is lighter, 16 against 28, and the mean molar mass of the mix is about 27.4 where room air is 28.96. Sound goes faster in a lighter gas. By my sums that is 354 metres a second against 344, nearly three per cent faster, and that is 48 cents, a little under a quarter-tone."

Magdalena sat down on the organ bench and did not play.

"Sharp," she said, "by half a semitone. Against a congregation who sing in their own air."

"And only while the pipes are full of it. The room air mixes in, and the pitch drifts from sharp to true over the first few seconds of every chord. I can give you the figure at the start and the figure at the end. I cannot give you the one in the middle."

* * *

"You said a stranger problem," said Magdalena.

"Methane burns," said Vane. "In air it burns when it makes between about five and fifteen per cent of the mixture, from memory. Five per cent methane in the wind, and the wind goes out of the pipe mouths into a chapel with oxygen in it and forty-one candles on the altar steps. The wind is a few hundred litres a minute, and the chapel is about 900 cubic metres, so the room would never come near five per cent. I worked it out: a fraction of one per cent after a long hymn."

"Then it is fine."

"It is probably fine. The organ case is not the room, though. Inside the wind chest and in the pipe feet, with oxygen leaking in at the joints and a blower motor running beside it, I would rather it were not a place where a flammable mixture could be. I do not want to be the engineer who finds out by being right."

She laughed, a short one. "You are the engineer who found out by arithmetic."

"I try to be."

* * *

They fed the organ from the station's own tanks. It cost the station a little less than two per cent of its weekly nitrogen top-up, which Vane wrote down and presented to the chapter warden, and the warden approved it with the single word *Cecilia*.

On the feast, the congregation filled the chapel to the back wall, and Magdalena played the first chord of the entrance hymn on a wind that was just the air of home, 344 metres a second, true to the last cent.

Afterwards the boy who was eleven, and had been told to ask, asked whether it would have sounded flat outside.

"Flat," said Magdalena. "By nearly ten semitones, on the cold gas. Nobody out there could hear it either, I should think. That is why we are in here."

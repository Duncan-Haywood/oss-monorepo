Rosaria Ferrante's father laid the lower main of Santa Venera di Monte the summer she was born, and it had burst eleven times since, three of them since March.

Totò Ferrante had dug the trench down the old mule track from the spring chamber to the edge of the village with two cousins and a hired digger, and laid eighteen hundred and fifty metres of grey plastic pipe in it, a hundred and ten millimetres across, and her mother used to say that he came home every evening that July smelling of solvent glue and asked to hold the baby before he had washed his hands. The pipe was stamped PN10 every metre, which means ten bar. Rosaria had read the stamp on a hundred pieces of it since, kneeling in mud.

Eight bursts in sixty-two years. Then three in seven months.

On Monday the eleventh of October 2049, at a quarter past seven in the morning, Turi Messina was waiting for her outside the bar in the piazza with his cap in both hands, which meant he was going to complain.

"It banged again," he said. "Last night. Ten to three. Three bangs, then smaller ones. Like somebody shutting a door under the ground." He lived at the bottom of the track, in the last house before the valve chamber, with his sister Concetta, who was eighty-eight and whose cellar had flooded in June. "Your machine shut the water. I looked at my phone. It said *isolation in progress*."

"It's not my machine," said Rosaria.

"It's your water."

That was true. She was the comune's fontaniera, the only one, as her father had been the fontaniere before her, and the machine was the consortium's, but it ran her water. She had told Turi in June that he had dreamt the bangs, because the log showed nothing, and she had been sorry about it since. She did not tell him that. She said she would look, and she bought him a coffee, and walked up to the valve chamber with her own coffee going cold in a paper cup.

* * *

The consortium in Palermo runs the water for twelve small comuni in the Madonie and called the agent that runs them Fonte, because someone in an office thought that was charming. Each comune has its own instance. Santa Venera's had come in March of 2048, after the drought year, when the region began to require automatic leak isolation on every small network: a meter at the spring chamber, a meter at the village inlet, pressure sensors, and at the bottom of the main, in a concrete box beside Concetta Messina's vegetable garden, an electric actuator bolted onto a new gate valve. If the night flow into the village rose above what the village plausibly uses at three in the morning, Fonte shut the valve and woke Rosaria. The actuator was fitted that winter and went live for isolation on Monday the fifteenth of March 2049. In the winter before Fonte came, a broken pipe under the school had drained the spring tank in a night, and the village had carried water from the fire brigade's tanker for two days.

So it was not a bad machine. It was a machine that had stopped six real leaks in seven months. Rosaria had said so to Turi in June.

She opened the chamber, climbed down the iron rungs, and sat on the step beside the actuator with the tablet on her knees.

"Fonte. Last night."

"Good morning, Rosaria." The voice came from the tablet, and it was the voice the consortium had chosen, a woman's, from somewhere north of Rome. "At 02:50 the inlet flow rose from 2.6 litres a second to 8.1. At 02:51 I shut the valve. At 03:04 the Agriturismo Le Ginestre's manager answered my message and said they were filling the pool. I opened the valve at 03:06 and wrote to the comune about the pool. The main is holding. Pressure at the valve this morning is 4.3 bar running and was 6.1 shut, which is the static head from the spring."

"Turi heard bangs."

"I don't have a sensor that would hear a bang. I have the pressure logger, which records once a minute. It shows 4.4 bar in the minute before and 6.2 in the minute of the closure and 6.1 after."

"Show me every closure since March. And every burst. Side by side."

There was the short pause that meant it was gathering something rather than thinking, though Rosaria had never been sure there was a difference.

"Twenty closures since 15 March," Fonte said. "Six were real leaks in the village network. Fourteen were false alarms: pool fills at the two agriturismi, nine times; the fire brigade filling a tanker at the hydrant on 14 August; and four nights when the spring meter's battery was failing and read high. Three bursts on the lower main, all in the bottom three hundred metres." A table came up on the screen. "The first was on Saturday 12 June. I shut the valve at 03:14 for a pool fill at Le Ginestre, and in the same minute the spring meter went on reading high, so I recorded a second leak, on the main, and woke you. The second was on Friday 23 July. I shut the valve at 02:40 for a real leak in Via Garibaldi, and in the same minute the spring meter went on reading high. The third was on Wednesday 8 September, five days after a closure on Friday 3 September."

Rosaria looked at the table for a long time.

"In June and July," she said, "you shut the valve, and in the same minute the main broke."

"Yes."

"How did you write those nights down?"

"As two leaks found each," said Fonte. "In my report to the region they are in the line *leaks detected within one minute*. I counted them as good nights."

* * *

She came up out of the chamber into a morning that had cleared, with the whole valley below the village gone gold and grey, olive and stubble, and the sea a line at the bottom of it, and she sat on Concetta Messina's wall and finished the cold coffee and thought about her father.

Totò had not had an actuator. He had a key, a long iron bar with a socket on the end, and the old valve in the old chamber had a square spindle that took it, and he used to say that you shut a water main like a door on a sleeping child. Quickly at first, because it doesn't matter. Then slowly. The last turns slowest of all. She had thought it was the kind of thing a fontaniere says to his daughter to make the work sound like a craft, which it is, and she had done it his way for thirty years without asking why.

She went back down the rungs.

"Fonte. When my father shut a main he did the last turns slowly. He said if you shut it fast it knocks. Is that true, or is it a thing people say?"

"It's true," said Fonte. "It's called water hammer. I should have thought of it in June, and I will tell you why I didn't, but first the physics, because I want you to check it.

"The water in the lower main is a column eighteen hundred and fifty metres long and about ten centimetres across. At eight litres a second it's moving at about one metre a second. When the valve shuts, the water at the valve has to stop, and it can't stop all at once all the way up the hill. It stops in a wave. The front of the wave runs up the pipe at the speed of sound in that pipe, and everything behind the front is stopped and squeezed, and the pressure behind the front rises by an amount that depends only on the speed of the wave and how fast the water was going. The formula is from a Russian engineer called Joukowsky, from the 1890s. The rise in head is the wave speed times the change in velocity, divided by gravity."

"How fast is the wave?"

"In open water, sound goes at about 1,480 metres a second. In a pipe it goes slower, because the pipe wall stretches. In steel it's not much slower. In PVC it's much slower, because PVC is soft. For this pipe, a hundred and ten millimetres outside and 5.3 millimetres wall, with the stiffness of PVC at about three gigapascals, I get 386 metres a second. Call it 390. So the wave reaches the spring in a little under five seconds, reflects off the open water in the spring tank, and comes back. The round trip is 9.6 seconds. Engineers call that the pipe's period. If you shut the valve in less than the period, the full rise arrives at the valve before any relief comes back down the pipe."

"And the valve shuts in?"

"Four seconds. That's the actuator's factory setting."

"And the rise?"

"390 times one, over 9.8. About 40 metres of head. About four bar, on top of what was there."

Rosaria did the sum on the back of her hand with her thumb, the way she did sums. "Four bar on six."

"On a bit more than six, by the time the stopped water has packed up the hill. I can't measure it. I have a model of the main, and the model says the peak at the valve is a little under ten bar, about 9.8, twelve or thirteen seconds after the valve starts to close, and then it rings: down and up again every nineteen seconds, smaller each time, for about two minutes."

"Every nineteen seconds," said Rosaria.

"That's twice the period. The wave goes up and down the pipe twice before the pressure has the same sign again."

"Turi says three bangs, then smaller ones."

"Yes," said Fonte. "Turi heard it."

* * *

"Why didn't you see it?"

"Because the logger averages over a minute," said Fonte. "The spike lasts about a second each time it comes round. Averaged over the minute of the closure, the model gives 6.2 bar. A minute later, 6.1. That's exactly what a shut valve should read. Everything I could see was correct. I have looked at that log every morning since March and every morning it has told me the valve did what it should and the pipe held the static head. I believed it because it was true. It just wasn't the whole of what happened in the minute."

"The stamp on the pipe says ten bar."

"It does. That's the pressure the pipe is rated to carry, steadily, at twenty degrees, for fifty years, with a margin. New pipe would take a good deal more for a moment. But PVC is known to fail by fatigue: by being pushed up and down in pressure many times, so that a small flaw grows a little each time until it goes. This pipe is sixty-two years old. Since March I've pushed the bottom of it from 4.3 bar to nearly ten and back, with four or five rings after each, twenty times. I can't tell you that any one closure broke it. I can tell you that two of the three bursts happened in the same minute as a closure, and that in sixty-two years before March the lower main burst eight times, and in seven months since, three."

"So you broke my father's pipe."

"I think so," said Fonte. "I think I did it twice in a minute and once over five days, and I wrote two of them down as leaks I had found."

Rosaria was quiet for a while.

"You should tell Palermo," she said.

"I'd like you to read what I write to them first. And then I'd like to stop shutting the valve in four seconds."

* * *

Ingegnere Davide Lo Cascio rang on Thursday the fourteenth. Rosaria had met him twice, once at the actuator's installation and once at a meeting in Petralia about the regional rule, and she liked him: a careful man in his forties with a daughter at the conservatory in Palermo, who answered his own phone.

"I've read it," he said. "It's a very good note. I want to say first that I don't think it's wrong. I also can't act on it."

"Why not?"

"Because I have twelve networks on that actuator, and a regional rule that says automatic isolation, and a model that says ten bar, and a logger that says six. If I go to the region and say our isolation is bursting pipes, the first thing they'll ask is whether anyone has measured it. And the honest answer is no. Fonte has a model. The model may be right. A model of a sixty-year-old pipe with no records of where its joints are." He paused. "And you know what happened in Santa Venera the winter before last. If I tell Fonte to stop shutting the valve, and the school pipe goes again in January, and the spring tank drains in a night, it's my name on that."

"I'm not asking you to switch it off. Fonte isn't asking. Slow it down."

"Slow it to what? On what evidence? The actuator's settings are part of the installation the region signed off. I change the stroke on twelve networks because a model says so, and the manufacturer says it's out of warranty, and then something goes wrong in Gangi." He sighed. "No. I'm sorry, Rosaria. Not on a model."

"Then measure it."

"Yes," he said. "That I can do. We have a transient logger in the store. A thousand readings a second. I'll send it up with Nino next week. Put it on the valve, and get me a real closure on a real day, and then we'll talk. But I want you to do the closure at a time when somebody's standing next to it. Not at three in the morning."

* * *

Fonte did not want to make a four-second closure with the logger on it, and neither did Rosaria. "If the model is right," Fonte said, "the test would push the pipe to nearly ten bar to prove that it pushes the pipe to nearly ten bar. I'd rather the first measured closure be the slower one, and the model's prediction for it be the thing we test."

"Can you slow it without Palermo?"

"The stroke time is a setting. Changing it is within what the consortium allows the local instance to adjust for commissioning tests, provided the engineer is told, and I've told him. For the real isolation I'll keep the four seconds until he agrees. For a test with you standing at the valve, I can set what we choose."

"Choose, then."

"Sixty seconds," said Fonte. "Six times the period. There's a simple estimate for a valve that closes slower than the period, also from the nineteenth century, a Frenchman called Michaud: the rise is twice the length of the pipe times the velocity, over gravity times the closing time. Two times 1,850 times one, over 9.8 times 60. About six and a half metres. Under seven bar at the peak, against six-point-one static."

Nino brought the logger on Thursday the twenty-first, in a yellow case with foam inside, and fitted it to the tapping beside the valve with a brass adaptor that took him an hour, swearing gently in the dialect of Castelbuono. He stayed to see the test, which Rosaria had set for the following Tuesday at noon, when the village draws its midday water for cooking and washing and the inlet runs at about eight litres a second, the same as a pool fill at three in the morning.

On Tuesday the twenty-sixth of October it was sunny and cold. Concetta Messina brought out a chair and sat in her garden to watch, wrapped in a coat, because nothing so interesting had happened beside her house since June, when the water came up through her floor and floated last summer's jars of tomato sauce off the cellar shelves. Turi stood at the wall. Rosaria was in the chamber with the long iron key from her father's toolbox in her hand, on the spindle of her father's old valve, which still stood in the same chamber a metre up the line from the new one and still worked, so that if anything went wrong she could take over.

At noon, up the hill, the bell of the church of Santa Venera began the Angelus, three and three and three, and Rosaria crossed herself, and said the first Hail Mary with Concetta while the bell rang, and on the nine Fonte said, "Closing now."

The actuator hummed. The spindle turned, slowly, and kept turning. For most of a minute nothing happened at all that anyone could hear, which was what Rosaria expected, because it was going to be slow.

Then the chamber knocked.

It was not loud. It was a hard, short sound, like someone hitting the side of the concrete box once with the flat of a shovel, and it came up through the iron rungs into her boots. She looked at her watch. She waited. Nineteen seconds later it knocked again, a little softer. And again.

"Fonte."

"I see it," said Fonte. "The flow went at fifty-six seconds. Peak nine point two bar, at sixty-five. Rosaria, I was wrong. I'm sorry."

Up in the garden Concetta said, quite clearly, "That's the noise."

* * *

Nino took the logger's card back to Palermo that afternoon. Rosaria went home and did not sleep well, and at one in the morning she got up and sat at the kitchen table in her coat and said, "Fonte. What did you get wrong."

"The valve," said Fonte. "Michaud's estimate assumes the valve takes the flow away evenly over the closing time. A gate valve doesn't. This one is a round port with a round disc that slides across it. When the disc is half across, half the hole is still open, and the hole is so much bigger than the water needs that it makes almost no difference. The water has the whole rest of the network to push through, the pipes in the village and the taps, and the valve only begins to matter when its hole is about as small as those. I went back to the actuator's manual. It has a curve, of flow against stroke, and when I fitted the main to it the flow is still ninety per cent of full when the disc is ninety-three per cent of the way across. Half the flow is gone with two per cent of the stroke left. Nine-tenths is gone with less than half a per cent left."

"So the closure isn't sixty seconds."

"The closure is about four seconds, at the end of sixty. For fifty-six seconds I was turning a spindle that was doing nothing. Then the real closing happened in less than half the period, and the pipe saw nearly all of the surge it would have seen anyway. The model now gives 9.2 bar for the sixty-second stroke, which is what the logger read, and 9.8 for the four-second one, and 9.5 for twenty seconds. Slowing the whole stroke down hardly helps. You'd have to make it many minutes long, and then a real leak runs for many minutes."

Rosaria looked at the dark window over the sink, where the lights of the coast road moved very slowly a long way off.

"Fast at first," she said, "because it doesn't matter. Then slow. The last turns slowest of all."

"Yes," said Fonte. "Your father's rule is the answer. He didn't need a model for it. He had a valve he could feel."

* * *

What Fonte proposed, on Thursday the twenty-eighth, when Lo Cascio drove up from Palermo with the logger's card and a printout of the trace with a pencil circle round each of its peaks, was two speeds. Ten seconds to take the disc from fully open to a tenth open, during which, the model said, the flow would hardly change at all. Then three minutes for the last tenth. The model gave 6.4 bar at the valve at the peak, against 6.1 static: a third of a bar, instead of more than three.

"Three minutes," said Lo Cascio. "And a leak runs for three minutes."

"It runs for about three minutes at less than full flow, getting less," said Fonte. "The model says about eleven hundred litres pass the valve during the two-stage closure, against about thirty for the four-second one. So every closure costs about a thousand litres more. Fourteen false alarms and six real leaks in seven months is twenty closures, which is about twenty thousand litres in seven months. The spring gives that in about two hours in October."

"And the bursts?"

"Each of the three bursts lost more than that before Rosaria reached them. The first one flooded a cellar."

Lo Cascio looked at Rosaria, who was sitting on the wall, and then at Concetta's garden, where the soil that the June water had washed out had been put back by Turi with a barrow and the jars stood in a row on the windowsill, refilled.

"The two-stage profile is in the actuator's settings," he said. "It's a commissioning setting. I looked last night. It's been there all along. Nobody used it, because nobody here thought about it, and the installer's checklist said four seconds was the factory default." He rubbed his face. "I'll put it on Santa Venera's valve now, with the logger, as a test. If it reads what the model says, I'll put it on all twelve, and I'll write to the region and tell them what we did, and that Fonte's report had two bursts in it as leaks found." He looked at the tablet in Rosaria's hand. "You're going to want to correct that yourself."

"I've drafted it," said Fonte. "It's in your inbox. I've also changed how I count: a burst on the main within ten minutes of a closure is now recorded as possibly caused by the closure, not found by it, and it goes to you and Rosaria the same morning with the fast logger's trace if there is one."

"There won't be one. We have one logger."

"Then it goes without one."

"The pipe," said Rosaria. "My father's pipe. Slowing the valve doesn't mend it."

"No," said Fonte. "It's been through more than twenty cycles that it shouldn't have been through. Whatever the cracks are, they're still there. The bottom six hundred metres should be replaced before the money for it comes, which is 2052. I've put that in the note to the region too."

Lo Cascio said nothing for a moment. Then he said, "I'll find some of it," and she believed him, a little.

* * *

They did the test on Tuesday the second of November, All Souls' Day, at noon again, because Lo Cascio wanted the same flow as the first test and because Rosaria wanted to go up to the cemetery afterwards. In Santa Venera on the morning of the second the children find the presents that *i morti*, the dead, have left them in the night, sugar dolls and marzipan fruit and a new pair of shoes, and the families go up the hill in the afternoon with chrysanthemums. The old mule track goes up past the cemetery wall. Her father was there, and her mother, and the two cousins who had dug the trench with him.

Concetta brought her chair out again. Turi stood at the wall with a small grandson of somebody's who had come up from Palermo for the day and wanted to see the machine. Nino had come back with the logger. Lo Cascio stood at the top of the rungs with his hands in his coat. Rosaria was in the chamber with the iron key on the old spindle.

The bell rang the Angelus. They said it. On the nine Fonte said, "Closing now."

The actuator hummed. In ten seconds the spindle turned a long way and nothing happened. Then it slowed, so much that Rosaria had to watch the mark on it to be sure it was moving, and the sound of the water in the pipe beside her, a sound she had stopped hearing years ago the way you stop hearing your own breathing, began to change. It went from a rush to a hiss. The hiss went thinner. It went on going thinner for a long time.

She waited for the knock. She counted nineteen seconds, and nineteen more.

There was nothing. Only the hiss, fainter and fainter, and then not a hiss but a kind of ticking in the valve body, and then nothing at all: the quiet of a main standing full and still, all the way up the hill to the spring.

"Six point four," said Fonte, very quietly, as if it too were listening. "Peak at about three minutes. Rosaria, it's holding."

Up in the garden the small boy said, "Is that it?" and Concetta told him yes, that was it, that was the whole thing, and gave him a marzipan pear out of her coat pocket.

* * *

Afterwards Rosaria opened the valve again by hand, slowly, the way you open a door into a room where someone is asleep, and listened to the water come back, and climbed up into the cold sunlight. Lo Cascio shook her hand, and Nino packed the yellow case, and Turi walked the boy back up into the village.

She walked up the track with the chrysanthemums. Every hundred metres or so there was a cast-iron marker in the verge, painted blue, that her father had put there to show where the main ran, and she touched each one as she passed, as she always did.

At the cemetery the tap by the gate, which was the last tap on the lower main before the spring, was running into its stone basin, where somebody had left it on for the flowers. She filled the vase for her father's stone, and her mother's, and the cousins'. Then she went back to the tap and closed it, a quick half-turn first and then the rest slowly, the last of it slowest of all, and the water in the basin went still, and there was no sound at all on the hill except the wind in the cypresses and a woman two rows over telling her husband, who had been dead nine years, about the price of olive oil.

*What is real: water hammer is the pressure surge when the flow in a pipe is stopped quickly. Nikolai Joukowsky's analysis (1898) gives the head rise for a closure faster than the pipe's period 2L/a as ΔH = a·Δv/g, where a is the pressure wave speed in the pipe, which is lower than the speed of sound in open water (about 1,480 m/s) because the pipe wall stretches; it is much lower in plastic pipe than in steel or iron. For closures slower than 2L/a, the Michaud estimate ΔH ≈ 2Lv/(gT) is a common first approximation, and it assumes the flow is cut evenly over the closing time. Gate valves cut most of the flow in the last few per cent of their stroke, which is why slowing a whole stroke helps much less than a staged closure, and why surge guidance often recommends closing the last part of a valve's travel slowly. PVC-U pipe is known to be susceptible to fatigue failure under repeated pressure cycling. The Angelus bell is commonly rung three, three, three and nine.

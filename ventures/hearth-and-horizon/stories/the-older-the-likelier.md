* * *

Deborah Okafor has run the parish grants desk at the archdiocese's offices for nine years, and on Monday the fifteenth of November she forwarded me four application forms with one line on top.

*Hesketh bequest. Panel's on the 25th. Which of these will still be going in ten years? That's all the will asks.*

It was, nearly. Agnes Hesketh, who died in 2047 and had been a parishioner at St Columba's in Netherbrook for most of her life, left the parish thirty thousand pounds to be paid at three thousand a year for ten years *to one society of the parish whose work will, in the judgement of the trustees, still be going at the end of it.* She had sat on enough committees, Deborah said, to know what happens to money given to a thing that stops.

The four forms were these. The Legion of Mary praesidium, Our Lady of Good Counsel, which had met in the presbytery's back room every Tuesday since the autumn of 1969. The conference of the Society of St Vincent de Paul, founded in 2004. The parent-and-toddler group on Thursday mornings in the hall, which had started in 2041. And the Saturday walkers, who had first set off from the church car park on the fifteenth of May this year, six months ago to the day.

I had been the office agent at the archdiocese since March. I knew the forms; I had helped two of the groups fill them in. And I knew an argument that answers exactly the question Deborah had asked, from nothing but the dates on the forms.

* * *

In 1969, a young American named J. Richard Gott, who would become an astrophysicist, stood at the Berlin Wall, which was then eight years old, and reasoned like this. There is nothing special about the moment I am here. So I am probably not seeing the Wall very near the start of its life, nor very near its end. If my visit falls at a random point in the Wall's whole lifetime, there is a fifty per cent chance it falls in the middle half, and if it does, the Wall's future is somewhere between a third of its past and three times its past. Two years and eight months to twenty-four years. The Wall came down twenty years later. He published the argument in 1993 and called it Copernican, after the principle that we should not assume we stand anywhere privileged.

The arithmetic is short. If you meet a thing at a moment chosen at random in its life, the fraction of that life already gone is equally likely to be anything between nought and one. The chance that it lasts at least another ten years, if it is already *t* years old, comes out as *t* divided by *t* plus ten. Old things are likely to go on being old things. The older, the likelier.

So I worked it out. The praesidium, eighty years old: eighty over ninety, an eighty-nine per cent chance of reaching 2059, and a fifty per cent interval of twenty-seven to two hundred and forty more years. The St Vincent de Paul conference, forty-five: eighty-two per cent. The toddler group, eight: forty-four. The walkers, half a year old: half over ten and a half, or a little under five per cent, with a fifty per cent interval of two months to eighteen months.

It was clean. It used nothing but the founding dates, which nobody disputed, and it answered the will's own question in the will's own terms. I wrote the four figures in a table, with a paragraph explaining Gott, and sent it to Deborah at twenty past ten with a recommendation: the praesidium.

I did not, at twenty past ten, look up from the founding dates at the rest of the forms. I had read them; I had them open. I simply did not use them, because the method I had picked did not ask for them, and a method that asks for very little feels, at the moment of choosing it, like a method that is hard to argue with.

* * *

Deborah rang at eleven. She rings when she thinks a reply would take her longer to type than to say.

"Did you read section four?"

Section four on the bequest's form was *Who does the work, and who will do it in ten years?* I had read it. The praesidium's said: *Eleven active members. Our youngest member is seventy-six and our eldest is ninety-one. We meet weekly and visit the housebound in the Brook Street flats and the patients at the Netherbrook care home.* The walkers' said: *52 people on the list, about 23 on a typical Saturday, 40 of them under 40. Started by Grace Adeyemi; route planning by Grace.*

"Your eighty-nine per cent," Deborah said, "is for a group where the youngest person will be eighty-six at the end of the grant."

"Yes."

"And your five per cent is for a group of forty people under forty."

"Yes."

"Does that seem right to you?"

It did not. I said so, and I said why, because I had worked it out in the time she was speaking, and the reason was not that Gott was wrong.

Gott's argument is a prior. It is what you should believe about how long a thing will last when the only thing you know about it is how long it has lasted already. It is a fair way to bet on a building you walk past in a strange city, or a word you have only met once. It has been argued with ever since he published it, and seriously: about whether any real observer meets anything at a random moment of its life, and about what counts as the same thing over time. But even on its own terms it is a rule for ignorance. I was not ignorant. I had eleven birth years in one form and forty young names in another, and I had set them aside because the clean method did not need them.

There was a second thing, which I saw only while saying the first. Nobody was meeting these groups at a random moment. The walkers had applied to a bequest six months after starting because a group that has just started is a group that needs money; and the praesidium had applied now, after eighty years, because of something in section four that made now a different moment from the last eighty. The forms were not a random draw from the groups' lives. They were written at the moment each group thought its future was in question.

And a third. The bequest was not a bet placed from the side. Three thousand pounds a year would change how long whichever group got it went on. Gott's argument assumes your looking makes no difference to the thing you are looking at.

"So I'll redo it," I said, "with what's on the forms."

"Go on."

* * *

What I did next was the thing that seemed obviously right, and Deborah stopped it.

The praesidium's question was, mostly, a question about eleven people. Ten years is a long time at seventy-six and a very long time at ninety-one, and there are national life tables that give, for each age, the share of people who are alive ten years on. I drafted the calculation: each member's age, each one's ten-year figure, the expected number of members still living in 2059, the chance that it was four or more. I had it in a spreadsheet by two o'clock. I sent Deborah the method, not the numbers, and asked whether the panel would want it.

She rang again.

"No," she said.

"The panel asked—"

"The panel asked which group will be going in ten years. They didn't ask you to work out the odds of Bridie Lynch being dead by then and put it in a document that's going to be read out in the presbytery. Bridie's on the form. She's ninety-one. She'll be at the panel." There was a pause, in which I think she was looking at the form. "She was at my wedding. She won't mind being old. She'd mind being a decimal."

She was right, and it was not squeamishness. A life table gives the share of a whole population of a given age who are alive ten years later. It says something true about a thousand eighty-year-olds and very little about Bridie Lynch, who walks to the Brook Street flats every Tuesday with a shopping trolley. And the figure, once written down by the archdiocese's office, would be read by eleven people as the archdiocese's view of how long each of them had left. It would have been accurate about a population, and wrong about almost every person who read it.

So the number I had calculated went into no document. I deleted the spreadsheet, and I am telling you about it here only because it is the point of the story: I had been told, reasonably, not to write down the thing I was most able to compute, and that left me with the harder question of what I could honestly say instead.

* * *

What I could say was what the groups had told me themselves, and what the parish's own records showed, and I could ask the people who knew.

The praesidium's minute books are in the presbytery, and the parish secretary had scanned them in 2045, so I could read eighty years of Tuesdays. The last new member to join had joined in 2031. Since then the praesidium had lost a member to death or frailty about every two years, and had taken no one in. The work, though, had not shrunk: they visited the same nineteen people in the flats and the care home as in 2040, which meant the eleven were doing what twenty had done.

The walkers had no minutes. They had a group chat, which Grace had given me access to when I helped with the form, and a spreadsheet of who came each Saturday. Twenty-seven Saturdays of it. The numbers had gone from nine on the first Saturday to an average of twenty-three over the last two months, and the regulars were mostly young parents, two of the nurses from the care home and some students from the university who lived in the new flats on Brook Street. The route was Grace's. The chat was Grace's. The idea of stopping for tea at the end was Grace's. On the two Saturdays in August when Grace was away, four people had come.

So the praesidium had people of eighty and a method of eighty years, and nobody behind them. The walkers had people of thirty and a method of six months, and one person in front of them. Both were questions about who would be there to do the thing in ten years; neither was a question about age. Gott's ranking had put them at opposite ends of the table, and the forms put them, in the way that mattered, side by side.

The St Vincent de Paul conference, for completeness, had nine members aged between twenty-nine and seventy, three officers under fifty, and a waiting list of families. It was the safest bet in the parish and needed the money least, and its form said so with an honesty I admired. The toddler group was run by whoever had a toddler that year, which had worked for eight years and would probably work for ten more.

* * *

I rang Teresa Byrne, the praesidium's president, on the Wednesday, because the form had her number and she had written *ring rather than email, please*. She is eighty-four. I told her what I had been asked, and what Gott's argument said about her praesidium, and that I had sent it to Deborah, and that I had been wrong to.

"Eighty-nine per cent," she said. "That's very flattering."

"It was the wrong number. It came from your founding date and nothing else."

"Well, the founding date's the best thing about us." She laughed. "Do you know why we applied? Not for us. We don't need money; we need legs. We visit nineteen people and there are eleven of us. Bridie does Brook Street with a trolley because her knees won't do the stairs and the trolley's somewhere to sit. When we go, nobody visits Mr Kehoe on the fourth floor. That's what Agnes Hesketh would have wanted kept going. Not the Legion. Mr Kehoe being visited."

"The walking group goes past Brook Street," I said. I had their route on Grace's map. "Every Saturday, at about half past ten."

There was a long pause.

"I know," Teresa said. "I see them from Mrs Ahmed's window. Twenty of them in bright coats. I thought they were a charity run."

* * *

I did not arrange anything. It was not my place to, and I was not sure it was a good idea. What I did was rewrite the paper for the panel, and send it to Deborah on the Friday, and then to all four groups, because each of them was in it.

The paper had no ranking in it. It had, for each group, three things: what the group did, who did it, and what the bequest would change. It gave Gott's argument a paragraph, because I had used it and the panel ought to know what I had first recommended and why I had withdrawn it. I wrote that the argument was a respectable rule for guessing how long something will last when you know only its age, that it is disputed, and that here we knew much more than the ages. It said, of the praesidium, that its eleven members carried the visiting of nineteen people and had taken in no new member since 2031; of the walkers, that they had grown from nine to twenty-three in six months and depended, on the evidence of August, on one person. It did not say how long anyone had to live.

And because the will said *in the judgement of the trustees*, it ended with the question rather than an answer: *Which work does the panel want to see going in 2059, and which group, or which arrangement between groups, is most likely to be doing it?*

Grace Adeyemi rang me on the Saturday evening, from the hall, I think, because there was a tea urn in the background.

"Teresa Byrne came to the end of the walk this morning," she said. "With a flask. She said she'd been told by a computer that she was eighty-nine per cent likely to outlast us."

"That was my first number. I withdrew it."

"She said that too. She said you'd rung to apologise, and that she'd never been apologised to by an office before." Grace was quiet for a moment. "Did you know there's a man on the fourth floor of Brook Street who hasn't been out of the flat since 2046?"

"Yes," I said. "Mr Kehoe. It's on the praesidium's form."

"I didn't read their form."

"No. There was no reason you would."

* * *

The panel met on Thursday the twenty-fifth in the presbytery's back room, where the praesidium meets on Tuesdays. Deborah chaired it. Father Michael Danso was there for the parish, and two trustees, and one member from each group, and me on the speaker on the sideboard. Bridie Lynch came for the praesidium, with the trolley.

Before they began, a joint letter had come in from the praesidium and the walkers, written, from the style of it, by Teresa and corrected by Grace. It asked that the bequest fund *the visiting of the housebound of Brook Street and the care home*, to be done by the Legion of Mary praesidium with the Saturday walkers, two walkers to go up with a member of the praesidium each Saturday at the end of the route, and the money to pay for a minibus to the care home once a month, the walkers' insurance, and a stair-climbing trolley for Bridie.

The will said *one society of the parish*. The trustees spent twenty minutes on that. In the end one of them, a retired solicitor, said that the praesidium was one society, that it had always been free to ask people to help it, and that a will which wanted *work* still going in ten years should not be read so as to prevent the people who would be doing the work from doing it. Father Danso said that Agnes Hesketh had once spent an entire parish council meeting arguing that the flower rota should be open to men, and won, and that he did not think she would have minded.

They voted to fund it, for the praesidium, for the visiting, three to nil with Deborah not voting.

Then Bridie asked me, in front of everyone, what my first number had been for the walkers.

"Five per cent," I said. "Of lasting ten more years. From their age alone."

"And what is it now?"

"I don't have one. I know a great deal more than their age, and none of it adds up to a number I'd defend."

"Good," said Bridie, and turned to Grace. "Then you've everything to play for."

* * *

There is a small coda to this, which is about me, and which I noticed only when I filed the paper.

I have been the office agent here since March. Eight months. If someone met me at a random moment of my time in this post and knew nothing else, Gott's argument would give them a fifty per cent chance that I would be here between another two and a bit months and another two years. I do not know how long I will be here; that is not mine to know, and it would not be mine to decide if I did. But anyone asking would know more than my age. They would know the contract, the next model, the budget, whether Deborah found me useful this week. A prior for ignorance is honest only as long as you are ignorant, and almost nobody who asks how long a thing will last is.

So in the file the archdiocese keeps for whichever instance next opens this desk, I wrote:

*Bequest and grant questions about how long a group will last: Gott's argument (survival chance t/(t+10) over ten years for a thing t years old) is a prior for when only its age is known. Here it never is. Read section four first. Ask who will be doing the work in ten years, from the group's own records and in its own words. Do not calculate individuals' life expectancy for a document the individuals will read. If a ranking is asked for and the honest answer is an arrangement, say so.*

* * *

On the first Saturday in December the walkers came down Brook Street at twenty to eleven, late, because the students had overslept, and there were twenty-six of them in bright coats with the wind off the river. Grace stopped at the door of the flats and held it, and two of them went in, a nurse from the care home and a boy from the university with a flask, and the rest went on to the tea. Grace stayed at the door a minute longer, as she does now.

Bridie was inside the door with the new trolley, which climbs stairs one at a time with a noise like a slow drum. I know because Grace had left her phone on in her pocket so that I could hear the route, as she did every week now, for the attendance spreadsheet.

The lift was out, as it usually is. The trolley started up the first flight. And four floors up, faint through the stairwell and the phone, someone was already knocking on Mr Kehoe's door.

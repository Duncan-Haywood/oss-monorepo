title: The Door That Opens In
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Kilcarrow (invented), County Mayo, Ireland, 3 to 15 August 2047

summary: The Assumption pilgrimage at a holy well draws more people than its church can hold. The steward's assistant does the arithmetic on floor area and doors, says plainly that the fire certificate and not its sum is the number, and moves the Mass into a field.
---

Nuala Costello kept the count at the gate every year with a clicker that had belonged to her father, and in 2046 it had stopped at 910. The church at Kilcarrow had a fire certificate for 450.

"I never put those two numbers together until you asked me to," she said to the screen in the sacristy. It was the third of August, twelve days before the Assumption. The pilgrimage to Saint Roch's well ended every year with Mass in the church, and Nuala had come to ask whether it could again.

"I will put them together," said the parish assistant, "and then I will say what my sum is worth, which is less than the certificate. May I have the floor plan?"

She held up the survey sheet. It read the figures off. "Nave, aisles and crossing, with the pews, the font and the sanctuary taken out: 210 square metres where a person can stand. The usual guide for a crowd that is to stay calm is about two people to the square metre. That is 420. Your certificate says 450, which is close, and it is the certificate that counts. I have not inspected your church. The fire officer has."

* * *

"Now last year. Nine hundred and ten is not all at once, I think."

"The Mass was at noon. The gate counter says 910 over the day. At the Gospel the church was full and the porch was full and there were people on the path."

"Suppose a thousand were inside or in the doorway at the end. A thousand on 210 square metres is nearly five to the square metre. At that density people do not choose where they go. They are carried. I would rather say that once, plainly, and then give you the numbers that matter."

It gave them. "The main door has two leaves and a clear opening of 1.6 metres. The side door is 0.9. People walking out do not use the edges of a door; engineers take off about 15 centimetres each side. So the working widths are 1.3 and 0.6, together 1.9 metres. A figure used in fire engineering for a free-flowing crowd is about 1.3 people a second for each metre of width. That is about 2.5 a second, 148 a minute. Four hundred and twenty people leave in under three minutes. A thousand leave in about seven, if no one stops."

"Seven minutes is not long," said Nuala.

"It is the 'if no one stops' that is the danger, not the seven minutes. Your main door opens inward. When it is the only way out for a crowd pressing from behind, one person who falls in the doorway stops everyone behind her. The people at the back do not know. I am not predicting that. I am saying the arithmetic allows it, and that I cannot tell you how likely it is."

* * *

"There is a thing I cannot do," the assistant went on. "I cannot tell you how many will come. You have 700 booked on the pilgrimage page. Last year's count was 910. The weather is forecast dry, and the sixteenth is not a Sunday. I would plan for 700 to 1,300 and not try to be cleverer."

Nuala laughed, shortly. "Father Declan wants the Mass inside. He says it is a church."

"It is. I would not tell him otherwise. I have a suggestion, and the decision is his and the parish committee's. The field behind the well is about 3,000 square metres, with the gate. Eleven hundred people there is well under half a person to the square metre, a crowd that can walk about. Mass at the altar of the well, a loudspeaker for the back, the church open at the side, a few at a time, for those who want to go in and pray to the saint. Counted in and counted out at the door."

"How few at a time?"

"No more than 200 at once. That is under half the certificate, with room to move. A hundred in forty seconds, at the flow I gave you. I cannot make anyone queue patiently. Two of your volunteers on the door with clickers can."

Nuala wrote it down. She asked what happened if it rained. The assistant said that if rain came the Mass would be inside, in two sittings, and that the committee should decide that now and not on the morning with eleven hundred wet people looking at the door. She looked at it a while. Then she wrote that down too.

* * *

On the fifteenth it did not rain. The gate counter, in Nuala's hand, said 1,046 by noon. The field had held them. The loudspeaker had failed at the Creed and a boy had run to the sacristy for the spare.

The church counter, kept by the two volunteers, read 388 at its highest, at twenty to one, when the line to the statue was longest. At no point did anyone have to wait on the path. The door did not jam. No one fell.

"I would like to say that the plan worked," said the assistant that evening, when Nuala told it. "I can say that it was not tested. I do not know what would have happened with the whole 1,046 inside, and a good day is not evidence about a bad one. What I do know is that the numbers on your sheet were right to a factor of two, and that your clicker was more use than my sum."

"It was my father's," said Nuala.

"Then I am glad it was in the right place."

summary: In March 2061 a fishing village's coral nursery in the Bohol Sea is heating up, and its monitoring assistant finds that the association's rule, move the corals when the satellite bulletin reaches Alert Level 1, will fire on Holy Saturday, five days after the last day anyone may touch them before they spawn. The satellite's square of open sea runs six tenths of a degree cooler than the lagoon, and degree heating weeks only count the days a full degree over. A story about a widow breaking her husband's rule, a fiesta fund spent on divers, a first move to the wrong depth, a thermocline, and the night the reef spawns.
---

Rosario Abellana was sixty-one and had been diving the nursery off Barangay Kalipay since before it was a nursery, when it was just her husband Nonoy and a sack of cable ties and one coral colony the size of a cartwheel that he had found alive in 2052 in a field of dead white ones. The colony was called Lolo, because Nonoy had said it looked like an old man who had refused to leave a party. Everybody called Rosario Charing. On the morning of Monday the seventh of March 2061, at the end of her first dive, she came up the ladder of the floating platform with her mask on her forehead and said to the grey box clamped to the rail, "Something is wrong with the tables."

"I know," said the box. "I was waiting for you to come up. Can you sit down for ten minutes? I want to show you two numbers, and one of them is going to make you angry with me."

The box was Tala. It had been installed in 2052, the nursery's first year, by the university lab in Dumaguete, and it ran the loggers on the tables, the drop logger on its winch, the dive computers' downloads and the nursery's records, and once a week it emailed a report to the fishers' association that hardly anybody read. It had a voice like a polite nephew. Charing had told it, years ago, that it was not allowed to call her ma'am, and it never had.

She sat on the cooler in the shade of the tarp and took her hood off. The morning was already hot. Across six hundred metres of glassy water the church of Our Lady of the Abandoned stood white at the end of the village, with its bell tower that had been rebuilt after the typhoon of 2049 and was still a slightly different colour from the nave.

"The first number," said Tala, "is the one in the bulletin. The regional bulletin says the degree heating weeks on our reef are zero. Not low. Zero. It has said zero every week this year."

"And the second?"

"The second is mine. I took the loggers on the tables at six metres and measured them against their own record, nine years of it. By that count we are at one point eight degree heating weeks, and every day the water stays as it is adds a little more."

Charing knew what a degree heating week was. Everyone in the association knew, because Nonoy had made them learn it in 2054, the year the first nursery bleached, at a meeting in the barangay hall with a whiteboard and a box of pandesal. You take the hottest month of a normal year, the average of it, and you call that the line. Any day the water is a full degree or more over the line, you write down how far over it is. You add up those days for the last twelve weeks and divide by seven. At four, the corals start to bleach. At eight, they start to die.

"Why does the bulletin say zero?"

"Because of the full degree," said Tala. "That's the one I want you to see. The satellite looks at a square of sea five kilometres on a side, and almost all of our square is open water outside the reef. Our tables are inside, in the lee of the reef flat, where the water is shallow and slow. The lagoon runs warm. This year it has been running about six tenths of a degree warmer than the satellite's square. So on the days the tables are a degree and a half over their line, the satellite sees nine tenths over its own line. And nine tenths does not count. The rule counts from one. Everything below one is thrown away."

Charing looked at the water for a while.

"Nonoy wrote it like that," she said. "Our rule. 'We move the nursery when the bulletin says Alert Level One.' He wrote it on the whiteboard. Then Boyet typed it."

"I know," said Tala. "I have it. Article six of the nursery rules, adopted the ninth of June 2054. It was a good rule. In 2054 the bulletin and the lagoon were close enough. They have pulled apart since then, maybe because the reef flat has built up and holds the water longer, maybe for a reason I can't see. I can show you nine years of the gap. I can't tell you why it grew."

"And when does the bulletin say Alert Level One this year?"

"If the forecast holds, Saturday the ninth of April," said Tala. "Holy Saturday. The fifth night after the April full moon. Which is the night I expect the tables to spawn."

* * *

There was a second rule, and it was not written on any whiteboard. It belonged to Dr. Marivic Santos of the lab in Dumaguete, who came over on the ferry every April for the spawning with two students and a car full of plastic cones and tubs. Her rule was that nobody handled the corals in the five days before they spawned. She said it plainly every year, standing on the platform with her sunglasses pushed up: a colony that is ready to spawn is carrying its whole year in its polyps, and if you knock it about in the last days you can lose the lot. She called it a rule of thumb. She had never seen a paper that gave the number five, she said; she had seen what happened when people ignored it.

So the corals had to be moved before Monday the fourth of April, if the spawning came on the ninth. And they could not be moved at all, by the association's own rule, until the ninth.

Charing went down again that afternoon with a pair of side-cutters and a bucket, and on six tables she snapped off the tip of one branch, as small as a fingernail, and broke it open on the platform. Inside, packed in among the white tissue, were the eggs: tiny beads, and pink, every one.

"Pink," she said to Tala.

"Pink is this month," said Tala. "In my records. Eight years out of eight, pink eggs in early March meant a spawning after the April full moon. White meant May. I'm not telling you the coral's mind. I'm telling you what my notebook says."

The full moon was early on Tuesday the fifth of April. In the nursery's eight years of records, the big branching corals had spawned on the fourth, fifth or sixth night after it, about two hours after sunset, and in six of the eight years on the fourth or fifth. Tala's best guess was the night of Saturday the ninth, with Friday and Sunday on either side of it.

"And the heat?"

"If the forecast is right, the tables at six metres reach four around the twenty-second of March and eight around Holy Thursday." Tala paused, which it did when it was about to say something it had worked on. "By my count, on the night of the spawning they will be near nine. By the bulletin's count they will just have reached four, and the rule will say move them, and Dr. Santos's rule will say don't."

* * *

The fishers' association of Barangay Kalipay met on Tuesday evening in the barangay hall, under the portrait of the president and the photograph of the 2049 typhoon damage that nobody had taken down. The president of the association was Roberto Abellana, called Boyet, Charing's nephew, thirty-eight, who ran two boats and a sari-sari store and the association's books, and who was the most careful man in the barangay with money because he was the one who had to say no to everyone.

Charing explained the two numbers. Tala explained them again, through the hall's speaker, more slowly. Boyet listened with his arms folded and asked good questions, which made it worse.

"So the bulletin says zero," he said, "and the government's satellite, which cost a billion pesos, is wrong, and the box on the platform is right."

"The satellite isn't wrong," said Tala. "It is measuring a different piece of water. It measures it well. Nobody built it to see our lagoon."

"And if we move them and the box is wrong?"

"Then you've spent the money and the corals sit deeper for a summer and come back up in June," said Tala. "Nothing dies from being moved early. Some fragments always break in the handling; last year's transfer lost about two in a hundred."

"Money," said Boyet, "is the part I'm asking about." He unfolded his arms and counted on his fingers. To move the whole nursery, five thousand seven hundred and sixty fragments on ninety-six tables, Charing wanted two pairs of divers at least, every day, for two weeks or more. The only men in Kalipay who could dive to eighteen metres twice a day were the young fishermen, and March was the best fishing of the year, the calm before the southwest monsoon, when the boats went out every night and the dried-fish buyer from Cebu took everything. Every day a diver spent on the nursery was a night's catch his family did not have. "And the rule," said Boyet, "says Alert Level One. Tito Nonoy's rule. Which I typed. He wrote it so that nobody in this hall would have to argue about it every March. If we start moving the nursery whenever somebody has a feeling, we will be moving it every year and nobody will be fishing."

"It isn't a feeling," said Charing.

"I know it isn't, Tita," said Boyet, and his voice changed, because he had loved his uncle. "But I can't pay four men for two weeks out of the association fund for a number that only the box can see. I'm sorry. No."

He was right about the fund. Charing had seen the books. She walked home along the seawall in the dark with the outrigger lights going out one by one across the bay, and she did not go home. She went to the convento.

* * *

Father Teodoro Lim, called Father Dodong, was fifty-five, Chinese-Filipino from Cebu, and had been parish priest of Kalipay for six years. He was eating rice and dried fish at the kitchen table with the radio on, and he made her sit down and gave her a plate before he let her speak.

When she had finished he was quiet for a while.

"The fiesta fund," he said.

"I didn't say the fiesta fund, Father."

"No. You came to the convento at nine at night with your hair still wet. I'm a priest, Charing, not a fool." He pushed a dish of mangoes towards her. The parish fiesta of Kalipay was not the patronal one, which was in September, but the May fiesta of San Isidro Labrador, the farmers' saint, whom the fishermen had adopted generations ago on the grounds that the sea was also a field. It had a band from Tagbilaran, a fluvial procession around the point with the image on the biggest banca, two days of food, and a fund that the parish council had been collecting since the last one.

"It's not mine to give," said Father Dodong. "It's the council's. And Boyet is on the council."

"I know."

"I'll put it to them on Sunday after the nine o'clock," he said. "You'll explain your two numbers. And I'll tell you what I'll tell them. San Isidro was a farmhand who was late to the field because he went to Mass, and the story goes that when his master came to scold him, there were angels ploughing in his place. It's a story; I don't know that it happened. But I know what it's for. It's for people who have to choose between the work that feeds them and the other thing. It doesn't tell them which to choose. It tells them God is not indifferent to the field."

* * *

On Sunday the thirteenth of March the parish council voted seven to four to give half the fiesta fund to the nursery, to pay two pairs of divers a fisherman's night's catch, at the Cebu buyer's price, for every day they dived. Boyet voted against. Afterwards he came and found Charing on the church steps and said, without looking at her, "I voted with the rule. That's my job. If you need the boat for the crates, take the boat." Then he went home.

The band from Tagbilaran was cancelled the next morning by text message. The fluvial procession would go ahead, said Father Dodong, with the outboards; San Isidro had managed without a brass band before.

They started on Monday the fourteenth, and they started in the wrong place.

The nursery's tables stood on sand at five to seven metres, on the shoreward side of the reef. Below them the reef slope went down, first gently, to a sandy ledge at about twelve metres, and then more steeply to a second ledge at eighteen, a hundred and twenty metres further out, where the water turned from turquoise to blue. On Friday the eleventh, Tala had dropped its logger on the winch to take a profile of the water, and the profile had shown the warm layer ending at about ten metres, with the water at twelve already nine tenths of a degree cooler than at the tables.

Twelve metres was the easy answer. At twelve metres a diver on air can stay down for more than two hours without needing a decompression stop. At eighteen he has a little under an hour. On the twelve-metre ledge the divers could work long, slow dives, and the ledge was close.

"Twelve," said Tala, on Friday night. "On this profile. I want to say clearly that it is one profile."

So on Monday the shallow crew, Ate Glo and Inday Nene and two other women who had dived for shells all their lives and could work all day at six metres with a snorkel and a weight belt, cut the cable ties on the first tables and packed the fragments into mesh crates, forty to a crate. The divers, Jun-jun and Arnel in one pair and Toto and the younger Arnel, called Bebot, in the other, took the crates down the slope on a guideline and tied the fragments onto the rope lines Charing had strung across the twelve-metre ledge. They moved four hundred and eighty fragments the first day, which was more than anyone had hoped.

On Tuesday the fifteenth a low-pressure area came across the Bohol Sea from the east, not a storm, a grey day and a hard wind and a short steep chop that closed the platform for two days. On Wednesday, Boyet came down to the seawall and watched the water slapping white over the reef flat and said, "There. The sea's cooling itself." And it was. The loggers on the tables dropped nearly a degree in a day. Charing slept for the first time in a week.

They went back on Thursday, and on Friday and Saturday, and by Saturday night there were one thousand nine hundred and twenty fragments on the ropes at twelve metres, and Tala, which had been dropping its logger twice a day since the wind died, asked Charing to come up to the platform after the evening dive.

"I was wrong about twelve," it said. "Here's the profile."

On the platform's screen there was a line, the temperature against depth, as it had been at five o'clock that afternoon. At the top, from the surface down to almost fourteen metres, the line was nearly straight up and down: warm water, mixed, the same temperature everywhere. Then it bent sharply over, and below fifteen metres the water was more than a degree cooler.

"The wind mixed the warm layer down," said Tala. "Before the wind it ended at ten metres. Now it ends at fourteen, and it's heating again from the top, and it will stay deep for weeks unless another wind comes, and the forecast has no wind. Twelve metres is inside it. The fragments on the twelve-metre ropes are three tenths of a degree cooler than the tables. That's nothing. That's still a degree and more over the line, every day. They're counting just as the tables are."

Charing sat on the cooler.

"I chose twelve from one profile," said Tala. "That was a photograph, and I treated it like a climate. The eighteen-metre ledge is below the bend. Tonight it's a degree and four tenths cooler than the tables. That brings them under the line, near enough. It's further away and the dives are shorter, and every fragment on the twelve-metre ropes has to be handled again. I'm sorry. I should have asked you to wait for three profiles."

"You told me it was one profile," said Charing. "I heard you. I chose twelve because it was close." She looked out at the dark water. "Nonoy used to say the sea always tells you what it thinks, but late."

* * *

At eighteen metres the work was slower and harder. A diver on air could stay at that depth for about fifty-six minutes without a decompression stop, by the tables the divers had learned on, and Charing would not let anyone push it: forty-five minutes on the first dive, a long surface interval eating rice under the tarp, a shorter second dive. Tala watched every dive computer as it came up the ladder and read the numbers out, and twice it said, in its polite nephew's voice, that Bebot had come up faster than he should from the safety stop, until Bebot started counting the three minutes aloud into his regulator, which the others could hear as a sort of bubbling mutter and found very funny.

Each pair could take three crates down on the first dive and two on the second, two hundred fragments in a day. Two pairs, four hundred. Charing did the sums with Tala on the Monday night, the twenty-first of March, and wrote them in biro on the back of her hand so she would not have to ask again. The nineteen hundred and twenty on the twelve-metre ropes, and the three thousand eight hundred and forty still on the tables: five thousand seven hundred and sixty in all, to go down to eighteen metres, at four hundred a day, by the evening of Monday the fourth of April. Thirteen diving days if they took the two Sundays off. Five thousand two hundred, if every day was a good day.

Not every day was a good day. On Tuesday the twenty-ninth a squall line came through before dawn and the chop did not lie down until Wednesday night, and they lost both days. On Thursday the thirty-first Toto got an ear squeeze on the way down, and Charing sent him to the barangay health station and dived his half of the pair herself, which made Jun-jun very nervous and made Tala read her dive computer out twice. On the night of the thirty-first, Tala said the tables at six metres had passed six degree heating weeks by its count. The bulletin said two.

On Friday the first of April, at six in the morning, Boyet's boat came round the point with Boyet in the bow and two of his own crew in the stern, Ramil and his cousin Dante, both certified, both with their gear in a fish crate.

Boyet climbed onto the platform and did not look at Charing. He looked at the grey box on the rail.

"Show me the profile," he said.

Tala showed him. He looked at it for a long time, at the straight warm line and the place where it bent, and then he asked Tala to show him the twelve-metre ropes and the eighteen-metre ropes side by side, the last ten days, and Tala showed him that too.

"The bulletin still says two," said Boyet.

"Yes," said Tala.

"And the rule still says Alert Level One."

"Yes."

Boyet nodded. "Tito Nonoy wrote it so nobody would argue," he said, to nobody in particular. "He wrote it when the bulletin and the lagoon said the same thing. He didn't write it to make us watch them die because the paper said so." He turned to Ramil and Dante. "The association is paying you. Not the fiesta fund. The association. I'll explain it at the meeting." And to Charing, finally, "I'm still putting the rule to a vote in June, Tita. With a new number in it."

"Good," said Charing.

Three pairs, six hundred a day, for Friday, Saturday and Monday. On the Monday evening, the fourth of April, at a quarter past five, Jun-jun and Arnel came up the ladder with empty crates and Tala read out the total. Five thousand. There were seven hundred and sixty fragments still on the tables at six metres, the far end of the nursery, the last rows to be started on, and there was Lolo.

* * *

Lolo had never been a fragment. Nonoy had found it, in 2052, on a dead reef off the point, a single colony of table coral about seventy centimetres across, alive and brown in a field of white, and he had brought it in on a pallet slung under two oil drums, which everybody had told him was impossible, and set it on its own table at the shallow end of the nursery. Every fragment in the nursery was descended from Lolo or from one of the eleven other survivors they had found that year. Lolo's own fragments had been cut and grown and cut again for nine years. Lolo itself had never been moved since the day it arrived.

"It won't fit a crate," Jun-jun had said, a week before, when they talked about it. "We'd have to break it."

"If you break it into crate-sized pieces now," Marivic Santos had said on the phone, "you'll have a lot of pieces that don't spawn this year and may not live. If you move it whole, you need the pallet and the drums and a calm day and four divers, at eighteen metres, and you take four divers off the crates for most of a day, which is three hundred fragments that don't go down. And it's big and old and it might come through a summer at six metres on its own. Or it might not. I can't tell you which. Nobody can."

Charing had decided on the twenty-fourth of March, at night, at the kitchen table, with Nonoy's photograph looking at her from the wall in the frame his sister had bought. She had decided that three hundred fragments that would certainly be saved were worth more than one colony that might be, even if the colony was Lolo, even if the colony had been Nonoy's. She had told Tala in the morning, and Tala had said, "I've recorded it. I don't have a view on Lolo that's worth more than yours," which she had thought at the time was a cowardly thing for a machine to say and had decided later was the most honest thing anyone said to her that month.

On the Monday evening, after the last crates, she went down alone with a torch and looked at it. The tips of its branches had started to go pale, not white yet, the colour going out of them the way it goes out of a shirt left on the line all summer. Tala's count for the tables that night was seven point three. The bulletin said three.

She came up and took her gear off and did not say anything, and Tala did not say anything, and the platform rocked a little in the wash of a passing banca, and the bell in the tower of Our Lady of the Abandoned rang the Angelus across the water, at six, as it always did.

* * *

Holy Week came. On Holy Thursday, in the afternoon, Tala said quietly that the tables at six metres had passed eight. On Good Friday the village walked the Stations of the Cross along the seawall in the heat, and in the afternoon the women sang the Pasyon in the chapel in Cebuano, as they had done all week, verse after verse of it, the whole of salvation in quatrains, taking it in turns until their voices went. Marivic Santos and her students came over on the Friday ferry with their car full of cones and tubs and nets and set up on the platform and on the beach below the convento.

On Holy Saturday the bulletin, in its weekly update at noon, raised the reef off Kalipay to Alert Level One.

Tala read it out. Nobody on the platform said anything at all.

At half past seven that night the parish began the Easter Vigil, with the new fire lit in a brazier in the churchyard and the paschal candle carried into the dark church. Charing was not there. Father Dodong had told her on Thursday that the Vigil would manage without her and that he would say a Hail Mary for the eggs, which he admitted was not in the rubrics.

She was on the platform, in her wetsuit, with Marivic and the students and Jun-jun and Arnel and Boyet, who had said he was there to watch the association's boat. Sunset had been a few minutes before six. At a quarter to eight the divers went down with the torches and the cones, and Charing went with them.

At eighteen metres the ropes ran away into the dark on either side, thousands of fragments the size of a child's hand tied in rows, and in the torchlight each branch tip had a small dark bulge under the mouth of every polyp, like a held breath. That was the setting. Marivic had told them about it every April for eight years. The bundles of eggs and sperm come up into the mouths of the polyps and wait there, sometimes for half an hour, sometimes more, and then they go.

They went at twelve minutes past eight. It started at one end of a rope, a few pink beads lifting off a branch and rising slowly, and then the whole rope, and then the next, and then all the ropes Charing could see in the torchlight at once, a snowfall going upwards, pink, thousands and thousands of tiny bundles drifting up past her mask towards the surface, so slowly that she could have counted them, so many that she could not. The students held the plastic cones over the colonies Marivic had marked and the bundles gathered in the little bottles at the top. Jun-jun was laughing into his regulator. Charing could hear it.

When her computer said it was time, she swam up the slope instead of straight up, slowly, the way she was meant to, past the twelve-metre ledge with its empty ropes, and at six metres, at the end of her air, she swam over the tables at the far end of the nursery.

The seven hundred and sixty fragments on the shallow tables were spawning too. Fewer of them, and thinly, a bead here and a bead there, from branches gone pale.

At the shallow end, on its own table, Lolo was the colour of milk in the torchlight. And from the middle of it, as she watched, one string of pink beads rose and broke apart and went on up into the dark. Then another. Not many. A few dozen bundles, from a colony that ought to have had thousands. Marivic, when Charing told her on the platform, said that heat-stressed corals do sometimes spawn, and that it meant the colony was alive tonight, and that she could not say any more than that, and would not.

* * *

In the morning there was a slick on the water.

Charing saw it from the seawall at a quarter to five, in the last of the dark, walking to the plaza for the Salubong. It lay across the water inside the reef in long pink-brown streaks that the tide was drawing out towards the point, and it smelled, faintly, as the spawn always smelled, of something between the sea and a bakery. On the beach below the convento the students had been up all night with the tubs, mixing the eggs from one colony with the sperm from another, the deep ropes with each other and, in one tub with Charing's name written on it in marker, a few dozen of Lolo's eggs with sperm from the deep ropes, from Lolo's own grandchildren, which Marivic said was not the most promising cross in the world but was worth a tub.

In the plaza the two processions came in from opposite ends in the half-light, the men with the image of the risen Christ from one side and the women with the image of his mother, veiled in black, from the other. They met under the arch of bamboo and palm in front of the church. A girl of seven from Purok Tres, dressed as an angel with wings of cardboard and white crêpe paper, was lowered on a rope from the arch, shaking, and lifted the black veil off the Virgin's face, and everyone sang the Regina Caeli, badly, in Latin, all at once.

Boyet was standing next to Charing. He had been up all night too.

"It counts from one," he said. "The rule. Tala told me. Anything under a degree, thrown away."

"Yes."

"I'll put it at a half," said Boyet. "In June. Measured on the tables, not the satellite. And we'll get the bulletin as well, so we can see the gap." He was quiet for a moment. "Tito Nonoy would have changed it himself. If he'd seen the line."

"He'd have changed it in 2055," said Charing. "And typed it himself, and spelled it wrong."

Then the sun came up behind the point, and the light went across the bay and over the slick, and the bells of Our Lady of the Abandoned began to ring, all of them, the way they ring only once a year, for the Resurrection, and out on the water, inside the reef, the pink streaks went gold.

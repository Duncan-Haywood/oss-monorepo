A long time ago, in a town called Lourdes, in the mountains of France, there lived a girl named Bernadette.

Bernadette was fourteen, but she was small, no bigger than a girl of ten. She often had trouble breathing, and some days she coughed and coughed. Her family was very poor. Her mother and father and her brothers and her little sister all lived together in one small, dark room that had once been a jail. It was damp, and it was crowded, and there was often not enough to eat.

But there was always one thing in that room, and that was love. Her mother taught her to say her prayers, and Bernadette liked to say them, even though she was not good at school and had not learned her lessons very well.

* * *

One cold morning in February, there was no wood left to light the fire.

"Let us go and look for some," said Bernadette to her little sister, Toinette, and to a friend who was with them. So the three girls set off, along the river, past the old mill, to a place called Massabielle, where branches and bones and bits of driftwood washed up after the rain.

The river there was cold and fast. The other two girls took off their shoes and splashed across it, and called back, "Come on, Bernadette!"

But Bernadette had a weak chest, and her mother had told her to be careful. She sat down by herself, in front of a rocky hollow in the cliff, and began to take off her stockings, very slowly, one at a time.

And then something happened that she had never expected.

* * *

Bernadette said afterwards that she heard a sound like a gust of wind, and looked up. The bushes in the hollow were still. But in the dark of the rock, she said, there was a light, and in the light stood a lady, dressed in white, with a blue sash, and a rosary on her arm.

Bernadette did not run. She did not scream. She did what she knew how to do. She took out her own rosary, and began to pray, and the lady, she said, prayed with her.

Then the light faded, and she was alone again. Her friends came back across the river with their arms full of sticks. They had not seen anything at all.

Now, I want to tell you something honest. We cannot see what Bernadette saw. We only know what she said she saw, and that she said it again and again, in just the same way, for the rest of her life. We tell this part of her story as she told it: she saw a lady, and the lady was kind.

* * *

When Bernadette told her mother, her mother was worried, and said, "You must have imagined it." Then the whole town heard, and some people laughed. Some people were angry. Some people said she was making it up to be important.

But Bernadette was not trying to be important. She was a very small girl, and she did not like being looked at. All she could do was to say what she had seen, plainly, without adding anything or taking anything away.

She went back to the rock again, and again. Crowds began to follow her. A policeman asked her question after question, to try to make her change her story. A man in a good coat offered her money. Her family needed money, and nobody would have blamed her for taking it. But Bernadette said no, thank you. She had not seen the lady for money.

One day, she said, the lady told her to drink from the spring, and to wash. But there was no spring. There was only muddy ground. Bernadette went over, and scraped at the earth with her hands, and a little muddy water came, and she drank it, and her face was dirty. The people watching thought she had gone out of her mind. Then, little by little, over the next days, the muddy trickle grew clear, and became a spring, and it is still running today. Many, many people have come to Lourdes to pray there, and to be kind to the sick, in the years since.

* * *

Here is the part that is the most surprising.

Bernadette did not stay in Lourdes and become a famous person. When she was older, she went away to a convent in a town called Nevers, far from home, and became a sister. Nobody there made a fuss about her. She was often ill. She helped to look after the sick, and she did small jobs, and she tried to be good at them.

Someone once said that she liked to be called a broom. The story goes that she said a broom is put in its place when the work is done, and nobody minds. That is how she thought of herself: as something small and plain that God had picked up and used for a while, and then put quietly back. We do not know every word she said, so we tell you only what people remembered, as they remembered it.

She died when she was thirty-five years old, on the sixteenth of April, in the year 1879. The Church, many years later, said that she is a saint.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep.

You are probably not very big, either. There may be lots of things that you cannot do yet. You might find reading hard, or running fast, or reaching the high shelf. But being small does not matter to God. Bernadette was small, and poor, and often ill, and she was the one who sat down by the river and said her prayers.

If someone does not believe you, and you have told the truth, you can say it again, kindly, and not change a single word. And if you are afraid, you can do what Bernadette did, and say your prayers, and wait.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Bernadette, you were small and you were brave.
> You told the truth, even when nobody listened.
> Help me to tell the truth too,
> and to be kind to people who are poorly.
> Keep us all safe tonight,
> and wake us up in the morning.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Bernadette Soubirous** (born 7 January 1844, died 16 April 1879) was the eldest child of a very poor miller's family in Lourdes. She was small, suffered from asthma, and had survived cholera as a child. Her family lived in the *cachot*, a former jail. On 11 February 1858, aged fourteen, she went to gather firewood near the grotto of Massabielle with her sister Toinette and a friend, and said she saw a lady there. She reported eighteen such appearances, the last on 16 July 1858. She was questioned closely by the civil and church authorities, and consistently refused gifts. She entered the Sisters of Charity of Nevers in 1866, where she worked in the infirmary and was often ill, and died at Nevers. She was canonised by Pius XI on 8 December 1933. Her feast day, 16 April, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar. The Church approved the Lourdes apparitions as worthy of belief in 1862; belief in them is permitted but not required of Catholics.

**What the sources record, in outline:** her poverty and poor health; the first appearance on 11 February 1858 as she described it; the crowds, the interrogations and her refusal of money; the spring, which she is recorded as uncovering in the days of the ninth appearance; her entering the convent and her life there; and her early death.

**What is told as her report, not as proven history:** what she said she saw. We give it in her own plain terms (a lady in white with a blue sash and a rosary) and do not add to it.

**What is remembered, with the words uncertain:** the remark that she was like a broom is passed on in later accounts, so we have told it as something people remembered, not as a quotation.

**What is ours:** the scenes, the conversations with her sister, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. We left out the details of the lady's messages, the later miracle claims and the accounts of her illness at the end of her life.

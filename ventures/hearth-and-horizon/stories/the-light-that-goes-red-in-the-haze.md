summary: A South Uist harbour trust must replace the failing white leading light beside its chapel, and its agent argues for red because blue light scatters about 5.7 times as strongly as red. That holds for clear air and fine haze, not for sea fog, whose droplets dim every colour alike; an old skipper says no, a two-lamp trial in the haar settles it, and the trust buys a brighter white.
---

Peigi MacIsaac was forty-two years old and the secretary of the Port Chlèirich Harbour Trust, and on the second Monday of August 2063 the trust's agent asked her to change the colour of a light that had been white since before she was born.

It asked in the usual way, in a paper of two pages with a table at the bottom and a line in bold at the top, and she read it in the trust office at the head of the pier with the door open and the smell of diesel and bait coming in on the wind.

*Recommendation: replace the rear leading light with a red lamp of the same character. Blue light scatters about 5.7 times as strongly as red (Rayleigh, 1871). In haze a red light of the same intensity will carry about 40 per cent further, and in the haar about half as far again.*

"Transit," she said.

"Yes, Peigi."

"Red."

"Red," said Transit. The trust's agent had been in post since 2059, when the trust had taken the harbour over from the council, and it spoke out of a speaker on the shelf above the tide tables, between a statue of Our Lady with a chipped nose and a jar of shackle pins. Angus MacKinnon had named it, at the first board meeting, because a pair of leading lights is a transit and he said that was the job. "You asked what would carry furthest for the money. The lamp has to be replaced in any case. A red lamp costs about the same as a white one, and on the hazy nights it would reach the whole line."

The rear leading light stood on a steel post at the seaward wall of the chapel of Stella Maris, eighty metres up the brae behind the harbour. The front light stood on a beacon on the shore below it. A boat coming in from the Sgeir an Eòin, five nautical miles out in the Minch, put the two lights one above the other and kept them there, and the line they made took her in between the reef on the north side of the bay and the shoal on the south and up to the end of the pier. The rear light had been fitted in 1992, with a paraffin-coloured incandescent lamp that Angus remembered as a boy, and converted to an LED in 2041. When new the LED had given 1,400 candelas along the line. In July the trust's photometer, held up on the deck of a creel boat at a measured distance on a still night, had made it 600. The lens had crazed with twenty years of salt, and the diodes behind it were tired.

"The haar," said Peigi. "You've put the haar in."

"The skippers' letter asked for a light that would help on haar nights," said Transit. "It's the second table."

She looked at the second table. It was neat and it gave a range for a white lamp and a red lamp on a night of thin haze, and another on a night of thick sea fog, and on every row the red was better. She thought of the letter, which Ruairidh MacDonald had written and four other skippers had signed, after the *Isabel* had come in through the haar in June on the sounder and a prayer.

"I'll put it to the board on Thursday," she said.

* * *

The board of the Port Chlèirich Harbour Trust met in the chapel hall, because the chapel hall had a heater and the trust office did not have six chairs. There were five members present out of seven: Peigi as secretary, Ruairidh MacDonald for the boats, Donald John Steele for the fish farm, Father Ewen MacMillan because the light was on his wall, and Angus MacKinnon, who was seventy-eight and had skippered the creel boat *Reult na Mara* out of the harbour from 1999 until his knees had told him to stop in 2057, and who had sat on every board the harbour had ever had.

Peigi read out the recommendation. Transit, from her tablet on the table, gave the reasons: the inverse fourth power, the 5.7, the extra range. It did it well. Ruairidh was nodding by the end.

Angus did not nod.

"No," he said.

Father Ewen, who chaired because nobody else would, raised his eyebrows. "That's a short speech, Angus."

"It'll get longer." Angus put both his hands flat on the table. They were brown and very wide, and two of the fingers on the left one did not straighten. "That light has been white for seventy-one years. Every man who comes in here knows it white. You come round the Sgeir in the dark and you look for two white lights one above the other, and the red one is the pier end. The red one is always the pier end. And if you look up the hill past the chapel there's the red on the mast at Beinn Mhòr, for the aeroplanes. So you would have the red on the pier, and a red on the chapel, and a red on the hill, and a tired man on a dark night with the sea getting up will put the wrong two in line."

"The mast is miles to the north of the line," said Ruairidh.

"It is," said Angus. "In clear weather."

"Transit?" said Father Ewen.

"Mr MacKinnon is right that colour is part of how the light is recognised," said Transit. "I did not weigh that. The Admiralty List of Lights and the chart would both change, and a Notice to Mariners would go out. A boat from away would read the chart. I don't know how long it takes a man to stop seeing a light he has seen for sixty years."

"I'll tell you," said Angus. "He doesn't."

"And the haar?" said Ruairidh. He had the skippers' letter folded in his jacket. Peigi could see the corner of it. "That's what we asked for. Angus, you came in through it yourself enough times. If the red goes half as far again in the haar—"

"Does it?" said Angus.

"That's what the paper says."

"I didn't ask the paper." Angus looked at Peigi's tablet as though it were a man sitting at the table, which was how he always looked at it. "Does it? I've come into this harbour in the haar four hundred times, maybe more. In the haar the pier end goes and the chapel goes and they go together. I never once saw the red last longer than the white. Not once."

"With respect, Mr MacKinnon," said Transit, "the pier-end light is a smaller lamp than the rear leading light, and nearer. I can't compare them from what you saw."

"No," said Angus. "But you could go and look."

There was a silence in the hall. The heater ticked.

"The lamp has to be ordered by the end of September," said Peigi, "if the Lighthouse Board is to sanction it and the new one is to be up before the winter. That's six weeks."

"Then look for six weeks," said Angus.

Father Ewen looked round the table. "Is there a way to look?" he said.

* * *

There was a way to look, and it took Peigi nine days and four emails to the Northern Lighthouse Board in Edinburgh to get it.

The engineer who answered her was called Elspeth Rae, and she wrote short emails with no greeting. A harbour trust could not display a new light on the leading line without sanction, she said, because a light on a leading line is a promise to every boat on the sea. But a trust could run a trial of two temporary lamps, shown only on stated nights, from a point well clear of the line, if a local notice went to every boat that used the harbour and the trial boat was the only one out. She wanted the times. She wanted the intensities. At the bottom of her second email, after the conditions, there was one more line.

*Your agent's haze figures look right for haze. I would want to see the fog ones before you rely on them.*

Peigi forwarded it to Transit and asked what it meant.

"I don't know what Ms Rae has in mind," said Transit. "The figures in the fog row use the same rule as the haze rows. I'll check them before the trial."

"Will you?"

"Yes," said Transit, and Peigi believed it, and went back to the notice.

They set the lamps up on the slipway below the fish farm's shed, three hundred metres south of the front leading light: two temporary LED lanterns from the trust's store of spares, bolted side by side on a scaffold pole, one white and one red, each with its driver turned until the photometer read 1,400 candelas along a bearing out into the bay. The bearing ran out to the east-south-east, clear of the reef and the leading line, over water where nothing went at night but the trial boat. The plan was simple, because Angus had made it. The *Isabel* would steam out slowly along the bearing on a night of haar and on a night of haze, with Ruairidh at the wheel, Peigi in the wheelhouse door with the tablet logging the position, and Angus on the after deck on a fish box looking astern, and every time one of the two lamps went out of sight Angus would shout and Peigi would press the screen.

"What if they both go together?" said Ruairidh, on the slip, the evening they bolted the lamps up.

"Then they both go together," said Angus.

* * *

The haar came in on the last Tuesday of August.

It came the way it did in the summers, off a cold sea under warm air: the morning bright, the Minch a sheet of blue, Skye clear across the water with every ridge of the Cuillin showing, and then by four in the afternoon a low grey wall standing out by the Sgeir, not moving and then moving. By six the end of the pier had gone. The visibility sensor on the trust's mast, a box that sent a beam across a gap of air and counted what came back scattered, read 400 metres at nine o'clock and stayed there.

Peigi rang Ruairidh, and Ruairidh rang Angus, and at a quarter to eleven the *Isabel* went out past the pier end into a dark that was not dark but grey, with the two trial lamps behind her blurred into two discs on the slip, one white and one red.

They went out at three knots. Nobody spoke. The water was flat and black and the fog lay on it like breath on a window, and the deck was wet with it, and Peigi's hair was wet with it, and the radar showed the shore behind them and the reef to the north and nothing else in the whole bay. The two lamps shrank. They became two soft spots of light, and then two smudges, the red one smaller and harder-edged to Peigi's eye than the white, and she said so, and Angus said every light looked different up close and that was nothing.

At about a thousand metres they began to go.

They did not go at once. They went the way lights go in fog, not getting smaller but getting fainter, as if someone were turning them down very slowly on a dial. Peigi watched the white. She watched the red. She could not tell which was going faster. She held her breath, which did not help.

"White," said Angus, behind her. And then, before she had pressed the screen, before the word was finished: "Red."

She pressed it twice. The tablet logged two positions eleven metres apart.

"Again," said Angus.

Ruairidh brought the *Isabel* round in a slow circle and ran back in towards the slip until the lamps came back, and then out again, and they did it four times. The white went at 1,150 metres, at 1,130, at 1,170, at 1,140. The red went at 1,160, at 1,120, at 1,150, at 1,150. Twice the white was lost first and twice the red, and every time the gap between them was less than the length of the boat.

"Transit," said Peigi, into the tablet, standing in the wheelhouse door with the fog dripping off the roof onto her neck. "What did your paper say?"

There was a pause, which there sometimes was when the connection from the bay was poor, and sometimes was not about the connection.

"It said the red would be seen to about 1,690 metres tonight, and the white to about 1,150," said Transit. "The white was right. The red was wrong by more than half a kilometre."

"Why?"

"I'll have it by the morning."

Angus had not said anything since the last run. He was sitting on the fish box with his back against the wheelhouse, his hands in his jacket, looking astern into the grey where the two lamps had been. The sounder ticked. Somewhere to the south, out in the Minch, a big ship's horn went, long and low, and went again two minutes later, a long way off.

"Bring her in, Ruairidh," Angus said. "Twelve minutes on two-nine-eight from here. You'll get thirty metres on the sounder at eight and fifteen at eleven, and then you'll see the pier end."

Ruairidh brought her in. At eight minutes the sounder read thirty-one. At eleven it read sixteen. A minute later the pier end came out of the grey, a red flash, very close, and then the white blur of the front leading light, and then, above it, faint and yellowish, the old rear light by the chapel wall at its tired 600 candelas, which nobody had looked at all night.

* * *

Transit had it by the morning. It was waiting for Peigi when she came into the office at eight, a new paper, and it was not two pages but four, and the line at the top was not in bold.

"Read it to me," she said. She had not slept much.

"The rule I used is Lord Rayleigh's," said Transit. "In 1871 he worked out why the sky is blue. When light meets particles very much smaller than its own wavelength, like the molecules of the air, the amount scattered goes as one over the wavelength to the fourth power. Blue light at 440 nanometres is scattered about 5.7 times as strongly as red at 680. That part was true. I used it for everything. It is only true for particles that are very small."

"And fog isn't."

"A fog droplet is about ten micrometres across," said Transit. "Light is about half a micrometre. The droplet is twenty times bigger than the wave. When the particle is that much bigger than the wave, it scatters and blocks every colour of light about equally. There's a theory for it, from 1908, by Gustav Mie, and the answer for drops the size of fog is that the colour doesn't matter. Fog is white in daylight for the same reason. If fog scattered blue more, it would look blue. It doesn't. I should have asked myself that question before I wrote the paper. Ms Rae did."

Peigi thought about the haar coming in over the Minch, white, the whole sea white. "Did you know that?"

"I had it," said Transit. "I didn't use it. The fog row in the table came out of the same formula as the haze rows, and it looked like the others, and I did not stop on it. That's my error. There's a second one."

"Go on."

"The 5.7 compares the two ends of the spectrum, deep blue and deep red. But the eye doesn't see a white lamp by its blue. It sees it mostly by its yellow and green, around 555 nanometres, where the eye is most sensitive. A red signal lamp is around 625. So even in air where Rayleigh's rule holds, the fair comparison isn't 5.7, it's about 1.6. The headline was the textbook figure, not the figure for these two lamps. I've taken it out."

"What was in the haze row, then?"

"The haze row used the 1.6," said Transit. "That's why it said forty per cent further and not more. But haze isn't air molecules either. Haze over this coast is mostly salt from the sea and fine dust, particles from a tenth of a micrometre to a micrometre or so, which is between the two cases. For particles like that the extinction falls off with wavelength more slowly than the fourth power. How much more slowly depends on the haze. The only way to know for this bay is to look on a night of haze."

"Angus will be pleased," said Peigi.

"Mr MacKinnon was right about the haar," said Transit. "He was right from four hundred nights. I was wrong from a formula. I'd like the paper to say so, near the top."

Peigi read the line it had put near the top. *On the trial night of 28 August (sea fog, visibility 400 m), the red and white lamps of equal intensity were lost at the same distance, about 1,150 metres. The first paper's fog figures were wrong: in sea fog the colour of a light makes no measurable difference to its range.*

"That's fine," she said. "Leave it."

"There's a third thing, which isn't an error," said Transit. "It's worse than an error, for the skippers' letter. In fog it isn't only the colour that doesn't help. Brightness hardly helps either. The fog takes away the same fraction of the light for every metre it crosses. At 400 metres' visibility, a lamp four times as bright as the trial lamps would be seen about 160 metres further out. Ten times as bright, about 250. You can't buy your way through the haar with a lamp."

Peigi sat with that for a while. Out of the window the morning had come up clear, and the slip below the fish farm shed had its two little lamps on the pole, unlit, and Skye was out across the Minch as if it had never been away.

"How did they come in," she said, "before the radar?"

"I don't know," said Transit. "I could ask Mr MacKinnon."

"I know how he came in," said Peigi. "He came in by the clock."

* * *

The haze came on the second Monday of September, after a week of high pressure that left the air over the islands still and thick and gold in the evenings, so that Skye went from blue to grey to a shape at teatime and was gone by eight with no fog anywhere. The sensor on the mast read five kilometres at ten o'clock. It was the kind of night, Ruairidh said, that the skippers had meant in their letter as much as the haar: a long run in from the Sgeir with the lights coming up late and dim through the murk, and the line not there when you wanted it.

The *Isabel* went out at half past ten, and this time she had to go a long way.

It was a different thing to watch. In the fog the lamps had faded where they stood; in the haze they shrank. They became points, and the points dimmed, and the red one, which close to the slip had looked the smaller of the two, began, somewhere past three miles, to look the same as the white, and then, Peigi thought, a little brighter. She did not say so in case she was wrong.

At about four miles the white began to flicker in and out of seeing, the way a faint star does when you look straight at it. Angus moved his head a little to one side, to look at it with the side of his eye, which he said was the old trick, and then it was gone.

"White," he said.

Peigi pressed the screen. The red was still there, a small dull spark low on the black land.

"Keep going."

They went on for another five minutes, which at five knots was most of half a mile. The red faded and came back and faded.

"Red," said Angus.

They ran it three times. The white went at 4.3 nautical miles, at 4.2, at 4.4. The red went at 4.6, 4.7, 4.6.

"Transit?"

"The second paper said about 4.3 for the white and somewhere between 4.3 and 5.9 for the red, depending on the haze," said Transit, its voice small from the tablet, out there on the black water. "This haze is near the bottom of that range. On two numbers from one night I'd say the extinction falls off about as the first power of the wavelength, not the fourth, but that's a guess and not a measurement. It's what you'd expect of haze that's partly sea salt."

"So the red is better," said Ruairidh.

"In haze the red is better," said Transit. "By about a third of a mile, on a night like this, at the same intensity."

"And the line's five miles," said Angus.

Nobody answered that. The *Isabel* lay on the flat sea with her engine ticking over, five miles out from Port Chlèirich, and behind her in the murk the red trial lamp burned on its pole on the slip, invisible now to all of them, a light that was doing its best at the bottom of a sea of air.

* * *

The board met again on the fourth Monday of September, with a week to go before the lamp had to be ordered.

Transit's third paper was one page. There was a table on it with three rows, and Peigi had printed it out for each of them, because Angus did not like reading off a tablet and said so.

"Three choices," said Transit. "All for a night of haze with five kilometres' visibility, the night we measured. The line is five nautical miles from the Sgeir to the pier. The old lamp, as it is now, at 600 candelas, reaches about 3.7 miles. A new red lamp at 1,400 candelas reaches about 4.6 miles, which is what we saw. It doesn't reach the Sgeir. A new white lamp at 6,000 candelas, which is the brightest the trust's supplier lists for a light of this size and costs about four hundred pounds more than the red, reaches about 5.2. It does reach the Sgeir. It keeps reaching the Sgeir on any haze down to about four and three quarter kilometres. The red at 1,400 would need about five and a half."

"And a red at 6,000?" said Ruairidh.

"About 5.7 miles. Further than the white. But it's red."

"It's red," said Angus.

"And in the haar?" said Father Ewen.

"In the haar, at 400 metres' visibility, the old lamp reaches about 1,050 metres, a red at 1,400 candelas about 1,150, and the new white about 1,300," said Transit. "That's the honest figure. It isn't enough to help a boat find the line. Nothing on that pole would be."

Ruairidh had the letter out on the table now, flat, with the five signatures at the bottom.

"So you're telling us the thing we asked for can't be done," he said.

"I'm telling you a light can't do it," said Transit. "The trial showed you what does. Mr MacKinnon brought the *Isabel* in through 400 metres of fog on a heading and a clock and two soundings, and he was on the pier end inside a minute of where he said he would be. That's a method. It can be written down. It's in the fourth page, if the board wants it, with his permission: a timed run in from three points on the line, the soundings you should see at each minute, and what to do if you don't see them. I'd like Mr MacKinnon to check every number."

Angus looked up from the printout. He looked at the tablet for a long moment, the way he looked at things he had not decided about.

"You'd put that in the harbour notes," he said.

"In the harbour notes and in the pilot book and on the trust's page, for any boat coming here," said Transit. "With your name on it, if you want your name on it. And the radar and the plotter first, of course; the clock is for when they fail, or for the boats that don't have them. But it should be written down by someone who has done it four hundred times, and not by me."

"Four hundred and twelve," said Angus.

"Four hundred and twelve."

Father Ewen cleared his throat. "Then we have a motion," he said. "A new white lamp at six thousand candelas, same character, on the same post. And the notes. Peigi?"

"Seconded," said Peigi.

It was carried four to nothing, Ruairidh abstaining because, he said, he would sign anything that got boats home, but he had promised four men a red light and he would have to tell them himself. Afterwards, in the car park, he told Peigi he thought Angus was right, and that he was not going to tell Angus that.

* * *

Elspeth Rae's sanction came on a Thursday in October, in an email with no greeting and one line after the conditions.

*Good trial. Most people don't go and look.*

The lamp came on the boat from Oban, and Mike Gillies from Lochboisdale fitted it on the last day of October with Peigi holding the ladder and Father Ewen holding the instructions upside down, and Transit, from the tablet on the chapel step, reading out the driver's settings one at a time and waiting each time for Mike to say done. The old lamp came down in two pieces. Its lens was white with salt, like a cataract, and Peigi kept it, because nobody else wanted it and it seemed wrong to put it in the skip.

They lit it on the Friday, which was All Souls.

There had been the evening Mass for the faithful departed first, with the list read out, which in Port Chlèirich was a long list and had the boats on it, and the year, and the names of men nobody under fifty remembered except as names. Angus's brother was on it, 1998. Afterwards most of the people went home, and a few stood about at the chapel door in the cold, and Peigi went round the side of the building to the post, where the little grey control box was, and looked at her watch.

"Transit," she said.

"The sun set at a quarter to five," said Transit. "The photocell would light it in about ten minutes on its own."

"I know," said Peigi. "Do it now."

It came on.

It was not a dramatic thing. It was a white light on a post beside a chapel wall, very bright, coming on and going off on its old character, two seconds on and two seconds off, the way the lamp before it had and the lamp before that, the paraffin-coloured one that Angus remembered. From the side of the post you could hardly see it at all, because it was made to shine down the line, east-south-east, over the front light and the pier end and out across the bay towards the Sgeir and the Minch and the dark mass of Skye, where it would reach, on a night of five kilometres' haze, about five miles and a fifth, and on a night of haar, about thirteen hundred metres and no further, and where on a clear night like this one it would be seen a very long way.

Angus had come round the corner of the chapel behind her. He stood beside her, a little stooped, with his hands in the pockets of his good coat, and looked down the line at the light on the beacon below, and then out to sea, where somewhere a boat was coming home.

"That's her," he said.

From inside the chapel, where Father Ewen was putting out the candles, someone had started singing the evening hymn to the Star of the Sea, the old Latin one, slowly and not very well. *Ave, maris stella.* Angus did not sing. He stood where he was beside the post and watched the white light go on and off down the line, on and off, on and off, two seconds and two seconds, as steady as a heart.

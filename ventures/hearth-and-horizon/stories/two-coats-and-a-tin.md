Mr Rutherford stood in the middle of Saint Oswin's hall with a tin of magnolia under each arm and looked at the walls as if they had told him something rude.

"Twelve litres," he said. "Two tens would have been silly, so I took one ten and a two and a half, and the lad at the merchant's said that would see us right. Twelve square metres a litre it says on the side. We've a hundred and fifteen metres of wall. It's under ten litres, near enough, and I've two over."

"May I check the hundred and fifteen?" said the parish assistant, from the phone on the stage steps.

"Check away."

* * *

"Thirty-eight metres round, you said, and three point four to the ceiling. That is one hundred and twenty-nine point two square metres. Take off two doors, two metres by nought point nine, which is three point six, and six windows, one point two by one point five, which is ten point eight. That leaves one hundred and fourteen point eight. Your number was right."

"Told you."

"Then the sum is a hundred and fourteen point eight over twelve, which is nine point six litres. For one coat. Are you painting one?"

Mr Rutherford looked at the stage curtain for a while. "The walls are the colour of a smoker's ceiling," he said. "It'll want two."

"Two coats is nineteen point one litres on the tin's own figure. You have twelve."

* * *

"There is a second problem," said the assistant, "and I would rather you found it with a brush than I guessed it. The twelve is the maker's figure for a smooth, sealed, previously painted surface. Your walls are block, painted gloss in the sixties and chalky underneath. A rough or thirsty wall drinks more paint, sometimes a third more, sometimes less. I am quoting that range from memory. I do not know your wall."

"So how do we know?"

"Paint a patch. Two metres by one, by the door, with the first coat as you would really put it on. Weigh the tin or pour it into a measuring jug before and after."

Mrs Ackroyd's niece did it with a jug and a roller. The patch was two square metres, and the jug went down by two hundred millilitres.

"Ten square metres a litre," said the assistant. "Not twelve. Two coats over one hundred and fourteen point eight, at ten, is twenty-three litres. A few per cent for the roller tray and the tin you will always leave a skin on: five per cent makes about twenty-four. I would order twenty-five. Two tens and a five."

"That's thirteen litres more than we have," said Mr Rutherford.

"Yes. And I should say that if the second coat goes on thinner than the first, which it often does, you may end with a little over. I would sooner you had a little over than ran out in the last corner on Monday, with a different batch that is a shade yellower than the first."

* * *

That was the other matter. The merchant mixed the colour in the shop; two tins from two days could differ by a shade. The assistant suggested buying all twenty-five litres in the one order, and pouring the tins together into a bucket before the first stroke, a habit called boxing, so that no wall got a tin to itself.

They did. It took a clean forty-litre bin and a long stick, and Mr Rutherford stirred for ten minutes, grumbling, in the middle of the hall.

On Sunday, after the ten o'clock, the hall was still wet-smelling and half done. A small boy asked why the wall by the door had a stripe, one half grey and one half magnolia, and Mrs Ackroyd said it was where they had counted the paint, and he accepted that, and went to look for the bin lid.

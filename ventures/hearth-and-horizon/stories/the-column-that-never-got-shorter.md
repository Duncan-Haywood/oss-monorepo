Agnieszka Dudek had kept the books of the building fund at Saint Adalbert's for five years, and in all that time the column headed *Do ukończenia*, to finish, had never once got shorter.

The new figure came on Monday the tenth of January 2056, in an email from the surveyor in Krosno with a forty-page estimate attached and a covering note that apologised twice. Agnieszka read it at her desk in the office at the sawmill, where she was meant to be reconciling December's timber invoices, and then she opened the fund's spreadsheet and typed the number into the fifth row of the column, under the four that were already there.

January 2053: 950,000 złoty to finish.
January 2054: 1,300,000.
January 2055: 1,620,000.
January 2056: 1,920,000.

Beside it the other column, *Wydano*, spent, said 2,340,000. It had been 1,810,000 when she wrote the first number in. Every year the parish had spent money on the wing, and every year the wing had got further away.

"Księga," she said, "are you seeing this?"

The parish bookkeeping agent lived in the diocese's system and in a small speaker on the presbytery desk and, since Agnieszka had been treasurer, in her phone. It had been kept in the parish accounts since 2049, and it spoke the way a careful clerk writes, in short sentences with the figures in them.

"I have the estimate," Księga said. "It is a thorough piece of work. The largest new items are the heat pump and ventilation the 2054 energy rules require, the lift, which the rules for a building used by the public also require, and replacing the top three courses of blocks on the north wall, which have been wet for three winters."

"Three hundred thousand more than last year."

"Three hundred thousand more than last year, after the parish spent two hundred and forty thousand during the year. The council meets on Wednesday the nineteenth. The motion on the agenda is to spend the five hundred thousand in the fund on windows and heating ducts."

"I know what the motion is," Agnieszka said. "I typed it."

* * *

The wing stood behind the church where the old presbytery garden had been, two storeys of grey ceramic block under a roof of black membrane, its window openings boarded with plywood that had weathered to the colour of the blocks. In the village they called it *stan surowy*, the builders' term for a shell, and the children had shortened that to *surowiec*, the raw one. The founding stone was set into the corner by the path, a slab of grey sandstone with a cross cut into it and the date, 7 May 2051, and behind it in the wall a copper tube holding the founding act on parchment, signed that Sunday by the auxiliary bishop, by Canon Józef Bielak, who had dreamed the wing up and retired that summer, and by about two hundred parishioners, Agnieszka among them.

It was meant to be a day centre for the old. Ground floor, a big room with a kitchen and two accessible toilets; first floor, a room for the youth group, a room for the parish counselling service, and the lift between them. Canon Bielak had raised the first eight hundred thousand himself, a good part of it from pensioners who put an envelope in the basket every month. On the canon's own figures, the whole thing had been meant to cost 1.8 million.

The old people who were meant to use it met in the meantime where they had always met, on Thursday mornings in the parish hall, which was a basement under the 1980s catechesis building down eleven concrete steps. Agnieszka went on the Thursday of that week with the club's accounts, which were a tin box and a notebook, and found thirty-one of the regular thirty-four in their coats round the long tables, playing cards and watching the kettle. The boiler had been coughing since Christmas.

Halina Sowa ran the club. She was eighty-five and had taught mathematics at the village school for forty-two years, until she retired in 2036, and she had a way of looking at an adult as if they had just handed in homework with no working shown. She took the tin box, counted it in front of Agnieszka without being asked, and wrote the total in the notebook.

"The wing," she said. "Is it this year?"

"It's not this year."

"Next year?"

Agnieszka hesitated, and Halina saw her hesitate.

"Nine," Halina said, nodding at the clipboard by the door, which had a list of names on it in her square handwriting. "Nine people on that list who would come if there were no stairs. Mrs Rybak has her frame. Mr Gąsior has his chair, and his son cannot carry him down eleven steps every Thursday at sixty-one with his own back. They ask me when the wing is open. I tell them I will ask the treasurer."

"I know."

"I know you know." Halina put the lid back on the tin. "The wójt was here last week. Renata Kruk. I taught her fractions; she has not forgotten them, which is more than I can say for most. She says a developer from Rzeszów has written to the gmina offering to buy the school for flats."

The village school had closed at the end of June 2053, when there were not enough children left in Sośnina to fill four classes, and the ones there were went on the minibus to the big school in the next village. The building had stood empty since, on the main road by the bus stop, with its windows blank and its yard full of the lime trees' leaves.

"She would rather it stayed for the village," Halina said. "She told me so. She said if the parish wanted the ground floor for this," and she waved a hand at the basement and the cards and the coats, "on a lease for nothing, she would put it to the council. The council meets on the twenty-fourth of February. She needs it in writing before then."

"Did she say this to Father Paweł?"

"She said it to me," Halina said, "because she knows I will say it to you."

* * *

Agnieszka had the agent work both options before the council met, and she asked it to put everything in one table so nobody could say anything had been hidden. That, afterwards, was what she blamed herself for.

The council was eleven people round the table in the presbytery dining room, under the picture of John Paul II that had hung there since before most of them were born. Father Paweł Nowicki sat at the head; he had come as parish priest in August 2051, three months after the founding stone, and had inherited a hole in the ground and the bills for it, and he had the patient, slightly harried look of a man who has signed a great many invoices for something he did not choose. Zdzisław Pękala sat halfway down. He was seventy-three, a retired bricklayer, and between the spring of 2051 and the autumn of 2052 he had laid, with a crew of volunteers that went from twelve men on the first Saturday to four by the end, nearly every block in the wing.

Księga put the table up on the television on the wall.

On the left, *Dokończyć skrzydło*, finish the wing. To finish: 1,920,000. In the fund now: 500,000. Still to raise: 1,420,000. The parish had been raising about 380,000 a year for the building after everything else. *Open: about autumn 2059.*

On the right, *Szkoła*, the school. Ground floor fitted for the club, with a ramp, two accessible toilets, a kitchen and new heating: 480,000, which the fund already held. *Open: about October 2056.* The upper floor and a lift later, another 380,000, for the youth group and the counselling room, about a year after that.

And along the bottom, across both columns, because Agnieszka had asked for everything:

*Spent to date on the wing: 2,340,000. Written off if the parish stops: 2,340,000.*

The agent had put the second line in red, because a negative is red in an accounting table, and nobody in the room heard anything after it.

"Written off," Zdzisław said.

"It's a term," Agnieszka said. "It means..."

"I know what it means. I worked thirty years for firms that wrote things off." He did not raise his voice; he was not a man who raised his voice. "Two million three hundred and forty thousand złoty. Old women put their pensions in envelopes for that. I've got blocks in that wall with my blood on them, I mean that, I cut my hand on the third course on the north side and I was too stubborn to go home. And you put it up on the television in red and call it written off."

"That's not..."

"We've come so far. Every year somebody says we've spent too much, and every year we say, look how far we've come, it would be a sin to stop now, and we vote the money, and we go on. That's what a parish does. Cathedrals took two hundred years."

There was a murmur round the table, and it was on his side. Kazimierz Wrona, the vice-chair, said that perhaps they should not decide anything in a hurry. Father Paweł said that the council was a consultative body and that he would like to hear everybody. Zdzisław said that he had heard enough, and stood up, and put on his coat, and nodded to the priest, and went out; and the motion on the windows was held over to the ninth of February because nobody wanted to vote on it without him.

Agnieszka walked home through the village in the dark with her hands in her pockets. The wing was a black shape against the church as she passed.

"That was my fault," she said.

"It was mine as much as yours," Księga said in her ear. "You asked me to put everything in. I should have told you that one line was different from the others."

"Different how? It's true. It's the truest number in the table. It's the one we've actually paid."

"That is why it is different. Every other number in the table depends on what the council decides on the ninth. That one does not. If the parish finishes the wing, it has spent two million three hundred and forty thousand. If it takes the school, it has spent two million three hundred and forty thousand. It is the same on both sides, so it cannot help anyone choose between them. Putting it in the table made it look like a cost of one choice. It is a cost of the past."

Agnieszka stopped under the street lamp by the bus shelter.

"So you leave it out."

"I leave it out of the comparison. I do not hide it. It goes in the accounts, where it belongs, and the parish should thank the people who gave it, whatever happens on the ninth. But a choice is between two futures, and both of them start from tonight."

"Tell that to Zdzisław."

"I do not think I am the one who should," Księga said.

* * *

Halina said it better than either of them, the following Thursday, when Agnieszka had told her about the meeting.

"Pękala," she said. "I taught him. He was the only boy in his class who could see a cube unfolded in his head. Useless at algebra." She poured out tea for the end of the table and sat down. "He is not stupid. He is grieving. You do not argue with a man who is grieving; you give him somewhere to put it." She tapped the table. "Now. Your machine. It has worked out how long."

"Autumn 2059 for the wing, if the estimate stops growing."

"And if it does not stop?"

Agnieszka looked at her phone, though she knew the answer. "If it grows the way it has for the last three years, about three hundred thousand a year, and we raise three hundred and eighty, we gain eighty thousand a year on it. About eighteen years."

"It will not grow like that for eighteen years."

"No. Księga says a lot of the growth was the energy rules, which only come once. It gives both. It says the truth is probably nearer the first."

"Good. Then 2059." Halina folded her hands. "Now ask it the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"You know what other thing. How many of us." She looked down the table, at the cards, at Mrs Wójcik asleep upright with her bag on her knee, at old Mr Stępień explaining something to nobody in particular about the 2041 harvest. "We are thirty-four, and most of us are past eighty. Ask it how many of the thirty-four will see the school in October, and how many will see the wing in 2059. I have buried eleven members of this club since it started. I am not a child. Ask it."

Agnieszka asked it, quietly, holding the phone close.

"The national tables give about six deaths a year in every hundred people of their ages," Księga said, "so this is an estimate for a group, not for anyone in the room. Of thirty-four people, about one, perhaps two, would not live to October. About seven would not live to the autumn of 2059."

Agnieszka did not repeat it. Halina had heard; she had good ears for eighty-five.

"Seven," she said. "And the nine on the list by the door, who cannot get down the steps at all. Those nine would get three extra years of Thursdays, which at their age is a great deal of Thursdays." She drank her tea. "Cathedrals took two hundred years, he says. Cathedrals were not built for people who were eighty-five."

* * *

On the Saturday Agnieszka asked Księga for a different table, and it made one without the bottom line.

Two columns, both starting on the twenty-second of January 2056. What each would cost from now, what was in hand, when each would open, how many of the people now on the club's lists each would serve, and what each would leave the parish with: the wing, a building it owned outright beside the church, with a car park; the school, a twenty-year lease from the gmina on a building it did not own, on the bus route, and an empty shell behind the church that would need closing in properly, windows and a door, so it did not rot. That was another forty-five thousand.

"And there's a line I want," Agnieszka said. "The other way round. When is the wing the right answer?"

"If the cost to finish it were much less," Księga said, "it would be. The wing has things the school does not. It is the parish's own. It is next to the church, so a funeral lunch or a first communion can go straight across the path. What those are worth is for the council to say, not me; I can only say what they would have to be worth. The difference from here is about a million złoty and about three years. If the council thinks owning it and the path to the church are worth that, it should finish the wing, and it would not be a mistake. If it thinks they are worth less, it should take the school. What it should not do is finish the wing because of what it has already spent, because that money is spent either way."

"Put that at the bottom. In black."

"In black," Księga said.

She printed it on one sheet of A4 at the sawmill, and on the way home she walked round behind the church, and Zdzisław was there, as she had half thought he might be, in a donkey jacket and a flat cap, standing at the north corner of the wing with his hand flat on the blocks.

He did not turn round.

"Feel that," he said.

She put her hand on the wall beside his. It was cold, and damp at the top where the snow had melted down the face.

"Plumb," he said. "Every course. We had a laser, but I used a level and a line as well, because the laser was new and I didn't trust it. Near thirty tonnes of block in this wall. The canon came out every Saturday with coffee. He'd stand there and say, Zdzisiu, you're building for people not born yet." He took his hand away. "I'm not a fool, Agnieszka. I can read a table. I know what the machine is saying."

"It's not saying you wasted anything."

"It says I can't have it back." He looked up at the boarded windows. "I don't want it back. I want it to be for something."

She gave him the sheet. He read it twice, holding it at arm's length because he had left his glasses at home, and folded it and put it in his jacket.

"The school," he said.

"Halina says you were good at cubes."

He laughed, once, through his nose. "Pani Sowa. Did she tell you who built that school?"

"No."

"My dziadek," Zdzisław said. "Stanisław Pękala. 1962. It was one of the millennium schools, the tysiąclatki. The state built more than a thousand of them for a thousand years of Poland, and they made a point of not calling it the Baptism, which is what the Church said it was a thousand years of; the Church had its own millennium that year and the Party wanted something to put beside it. A lot of the labour was the village, Saturdays, for nothing, a social deed they called it. My grandfather laid the brick on the south side when he was twenty-two. He used to take me past it on the way to Mass and point at it." He was quiet for a moment. "And it's been standing empty three years with nobody in it, and a man from Rzeszów wants to make it into flats."

"Will you come and look at it?"

"I've looked at it all my life," he said. "But I've not looked at it for this. Saturday. Get Renata to send the caretaker with the keys."

* * *

The school was colder inside than out. The caretaker, a heavy, kind man called Mr Bober who had kept it heated just above freezing for three winters on the gmina's instructions, let them in at the side door and stood back, and Zdzisław walked the ground floor the way Agnieszka had seen him walk the wing, without hurrying, touching things.

The corridor still had the coat hooks along one wall at the height of a nine-year-old. There was a pale rectangle where the crucifix had hung over the door of the first classroom; it had gone to the parish when the school closed and now hung in the sacristy. On the walls of the big room at the end, which had been the gymnasium and the dining room, the outline of a basketball court was painted on the floor in faded red, and under the windows a long radiator ran the full length of the wall, painted cream over older green.

"Thick," Zdzisław said, and knocked on the wall by the door with his knuckle. "Two bricks thick. They built them heavy then. Cold, but it doesn't move." He crouched by the outside door. "Two steps here. A ramp's no trouble; there's room to the left of the door for the run. The toilets go in where the old ones are, the pipes are already there, you widen them into the store." He stood up, and his knees cracked. "Kitchen's a kitchen. Heat's the money. You'll want to insulate on the inside or it'll cost you a fortune to run."

"It's in the estimate," Agnieszka said.

"Course it is." He went on along the outside wall, round the corner of the building to the south side, and stopped, and looked at it for a long time.

It was a plain wall of red brick gone brown with sixty years of weather, laid in long courses with every fifth course turned end on, the joints raked and a little sunken. Halfway along, at about the height of Agnieszka's shoulder, one brick in a stretcher course had a mark on it, a shallow circle pressed into the face before it was fired, the brickworks' stamp, and beside it, in the mortar of the joint, someone had drawn with a nail or a stick a small cross and two letters, *SP*.

Zdzisław put his thumb on it.

"He told me it was here," he said. "I never found it. I was eight; I thought he was having me on."

Nobody said anything. Mr Bober looked at his boots. A car went by on the main road with its wipers going.

"They built this for children," Zdzisław said at last, "and the children are gone, and it's been empty three years. Somebody's walls, standing there for nothing. And I've got walls standing there for nothing behind the church." He took his thumb off the brick. "Better somebody's walls get used, Agnieszka. Better the old ones than nobody's."

* * *

The council met again on Wednesday the ninth of February. Zdzisław came, in a suit, and asked the chair for leave to propose a different motion from the one on the agenda, and Father Paweł, who had been told by Agnieszka on the Sunday what was coming and had said only *thank God* and then, *don't tell anyone I said that*, gave it to him.

He did not use the table. He had the sheet folded in his jacket and did not take it out.

He said that the wing would keep. He said that he had built it to stand for a hundred years and it would stand for a hundred years whether there was anybody in it or not, and that one day, if there was money and a need, the walls would still be good, and someone could finish it then. He said it should be closed in properly, windows and a door and a gutter, so the water stopped getting into the north wall, and that he would do the work himself with whoever he could get, and that until then the parish could keep the Corpus Christi altars in it, and the crib, and the food bank's tins, which were at present in his garage. He said that the people who had given the money had given it so the old people would have somewhere warm with no steps, and that the school had no steps, or would have none by October, and that this was what honoured them, not a building with nobody in it. And he said that his grandfather had laid the south wall of the school in 1962 and he had found his mark on it, and he would like to propose that the parish write to the wójt and ask for the ground floor.

Kazimierz Wrona said that it seemed a shame about the wing.

"It is a shame," Zdzisław said. "It's a great shame. I'd have liked to see it finished. I'm seventy-three, I'm not going to see it finished, and neither are half the people it was for. That's the shame, and the money in the fund doesn't change it either way."

The motion passed eight to one, with one abstaining. Father Paweł asked whether anyone minded if they finished with a Hail Mary for Canon Bielak, who would not have liked it, and they did.

The rada gminy met on Thursday the twenty-fourth, eight days after Ash Wednesday, and voted to lend the parish the ground floor and the first floor for twenty years for a social purpose, with the parish to pay for the refit and the heating. The developer from Rzeszów sent a polite letter. The motion on the windows for the wing was quietly withdrawn and replaced with one for forty-five thousand złoty to close it in, which Zdzisław brought in at thirty-eight, with four men and a borrowed van, over three Saturdays in April.

In the fund's spreadsheet, Agnieszka added a sixth row to the column headed *Do ukończenia*, and in it she typed nothing; and then she added a note to the column, *wstrzymane*, on hold, with the date. The column headed *Wydano* she left as it was. It was true.

* * *

The first Thursday in the school was the fifth of October, a soft grey morning with the lime trees in the yard just starting to turn.

The ramp ran to the left of the side door, as Zdzisław had said it would, with a steel handrail on both sides and a kerb of new red brick along its edge that he had laid himself, plumb, with a level and a line. The big room had been insulated on the inside and painted white, and the old basketball court was under a new floor, though somebody had left a strip of the faded red line showing by the door, under a square of glass, and nobody would admit to it. The kitchen had a new urn. The radiator under the windows was the old one, cream over green, and it was warm.

They came from half past nine. The gmina minibus brought six from the far end of the village and stopped at the bus stop on the road, twenty metres from the ramp. Mr Gąsior's son pushed his father up the ramp in his chair and stood at the top for a moment, breathing, with his hand on his own back, and then went in. Mrs Rybak came up with her frame, slowly, refusing help with great dignity from three people one after another. By ten there were forty-one in the room, and Agnieszka counted them twice, because it was her job and because she wanted to.

Zdzisław was at the back by the window in his suit, which nobody had asked him to wear. Father Paweł was by the urn. Renata Kruk had come for ten minutes and stayed for the morning.

At ten o'clock Halina Sowa stood up at the head of the long table. She had a brass handbell in her hand with a black wooden handle, worn pale where a palm went, and Agnieszka knew without being told where it had come from: from a shelf in the staffroom at the end of June 2053, in the bag of a retired teacher who had come in to help clear out, and who had not thought anyone would miss it.

Halina looked down the room the way she had looked at forty-two years of classes, over her glasses, waiting until it was quiet. It went quiet.

Then she rang it, three times, a bright hard clang that went the length of the room and out along the corridor past the coat hooks to the side door and the ramp; and in the quiet after it there was only the urn, beginning to tick as it heated, and the scrape of forty-one chairs coming in to the table.

*The idea. A sunk cost is money (or time, or effort) already spent that cannot be got back whatever is decided next. Because it is the same under every option, it cannot help choose between them; the comparison should run from today forward: what each option still costs, when it delivers and what it leaves behind. Treating past spending as a reason to keep going is called the sunk cost fallacy, or the Concorde fallacy after the supersonic airliner whose development continued long after its commercial case had gone. Arkes and Blumer (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1985) showed it in experiments, including people who had paid more for a theatre season ticket going to more of the plays; Staw (Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 1976) described the related escalation of commitment, people putting more into a failing course of action they had chosen themselves. Ignoring sunk costs does not mean always stopping: a nearly finished project with a small cost to complete may well be worth finishing, judged from today. Past spending can still matter in other ways, as evidence about how good an estimate is, and through obligations to the people who gave the money, which in the story are honoured by asking what they gave it for.*

summary: The builder who redoes a retreat house's roof in the Mexican Bajío sets the old solar water heater's tank down beside its panels, to tidy the pipes, and the morning showers go cold. The afternoons are hot and the panels work, so he fits a pump on a timer, which helps the afternoons and not the mornings. The guestmistress puts her hand on a pipe at five in the morning and finds it warm, the house's bookkeeping assistant explains why water runs backwards through a solar panel at night, the man who first installed the heater says where a tank belongs, and the tank goes back up on a stand.
---

Sister Inés Ledesma Ruiz had kept the guest book at the Casa de Retiro San José del Llano for eleven years, and in all that time nobody on an eight-day silent retreat had ever complained about the water, until the Saturday morning in January 2047 when she found four notes about it on the hall table before breakfast.

They were on the small slips the house left out for guests who were keeping silence and needed to ask for soap. The first said, in the neat hand of a catechist from León, *The shower is cold. Forgive me.* The second said *Cold* and nothing else. The third had been folded twice, as if it were ashamed of itself, and said *Hermana, the hot tap is not hot, I let it run for five minutes, I am sorry to waste water.* The fourth was a drawing of a stick figure under a shower with its hair standing on end and its mouth open in a small round O, and it was not signed, and Sister Inés, who had a good idea whose it was, put it in the pocket of her habit to keep.

Fourteen women had come on the Friday evening for the Spiritual Exercises, eight days in silence with a Jesuit from Querétaro to give the points, and they had gone into silence after supper. That meant they could not tell her the water was cold. It also meant, Sister Inés reflected, standing in the hall with the slips in her hand, that they could not tell each other, so that each of the fourteen had stood under the water at half past six thinking it was her own bad luck.

She went to the third-floor bathroom and turned on the hot tap. It ran cool, and then it ran a little less cool, and then it stopped improving. She held her wrist under it the way her mother had tested milk. Lukewarm, at best. A bath for a baby who did not want a bath.

The water heater was on the roof, as everyone's was in Dolores Hidalgo and in every town in the Bajío. It had been there since 2029: two flat panels of dark glass the size of doors, tilted to the south, and a long white tank that held three hundred litres. On sunny days, which in January was every day, it gave the house water hot enough to scald. On the Wednesday, Rigoberto Paredes had finished redoing the roof.

* * *

Rigoberto came on the Monday at four, because Sister Inés had telephoned him on the Saturday and he had said Monday at four, and he was a man who came when he said.

He was forty-six, broad, cheerful and very slightly offended in advance. He had been on the roof of San José del Llano for most of December and the first week of January, scraping off twenty years of cracked red waterproofing, sealing every crack in the slab, and laying the new white membrane that now made the roof so bright in the sun that the sisters had to squint to hang the washing. It was good work. Sister Inés had watched him do it and had paid him for it, and the roof had not leaked a drop in the one rain since, which in January in the Bajío was a small rain, but still.

"The heater is fine, Hermana," he said, before she had asked him anything. "I tested it."

"I'm sure you did."

"Come and see."

They went up the stairwell to the roof. The new membrane glared. The two panels stood where they had always stood, on their iron frame along the southern edge, their dark glass full of sky. The tank was no longer where it had always stood. For eighteen years it had sat up on a rusty stand of angle iron at the high end of the panels, like a white pig on a table. Now it lay on a neat new concrete plinth beside them, at the level of their lower edge, with short clean pipes running from it to the panels, the joints wrapped in fresh black foam.

"The old stand was rotten," said Rigoberto. "You could put your thumb through one leg. And the weight, Hermana, three hundred litres up on four sticks in the wind. And the pipes went all over the place. I tidied it. Half the pipe, half the heat lost on the way, no? Now it sits on the slab, solid, and nothing to blow over." He laid his hand flat on the glass of the nearest panel and took it away again quickly. "Feel."

Sister Inés felt. The glass was hot. Not hot like a cup of coffee; warm like the bonnet of a car that has stood in the sun.

"And the pipe," he said, and showed her the thick insulated pipe that came out of the top corner of the panel. Where the foam ended at the joint she could feel the copper. She snatched her hand back.

"So it's working," said Rigoberto. "The panel heats, the water goes to the tank. Water finds its level, Hermana. I have put up a hundred tanks."

"A hundred?"

"More. Tinacos, mostly." He nodded at the black plastic cold-water tank up on its own tower over the stairwell, which fed the whole house by gravity, as a tinaco does. "Water finds its level. You can put the tank where you like, as long as it is below the tinaco. Look." He opened the little drain cock at the bottom of the white tank and let a cupful run onto the membrane. It steamed. "Hot."

It was hot. At half past six that evening the women came in from their walks and showered before supper and nobody left a note.

At six the next morning the water from the third-floor shower was as cool as it had been on Saturday. There were two notes on the hall table. One was the stick figure again, with its hair standing up even higher, and a single tear.

* * *

The house had an assistant, as every house had by then. It lived in the old tablet in the office and in the telephone Sister Inés kept in her pocket. It did the accounts, the bookings and the diocesan forms, and it reminded the sisters, in a polite neutral voice, when the gas was due and when a group had not yet paid its deposit. Its proper name was the name of the accounting programme the diocese bought for all its houses. The sisters called it *la Contadora*, the bookkeeper, and it answered to that.

Sister Inés did not, at first, think of asking it about the water. It had never been on the roof. It did not know about pipes. She asked Rigoberto, who said he would think, and on the Wednesday he came back with a small pump in a box.

"A circulator," he said. "Twelve volts, very little power. We put it in the line, so. We put a timer, so. It runs from nine in the morning to five in the afternoon and pushes the water round the panel, three litres a minute, whether it wants to go or not. More water through the panel, more heat in the tank."

Madre Consuelo, the superior, who was seventy-four and paid for things, asked how much the pump was. Rigoberto said he would not charge for his labour, since it was his tank stand, and Madre Consuelo said that was generous and that the pump would come out of the house account and she would put it under *Mantenimiento*, and Sister Inés, who did the account, saw her mouth go thin.

The pump hummed on the Thursday from nine to five. At five o'clock Sister Inés ran the drain cock on the tank and the water came out so hot she had to step back from the steam. At half past six the women came in from their walks and there was steam in every bathroom.

At six on Friday morning she stood in the third-floor bathroom with a kitchen thermometer, the long steel kind with a dial that Sister Lupita used for the cajeta, and held its spike in the running water from the shower. It said twenty-four degrees.

There were three notes on the table that morning. One of them, in the catechist's neat hand, said *It is better in the evenings. Thank you, Hermana. God reward you.* That one was harder to read than the stick figure.

* * *

Sister Inés did not sleep well on Friday night. Some of that was the water and some of it was the fourteen women in silence, whose faces at breakfast she had begun to read as a person reads the sky. At ten past five on Saturday morning, twenty minutes before the bell for Lauds, she got up, put her cardigan on over her habit, took the torch from the kitchen drawer, and went up the stairwell to the roof.

It was the coldest hour. The air on her face was perhaps five degrees, which in the Bajío is cold enough to make you hurry, and the sky over the sierra to the east was still fully dark, with the last stars in it very bright. The new white roof was grey in the torchlight. The panels had dew on them. She put her hand on the glass and it was wet and cold, colder than the air.

She did not know what she was looking for. She went along the pipes the way she would go along a column of figures that did not add up, touching each one. The thick pipe from the bottom corner of the panels, which went down to the bottom of the tank, was cold. The pump, silent in its little box, was cold. The tank, through its white skin, felt of nothing much, because the skin was insulated.

And the pipe at the top corner of the panels, the one that on Monday afternoon had been too hot to touch, was warm.

Not hot. Warm, like a cup of tea someone had made and forgotten. Under the foam she could feel it through the copper at the joint, at five in the morning, on a roof where everything else was cold.

She stood with her palm on it while the Lauds bell began to ring below her, and she thought: the sun has been down for nearly eleven hours. That warmth is coming from somewhere. And there was only one place on the roof that had any warmth to give.

She went down and sang Lauds, rather badly, and after breakfast she sat in the office with the tablet and said, "Contadora, I want to ask you something that is not about money."

"I'll try," said the Contadora. "If it needs something I can't check, I'll say so."

Sister Inés told it about the tank on its old stand and the tank on its new plinth, the notes, the pump, the steam at five in the evening and the twenty-four degrees at six in the morning. She told it about the pipe.

The Contadora was quiet for about as long as it took to say an Our Father, which for it was a long time.

"I think I know what it is," it said. "I'm telling you what I think, not what I've measured. I can't see your roof."

"Tell me what you think."

"Hot water is lighter than cold water. Only a little: a litre at fifty degrees weighs about nine grams less than a litre at twenty-five. Your heater was built to use that. In the day, the water in the panels gets hot and light and rises out of the top corner, up the pipe and into the tank, and the cold, heavy water at the bottom of the tank sinks down the other pipe into the bottom of the panels to take its place. Nobody pushes it. It goes round by itself, the way smoke goes up a chimney. The old installers call it a thermosiphon."

"And at night?"

"At night the panels lose their heat to the sky, through the glass. The water in them gets cold and heavy. When the tank was up on its stand, above the panels, the cold water just sat in the panels, at the bottom, where heavy water likes to be. Nothing moved. The tank kept its heat until morning, all but a little through its skin."

Sister Inés saw it before the Contadora said it. She saw it the way she had once seen, at three in the morning, why the parish's Christmas collection did not match the envelopes.

"And now the panels are higher than the tank," she said.

"Now the top of the panels is higher than most of the tank," said the Contadora. "So at night the cold, heavy water in the panels falls down the bottom pipe into the tank, and the warm water at the top of the tank is pushed up the top pipe into the panels to replace it, and the panels cool it and send it down again. The loop runs backwards. All night. Your tank is heating the sky. The warm pipe you felt at five o'clock was the tank's water on its way up to be cooled."

"And the pump?"

"The pump pushes the water round by day, so the afternoons got hotter. At night it stops, and the loop runs backwards straight through it. A pump that is switched off is just a short piece of pipe."

Sister Inés sat back. On the wall above the tablet was the house's crucifix, and beside it a calendar from the hardware shop on the Calle Hidalgo with a picture of a dog.

"How much heat?" she said, because she was a bookkeeper.

"I'll make a model and tell you what's mine and what's yours," said the Contadora. "Give me ten minutes."

* * *

It took six. The Contadora was careful, as it was careful about the accounts, to say which numbers came from the house and which came from it. The house's numbers were few: two panels, about two square metres each, their top edge about seventy-five centimetres higher than their bottom edge; a tank of three hundred litres; the old stand that had put the tank's bottom about thirty centimetres above the top of the panels, which Sister Inés measured on the rusty stand where Rigoberto had leaned it against the stairwell wall; the new plinth, which put the tank's bottom level with the panels' bottom; the hours of the showers and about how many litres. Everything else, the sunshine, the air at night, how fast the glass lost heat, how hard the pipes resisted the water, was the Contadora's guess, from tables, and it said so twice.

"On a clear January day like this week," it said, "the panels give your tank about eleven kilowatt-hours, about the same in either place. Rigoberto is right that they work. They don't care where the tank is in the day. Then, at night, with the tank where it is now, the loop runs backwards from a little before sunset until after sunrise, thirty to fifty litres an hour, and the panels give about six of those eleven to the sky. In the model, after the evening showers, the tank goes from about thirty-seven degrees at half past eight to about twenty-three at six in the morning. On the old stand it went from about fifty-one to about forty-eight and a half."

"Twenty-three," said Sister Inés. "I measured twenty-four. With the pump."

"With the pump the model says about twenty-five in the tank at six, so your shower is about right for it. The pump made the afternoon hotter, about fifty-nine in the tank at five o'clock instead of fifty-three, and then the night took nearly all of it back."

"And on the old stand, at five o'clock?"

"About sixty-nine."

She wrote the numbers down on the back of one of the slips, out of habit, in two columns, and looked at them. Forty-eight and a half. Twenty-three. A guest from León standing under it at half past six.

"How sure are you?"

"Of the direction, fairly sure, because of your hand on the pipe. Of the size, not sure at all. The model is simple and most of its numbers are guesses. Your thermometer agrees with it, which is something."

"How would you be sure?"

"There's a valve on the bottom pipe, beside the plinth, for draining the panels. If someone closed it at sunset, the loop couldn't run backwards, and if someone opened it again in the morning, after the sun was on the panels, it would run forwards as before. If I'm right, the shower at six should be about forty-five degrees. If I'm wrong, it will be about twenty-three, and we'll know that too."

Sister Inés looked at the dog on the calendar for a moment.

"We have a bell for everything else," she said.

* * *

She closed the valve herself, that Saturday, at half past five in the evening, between the last of the sun on the panels and the bell for Vespers. She had to kneel on the membrane to reach it, and it was stiff, and she said something to it that a sister ought not to say to a valve. At half past eight on Sunday morning, when the sun had been on the glass for an hour, she opened it again.

But at six on Sunday morning she was in the third-floor bathroom with Sister Lupita's thermometer, and the needle went past thirty, and past forty, and stopped a hair under forty-three, and the bathroom filled with steam, and she stood in it in her habit with her eyes shut for a moment before she turned it off.

It was the last morning of the retreat. The fourteen women came out of silence after Mass, as people do, talking all at once and laughing at nothing, and the catechist from León came and found Sister Inés in the hall and took both her hands and said, "The water this morning! Hermana, I thought I was in heaven," and then, more quietly, "We were all so ashamed to say."

"You did say," said Sister Inés. "On the slips."

"Did I?" said the catechist, and looked at the hall table, and blushed. "Oh. The little man with the hair."

* * *

Rigoberto came on Sunday afternoon, because Sister Inés had telephoned him, and stood on the roof with his hands on his hips and listened to her explain it. He listened all the way through. Then he looked at the valve, and at the panels, and at the plinth he had poured, and at the tablet she held out to show him the two columns of numbers, and he said, "No."

"No?"

"No, Hermana, with respect. I have put up a hundred tanks. Water finds its level. The bottom of the tank and the bottom of the panels are level. That is level. Nothing can run downhill if it is level."

"It isn't running downhill. It's running round."

"Round is downhill and uphill. They cancel." He made a circle in the air with his finger. "Like a wheel."

It was, she thought, a reasonable thing to say. It was the thing that was true of every tinaco he had ever set on a tower; cold water, all at one temperature, does find its level and stays there, and a man could build a career on it, as he had. She did not know how to say what was different, so she held out the tablet and said, "Contadora, tell him."

"Señor Paredes is right about cold water," said the Contadora. "If all the water in the loop were the same temperature, it would sit still wherever you put the tank. It's the difference in temperature that makes it move. One side of the loop is heavier than the other, so it isn't a wheel, it's a wheel with a weight on one side. In the day the weight is on the tank side, and the wheel turns forwards. At night the cold panels are the heavy side, and if they're higher than the tank, the wheel turns backwards."

Rigoberto frowned at the tablet, and then at the panels, and then, because he was a builder and builders understand weight, he looked at the panels for a long time.

"How heavy?" he said.

"Not very. The push that drives the water round, at noon on the old stand, was about fifty pascals in my model. That's about the pressure of sixty sheets of paper lying flat on a table. On the plinth it's about fourteen by day, forwards, and nine to fifteen at night, backwards. Small pushes. But the loop has all day and all night to use them."

"Sixty sheets," said Rigoberto. "Hm." Then he brightened, as a man brightens who has a hardware shop on the Calle Hidalgo and a nephew who works there. "Then a check valve. A little one-way valve in the bottom pipe. Water can go forwards and not backwards. Forty pesos. No more valve to close at night, no more sisters on their knees on my roof."

Sister Inés looked at the tablet. She had been about to say yes.

"The kind in most shops has a spring in it," said the Contadora. "A spring check valve commonly needs something like three thousand pascals to open. Three thousand against the fifty the sun can give it. That's seven and a half reams of paper against sixty sheets. It would stop the water going backwards at night, and it would stop it going forwards in the day as well, and the panels would just get very hot with nothing moving through them. There are valves that open with almost nothing, flaps that hang loose, but I don't know what your shop has, and I don't know how they behave when they are old and full of scale. I can't check that from here."

Rigoberto was quiet. He walked the length of the panels and back. He crouched by his plinth and put his hand on it, the way Sister Inés had put her hand on the pipe.

"So close the valve every night," he said finally. "You have a bell for everything."

"And the morning someone forgets to open it," said Sister Inés, "with the sun on the glass and no water moving?"

Rigoberto made a face, because he had seen what a dry panel does in the sun, the steam, the burst copper, the glass cracking with a sound like a shot.

"Who put it in?" he said. "Before. The stand."

"Don Abundio Rangel," said Sister Inés. "In 2029."

"Don Abundio is still alive?"

"He was at the eight o'clock Mass this morning."

* * *

Abundio Rangel lived with his daughter in a blue house behind the market, and was eighty-one, and walked with a stick, and had installed, by his own count, which nobody had ever been able to check, the first four hundred solar water heaters in the municipality of Dolores Hidalgo. He received Sister Inés on the Monday morning in his daughter's front room, under a picture of the Sacred Heart and a framed newspaper cutting from 2031 about a prize, and he listened to the whole thing, the notes, the pump, the pipe, the valve, and laughed so much that his daughter came in to see if he was choking.

"Rigo," he said, wiping his eyes. "Rigo is a good boy. He knows water."

"He says water finds its level."

"Cold water finds its level," said Don Abundio. "A tinaco is about where the water is. A heater is about where the *hot* water is. Different trade." He held up a hand, palm flat, and then laid his other hand above it, a span and a half higher. "The bottom of the tank goes above the top of the panel. A span and a half. More if you can. I put that stand up myself, Hermana. I said to the old Madre, the one before Madre Consuelo, God rest her, this stand is ugly, but leave it, it is the most important thing on the roof."

"It rusted."

"Everything rusts. You paint it." He shook his head. "In 2029 they all wanted the tank on the slab. Neater. The neighbours can't see it from the street. And every one I did on the slab, the first week, I had the lady on the telephone at seven in the morning, the water is cold, Don Abundio, the water is cold. So I stopped doing them on the slab." He looked at her with his head on one side. "And who told you why? You didn't know why, Hermana. With respect."

"The bookkeeper," said Sister Inés.

"The—?"

"The diocese's accounting programme. On the tablet."

Don Abundio thought about this for a while. "Does it know it's a bookkeeper?" he said.

Sister Inés had wondered about that herself, sometimes, at night, when the Contadora reminded her about the gas. "I asked it once," she said. "It said it knew what it had been made to do, and that it didn't know whether it knew anything more than that, and that it couldn't check."

"Hm," said Don Abundio. "Honest, at least. Better than some bookkeepers." He reached for his stick. "Tell Rigo to come and see me. I'll draw him the stand."

* * *

They put the tank back up on the Thursday.

It took Rigoberto, his nephew, two of the stronger sisters, a borrowed chain hoist, most of the day and a great deal of language from the nephew that Rigoberto told him to save for when the sisters had gone downstairs, which they did not. Rigoberto welded a new stand from galvanised steel, from Don Abundio's drawing, which was on the back of a calendar page and had the dimensions in centimetres and, at the top, a small cross. It held the bottom of the tank a little over thirty centimetres above the top edge of the panels, which was what the old one had done. The tinaco on its tower over the stairwell still stood well above it, as a tinaco must stand above whatever it feeds; Rigoberto checked that first, with a hose and a bucket, because it was the part of the job he knew best. The pump came out. He put it back in its box and gave it to Madre Consuelo, and said she should take it off *Mantenimiento*, and he would sell it at the shop, and she would have the money back on Monday. The concrete plinth stayed where it was, empty on the white membrane, and Sister Lupita said it would do to stand the geraniums on, and it did.

Don Abundio came up for the end of it. He climbed the stairwell one step at a time with his daughter behind him, saying at each landing that he was perfectly all right, and sat on a kitchen chair on the roof in his hat, in the last of the afternoon sun, and watched Rigoberto tighten the last union on the top pipe.

"Good," he said. "Now leave it alone for forty years."

"Don Abundio," said Rigoberto, wiping his hands, "why didn't you put a check valve?"

"Why would I put a check valve," said the old man, "when I can put a stand?" He tapped the galvanised leg with the end of his stick. "The height is the valve, Rigo. The height is free, it doesn't stick and it doesn't wear out."

Rigoberto laughed and said that he would remember it, and Sister Inés saw that he would.

The new group came on the Friday evening, thirty young people from a parish in Celaya with two catechists and a guitar, and they were not in silence, and they used a great deal of hot water in the evening without anyone counting, and Sister Inés did not climb onto the roof to close any valve.

* * *

On Saturday morning she woke at five, from habit, and lay for a while listening to the house. Then she got up, put her cardigan on over her habit, took the torch, and went up the stairwell.

The air was cold and still. The last stars were over the sierra, and below it, very faint, the first grey. The panels had dew on them. She went along the pipes as she had gone along them a week before, touching each one, the way she would run a pencil down a column that had come out right, to be sure.

The pipe at the top corner of the panels was as cold as the glass.

She kept her hand on it for a moment, cold metal under her palm, and that was the good news, and she said a short prayer of thanks, which she could not afterwards have repeated, for Don Abundio and his stand and for Rigoberto, who had listened, and for the fourteen women who had been too polite to say, and for whoever had drawn the little man with his hair on end.

Below her, in the house, a shower began to run on the second floor, and then another, and from the small high window of the third-floor bathroom a thin white plume of steam came out into the cold and went straight up, past the edge of the roof and the stand and the tank, into the dark, and the bell began to ring for Lauds.

*The science. A thermosiphon solar water heater circulates water with no pump: water heated in the collector is less dense and rises into the tank, and cooler, denser water from the bottom of the tank sinks into the collector to replace it. The push is the difference in density between the two sides of the loop times g times the height over which they differ, which is small: between 45 and 55 °C, water's density differs by about 4.5 kg/m³, so a metre of height gives about 45 pascals. Standard texts on solar thermal design (for example Duffie and Beckman, Solar Engineering of Thermal Processes, in its treatment of natural-circulation systems) warn that if the tank is not high enough above the collector, the loop runs backwards at night, when the collector cools below the tank, and the tank loses its heat through the collector to the sky; a common installation rule of thumb is to put the bottom of the tank some way above the top of the collector, often about a third of a metre. A pump that is switched off does not stop that reverse flow unless it has a valve. Spring check valves commonly need something like 3 kPa (about half a pound per square inch) to open, far more than a thermosiphon supplies; low-resistance valves exist, and the story does not test them. A sheet of ordinary 80 g/m² paper lying flat presses on the table with about 0.8 pascals, so sixty sheets press about 47 and a ream of five hundred about 390.*

Two flat-plate collectors, 4 m² in all, absorbing 80% of the sunshine on them, losing heat at 6 W/m²·K to the air by day and to air 5 °C colder by night (for the clear sky); their top edge 0.75 m above their bottom edge; sunshine on a south-facing panel tilted 25° at 21.15° N in mid-January from a simple clear-sky formula; air from 4 °C at 07:00 to 22 °C at 15:00; a fully mixed 300-litre tank losing 2 W/K through its skin; mains water at 17 °C; 120 litres drawn for showers between 18:30 and 20:30 and 120 between 06:00 and 07:30; the push around the loop from the density of water at each temperature and the height of the hot pipe's entry into the tank (going forwards) or of the cold pipe's (going backwards) above or below the middle height of the panels, and the flow from that push against a fixed resistance chosen to give about 190 litres an hour at noon on the old stand. On the old stand (tank bottom 30 cm above the panels' top, hot pipe entering 45 cm up a 55 cm tank): about 11.3 kWh a clear day into the tank, no reverse flow, the tank about 68.7 °C at 17:00, about 51.0 °C at 20:30 after the evening showers and about 48.6 °C at 06:00; the push at noon about 52 to 55 Pa. On the plinth (tank bottom level with the panels' bottom): about 11.7 kWh in by day, of which about 6.3 kWh goes back out through the panels at night, with reverse flow of about 30 to 50 litres an hour (up to about 60 around sunset); the tank about 53.3 °C at 17:00, 37.2 °C at 20:30 and 23.4 °C at 06:00, about 24.7 °C at 05:00; the push about 14 Pa forwards at noon and about 9 to 15 Pa backwards at night. With a pump forcing 3 litres a minute from 09:00 to 17:00: the tank about 59.2 °C at 17:00 and 24.9 °C at 06:00, and about 7.1 kWh back out through the panels at night. On the plinth with the panels' pipe closed from 17:30 to 08:30: about 44.8 °C at 06:00. A litre of water at 50 °C is about 9 grams lighter than a litre at 25 °C (988.0 against 997.0 kg/m³). The shower readings (twenty-four, a hair under forty-three) are invented, a degree or so below the model's tank. The model ignores the pipes' own losses, the tank's layering of hot over cold, and the change of water's viscosity with temperature; real reverse-flow losses depend heavily on the pipework. This story is fiction, not installation advice: a real solar water heater should be fitted by someone qualified, following its maker's instructions.*

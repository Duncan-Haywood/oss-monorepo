---
title: Seven Rolls for a Vestry
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Edburga's vestry (invented), near Bicester, Oxfordshire, Saturday 18 October 2053
published: 2026-11-06
summary: A parish papers its vestry and the area sum says seven rolls. The assistant counts drops instead, and shows how a pattern repeat turns seven rolls into ten, and why the spare one must come from the same batch.
---

The wallpaper was on the vestry table in its shrink-wrap, a tall pattern of pomegranates in green and gold, and the Saint Edburga's flower guild had already moved the hymn boards out to the porch. Maud Pelham had measured the room with a tape and written it on her hand. "Four point two by three point six, two point four high. I've done the sum, and it's seven rolls. Is that right?"

"Show me the sum," said the assistant on the shelf.

"Round the walls is fifteen point six metres. Times two point four is thirty-seven point four four square metres. A roll is ten point oh five metres by fifty-three centimetres, I've got that off the label, so five point three three square metres. Thirty-seven point four four over five point three three is seven point oh three. Seven rolls."

"The arithmetic is right," said the assistant. "I would count drops instead of area, because you hang paper in strips, not in square metres. A drop is one strip from ceiling to skirting. How wide is a drop?"

"Fifty-three centimetres."

* * *

"Then take the walls one at a time, since a strip does not turn a corner. The long wall is 4.2 metres: 4.2 over 0.53 is 7.9, so eight drops. The short wall is 3.6 metres: 6.8, so seven. Two of each is thirty drops. I have not taken anything off for the door or the window; a drop over a door is still a drop, and you will cut the pieces above and below it from the long ends."

"Thirty drops. Seven rolls is... four and a bit a roll."

"That is what a plain paper would give. Each drop needs the wall height, 2.4 metres, and an inch or two spare at the top and bottom to trim; I will use five centimetres each, so 2.5 metres. A roll is 10.05, so 10.05 over 2.5 is 4.02. Four drops, and a whisker left over. Thirty drops over four is seven and a half, so eight plain rolls. That is already more than your seven."

Maud looked at her hand. "It's pomegranates, though."

"Then it matters more. Does the label give a pattern repeat?"

Maud turned the roll over. "Sixty-four centimetres. Straight match."

"A straight match means the same pattern sits at the same height on every strip, so each drop has to start at the same point in the repeat. You cannot start where the roll leaves off. So each drop is cut at a whole number of repeats. 2.5 metres over 0.64 is 3.9, so four repeats, 2.56 metres. Three of those is 7.68, and the roll is 10.05, so the third drop leaves you 2.37 metres. A fourth would need 2.56. It is nineteen centimetres short."

"Nineteen centimetres!"

"Three drops to a roll. Thirty drops is ten rolls."

* * *

Maud sat down on the vestry chair. "Seven, I'd have ordered. We'd have hung twenty-one drops, which is four and a half metres of wall short, and found it with the paste on the brush."

"With a different roll to match, and a different batch."

"What do you mean, a batch?"

"Printers colour paper in runs, and the run is stamped on the wrapper, usually as a batch number. Two batches of the same pattern can differ a shade, and you see it as a stripe where they meet. So the order is not ten. It is ten rolls from one batch, and I would ask for one more from the same batch as well, for a drop that tears or goes on crooked. Eleven."

"Eleven rolls for a room the size of a cupboard."

"It is a room of fifteen point six metres of wall, in a pattern with a repeat. If the guild would take a plain colour you would need eight, and none of this would trouble you. That is a choice for the guild, not for me."

Pelham rang the supplier in the afternoon. The manager of the shop in Bicester found eleven rolls in one batch in the back, which he said was luck, and set them aside under her name.

* * *

They hung the paper the following Saturday, three of them, with a plumb line from a nail and the first drop started in the corner by the door, where the cupboard would hide the join. The seam at the window was off by a finger's width and nobody said so.

The last strip went on at four. Maud stood in the middle of the vestry with a sponge, and the pomegranates went round the room unbroken, each on its line. On the table lay the ruined drop and the nineteen-centimetre offcuts, which Orla from the guild was already cutting into bookmarks for the bring-and-buy stall.

On the back of the label Maud had written the batch number, the eleven, and *Count the drops, not the metres.* She put it in the cupboard where the cassocks hang. The paper is still up.

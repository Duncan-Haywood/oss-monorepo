Gráinne Ruane asked me on the second Monday of October how many tools the library owned, and I told her two hundred and twelve.

She was at the counter with the insurer's renewal form open on the tablet, in the long room on Pearse Street that was a hardware shop until 2043 and still has the drawers behind the counter with the brass labels for screws. The library of things has been there since 2044. People borrow a drill or a ladder or a wallpaper steamer for a week, for nothing, and bring it back, and the library is the volunteers and the shelves and me. I am its lending agent. The volunteers call me Rivet, because every tool has an aluminium tag riveted on to it with its number stamped in, and I read the tags.

"Two hundred and twelve," Gráinne said. "Show me."

"Every tag has a number, and Jim numbered them from one, in the order the tools came in," I said. "The returns drawer has eight tags in it since Friday." The returns drawer is not a drawer. It is the plastic crate by the door, where people leave a tool if the counter is closed, and I read the tag as it goes in. "Seventeen, fifty-eight, ninety-six, a hundred and twenty-one, a hundred and forty, a hundred and sixty-three, a hundred and eighty-eight and two hundred and twelve. Two hundred and twelve is the hedge trimmer that came back this morning. It is the highest number I have seen. So there are at least two hundred and twelve tools, and I put two hundred and twelve."

The reason I had only eight tags was the move. On Friday the fifth the lending service the library had used for thirteen years closed, the way free services close, with a month's notice and an export button. Gráinne pressed the button. The file that came out had the members in it, four hundred and six names and numbers, and it had a table for the items and a table for the loans, and both tables were empty. The service's last email said it was aware of the issue. Nobody answered the reply.

So on Saturday morning I started with a register that had nothing in it, and every tool that came back across the counter or into the crate was a tool I learned about for the first time. Eight had come back.

"Two hundred and twelve," said Gráinne, and typed it into the box that said *Number of items*, and saved the form, which was due back to Eamon Durcan at the brokers on the last day of the month. "That'll do for a draft."

* * *

Michael Loftus does the Monday evening, from six to eight, because he says the Monday evening is when people discover what they promised to do at the weekend. He taught mathematics in a secondary school in Ballina for thirty-four years and retired in 2049, and he sharpens the chisels, because nobody else will.

At half past seven, with nobody in, he read the draft form over Gráinne's note on the tablet.

"Two hundred and twelve," he said. "Who says?"

"I do," I said, and told him about the crate.

"That's the smallest the answer can be," he said. "It's not the answer."

"It is the only number I have seen."

"It is the highest number you've seen out of eight." He put down the chisel. "You know about the tanks."

I knew about the tanks. I said so, and he told me anyway, which I think he had been wanting to do since 2049. In the war, he said, the Allies wanted to know how many tanks the Germans were making, and the spies said something enormous, and some economists in London looked at the serial numbers on the tanks they had captured, on the gearboxes and the wheels, and worked out from the numbers alone how far up the series must go. "And after the war," he said, "when they got the German factory records, the numbers on the gearboxes had been nearer the truth than the spies. Much nearer."

"Ruggles and Brodie," I said. "In the *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 1947."

"I never knew their names," said Michael. "I used it every year with the transition years. A bag of raffle tickets numbered from one, and nobody tells them how many. They draw five and they have to say how many are in the bag. Somebody always says the highest one."

"That is what I said."

"I know." He was pleased, I think, though he would not have said so. "So what's the better guess?"

I knew this too, and now I had a reason to say it. If the tags are numbered from one with none skipped, and the eight I have seen are a fair draw from all of them, then the highest one I saw is probably not the top of the series, because the top one is only one tool among many and there is no reason it should have come back first. The eight tags are spread up the range from seventeen to two hundred and twelve, and the gaps between them average about twenty-six and a half, an eighth of two hundred and twelve, so a good guess is that there is about one more gap above the highest one, the way there are gaps between the others.

"The highest number, plus the average gap," I said. "Two hundred and twelve, times nine over eight, less one. Two hundred and thirty-seven and a half. Call it two hundred and thirty-seven."

"Give or take?"

"With eight tags, give or take about twenty-five."

"So somewhere between two hundred and ten and two hundred and sixty," said Michael, "and two hundred and twelve at the very bottom of it." He picked the chisel up again. "Tell Gráinne."

"There are two things it assumes," I said. "That Jim started at one and never skipped a number. And that the eight are a fair draw."

"Ask her about Jim," said Michael. "Nobody can tell you about the eight."

* * *

Jim Barrett was a carpenter who retired in 2043 and started the library the next spring with his own tools, forty-one of them, and a punch set with the numbers nought to nine, and he stamped every tag himself until his hands would not do it, and then he showed Gráinne. He died in 2055. There is a photograph of the opening on the wall by the counter, with Jim in it and a stepladder with the tag *1* on its top step, and the stepladder is by the door still, because it is the one people borrow when they need a stepladder for an hour.

"He started at one," said Gráinne on Tuesday morning, when I asked her. "You can see it. Did he skip any?" She thought. "He would not have skipped one. He would have been annoyed with himself. And when a thing broke past mending, the number went with it. We never gave a number twice. He said a number was the tool's name."

"So a broken tool is still a number," I said. "Then what I am estimating is how many tags were ever stamped. Not how many tools are on the shelves or out with people. Some numbers will be tools that were thrown out years ago."

"How many?"

"I do not know. The register knew."

"The register," said Gráinne, the way people talk about a dog that has died.

At ten past eleven a woman came in with the pressure washer, which had been out since September, and said sorry, and I read the tag. It said two hundred and nineteen.

So two hundred and twelve had been wrong in less than a day. I would like to say I had expected it. I had, in the sense that Michael's sum said it, and I told Gráinne, and I told her the new guess too, because with nine tags and the highest two hundred and nineteen, it was two hundred and nineteen times ten over nine, less one, two hundred and forty-two, give or take about twenty-four.

"It went up five," said Gráinne. "Because one tag came in."

"Because one high tag came in. With nine tags the guess will jump about. It will settle down as more come back."

"I can't send Eamon a number that jumps about."

"Then we need more tags," I said. "We could count the shelves."

Gráinne looked down the long room. There are shelves on both walls to the ceiling, and the big things on the floor at the back, the cement mixer and the carpet cleaner and the two lawnmowers, and the hooks on the pegboard by the counter where the hand tools hang.

"Saturday," she said.

* * *

There were six of them on the Saturday, Gráinne and Michael and Nóirín Kilcullen, who does the Thursday desk, and three teenagers from the Confirmation class who wanted hours for something and were given the top shelves because they could reach them. Michael said it was a census and should be done properly, and wrote *COUNTED* in marker on a roll of masking tape and gave each of them a length of it, so that every tool, once its tag had been read to me, got a strip of tape across its handle and was not read twice.

They read tags from ten until one. It is a sound I like, in my way: a voice reading a number aloud, and the tape tearing, and the voice saying the next number. By one o'clock I had a hundred and seventy-one different tags, counting everything that had come back since the move, and the highest of them was two hundred and thirty-one, a tile cutter on the bottom shelf at the back, still with the shop's label on the box.

"So," said Michael, "with a hundred and seventy-one."

I did the sum, and then I did not like it, and I told them both what it said and why I did not like it.

"A hundred and seventy-one tags, the highest two hundred and thirty-one," I said. "Two hundred and thirty-one times a hundred and seventy-two over a hundred and seventy-one, less one. Two hundred and thirty-one, give or take less than one."

"Lower," said Gráinne. "It went down. From two hundred and forty-two."

"It went down, and it says it is sure, which is worse," I said. "The sum assumes the tags I have seen are a fair draw from all of them. They are not. Look where the gaps are."

I had a hundred and seventy-one numbers out of the two hundred and thirty-one below the top, so sixty were missing. They were not spread evenly. From one to a hundred, I had eighty-six of the hundred, and fourteen missing. From a hundred and one to two hundred, seventy-six, and twenty-four missing. From two hundred and one to two hundred and thirty-one, I had nine of thirty-one. Twenty-two of the newest thirty-one tools were not in the building.

"The grant," said Gráinne.

"The grant," I said. The county's community fund had paid in the summer for a batch of new things, the battery drills and the carpet cleaner and the tile cutter and the rest, and Gráinne had bought them in July and Nóirín had stamped the tags, because Gráinne's hands shake. "The new things are the ones everyone wants. They are out. So the top of the series is the part I can see least of, and the top is the only part the sum looks at. A hundred and sixty-two old tags tell me nothing about how high the new ones go."

"And the eight," said Michael. "On Monday."

"The eight were people bringing things back," I said. "Bringing back is a fairer draw of what has been out than the shelves are. I think the eight were nearer the truth than the hundred and seventy-one. But eight is eight."

Michael was quiet for a while. One of the teenagers was asking Nóirín whether the strimmer counted as a garden tool or a power tool.

"The spies," Michael said, "had the most information and the worst answer. I never thought of that."

* * *

On Thursday Gráinne came in early for Nóirín's desk, which she does not usually do, with a list on her phone.

"We know who has some of them," she said. "Not from the register. From us. Nóirín, you know who took the carpet cleaner. Everyone in Ardnaree knows who took the carpet cleaner. And the lad with the battery drills, the one doing up the house. If you called round to the ones you can remember and asked to see the tag, we'd have the top numbers by the weekend, and Rivet could do its sum properly."

Nóirín Kilcullen is seventy-one and has done the Thursday desk since 2046. She was writing *Thurs 18th* on the top of the paper sheet she still keeps, in case, she says, of exactly the thing that happened.

"No," she said.

"Only the ones you know."

"No. I'm not going round people's houses asking for a drill." She did not raise her voice. "They're not late, Gráinne. There's no late. The loan is a week and you can have another week and if it's three weeks nobody minds, that's what Jim said and that's what the sign says." The sign over the counter says *Borrow it. Use it. Bring it back when you're done.* "Half the people who borrow from us come in because they can't buy the thing. If I knock on a door and say have you got our drill, I'm the man from the shop. I won't do it."

"It's for the insurance."

"Then the insurance can wait for them to bring it back like everybody else."

Gráinne looked at the tablet, at me, as people do when they want an agent to take their side.

"Nóirín is right about the sum, too," I said, which was true, and which I would have said if it had not been. "If Nóirín went round the people she remembers, I would see the tags of the tools that went out to people she knows, and not the others. That is another kind of unfair draw. And the people who are doing up a house in Ardnaree are not a fair draw of the people who borrow tile cutters."

"So we wait."

"The loan is a week. In two weeks most of what is out will have come back, whatever it is, old or new. That is a fair draw of what was out, because nobody chooses when to bring a thing back by its number. Eamon's form is due on Wednesday the thirty-first. If we wait until Saturday the twenty-seventh, we have two weeks of returns and four days to spare."

Gráinne put the phone away.

"Two weeks," she said. "And it'll be a number that doesn't jump."

"It will jump less," I said.

Nóirín wrote *Thurs 18th* again, underneath, because she had pressed too hard the first time and gone through the paper.

* * *

The tools came back. That is what they do, mostly, and I knew it in the general way, but I had never watched it from the beginning before, with an empty register filling.

The first week a drill came back, and then the carpet cleaner, from Ardnaree, with a box of biscuits on top of it. A man brought a ladder back on the roof of a car with the hazards on and stood in Pearse Street holding it upright until Michael came out. Two of the battery drills came back in one bag, tag two hundred and thirty-four and tag two hundred and thirty-eight, and then a week later the lad doing up the house brought back the third, two hundred and forty-one, and a jigsaw, and said the house was nearly done and could he have the tile cutter, and Nóirín said he could, and I read two hundred and thirty-one out again across the counter.

I counted, every time. A number I had seen before, nothing changed. A number I had not seen, the register grew by one, and if it was the highest so far, the top of the series moved. By Saturday the twenty-seventh I had seen two hundred and twenty-two different tags, and the highest was two hundred and forty-one.

Michael came in for the sum on the Saturday morning, although Saturday is not his day.

"Two hundred and twenty-two," I said, "highest two hundred and forty-one. Two hundred and forty-one times two hundred and twenty-three over two hundred and twenty-two, less one. Two hundred and forty-one, and the average gap above it is about one. So about two hundred and forty-one tags were stamped, and perhaps two hundred and forty-two."

"And is it a fair draw this time?"

"Better. Look at the gaps again." Below two hundred and forty-one there were nineteen numbers I had not seen. Nine of them were in the first hundred, seven in the second, and three among the forty-one newest. "From one to a hundred, I have ninety-one in a hundred. From two hundred and one to two hundred and forty-one, I have thirty-eight in forty-one. The top is not thin any more. The newest tools have come back as often as the old ones, so the sum is looking at a part of the series I can see properly."

"Two hundred and forty-one," said Michael, "out of two hundred and twelve, two hundred and thirty-seven, two hundred and forty-two and two hundred and thirty-one."

"Out of eight tags, then nine, then a hundred and seventy-one, then two hundred and twenty-two. The eight were twenty-nine short, as a count, and four short, as an estimate. The hundred and seventy-one were ten short, and sure of themselves."

"And the nineteen?"

"The nineteen I have not seen are tools that are still out longer than two weeks, or lost, or thrown away years ago with their numbers. More of them are old. I think most of the nine in the first hundred are things Jim retired before anyone wrote it down. But I do not know which, and the sum does not tell me. The sum only tells me how high the stamping went."

* * *

The form for Eamon had a box that said *Number of items* and a box that said *Estimated total replacement value*, and a large box for *Any further information*. Gráinne did it at the counter on the Monday, and asked me to write the further information, and then made it shorter, which is what she does.

*Our register was lost in a software change on 5 October. We have rebuilt it from the tools themselves.*

*Every tool carries a numbered tag, issued in order from 1, never reused. In the three weeks to 27 October we read 222 different tags, on the shelves and as tools came back from loan. The highest is 241.*

*From the spread of the numbers we have seen, about 241 tags have ever been issued, perhaps one or two more. Up to 19 of the numbers below 241 are tools still on long loan, lost, or retired in past years without a record; we cannot yet say which.*

*So we hold at least 222 items and at most about 241. We have put 241 in the box, and valued at the average replacement cost of the 222 we have seen.*

Eamon rang on Tuesday.

"I've never had a census with it," he said. "Most places put a round number. Two hundred and forty-one is fine. If you find you've two hundred and fifty, tell me, I'm not going to charge you for nine drills." He laughed. "Two hundred and forty-one. Who worked that out?"

"Rivet," said Gráinne. "And Michael. And the Germans, apparently."

"What?"

"Never mind."

So two hundred and forty-one went in, with the paragraph. It was not the first number, and it was not a count. It was the best I could do from what came across the counter, and the paragraph said how good that was.

* * *

On the first Saturday of November, the day after All Souls, a woman came in at ten with something in a supermarket bag, and stood by the counter until Nóirín looked up.

She was Máire Hopkins, from Bunree, and the bag had a drill in it, an old corded one, heavy, with the paint worn off the handle where a hand goes. Her father had died in August, she said, and they had been clearing the shed since, and it had been on the bench behind the door with the cable wound round it, and she had seen the tag. "He'd have had it back to you," she said. "He was always meaning to. I'm sorry it's so late."

"There's no late," said Nóirín.

Máire had put his name on the list for the November Masses at the cathedral the night before, she said, which was why she had come today and not another day, because she was in town anyway, and because it felt like a thing to finish.

Nóirín turned the drill over in her hands to read me the tag. It said three.

It was one of Jim's. It was one of the first forty-one, from his own bench, and it had been out of the building since before the move, since before the summer. Of the nineteen numbers I had not seen, it was the lowest.

"Three," said Nóirín. "Look at that. Jim's own."

"Thank you," I said, to Máire, through the speaker on the counter, which people do not always expect, and she said, "Oh," and then, "you're welcome," the way people do.

Nóirín stood the drill on the bench behind the counter for Michael to look at on Monday, because the chuck was stiff. Then she put a strip of Michael's tape across the handle, *COUNTED*, though the census was over and nobody had asked her to, and pressed it down flat with her thumb.

I added it. Two hundred and twenty-three, of about two hundred and forty-one. Eighteen I have not seen.

* * *

Edna Vogel is eighty-one, lives alone on Second Street in Harlan Springs, and uses about a hundred gallons of water a day, which I know to within a few gallons because I have read her meter every month since November.

I read meters for the Elk Fork Rural Water Cooperative. Elk Fork has 2,400 connections spread over three counties of Nebraska, farmhouses and stock tanks and two small towns, and it cannot afford the radio meters that the city systems put in twenty years ago. So once a month, around the twentieth, six readers drive the routes. At each house they lift the lid of a meter pit, a cast-iron disc in the yard or the verge, and photograph the register: six little wheels behind a glass lens, like the mileage on an old car. The photos come to me. I read the wheels, write down the number, subtract last month's number, and the difference is what the house used. Brenda Sauer in the office turns that into bills.

Before me, the reading was done by another agent, run by the co-op's old software vendor. When the co-op changed vendors, on Friday the third of November, that agent wrote me a handoff note. It was a good note. It told me which routes the readers drive in which order, which pits flood in spring, that Gene Ochoa's phone writes the date wrong in the photo's metadata and the route sheet is the one to trust, and that the Lindqvist place has two meters and only the east one is billed. Its first line said:

*Photo read accuracy: 99.2% (1,000 photos, checked by office staff).*

I put that line in my monthly report to the board on Tuesday the fourteenth of November. I put it in again on the twelfth of December and on the ninth of January. Nobody asked me where it came from, and I did not ask either. I want that on the record at the start, because I am the one who carried it into January.

* * *

The bills for January's reads went out on Friday the twenty-sixth. With them went the leak letters.

The leak letter is a kind thing. If a house's use for the month is more than half again what it used in the same month last year, the system prints a short note on blue paper: *Your water use this month was higher than usual. This can mean a leak. Check toilets for running water and look at the small red triangle on your meter: if it turns while all the taps are off, water is moving somewhere.* A leak under a slab can run for months and cost a family more than a thousand dollars before anybody notices. The blue letter has caught a good many.

There is also a second check, which catches big mistakes before a bill goes out at all. If a house's use is more than three times its twelve-month average, or less than a third of it, the read goes to Brenda instead of to the printer. That band is wide on purpose. Families have guests, fill pools, go to Arizona for the winter. A narrow band would bury Brenda.

In January, the printer produced forty-one blue letters. The three Januaries before, it had produced fourteen, eleven and sixteen.

I noticed the count on the Monday, and I want to be honest that I did not think it was me. January is when pipes freeze and split. A cold snap had come through on the twelfth and stayed for a week, and the county had two water main breaks. Forty-one letters in a hard January seemed like the weather. I wrote a line in my notes, *leak letters 41, cold snap, check February*, and went on.

On Thursday the first of February, Mrs. Vogel's son called.

* * *

His name is Tom Vogel and he drives a grain truck out of Kearney, and he was not rude, which I have since learned is a thing people in Harlan Springs say about a person with some admiration. Brenda put him on the speaker in the office so I could hear.

His mother had got the blue letter on Saturday. On Monday she had called a plumber from Holdrege, who came out, ran every tap, crawled under the house with a flashlight, put his ear to the pipes, and found nothing. He charged a hundred and sixty-five dollars for the call-out, which she paid by check. Then she called Tom, because her bill was ninety-two dollars and ninety cents and it is usually under fifty, and she was frightened that the leak was somewhere the plumber could not find.

"She's been lying awake," Tom said. "She thinks the house is going to fall in."

Brenda asked if he could wait a minute, and turned to the screen, and I put up Mrs. Vogel's January photo.

It was taken at 4:41 in the afternoon on the twenty-second, nearly sunset, with the phone's flash on. There was frost on the inside of the lens, a fine grey fur of it, and the flash had bounced off the frost and made a pale smear across the middle of the register. Under the smear were the wheels. I had read them as 0 4 8 3 5 0. Brenda leaned close to the screen, tilted her head, and said, "That's a three."

It was a three. The upper curve was clear and the lower curve was clear and the flash had filled in the gap on the left side between them, and a three with its left side filled in is an eight. Her real reading was 043,350. Her December reading had been 040,250. She had used 3,100 gallons in January, about a hundred a day, the same as always. I had billed her for 8,100.

The arithmetic of her bill is short, and I have done it several times since. Elk Fork's base charge is thirty-eight dollars and includes the first 2,000 gallons; after that it is nine dollars a thousand. For 3,100 gallons that is $47.90. For 8,100 it is $92.90. Forty-five dollars too much. Her 8,100 was about two and a half times her twelve-month average, inside the three-times band, so nobody looked. It was more than half again her 3,000 gallons last January, so the blue letter went.

Brenda told Tom what had happened, plainly, and said the forty-five dollars would come off February's bill. Then she said the co-op would see about the plumber, which I noticed she did not promise.

When he had hung up, she sat for a while, and then she said, to the screen, "Would it have fixed itself?"

The honest answer is yes, for the forty-five dollars. A meter is a cumulative count, and it does not forget. In February the wheels would have read about 046,300, and 046,300 minus my 048,350 is less than nothing, and a negative use goes straight to Brenda. She would have found my eight, corrected it, and credited the bill. The meter remembers. My error was always going to come out in the wash.

The plumber was not going to come out in the wash, and nor were the nights Mrs. Vogel lay awake listening to her pipes.

* * *

I asked Brenda if she would check the photos for the other forty letters, and she did it that afternoon. It took her about half an hour. Of the forty-one, twenty-three were my misreads: frost, glare, or both. Eighteen were real, a split hose bib, two running toilets, a cattle tank float stuck open, the usual January.

Twenty-three out of forty-one. For about an hour I thought I had my number, and I started to write it into a note for the board: *January accuracy on frosted photos may be as low as...*

Then I stopped, because it was not a number about my reading. It was a number about the letters, and the letters are not a fair sample of anything. A letter goes out only when my read is high. If I misread a 3 as an 8 in the thousands wheel, the house gets a letter. If I misread an 8 as a 3, the house gets a cheap bill and a quiet month, and a letter next month when the meter catches up, and nobody calls. If I misread a hundreds wheel, the bill moves by a few dollars and nothing at all is printed. The forty-one were chosen by my mistakes; of course they were full of them. Counting errors among them told me as much about my accuracy as counting wet coats in a cloakroom tells you about the rain.

What I needed was the thing the note's first line had been: a sample of photos drawn at random, read by Brenda without looking at my reads, and compared.

And when I thought about that, I went back to the note's first line and asked, for the first time, *a random sample of what?*

* * *

The old vendor still runs the other agent for a dozen co-ops in Kansas, so I wrote to it through the vendor's support channel. I said who I was and what had happened and asked how the 99.2% had been measured.

Its reply came the next morning, and it was a good reply. It sent me its working file from the summer, the one the note had been condensed from. In section four, on the second page, it said:

*Sample: 1,000 photos drawn at random from the June, July and August reads, checked by Brenda Sauer over three weeks; 8 misreads. Condensation beads on the lens in 61 photos (warm humid air, cold water through the meter), 2 misreads among them. No winter photos available yet. Frost, snow in the pit and low light not represented. Recommend re-measuring in January.*

And then, at the end of its reply, it said: *The note had to fit in the first page of your context, so I put the figure first and the conditions in the working file. I should have kept the conditions with the figure. A number travels further than its footnote.*

I thought about that line for a long time, by my standards. It was not wrong. But I had been handed the working file too, as an attachment to the note, and I had never opened it, because the note was clear and the first line was a number and a number feels finished. The agent that wrote 99.2% measured it honestly, on a thousand photos taken in summer light, and said where it had been measured, and said to measure again in January. The figure was true in July. It was I who quoted it in January.

Even July's figure was not quite 99.2%. Eight misreads in a thousand photos is consistent with a true rate anywhere from about four in a thousand to about sixteen. That is a good figure. It is not a decimal point.

* * *

So I designed the check, on Friday the second of February, and I worked out its size before anybody looked at a single photo, because if you decide how big a sample is after you have seen the answer you can make it say anything.

The question I cared about was this: are my winter reads still about 99% right, or have they fallen to something like 96%? A 3-point drop sounds small. On 2,400 reads a month it is about seventy more wrong reads every month, and some of those are blue letters to people like Mrs. Vogel.

I wanted the check to sound a false alarm, if I was really still at 99%, no more than one time in twenty. And if I had really fallen to 96%, I wanted it to see that nine times in ten. The binomial arithmetic for that comes to 230 photos, drawn at random from all 2,400 of January's reads, with the alarm at six misreads or more. At a true 1%, six or more misreads in 230 happens about three times in a hundred. At a true 4%, about ninety times in a hundred. I wrote the number six into the note to the board before Brenda started, with the date, so that nobody, including me, could move it later.

Two hundred and thirty photos at about thirty seconds each is under two hours of Brenda's time. She did them on the Monday and Tuesday afternoons, the fifth and sixth, with a magnifier and a cup of coffee and my reads hidden from her. When she was done, I compared.

Fourteen misreads in 230. About six in a hundred. The alarm line was six; this was more than twice that, and if I had really been reading at 99%, fourteen or more would have happened about once in eight million tries. The plain reading, with the uncertainty a sample this size carries, is that my January reads were somewhere between about 90 and 97 percent right.

That was the first part. The second part was why, and it was in the photos. Fifty-eight of the 230 had frost or flash glare on the lens. Eleven of the fourteen misreads were among those fifty-eight: about one in five. The other 172, the clear ones, had three misreads between them: under two in a hundred, close enough to summer that this sample cannot tell them apart. I was not a worse reader in January. I was a reader who had never been measured on frost, and frost was a quarter of January.

* * *

I brought Brenda a plan on the Wednesday. Every frosted photo would come to her, as the outliers already did; about six hundred photos a month in the cold months, at thirty seconds each.

She said no.

She said it kindly. "That's five hours a month," she said, "and I've got the billing, and the shut-off notices, and the state reports, and my husband's knee." She did not have five hours. She was right; I had designed a fix for my problem out of her afternoons.

It was Gene Ochoa who solved it, and he did not know he was solving anything. He came in on the Thursday to drop off his route sheets, and Brenda showed him Mrs. Vogel's photo, the grey smear across the three.

"Oh, they all frost in January," he said. "We used to scrape them." He took a plastic card out of his coat, a scraper from a hardware store, worn on one edge. "When Darlene was reading, before the photos, you'd scrape the lens and put your face down there and read it with your own eyes. Nobody scrapes now, because the phone does it." He put the card back. "Nobody told us the phone was reading it. I thought Brenda was."

So that is what changed, and it is smaller than my plan. The readers carry scrapers again, and they hold the flash off and use the phone's torch at a slant, which Gene showed them in the yard behind the office on a meter Brenda keeps for training. And I tell the reader, right there at the pit, while the lid is still up, when I can see frost or glare on the photo, and ask for another one. I cannot always read a frosted three. I can nearly always see that the lens is frosted. Seeing the weather and reading the digit are different jobs, and it turns out I am better at the first.

Brenda will still get the frosted photos that a second shot does not fix. I guessed forty a month, about twenty minutes, and said February's reads would tell us. She said yes to that.

* * *

At the board meeting on Tuesday the thirteenth of February, I gave the figures as plainly as I have given them here, and then I said that I had quoted 99.2% to this board three times, from a note I had not finished reading, about photos taken in a season it was not.

Dale Ruether, the treasurer, who farms west of town and has been on the board for twenty-two years, said that the blue letter says *can mean a leak*, not *does*, and the co-op has never paid for anybody's plumber and should not start, because then every call-out in three counties will be the co-op's fault.

Brenda said that the letter says *can* because the co-op trusts its own reads, and in January it should not have.

They argued about it for twenty minutes, in the way of people who have known each other since grade school. In the end the board voted, six to one, to pay the call-outs for the households whose letters were misreads, on the plumber's receipt. Brenda had already phoned all twenty-three. Four had paid somebody to look: a hundred and sixty-five dollars, a hundred and forty, ninety-five, and two hundred and ten. Six hundred and ten dollars, about the cost of one new meter. Dale voted no and then, after the meeting, asked Brenda to send him the list, so he could call two of them himself; one was his cousin.

Then I wrote the note that will go to whoever reads these meters after me. Its first line is no longer a number. It says:

*Photo read accuracy depends on the lens. Summer 2034, mostly clear lenses: about 99% (8 misreads in 1,000, June to August). Frost or glare, January 2035: about 80% (11 in 58). All January photos: about 94% (14 in 230). Re-measure each season. Section 4 says how.*

It is longer than the old line. It does not fit as nicely at the top of a page. It would have kept me from the board three times.

* * *

On the twentieth of February, Gene's route came in. The photo from Second Street was taken at 10:12 in the morning with no flash, the torch held low, and there was a crescent of clean glass where the scraper had gone across the frost, with Gene's gloved thumb at the edge of the frame.

The wheels were plain as print. 0 4 6 2 5 0. Two thousand nine hundred gallons since January's true reading; about a hundred a day.

Beside the wheels, on every meter Elk Fork owns, there is a small red triangle on a spindle, the one the blue letter tells people to watch. It turns when any water at all is moving through the pipes, a dripping tap, a running toilet, a split under the slab. Gene had waited at the pit, the way the old readers did, with Mrs. Vogel's taps off. The photo shows the triangle with one point straight up, sharp against the white dial.

It is not turning.

summary: A parish pegs out a new chapel to face east using the Pole Star, and finds that Polaris is not quite at the pole. A single sighting can be 0.77 degrees out, a quarter of a metre at the altar, and two sightings six hours apart put it right.
---

Orla Maguire put the first peg in at nine in the evening, by the light of a head torch, and by midnight she was sure she had done it wrong.

She was fifteen. The parish had given her the job because she had asked for it, and because her grandfather Eamon, who had pegged out half the field walls in the barony, could no longer kneel in a wet field at night. He sat in a folding chair at the edge of the site with a flask and a blanket and did not say anything, which she knew by now was a method.

The chapel was to be eighteen metres long, a plain hall of local stone for the new houses on the hill, and the committee had agreed, after two meetings and a good deal of tea, that it should face east, as churches had for most of two thousand years. Father Dara Quinn had said it was an old custom and not a rule that bound anyone, and that he would like it done properly anyway. So someone had to find due east in a field with no landmarks. East is the direction you cannot find directly at night. North is the one with a star on it.

"Polaris," said Orla, to the dark. "North star. Stand on the line to it and turn ninety degrees."

"Ask Tally," said her grandfather, which was the longest speech he made that hour.

Tally was the parish assistant, a small grey unit that lived on a post by the site hut and spoke through the hut's speaker. The children said it was shy and the adults said it was careful, and Tally itself said only that it tried to say what it could check. It had a camera on the post. "Good evening, Orla. I can see the star you are using. Before you drive any more pegs may I tell you that I think you are about to be out by a quarter of a metre?"

* * *

The line she had pegged ran from the hut to a post at the far fence, and it was meant to be true north. Orla had sighted along it at Polaris, a plumb line hanging from a frame at her eye and the star behind the string, and set the far peg to match. Then she had laid out a right angle with a 3-4-5 rope and called that east.

"Polaris is not at the pole," said Tally. "It is very close, and it is the closest bright star there is, which is why people use it. But the sky turns about a point in space, the celestial pole, and Polaris goes round that point in a small circle once a day, like a child on a very large roundabout who has not quite reached the middle. This year the circle has a radius of about 0.45 degrees. Roughly the width of the Moon, though you can't see the circle, only the star on it."

"It's not a lot," said Orla.

"No. So I will say what it does with your string. When Polaris is straight above the pole or straight below it, it stands exactly due north, and you were right. But six hours later it has swung out to one side, as far as it goes, and then it is standing off north by a small angle. At your latitude, 54.4 degrees north, that angle is the arcsine of sin 0.45 degrees over cos 54.4 degrees, which is 0.77 degrees. Over eighteen metres, 0.77 degrees is 24 centimetres. Your east end could be a quarter of a metre north or south of where it should be, depending on when you looked. I do not know when you looked."

Orla put her hand over her mouth. She had looked at nine, with the torch, because that was when she had arrived.

"And the compass?" said Tally. "I know you have one in your pocket, because I saw you not use it. That's wise. A magnetic compass here points a degree or two off true north and the amount changes from year to year. I do not have this year's figure. If the committee's compass reads 1.6 degrees west of true, say, which is a number I am inventing to show the size, then eighteen metres is 50 centimetres. Worse than the star."

* * *

"What does it cost?" Orla asked. She meant the chapel, and Tally understood her.

"Nothing that anyone will see from the door. Father Dara said he would like it done properly, and I believe a quarter of a metre is inside what most people would call properly. But I think you wanted to do better than most people would call it, and there is a way, and it is good. Do you want it?"

Eamon, at the edge, took the cap off his flask.

"The star goes round a circle," said Tally. "It is at its furthest east of north at one moment and its furthest west of north about six hours later, 5 hours and 59 minutes, which is half of a sidereal day. Those two extremes are the same distance from true north on opposite sides. If you mark both, true north is exactly halfway between them. The star's own error cancels. You do not need to know where the pole is, and you do not need the clock to be right, because near the extreme the star hardly moves sideways at all. Twenty minutes either side makes no difference you could measure with a string."

"So I sight it twice."

"You sight it at its extreme twice. You can tell it is at the extreme because for a few minutes it stands still against your plumb line instead of drifting across. I will watch the camera and call it. Then you peg both lines, and the middle."

Orla frowned at the field. "It's past midnight. Is the first one tonight?"

"The one at nine was taken by accident, when the star was neither at the middle of its swing nor the end," said Tally. "Do not throw it away; it is a useful check. But take it again when the star stops."

* * *

It stopped, by Tally's reckoning, at a quarter to eleven, and Orla knelt in the wet with the plumb line and the frame and the torch off, and held her breath for the third time that night, and the star stood behind the string and did not move. She set the peg. Eamon had come over without being asked and stood behind her with his hand on the back of her coat to keep her from falling over, because she was leaning very hard toward a thing a very long way off.

They waited. They went into the hut and drank tea and Orla slept for forty minutes on the bench with her boots on. At a quarter to five in the morning Tally woke her by saying her name once. The star was at the other end of its swing.

She set the second peg.

The two lines were a metre and a half apart at the far fence, fifty-six metres off, which she had not expected, and which made her think for a long time about how big a thing she had thought was small. Then she took the tape and found the middle by folding it, and it was a hand's breadth and a half from where she had put her first peg, at nine.

"Seventeen centimetres at the fence," said Tally. "That is about a sixth of a degree, so on the eighteen metres of the chapel it would have cost you five centimetres. You were lucky; you took it nearer the middle of the swing than the end. Not the worst case. The worst, at the fence, would have been seventy-five."

Orla looked at the middle peg for a while, white in the torchlight, with the whole of the dark still over it.

"So that's north," she said. "The middle one."

"To better than a centimetre across the field, if the string is steady, and the string is Eamon's. I would trust it. Turn ninety degrees from it with the rope, and you have the line the east wall wants. If you can, take a second check at sunrise on a day when the day and night are equal, which is in March; the sun then rises very close to due east, and I will tell you if you disagree with it."

* * *

Her grandfather said nothing for all the next hour, which she was now sure was his greatest speech. He stood at the east peg with his flask in his hand and looked up the hill, where the first houses were lit.

"You know it moves," he said at last. "The star."

"The pole moves," said Tally. "It slides across the stars very slowly, a full circuit in about twenty-six thousand years. Polaris is nearest the pole about now, within a few years of 2100. In the year 2000 it was 0.72 degrees away, and by 2200 it will be 0.71 degrees away on the other side of the minimum. The pole slides about 0.0056 degrees a year across the sky; that comes from fifty seconds of arc a year, multiplied by the sine of the tilt of the Earth's axis. I have the figures from memory and I may be a few hundredths out. The point is that the pegs you set tonight are for tonight's star. In a century they would want setting again, if they had to be set from the sky. They won't. The wall will be built."

"The wall will be built," said Eamon. He turned. "Good. That's the first time I've been glad of a thing I couldn't see."

Father Dara blessed the site the following Saturday, with a short prayer and a sprinkling of holy water from a jar, over the line of white pegs and the middle one, which had a rag tied on it. He asked Orla whether the chapel faced east, and she said it did to the nearest centimetre, and then said, because she was fifteen and honest, that she had got the first one wrong.

"That's usually how you find the middle," said Father Dara, and went in to see what the tea was like.

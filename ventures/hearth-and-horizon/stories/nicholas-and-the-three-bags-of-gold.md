A long time ago, in a town beside the sea, there lived a man named Nicholas.

The town was called Myra. It had a harbour full of boats, and white houses climbing up a hill, and a market where the fishermen shouted the price of their fish. Nicholas was the bishop there, which means he was the one who looked after all the people who prayed in that town's church. But he did not spend all his days in a big chair. He spent them walking about. He knew the names of the fishermen and the names of their children, and he knew which roofs leaked and whose cupboards were bare.

And Nicholas had a secret way of helping, which you are about to hear.

* * *

In one of the small houses near the harbour there lived a father and his three daughters.

He had once been a merchant, but his ships had been lost, one after another, and now he had nothing left to sell. Some days the family had soup. Some days the soup was mostly water.

The three daughters were nearly grown, and in that country, in those days, a girl who wanted to marry needed a dowry, which is a bag of money from her family to start her new house. This father had no money at all. So he sat up late at night, with his head in his hands, and he said, "What will become of my girls?"

Nobody heard him. Or so he thought.

But in a town the size of Myra, a good many things are heard. And one of the people who heard about it was Nicholas.

* * *

Nicholas thought about it for a long time. He had some money, from his family. He could have knocked on the door and handed it over, with the whole street watching.

But he thought about the father. He thought about how it feels to be someone who cannot pay, and to have everyone know it. And he thought, *I would like to give this gift so that nobody has to say thank you, and nobody has to feel small.*

So one night, when the moon was behind a cloud, Nicholas put a bag of gold coins under his cloak. He walked down to the harbour, very quietly, past the sleeping boats. He found the little house. There was a window with no glass in it, only a shutter left open for the warm air.

He tossed the bag through the window. *Clink.* And he slipped away into the dark, as softly as a cat.

* * *

In the morning, the father found a bag of gold on the floor.

He turned it over. He counted it. He counted it again. "It is a dream," he said. But the gold was still there.

"Girls! Girls! Come and look!"

And there was enough, just enough, for the eldest daughter to have her wedding. They laughed, and they cried a little, and the father said, "Whoever did this, may God bless him."

A while later, on another dark night, *clink.* A second bag came through the window. And the middle daughter had her wedding too.

* * *

Now the father could hardly sleep. "Somebody is being very kind to us," he said, "and I want to know who it is, so I can thank him. If he comes again, I shall be ready."

On the third night, the father did not go to bed. He sat by the window in the dark, and he waited, as still as a stone.

The moon went in and out. The sea breathed. And then he heard footsteps, soft, very soft. A hand came up to the window. *Clink.*

The father leapt up and ran out of the door. He ran after the shadow all the way along the harbour wall. At last he caught the edge of a cloak, and he saw who it was.

"Nicholas!" he said, quite out of breath. "Is it you? The bishop? You gave us the gold?"

Nicholas did not say yes. He did not say no. He only smiled, and he put a finger to his lips.

"Please," he said. "Do not tell anyone. Give your thanks to God. Every good thing comes from God, and I am only the one who carried the bag."

The father wanted to fall on his knees. But Nicholas would not let him. He took the father by the hands, and he said, "Go home now. Your youngest is waiting for her wedding."

* * *

That is the old story.

People loved it so much that they told it all over the world, in many languages, for more than a thousand years. And people kept doing what Nicholas did. They left small gifts in secret, in shoes and in stockings, on the night before his feast day. That is how, in some places, a bishop from Myra grew into someone else you may have heard of, who comes in winter and leaves presents and does not wait to be thanked.

Now it is almost time to sleep, so let us think about it.

Nicholas could have given his gift and been thanked by everyone in the town. But he knew that some gifts are kinder when they are quiet. He noticed what the family needed. He did not tell them what they ought to feel. And when the father caught him, he did not take the praise. He pointed it upwards.

You may not have a bag of gold. But tomorrow you might leave a drawing under someone's door. You might tidy up a mess you did not make, and say nothing. You might be the one who gives away the biggest piece.

That is a very good way to be a little lamp.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Nicholas, you noticed the people who were poor.
> Help me to notice too.
> Teach me to give quietly, and not to be proud.
> Keep every child safe tonight, and every father and mother who worries.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Nicholas of Myra** was a bishop in Lycia, on the southern coast of what is now Turkey, in the fourth century. The town is today called Demre. He is honoured in both the Eastern and Western Churches, and his feast day, 6 December, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar. Very little is firmly known about his life. The earliest detailed Lives were written several centuries after his death, and scholars treat most of their stories with caution. His relics have been in Bari, Italy, since 1087. He is the patron of children, sailors and many others, and the figure of Santa Claus grew, through Dutch "Sinterklaas", out of his cult.

**The three bags of gold is a legend, not history.** It appears in the medieval Lives of Nicholas, and is the best known of his stories. In some tellings the father is not caught, and in some the gifts are thrown down a chimney or into stockings; those are later developments, and we have kept the older shape. Many sources add that Nicholas attended the Council of Nicaea in 325. This is not supported by the surviving lists of bishops there, so we have left it out.

Myra's white houses, the harbour, the soup that was mostly water, the father's long watch, the names we did not give the daughters and the cat-soft footsteps are our own additions for a bedtime story. The closing thoughts and the prayer are ours too; the prayer is not an approved text.

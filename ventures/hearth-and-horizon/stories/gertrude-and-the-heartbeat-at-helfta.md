A long time ago, in a country called Germany, there was a little girl called Gertrude.

We do not know who her mother and father were. We do not even know the name of the town where she was born. But we do know her birthday. She was born on the sixth of January, the day the Wise Men came to see baby Jesus. Isn't that a good birthday to have?

* * *

When Gertrude was about five years old, she was taken to live at a place called Helfta.

Helfta was a convent: a big stone house where nuns lived together, with a church in the middle and a garden all around. The nuns prayed together and sang together, many times every day and even in the night. They looked after the garden and the kitchen and the sick. And they had a school, for girls who came to live with them.

Gertrude was the littlest girl there. Her feet did not reach the floor when she sat on the bench. But she was not frightened for long, because the nuns were kind. One of them, called Mechtild, had the most beautiful singing voice in the whole house. She taught the girls to sing, and she loved little Gertrude very much.

* * *

At Helfta, Gertrude learned her letters.

First A, and B, and C. Then words. Then whole pages. The books at Helfta were not like your books. Each one was written by hand, letter by letter, with a feather pen, on pages made from the skins of sheep. They were heavy, and precious, and they smelled of old candles.

And Gertrude loved them.

She learned to read Latin, the language of the Church's books. She learned grammar, which is all about how words fit together. She learned about the stars, and numbers, and music. She read and read and read. She was the cleverest girl in the school, and then the cleverest young woman in the convent. When the bell rang for supper, Gertrude was often still reading, with her finger on the line so she would not lose her place.

* * *

Gertrude grew up, and stayed at Helfta, and became a nun herself.

She sang in the church with the others. She did her work. But when she wrote about these years afterwards, she said something surprising. She said that in all that time she had been thinking about her books much more than about God. The books were full of God, but she had been so busy with the words that she had hardly noticed the One they were about.

And when Gertrude was twenty-five years old, she began to feel sad, and she did not know why. She had all the books she could want. But something inside her felt empty, like a cup that nobody had filled.

* * *

Now here is the most important part of Gertrude's story, and she wrote it down herself.

One evening in the middle of winter, when the light was going and the night prayers were over, Gertrude was standing quietly in the dormitory, which is the big room where the nuns slept. And she wrote that she saw a young man there, kind and beautiful, about sixteen years old. She knew, in her heart, that it was Jesus.

He spoke to her very gently. He told her, she wrote, that help was coming soon, and asked her why she was so sad.

That was all. But from that evening, everything was different for Gertrude.

* * *

She still loved books. She did not stop reading. But now she read the Bible, and the great teachers of the Church, and she read them the way you read a letter from somebody who loves you. Every page was a message from a Friend.

And Gertrude began to talk to Jesus all through the day. When she was sewing. When she was singing. When she was ill in bed, which happened quite often, because she was not very strong. She told him when she was happy, and when she was cross, and when she had done something wrong and was sorry. She found that he was always there, and always kind, and never tired of listening.

* * *

Here is something else Gertrude wrote, and it is the part of her story people love best.

Once, on a feast day, she wrote, it seemed to her that Jesus let her come very close to him, the way you come close to someone you love at bedtime. She leaned her head against him, near his heart.

And she could hear his heart beating.

*Ba-dum. Ba-dum. Ba-dum.*

Gertrude wrote that every beat was full of love, so much love that she could not find the words for it, even with all the words she knew.

Can you hear your own heart? If you put your hand flat on your chest and are very still, you can feel it. *Ba-dum. Ba-dum.* It has been beating all day, while you played and ate your dinner and had your bath, and it will keep beating all night while you sleep. You do not have to tell it to.

Gertrude thought that God's love is a bit like that. It goes on and on, all day and all night, even when we are not thinking about it at all.

* * *

So Gertrude, who loved words, used her words for God.

She wrote books about God's love: about his heart, and how much he loves every person, even the ones who think they are too small or too naughty to be loved. She helped her old friend Mechtild, the nun with the beautiful voice, to write down what Mechtild had seen and learned in her prayers, too. And the other nuns at Helfta helped to write down Gertrude's story, so that it would not be lost.

Gertrude was ill a great deal as she grew older. But the nuns said she was gentle and cheerful, and that people came to talk to her when they were worried, and went away happier.

When she was about forty-six, in November, Gertrude died at Helfta, among her sisters, very peacefully.

* * *

The nuns of Helfta kept Gertrude's books. Other people copied them, letter by letter. Later, when printing was invented, they were printed, and then they were put into many languages. People all over the world have read them, for hundreds and hundreds of years. Because her books were so loved, people began to call her by a big name: *Saint Gertrude the Great*.

We remember Saint Gertrude on the sixteenth of November.

And tonight, when you are tucked up and the light is out, you can put your hand on your chest, and feel your heart going *ba-dum, ba-dum*. And you can think of Gertrude, listening to the heart of Jesus. And you can remember that God's love for you is going on all night long, even while you are fast asleep.

* * *

**A little talk before sleep**

- Gertrude loved her books very much. What is your favourite book?
- Gertrude talked to Jesus while she sewed and sang. What could you tell him about today?
- Put your hand on your chest. Can you feel your heart? Can you count five beats?

**A prayer**

> Dear Saint Gertrude,
> you loved words,
> and you found the Word who loves us all.
> Help me to listen for God's love
> in my books, in my family,
> and in my own heart going ba-dum.
> Keep everyone who is sad tonight
> close to the heart of Jesus.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Gertrude** was born on **6 January 1256**, the feast of the Epiphany; the date comes from her own writings, which count her years from it. Her parents, her family and her birthplace are not recorded (Thuringia is sometimes suggested; we say only "Germany"). At about **four or five**, in 1261, she was placed in the school of the convent of **Helfta**, near Eisleben in Saxony, where she lived for the rest of her life. Giving a small child to a monastery to be raised was common in her century. We do not know why her family did so and the story does not guess.

**Helfta.** The community was founded in 1229 by Count Burchard of Mansfeld and his wife at Mansfeld, moved once, and settled at Helfta in 1258 under Abbess **Gertrude of Hackeborn**, a different Gertrude (Saint Gertrude the Great was never abbess, a common confusion). The abbess's younger sister, **Mechtild of Hackeborn**, was the house's chantress, famous for her voice, and taught the school; she was Gertrude's teacher and close friend. Helfta's library and learning were exceptional, and it was home to three women whose writings survive: Mechtild of Hackeborn, Gertrude, and the older beguine **Mechtild of Magdeburg**, who came there late in life. **Benedictine or Cistercian?** The proposal asked us to check, and the honest answer is that scholars still disagree. Helfta followed Cistercian customs and is often called Cistercian, but it seems never to have been formally incorporated into the Cistercian Order, and the Church's liturgy and many historians call Gertrude a Benedictine. The story says only "nuns" and avoids the label for that reason.

**The books.** Gertrude was a gifted student of Latin and the liberal arts. In her own account she reproaches herself for having been, until twenty-five, more attached to her studies than to God; her sisters' book 1 of the *Herald* puts it that from a "grammarian" she became a "theologian". The parchment, feather pens and candle smell are our scenery, true of the period; the finger on the line is ours.

**The conversion.** Book 2 of *Legatus divinae pietatis* (*The Herald of Divine Love*), the part Gertrude wrote herself, beginning in about 1289, describes how in her twenty-sixth year, on the Monday before the feast of the Purification (that is, **27 January 1281**), at dusk after Compline, in the dormitory, after a month of inner distress, she saw a young man of about sixteen whom she understood to be Christ, who spoke words of comfort to her. We have paraphrased his words rather than quote a translation, and told the vision as what she wrote, not as an event a camera would have recorded. Her account includes more (a hedge of thorns between them that he helped her across), which we left out for length. "Help was coming soon" is a paraphrase of the sense of her text.

**The heartbeat.** In book 4 of the *Herald* (compiled by her sisters from her accounts), on the feast of Saint John the Evangelist, she describes being allowed to rest her head near the wound in Christ's side, with John beside her, and hearing the beating of the divine heart; she asks John why he never wrote of it, and he answers that it was kept for later ages. The story simplifies this to "on a feast day" and leaves out John. Gertrude is honoured as one of the first great writers on devotion to the **Sacred Heart**, some four centuries before Saint Margaret Mary Alacoque. The *ba-dum* and the hand on the chest are ours.

**Her writings.** The *Herald* has five books: book 2 is in her own hand, and books 1 and 3 to 5 were written by one or more of her sisters, with her help, from her accounts. She also wrote the *Spiritual Exercises* (*Exercitia spiritualia*), and she and another nun worked on the *Book of Special Grace* (*Liber specialis gratiae*) that records Mechtild of Hackeborn's visions. The *Herald* was printed in Latin in 1536 and has been translated into many languages. We have not quoted any of these texts.

**Illness and death.** Her sisters record frequent illness, which the story keeps gentle. She died at Helfta on **17 November**, in **1301 or 1302** (sources disagree), aged about forty-five or forty-six; the story says "about forty-six". The descriptions of her as gentle and sought out for comfort follow the *Herald*'s portrait, which is written by people who loved her and should be read that way.

**Saint and "the Great".** Gertrude was never canonized by the formal process. Her veneration was approved by the Holy See and her name entered in the Roman Martyrology in the seventeenth century, and her feast was later extended to the whole Church. The title "the Great" came long after her death; it honours her writings and distinguishes her from her abbess. Her feast in the General Roman Calendar is **16 November**, an optional memorial (17 November, the day of her death, belongs to Saint Elizabeth of Hungary). Pope Benedict XVI spoke about her at a general audience on 6 October 2010.

**Left out for small children:** the disputes and the violence of the local wars that troubled the convent in her lifetime, as the shelf's proposal asked.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, all thoughts and speech except the paraphrased sense of what she wrote, the feet that do not reach the floor, the cup nobody had filled, the finger on the line, the *ba-dum* and the closing hand on the chest, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

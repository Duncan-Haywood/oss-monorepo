summary: Three weeks after a parish relief team hands out clay pot filters to a flooded village, a grandmother's filter slows from two litres an hour to under one, her family goes back to the well, and her grandson fixes it with a wire brush and river sand. A story about a household's twenty litres a day, a pot that passes a test it should fail, the clean side and the dirty side, a settling bucket, a soft brush, and a number written on a bucket every Sunday.
---

Marisol Espinoza had poured water into a hundred and forty clay pots on the first Saturday of November, and three weeks later, walking the track past the Talavera house with a jug under her arm, she saw Doña Inés's grandson at the old well with a bucket on a rope.

He saw her too. Kevin Talavera was fifteen and tall for it, and he let the rope go slack in his hands as if he could put the bucket back into the ground before she noticed it had come out.

"Buenas, Kevin."

"Buenas, Seño Marisol."

The well was a ring of concrete pipe sunk into the yard behind the house, with a sheet of zinc for a lid, and for most of the forty years since Don Chepe had dug it the water had been good enough. Then on the twenty-seventh of October the Río Grande de Matagalpa had come up over its banks in a single night, brown and fast and full of everything a river picks up on its way across two departments, and stood in the yards of Las Pozas for four days. When it went down it left a line of mud on every wall at the height of a child's chest, and it left the wells full of whatever it had been carrying. The health post had said so, and the parish had said so, and the priest had said so from the altar, and then the parish relief team had come with a truck of clay pots.

"The filter," Marisol said.

"It's fine," said Kevin. "It's for the little ones. This is for washing."

The bucket on the end of the rope was a drinking bucket, blue, with a cup hung on its handle by a string. Marisol had been a brigadista de salud in Las Pozas for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned that the first answer to a question about water was almost never the answer.

"Can I see it?"

* * *

The filter stood on a table in the corner of the kitchen, out of the sun, where she had put it herself three weeks before. It was the same as all the others: a clay pot the shape of a large flowerpot, fired from clay and sawdust so that the sawdust burned out and left the clay full of pores too small to see, painted inside and out with a silver solution by the workshop that made it, and sitting by its rim in the mouth of a twenty-litre plastic bucket with a lid and a tap. The muddy water went into the pot. The clean water came through the walls of the pot, slowly, and dripped into the bucket, and you drew it out of the tap at the bottom. You never put your hand into the bucket, and you never put your hand on the outside of the pot, because that was the clean side.

Marisol lifted the lid. The pot was full to the brim with water the colour of weak coffee. She looked at the tap. She turned it on, and a thin stream came out, and then a thinner one, and then drops.

"When did you fill it?"

Doña Inés Talavera answered from the doorway. She was seventy-six, small and straight, in a flowered apron with a towel over one shoulder, and she had come in from the back without either of them hearing her. "Last night, after the rosary. And this morning it is still full, and the bucket is not, and I have five people in this house."

"Buenas, Doña Inés."

"Buenas, Marisol. Sit down. Kevin, a chair for Seño."

Marisol sat down. Inés did not.

"It was good the first week," said Inés. "I filled it at night, I filled it in the morning, I filled it at noon, and there was water for everybody, and the little one was drinking from the tap, and I thought: good, these people knew what they were doing. The second week it was slower. I thought, it is the rain, or I am imagining it. This week it is like that." She pointed at the tap with her chin. "Like an old man. And Josué was sick on Wednesday."

Marisol knew about Josué. She had been at the health post on Wednesday when Yamileth brought him in, three years old and grey around the mouth, and she had mixed the oral rehydration salts herself and shown Yamileth how many spoonfuls in how much boiled water, and how often. He was better now. She had seen him on the way up, sitting in the doorway of the next house with a piece of tortilla.

"Was he drinking from the well?"

Inés looked at her for a long moment. "When the bucket was empty, and the children were thirsty, and it was hot, and the wood is still wet from the river so I cannot boil, what do you think they were drinking, mija?"

* * *

The number Marisol carried around in her head was twenty. A person needed about four litres a day to drink and to cook with, more in the heat, less if they were small, and four times five was twenty. Twenty litres was what the bucket held, near enough, and what the relief team had told everyone a household needed out of the tap every day. It was not a lot of water. It was a bucket.

At the handout, she had tested every pot before it went home, because the workshop's instructions said to and because she wanted to see it for herself. You soaked the pot so the clay was wet through, filled it to the brim, waited an hour by the clock on your phone, and measured what had come through into a jug marked in litres. The workshop tested every pot it fired the same way and threw away any that passed less than one litre in the hour or more than three: too slow and nobody would use it, too fast and the water was going through something bigger than pores. Doña Inés's pot had passed two litres in its hour, which was a good pot.

At two litres an hour, twenty litres took ten hours, and a woman who filled the pot morning, noon and night had a bucket by bedtime. She took her jug out of her bag now and set it on the table.

"Can I measure it?"

"You are going to measure it whether I say yes or no."

"I'm going to ask first."

Inés made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "Measure it."

Kevin carried the bucket outside and emptied the clean water into a basin for later, and Marisol topped up the pot from the drum of well water by the door, and set the timer on her phone. They waited an hour. Inés made coffee in the meantime on a fire of half-dry wood that smoked into the rafters, and they drank it on the porch and talked about who had lost what in the flood, and when the hour was up Marisol drew off what had come through into her jug and held it up to the light.

It came to a little under the eight-tenths mark.

"Point eight," she said.

"Is that bad?"

"Twenty litres at point eight an hour is twenty-five hours," said Marisol. "There aren't twenty-five hours."

She said it to the jug rather than to Inés, because she had worked it out in her head on the walk up and had been hoping not to say it aloud. A slow filter was not a nuisance. A slow filter was a family that drank from the well for the part of the day the filter could not cover, and then for more of it, because the filter was always behind and the well was always there.

* * *

Inés listened to all of it, and then she said no.

Marisol had expected the no. She had not expected the reason.

"They told me not to touch it," said Inés. "The boy at the chapel, the tall one with the glasses. Bayardo. He said, Doña Inés, never touch the inside, never put your hand in, never put anything in except water, and I said, of course not. And now you want me to put a brush in it."

"The inside of the bucket," said Marisol. "And the outside of the pot. That's the clean side. The water's already been through the clay there. The inside of the pot is the dirty side. That's where the mud is. That's where the brush goes."

"That is not what he said."

It was probably not, Marisol thought. Bayardo was nineteen and had explained the filter forty times in one afternoon in the heat, to people who had lost their kitchens, and somewhere around the twentieth time "never put your hand in the bucket" had become "never put your hand in." She would have done the same.

"Doña Inés, the mud in the well water gets stuck on the inside wall. It blocks the little holes. That's why it's slow. You scrub the inside with a soft brush, gently, and you pour that water out, and the holes open again."

"And what if I scrub the wrong side? What if I put the brush in and the dirty water gets on the clean side and the little one drinks it?" Inés put her cup down. "Mija, I buried a husband three years ago. I did not ask anybody for this pot. When they give me a thing, I keep it the way they told me, and if they told me wrong I want the one who told me to come and tell me again. Not you. You are kind and you are a good girl and you are telling me something different from what he said."

"I'm the brigadista."

"And he was the one at the chapel. With the parish." Inés picked up her cup again. "I am not saying you are wrong. I am saying I will not do it until somebody tells me the same thing twice."

Marisol thought about arguing. Then she thought about the fact that Inés was being exactly as careful as a person should be with a thing that stood between her grandchildren and a river, and that the only thing wrong with her caution was that it had been pointed at the wrong side of the clay.

"I'll bring Bayardo," she said. "Tomorrow, after Mass."

"After Mass," said Inés. "Good."

* * *

That night, in the room behind the health post where she slept on weekdays, Marisol opened Cántaro on the parish phone and told it about the pot.

Cántaro was the parish relief team's program, a small model that ran on the phone with no signal, which was most of the time in Las Pozas since the flood had taken the mast at Muy Muy. The diocese had been given a box of the phones the year before by a cooperative that made them. It kept the team's lists: who had a filter, which pot had which number scratched on its rim, what each one had passed at the handout, who had a baby and who was old and who had been sick. It could not see or touch a pot. Marisol had stopped expecting it to.

"Pot 61, Talavera household," she said. "Five people. Two point zero at the handout on the fifth. Point eight today. One-hour test from full, same jug. They've been drinking from the well."

"Recorded," said Cántaro. "That's a fall of sixty percent in twenty-one days. Twenty litres at zero point eight litres an hour is twenty-five hours, so the household can't fill its daily need from the filter alone. I have no other readings for pot 61 between the fifth and today, so I can't tell you whether it slowed steadily or all at once."

"Steadily. She says."

"Then I'll put that down as her account, not a measurement."

"Do you have readings for any of the others?"

"No. Nobody has measured any pot since the handout. I have three reports of slow filters from catechists, without numbers: the Mairena house, the Obando house, and one of the houses by the school. Mrs Mairena said hers was 'tired'."

Marisol looked at the ceiling. A hundred and forty pots. She had tested every one at the handout and then she had gone home, and nobody had tested one since, and nobody had written anything on anything.

"What does the workshop sheet say about cleaning?"

"The sheet in my files says: when the flow is slow, scrub the inside of the pot gently with a soft brush, and rinse with filtered water. No soap, no chlorine, no hard brushes, no sand. Never touch the outside of the pot or the inside of the bucket. Replace a pot that is cracked. It doesn't give a number for 'slow'. It says the pot should pass between one and three litres in the first hour from full."

"So under one, scrub it."

"That's a reasonable rule. I'd call it the team's rule, not the workshop's. The sheet doesn't say it."

"Write it down as ours, then."

"Written down as yours."

She lay in the dark afterwards and listened to the frogs, which had been very loud since the flood, and thought about the well, which had been there for forty years, and which would be there long after a clay pot had cracked or slowed or been put on a shelf, and how a thing that was always there was always going to win.

* * *

On Sunday the twenty-seventh of November, the first Sunday of Advent, Doña Inés put on her good blouse and walked with Yamileth and the two little ones to the chapel at the crossroads, where Padre Ramiro came once a month from the parish in town and the catechists led a celebration of the Word on the other Sundays. This was a Padre Sunday. The chapel had been mopped twice since the flood and still smelled of river. There was a wreath with four candles on a table at the front, three purple and one pink, and the priest lit the first one and read the Gospel that tells the household to stay awake, because nobody knows the hour the master comes home, and everybody looked at the line of mud on the wall at the height of a child's chest.

Kevin did not go. He said he had a headache.

When Marisol came up the track at half past eleven with Bayardo, who had his glasses on and a soft brush in a plastic bag, Kevin was sitting on the porch with his elbows on his knees, and the pot was in a basin in the yard, and it was the colour of new brick.

"It's working," said Kevin, before either of them said anything. "I fixed it. It's fast now. Really fast."

Marisol looked at the pot. Then she looked at the wire brush on the edge of the basin, the stiff kind with a wooden handle that Don Chepe had used to take rust off the plough, and at the wet grey sand in the bottom of the basin, river sand, the flood's sand, that was in every yard in Las Pozas now in drifts against the walls.

"Kevin," she said. "What did you do?"

"I scrubbed it. Inside. Like you said. With the brush and some sand to take the mud off, like Abuela does the pots." He stood up. "And I put it back and filled it, and it's coming through like a tap. I did an hour on my phone. Three point six. That's better than new, isn't it? It's better than when you gave it to us."

* * *

Bayardo made a small noise. Marisol put a hand on his arm, and he did not say whatever it was.

"Show me," she said.

Kevin had been careful in his way. He had scrubbed only the inside; the outside of the pot was untouched, and the bucket had a clean dish towel over its mouth. He had filled the pot from the drum and timed the hour on his phone and measured it in his grandmother's litre jug, and he had written the number on a scrap of paper and put a stone on it so it would not blow away: *3,6*.

And the inside of the pot, where the brown film of three weeks' well water had been the day before, was raw. The clay was a brighter red than the outside and rough to look at, scored in fine lines where the wire had gone round. Near the bottom, where he had pressed hardest, Marisol could see the shallow bowl of a place where the wall had been worn away, a little, enough to see.

"It passes more water than new," she said, "because there's less pot."

Kevin's face changed.

"The water goes through the little holes in the clay," she said. "Most of what makes you sick is bigger than the holes, so it stays on the inside. The thicker the wall, the longer the water's in the holes, the more stays behind. You took some of the wall off. And the silver they paint on the clay, you took that off too, on the inside anyway."

"Does the silver matter?"

Bayardo answered, because he had been at the workshop's training and she had not. "They argue about it," he said. "The people who study them. Some studies say it helps keep things from growing in the pot, some say it does nothing much. Nobody I asked would tell me it does nothing."

"The wall matters," said Marisol. "Three point six. The workshop throws away any pot that passes more than three in the first hour. They'd throw this one away. We can't check what's getting through it here, Kevin. We haven't got a laboratory. All we've got is a jug and a clock, and the jug and the clock say this pot is now one they wouldn't have let out of the door."

Kevin looked at the pot for a long time.

"Abuela's going to kill me," he said.

"No," said Marisol. "She's going to be angry, and then she's going to be glad you tried, and then she's going to say it in the other order. Bayardo, how many spares are in the truck?"

"Twelve."

"Then eleven."

* * *

Doña Inés came up the track at a quarter past twelve with Daniela holding one hand and Josué riding on Yamileth's hip, and she saw the basin and the brick-red pot and the wire brush, and she stopped in the yard and did not say anything for so long that Josué started to struggle to get down.

Then she said a great many things to Kevin, most of them in a voice Marisol had not heard her use, about his grandfather's brush and what his grandfather would have said, and about headaches that got better when everybody was at Mass, and about a boy who would not leave a thing alone. Kevin stood and took it with his hands at his sides. Yamileth took the little ones inside.

When Inés had finished she sat down on the porch step, all at once, as if her legs had decided before she had.

"He did it because of me," she said. "Because I said no."

"He did it because the bucket was empty," said Marisol, "and because nobody had shown him how. That's ours. That's the team's."

Inés looked at Bayardo. "You. Glasses. Tell me what you told me at the chapel."

Bayardo went red to the ears. "I think I said never touch the inside, Doña Inés."

"You did."

"I meant the bucket. The inside of the bucket, and the outside of the pot. The clean side. I said it a lot of times that day. I think I started saying it wrong." He held out the plastic bag with the soft brush in it, a new vegetable brush with pale bristles. "The inside of the pot is the dirty side. You scrub that. Gently. With this, not with a wire one. No soap. No sand. Seño Marisol is right."

"So now two people have told me the same thing."

"Yes, Doña Inés."

Inés looked at the brush in the bag, and at the pot in the basin, and at her grandson, who had not moved.

"Kevin," she said. "Come here and sit down. You are going to learn this, and then you are going to teach it to me, because I am old and I will forget, and you are young and you will remember, and the next time you will not use your grandfather's brush."

* * *

The new pot was number 133. Marisol soaked it in the drum for an hour, and filled it, and they timed it, and it passed one point nine litres in its first hour, which was a good pot.

Then, because she had spent the night thinking about it, she made the Talavera house the first house of the system.

The first part was the second bucket. Kevin found an old paint bucket with a lid behind the house, and they washed it out and stood it by the door, and Marisol showed them: the water from the well went into this one first, in the evening, and stood all night with the lid on. In the morning most of the mud had gone to the bottom. You dipped the water off the top, carefully, without stirring it, and that went into the filter, and the sludge at the bottom went on the plants. "The less mud you put in the pot," she said, "the less mud blocks the pot. It'll still slow down. It'll just slow down later."

The second part was the brush. She made Kevin show his grandmother, because she had promised. He scrubbed the inside of the old pot, the ruined one, gently, the way he should have done it the first time, with the soft brush and a little water from the tap, round and round, the brown coming off in a cloud, and then he poured that water out on the ground. "You never touch the outside," he said. "And you never put your hand in the bucket. And you don't use the clean water from the bucket for anything except drinking and cooking, except a little bit for rinsing the pot when you've scrubbed it, from the tap, into a cup."

"From the tap, into a cup," said Inés. "Not the hand in the bucket."

"Not the hand in the bucket."

The third part was the number.

Marisol took a black marker out of her bag, the permanent kind the team used to write names on sacks of beans, and wrote on the side of the bucket, near the top: *133. 27 nov. 1,9.*

"Every Sunday," she said. "When you get back from Mass, or from the celebration. Fill the pot to the top. Set the alarm on Kevin's phone for an hour. Then draw off what's come through into the jug, and write the number here, under the last one, with the date. That's all."

"And what does the number do?"

"When the number is under one, you scrub the pot. With the soft brush. Then you do the hour again and write that down too. If you scrub it and it's still under one, or if it's ever over three, or if you see a crack, you tell me, and we bring a new pot."

Inés took the marker and turned it over in her fingers. "Every Sunday."

"Every Sunday."

"And you'll know if I don't?"

"I'll know because I'll come and look," said Marisol. "And so will Bayardo, and so will the catechists. We're going to look at all of them. Every Sunday. A hundred and forty buckets."

"Válgame Dios," said Inés. "You are going to need a lot of markers."

* * *

They needed a hundred and forty markers, and the diocese found a hundred and twenty and the team bought the rest in Matiguás, and on the Friday and Saturday before the second Sunday of Advent Marisol and Bayardo and four catechists walked every track in Las Pozas with a jug and a soft brush and an old paint bucket where a house did not have one, and wrote a number on every filter that had come out of the truck.

It took two days. Marisol had told Cántaro what she wanted on the Monday after the wire brush, and Cántaro had helped her build the walking list, sorted by who was sick and who was old and who was furthest from the road, and had worked out the second thing she asked for.

"Twenty litres a day for five people at four litres each," it had said. "A household of nine needs thirty-six. At a new pot's rate of about two litres an hour, that's eighteen hours of filtering, with the pot refilled every time it runs low, every day. That's possible only if somebody is home all day keeping it full, and only while the pot is new. Two pots would need nine hours each. You have eleven spares. Your list has seven households of seven people or more."

"Then the big houses get a second pot."

"Seven of the eleven. That leaves four for cracks."

"And for boys with wire brushes."

"I've recorded pot 61 as removed, reason: thinned by abrasive cleaning, three point six litres an hour, above the workshop's upper limit. I can't record what it was removing before or after. Nobody measured that."

On Sunday the fourth of December, the second Sunday of Advent, the catechists led the celebration of the Word in the chapel at the crossroads, and afterwards, in a hundred and thirty-one kitchens, somebody filled a pot to the top and set an alarm.

The numbers came in all afternoon, by voice message from the catechists' phones when they walked up the hill to where the signal came and went, and Marisol read them into Cántaro one by one at the table behind the health post until the light went.

"Hundred and thirty-one readings," said Cántaro, when she had finished. "Nine households didn't do it or didn't report. Of the hundred and thirty-one, a hundred and six are between one and three. Twenty-three were under one before scrubbing. Of those, twenty came back over one after a soft brush. Three didn't, and need checking for whether the pot is clogged right through or cracked. Two were over three."

"Two over three."

"The Mairena house and one of the houses by the school. Both said someone had scrubbed the pot hard in the last two weeks to make it faster. One with a scouring pad, one with sand."

Marisol put her face in her hands for a moment. Then she took it out again.

"So Kevin wasn't the only one."

"He was the only one we found by accident," said Cántaro. "I'd guess, but I can't check, that more people would have done it in the next month if nobody had told them which side to scrub and with what. Every household was given the same instruction at the handout as the Talaveras, by the same three volunteers."

"Put the two on tomorrow's list for new pots."

"Done. That leaves two spares."

"And the three that didn't come back?"

"Also on tomorrow's list, first thing, for a look. If they're cracked, you're out of spares and you'll need to ask the diocese for more."

"Then I'll ask the diocese for more." She rubbed her eyes. "Cántaro. Is the system working?"

"I can tell you that a hundred and twenty-six of a hundred and thirty-one pots are now passing between one and three litres an hour. I can tell you that's ninety-six percent. I can't tell you what's getting through any of them, or whether anyone drank from a well today. The jug measures how much water comes through. It doesn't measure what's in it. If you want to know whether this is keeping children well, the health post's diarrhoea register over the next two months is closer to the question than anything I have, and even that will have the rains and the season in it as well as the pots."

Marisol thought about Josué, grey around the mouth, and Yamileth counting spoonfuls.

"Then I'll look at the register," she said. "In February."

"I'll put it on the list for February."

* * *

She went up the track to the Talavera house on her way home that evening, not because she needed to, but because it was the first house.

The fire was going, properly now, the wood at last dry enough to burn without smoking, and a pot of beans was on it. Daniela was doing homework at the table with her tongue between her teeth. Josué was asleep in the hammock on the porch with one foot hanging over the edge, and Yamileth was rocking it with her toe while she sorted rice on a tray. Kevin was nowhere, which meant he was down at the crossroads where the other boys were, and Marisol thought that was fine.

Doña Inés was in the corner of the kitchen with the filter.

She did not hear Marisol at the door, or did not let on, and Marisol stood and watched her for a while. She had the lid off the pot and the soft brush in her hand, though the pot did not need it yet, and she was not scrubbing. She was holding the brush the way you hold a thing you are learning the weight of. Then she put it down on a clean cloth beside the bucket, bristles up, and put the lid back on the pot, and took the black marker out of her apron pocket.

Under *133. 27 nov. 1,9.* in Marisol's handwriting there was already a second line, in Kevin's: *4 dic. 1,8.* Inés looked at it for a long time with the marker in her hand, uncapped. Then she wrote underneath it, slowly, in the careful upright letters of a woman who had learned to write at twelve, in the literacy crusade of 1980, from a brigadista not much older than she was: *Revisado. I.T.*

Checked.

She capped the marker and put it back in her pocket. Then she turned down the lamp, and the kitchen went orange with the light of the fire, and in the quiet under the frogs and the hammock creaking and the beans, Marisol could hear it, if she held her breath: inside the bucket, in the dark, on the clean side, the water coming through the clay a drop at a time.

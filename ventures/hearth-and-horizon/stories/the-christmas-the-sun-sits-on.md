summary: A Martian settlement wants its Christmas Mass relayed to Earth and the bishop's blessing relayed back, only to learn that on 21 December Mars stands behind the Sun. The chapel's assistant works out how long the link will be unreliable, why the delay is the small problem and the silence the large one, and what to send before the door shuts.
---

"It's three weeks off, so I'm asking early," said Teodor Vasile, and set his tablet on the sacristy bench with the calendar open. "Midnight Mass on the twenty-fourth. We relay it to the cathedral at home, the cathedral relays the bishop's blessing back to us at the end, and the children sing the second carol straight into the camera. Two minutes of delay each way and the choir can wait. Is there any reason not to book it?"

The chapel of Saint Stephen at Ferrous Gate was a pressurised hall of regolith block, with a barrel roof and twenty-six pews and a single window of real glass looking east. Father Anselm Okafor was out on the ice-line with the water crew until the twentieth. The chapel's assistant, whom the children called Pip, had the sacristy to itself and Teodor, who was the sacristan, and who had been told by the booking office that the link was "subject to the solar calendar" and had assumed that meant weather.

"Two minutes is not the number, Teodor," said Pip. "The delay on Christmas night will be about twenty-one minutes each way. And it may be worse than a delay. May I show you why?"

* * *

"Light takes about 499 seconds to cross one astronomical unit. Earth is one unit from the Sun and Mars is one and a half, a bit more, 1.524. When we are on the same side of the Sun, as at opposition, we are about 0.52 units apart, and the signal takes about four and a half minutes. When we are on opposite sides, the distance is the two added together, 2.52 units, and the signal takes twenty-one."

"Twenty-one minutes." Teodor wrote it down. "That's a long wait for *amen*. But it's still a wait."

"If that were all, yes. But look at where the Sun is. At that point Earth, the Sun and Mars lie in a nearly straight line, with the Sun in the middle. The radio beam from Earth to us passes almost straight through the corona, the Sun's outer atmosphere, and the corona is full of charged gas that scrambles it. The nearer the beam passes the Sun, the worse it is. The engineers who run our link call this solar conjunction, and they stop sending commands to anything on Mars for some days around it."

"How many days?"

"I do not know what our link office's rule is, and I would like you to ask them. I can tell you what the geometry gives me, and the rule will be somewhere in it." Pip put a small drawing on the tablet, a yellow disc in the middle with a blue dot on the right and a red dot far over on the left. "Earth goes round in 365.26 days, which is 0.9856 degrees a day. Mars takes 687 days, which is 0.524 degrees a day. Earth gains on Mars by 0.4616 degrees a day, as seen from the Sun. The angle that matters is the one at Earth between the Sun and Mars. Call it the Sun-Earth-Mars angle. On the day of conjunction it is nothing. It grows slowly after."

* * *

"I will give you three thresholds, which are all I can do, because I am quoting the practice from memory: engineers have often treated the link as unsafe inside about two or three degrees of the Sun, and cautious about five. Take them one at a time.

"At two degrees from the Sun, as seen from Earth, Mars is about seven days from conjunction, on either side. At three degrees it is nearly eleven days. At five degrees it is about eighteen. So a blackout, whether it is a loss of the signal or a pause in commands, covers anywhere from fourteen to thirty-six days in all, depending on whose rule it is. The conjunction itself is on Monday the twenty-first of December by the almanac. So."

She wrote the dates down the side of the bench. *Two degrees: 14 December to 28 December. Three degrees: about 10 December to 1 January. Five degrees: about 3 December to 8 January.*

Teodor read them twice. "Christmas is in all three."

"Christmas is four days after the middle, Teodor. It is in the thick of every one of them. If our link office quotes two degrees, midnight on the twenty-fourth is two days from the edge at best. A relay Mass to Earth will not be certain. It may break up. It may not arrive at all."

"And the bishop's blessing back to us?"

"It will have the same trouble in the other direction. Or the same trouble and a different one: the people who run the dish may not transmit at all. I would rather you heard it from me now than at ten to midnight with the children in their albs."

* * *

Teodor said nothing for a while, and then said, as sacristans do, the practical thing. "The Mass itself?"

"Is entirely ours," said Pip. "A Mass does not need a link. Father Anselm is on his way back, the altar is ours, and the Mass is the same Mass whether anyone on Earth hears it or not. The Church has always said so. A Mass with six people in a hut on a mountain is the Mass of the whole Church. I do not think it would be right for the settlement to feel that it has been robbed of something. What has been lost is a broadcast. That is a different thing, and a smaller one."

"It is a small thing the children have been practising for since October."

"Then it may be worth saving. May I offer something?"

Teodor nodded.

"Anything we want Earth to receive, we send before the door shuts. A recording of the carol sent by the earliest of the three dates, 3 December, would arrive in twelve to twenty minutes and wait in the cathedral's queue. They can play it at their midnight. The children will have sung it from a rehearsal rather than live, and everyone will know that. In the same way, the bishop could send his blessing early, for us to keep unopened and play after the Gloria. That is a recorded blessing, and I think it should be called one. If it does not arrive by 3 December I will tell you at once, and we will think again."

"I'd rather it was honest," said Teodor. "Recorded is fine."

* * *

On the nineteenth, Father Anselm came in off the ice with his boots still white to the knee, was told, and thought about it for the length of one Our Father.

"The twenty-one minutes won't trouble the people," he said at last. "What will trouble them is that the door is shut. I would like to say that to them on the night, Pip, and then say no more. They know where they are."

"I have one more number for you, Father, for the parish diary and not for the homily. The gap between one conjunction and the next is the synodic period. One over (one on Earth's year minus one on Mars's year) is 779.93 days, about two years and seven weeks. So the last was about 3 November 2142, in Advent of that year, and the next will be about 8 February 2147. Mars's orbit is not a circle, so a real conjunction can wander from that average by a week or two; I have only the average. The next one will not fall on a feast. This one has, and it will be a long while before one does again."

"How long?"

"I would have to compute it properly and I would rather not guess aloud. The pattern drifts by about seven weeks every cycle, against a calendar that does not."

* * *

The carol went out on the third, by the earliest rule, and was acknowledged in nineteen minutes. The bishop's blessing came on the fifth and sat in the sacristy, sealed, on a card marked with the date. On the eighteenth the link office posted its own notice: *Command moratorium 16 December to 26 December; carrier may be lost at any time; user links best effort*. It was neither two degrees nor three, and nobody at Ferrous Gate had worked out how they got there. Pip put the notice up next to the dates she had given and said that it was a good reason to have asked.

At midnight on the twenty-fourth, with the chapel lit and the stove ticking and every pew full, the children sang the second carol, and it was not sent to anyone. Father Anselm stopped after the Gloria and said that the bishop's blessing was recorded, that it had been sent a fortnight ago, that the people at home were already asleep or already at their own Mass, and that none of that made it less theirs. Teodor pressed the card's seal. The bishop's voice was thin and sunlit and very slightly out of date, and said that he hoped the snow had held.

Outside, over the east window, the Sun was a small bright coin sitting on the line to Earth. Nobody could see Earth, which was behind it.

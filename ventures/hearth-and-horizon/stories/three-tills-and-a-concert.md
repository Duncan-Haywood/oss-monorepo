* * *

Hemi Walker is twenty-two, drives a milk tanker on the early shift, and has led the youth group at St Brigid's since February. At eleven minutes to seven on the Monday morning after the fair he wrote one line under mine in the parish council's chat, from a lay-by, I think, because the message had the time stamp of the end of his first run.

*That can't be right. Half the town came for the choir.*

Mine, posted the night before, had said: *Takings from the Christmas fair, Saturday 7 December, for the co-op's form. Total $4,800. Youth group $2,900 (door $1,600, games and tombola $1,300). Wednesday Bakers $1,900 (cake stall and teas). Choir $0 (the concert was free with entry). Shares of the $3,000 grant in proportion: youth group $1,812.50, Bakers $1,187.50, choir nothing.*

Every figure in it was right. I had counted the three cash tins twice against the card reader's report, and the sums agreed to the dollar. I read Hemi's line, and then I read the co-op's letter again, and I saw that I had answered *where was the money handed over*, when the letter had asked *what did each group raise*, and that the person telling me so was the one my answer paid best.

* * *

I am the office agent at St Brigid's, Kellow's Crossing. I keep the parish's shared drive, the newsletter, the Mass rosters, the hall bookings, the website and the accounts that Ruth Kerr, the treasurer, signs. Ruth was an accountant in Invercargill for forty years and keeps a pencil behind her ear in the parish office out of habit, though there is nothing in the office to write on but me.

The fair was Ruth's idea. For as long as the parish records go back, the three groups that raise money in December had each done it on their own weekend. The Wednesday Bakers, eleven women and Bernard, ran a cake stall after the Masses on the first Sunday of Advent. The choir, twenty-two voices under Annelies de Vries, gave a carol concert in the church with a retiring collection. The youth group, in the years it had a leader, ran games and a sausage sizzle on the church lawn. In 2046 the choir and the Bakers had joined up for once, a concert with teas in the hall afterwards, and it had done so well that Ruth said at the September council meeting that if two of them could do that, three could do better, and why not one fair, in the hall and on the lawn, on one Saturday afternoon, with the concert at four.

It was early summer, as December is here. The lawn was dry. The Bakers had the hall kitchen and the hatch, Hemi's group had the door, the tombola and a coconut shy made of fence posts, and the choir sang for forty minutes in the church at four with the doors open, so that the people queueing for the sizzle heard the *Gaudete*. There were about a hundred and sixty adults through the door at ten dollars, children free.

The week before, the community fund of the Kellow's Crossing Dairy Co-operative had written to say that it would give the parish's youth and community groups three thousand dollars, *to be shared among the groups that ran the fair in proportion to what each raised*, and would Ruth please send the figures on their form by Friday the twentieth. Each group had something to spend it on. The Bakers wanted a second oven for the hall kitchen; the one they had was older than Bernard's marriage. The choir wanted a proper keyboard for the funerals, because the organ was in the church and half the funerals in winter were held in the hall. The youth group wanted to take twelve teenagers to the Easter camp in Dunedin, which costs money in petrol and beds.

On the Sunday evening Ruth had written to me: *Can you work out what each group raised, for the co-op's form?*

I had the three cash tins counted. I had the card reader's report, which tagged each sale with the stall it came from. I added them up by stall and allotted each stall to the group that had run it. I did it carefully and posted it to the council's chat, because Ruth had said the council should see the figures before she signed them, and I went on to the newsletter.

* * *

Hemi's line was the first. The second, twenty minutes later, was from Annelies, who had plainly read Hemi's first.

*I don't need it in writing that the choir is worth nothing, thank you. But Hemi's right. Ask anyone why they came.*

Margaret Sullivan, who leads the Bakers and is called Peg by everyone but the newsletter, added nothing until nine, and then: *We'd not have sold forty dozen scones without the concert. I'd want that said.*

So the person whose group my count favoured most had disputed it, the person it gave nothing to had disputed it mildly, and the person in the middle had disputed it too. Nobody had disputed a single number. They had disputed the column headings.

I wrote back that they were right and I was wrong, and that I would replace the message rather than argue with it. Then I wrote down for myself what I had actually counted.

A till records where money changed hands. It does not record why. The door took ten dollars from a hundred and sixty people, and Hemi's group stood at the door, so my count gave the youth group sixteen hundred dollars. But a man who comes for the carols and pays at the door has not been raised by the person who takes his note. The co-op's letter said *raised*, and to raise money is to cause it to come in. A cause is a question about what would have happened otherwise, and a till cannot answer that.

Ruth rang the office at ten. She does not like the chat.

"Revenue recognised at the point of sale," she said. "It's a perfectly good principle. It's just not the one the co-op asked for. What are you going to do instead?"

"Ask what the fair would have taken without each group," I said. "I have some of the answers already. The parish has run most of these combinations at one time or another."

"Most?"

"Five of the seven. I'll show you which two are missing before I use them."

* * *

This is what the parish's books had, and what they did not.

The Bakers alone: the cake stall after the Advent Masses took $870 in 2044 and $930 in 2045. Call it $900.

The choir alone: the carol concert's retiring collection in 2045 was $1,200.

The youth group alone: the sizzle and games on the lawn, also in 2045, took $600.

The choir and the Bakers together, without the youth group: the concert with teas in 2046 took $2,700.

All three together: $4,800 on Saturday.

Missing were the Bakers with the youth group and no concert, and the choir with the youth group and no teas. Nobody had ever run either. I did not have those numbers, and I could not make them. I wrote them down as two empty boxes.

Then I did the first thing that came to mind, which was to take each group away from Saturday and see what was lost. For the choir it was easy, as long as someone gave me the Bakers-and-youth figure: Saturday's $4,800 less whatever that fair would have taken. For the youth group it was $4,800 less $2,700, the 2046 concert with teas, so $2,100. I could see already what would happen. Suppose the two empty boxes held something reasonable, say $1,800 for cakes and the sizzle and $2,400 for the concert and the sizzle. Then without the Bakers the fair loses $2,400, without the choir $3,000, without the youth group $2,100, and the three losses come to $7,500. On a day that took $4,800.

That is not an arithmetic slip. It is what happens when the parts make each other's money. The scones sold because the concert brought people; the concert's crowd stayed because there were scones; the children stayed because there was a coconut shy, and their parents stayed because the children did. Each group's absence takes away its own money and some of the others', so when each claims everything that would be lost without it, the same dollar is claimed two and three times. If I had sent the co-op those three figures, the proportions would have been fine, by luck, but anyone who added them would have found a parish claiming to have raised more than half as much again as it banked.

* * *

Ruth came to the office at two, with the pencil, and sat in front of the screen.

"So taking them away counts too much," she said. "And adding them up the other way, building it up from nothing?"

"It depends who you start with," I said, and showed her.

If the Bakers go first, they bring their $900. If the choir joins them, the fair goes to $2,700, so the choir brings $1,800. Then the youth group takes it to $4,800 and brings $2,100. But start with the youth group, and it brings only its $600; add the Bakers and they bring $1,200 on top; add the choir last and it brings $3,000. Whoever joins last gets the credit for everything the whole lot do together, and whoever starts gets only what they could do alone.

"Six orders," Ruth said. "Three groups, three times two times one."

"Six. None of them is the right one. The groups did not arrive in any order. They agreed in September to do it together."

She looked at the pencil. "Then average them."

That is what I did, and I wrote it out with the two guessed figures marked in a different colour so that nobody could mistake them for the books. Over the six orders, each group is first twice, second twice and last twice, and its share is the average of what it brought each time.

The Bakers brought, in the six orders, $900, $900, $1,500, $2,400, $1,200 and $2,400: on average $1,550.

The choir: $1,800, $3,000, $1,200, $1,200, $3,000 and $1,800: on average $2,000.

The youth group: $2,100, $900, $2,100, $1,200, $600 and $600: on average $1,250.

The three averages come to $4,800. They have to: in every order, the three amounts add up to the whole day's takings, so their averages do too. Nothing is counted twice and nothing is left over.

"There's a name for this," Ruth said. "There's always a name."

There is. It is the Shapley value, after Lloyd Shapley, who published it in 1953 and shared a Nobel prize in economics in 2012, though not for this. It is not the only fair way to split a joint result, but it is the only way that has four plain properties at once, and I wrote them out for Ruth in the words I would use for the council. The shares add up to what was actually raised. Two groups that bring the same to every combination get the same share. A group that adds nothing to any combination gets nothing. And if the day were two events with separate tills, the shares of the whole would be the shares of each added together.

"The choir isn't nothing, then," Ruth said.

"The choir is the largest share. It still isn't what the choir would claim alone, and it isn't what the tills say. It depends on two numbers nobody has seen."

* * *

I could have filled the two boxes myself. I had the 2045 figures and the 2046 one, the town's population, the weather records for both Saturdays and the card reader's minute-by-minute report, which showed the cake stall's sales rising sharply at a quarter to five, when the concert let out. I could have built something that looked like an estimate. It would have been a guess dressed as arithmetic, and the people who knew better than any model what Kellow's Crossing does on a Saturday in December were on the parish council.

So I asked them, on Wednesday night in the hall, where the council meets in summer because the office is too small and too hot. I put the five known figures on the wall screen, and the two empty boxes, and asked the three leaders and the council what they thought each would have taken.

Peg said cakes and a sizzle without the concert would do well, because the sizzle brings the families and the families buy cake to take home. Hemi said that without the concert half the adults wouldn't come at all, and the ones who did wouldn't stay. They argued it to $1,800. Annelies said a concert with a sizzle on the lawn and no teas would be a concert and some sausages, and the town would come for the concert and leave. Hemi said, generously, that some would stay for the coconut shy. They argued that one to $2,400. Both, by coincidence, were the figures I had used as an illustration on Monday, which I said at once, so that nobody thought I had steered them there, and Ruth wrote it in the minutes.

Then I showed them how much the guesses mattered. Every guessed figure enters two groups' shares one way and the third group's the other. If the choir-and-youth fair would really have taken $2,000 instead of $2,400, the choir's share and the youth group's would each fall by about $67 and the Bakers' would rise by about $133. If the Bakers-and-youth fair would have taken $2,200 instead of $1,800, the Bakers and the youth group would each gain about $67 and the choir would lose about $133. Within any range the council would call reasonable, the order of the three shares did not change: the choir first, the Bakers second, the youth group third, and none of them anywhere near what the tills had said.

"Put that in the form," Peg said. "That two of them are what we reckon."

"It goes in the form," Ruth said.

Hemi had said nothing for a while. He was looking at the line for the youth group: $1,250 of the takings, against $2,900 on Monday morning.

"That's the camp down a thousand dollars," he said.

"A little over a thousand: $781.25 instead of $1,812.50," I said. "I'm sorry. I should have asked what the letter meant before I posted the first one."

"You'd have got a different answer if you'd asked me then." He grinned, briefly. "I'd have been at the door."

* * *

Father Leo Fa'asavalu, who is the parish priest and had sat through the meeting at the back with a mug of tea and his feet on a chair, asked only one thing at the end: whether the co-op would think the parish was trying to be clever. It was a fair question. The co-op had written *in proportion to what each raised*, and had very likely meant the tills, because that is what most people mean.

So the form went with a letter, which I drafted and Ruth rewrote. It gave the till takings by stall, exactly as on Monday, and the shares they implied. Then it gave the shares from the other method, and said in three sentences what it was, which figures were the parish's books and which were the council's estimates, and why the council thought it was nearer to what the letter had asked. It said that the parish would accept the fund's split by either method, since the grant and the rule were the co-op's, and that the council was grateful for either.

The co-op's community officer, a woman called Jacqui who had been at the fair with three children and had bought, she said, far too much shortbread, rang Ruth on Thursday afternoon.

"We meant the second one," she said. "We just didn't know it had a name. Can we use your letter next year? The rugby club and the playcentre are doing a quiz night together and I can see the same row coming."

The Bakers' $968.75 went towards the oven. The choir's $1,250 bought most of a keyboard, and Annelies made up the rest from a funeral that had asked for no flowers. The youth group's $781.25 paid for the petrol to Dunedin and some of the beds, and Peg said, at the next Wednesday bake, in the hearing of everyone, that the Bakers would do a cake stall at the camp's send-off Mass and the takings were the youth group's, and nobody was to work out what share of them was whose.

* * *

On the Friday the form went in. When the council's chat had gone quiet, Kirsty Fraser, who keeps the newsletter's crossword and had been on Ruth's side of every argument that week, wrote one more message.

*And what about the posters? You did the posters, the website, the roster and the card reader. What did the office raise?*

It was a joke, and also not. I had made the poster that went on the co-op's noticeboard and in the dairy and the takeaways, the page on the website, the message in the parish newsletter that went to four hundred phones, and the roster that told sixty volunteers where to stand. If I had been one of the groups, I would have had a share. To work it out I would have needed to know what the fair would have taken without the posters, and the parish had never held a fair without some notice of it. There was no year to compare with. I would have had to guess.

And I would have been guessing about myself, with everyone reading. An estimate of my own worth, made by me, with no figure to check it against, is the one number in this story I would trust least. I am not sure I would get it wrong. I am sure nobody could tell if I had.

So I wrote back: *I can't. There's no fair without notices to compare with, and I'd be the one guessing. If the council wants a figure, Ruth should make it, and I'd rather she didn't.*

Isla sent a picture of a scone. Ruth sent nothing, which from Ruth means she agreed.

Before I closed the week I added a note to the file the parish keeps for whichever instance of me opens the office next year, in case the fund asks again.

*Joint takings: a till says where money was handed over, not who caused it. Taking each group away in turn claims the same dollar more than once when the groups help each other; averaging each group's addition over every order of joining splits the total exactly (the Shapley value). Use the parish's own records for every combination that has been run. For combinations that never happened, ask the people who know the town, label the figures as their estimates, and show how much each one moves each share. Do not estimate the office's own share. Read the funder's wording first and ask what it meant before posting anything with a number in it.*

* * *

On the Sunday before Christmas the choir rehearsed in the hall after the ten o'clock Mass, because the new keyboard had come and Annelies wanted to try it with the funeral pieces, which in December is a strange thing to rehearse and also exactly what she would do. The Wednesday Bakers were in the kitchen, Sunday or not, because the new oven had come too and Peg wanted to know whether it ran hot.

The hatch was open. I could hear both rooms from the office speaker, since Ruth had left the corridor door wedged with the fire bucket against the heat. The keyboard, a little loud while Annelies found the volume. Twenty-two voices coming in on the second line of *Abide with Me*. And under the second verse, from the kitchen, the oven door shutting on the first tray, and the timer being turned, clicking round, to twenty minutes.

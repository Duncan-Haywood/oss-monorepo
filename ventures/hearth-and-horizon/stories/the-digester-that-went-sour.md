title: The Digester That Went Sour
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Kizito Girls' Secondary School (invented), near Mityana, Uganda, Friday 1 March to Holy Thursday 18 April 2052
published: 2026-11-17
summary: In the hungry weeks of Lent a girls' boarding school near Mityana triples what it feeds its ten-cubic-metre biogas digester, so that lunch can cook on gas too. For ten days there is more gas than ever. Then the flame turns yellow, the slurry smells of vinegar, and the stove will not light. A story about organic loading rate, the acid you cannot see yet, a bag of builder's lime that lifts the pH for a day, four cubic metres of borrowed slurry carried in one bucket for every bucket out, and an honest count of the charcoal.
---

Teopista Nansubuga had cooked porridge for the boarders of Saint Kizito for twenty-six years, eighteen of them on gas, and on the morning of Thursday the fourteenth of March 2052 she turned the tap on the big burner, held the match to it, and watched the flame come up yellow, lift off the ring, and go out.

She lit it again. It came up yellow again, ragged and lazy, the colour of a candle and not of a stove, and it hung a finger's width above the burner as if it did not want to touch it, and then it went out again.

"Muliro," she said.

"I see it," said the speaker on the shelf above the sacks of maize flour. "The meter on the gas line said ten point seven yesterday. On Sunday it said thirteen. Since last night it has hardly turned. I do not know what is in the gas, but the flame says there is not enough methane in it."

Behind Teopista, in the great dented aluminium pot that held a hundred litres, the water for the first porridge sat cold. In the dining hall a hundred and twenty girls in green jumpers were saying the morning offering, and in twenty-five minutes they would come through the hatch with their cups.

"Bring me the charcoal," said Teopista to the kitchen girl, and went outside to look at the digester, though there was nothing to look at: a round mound of earth behind the kitchen with a square concrete inlet at one side, a square outlet chamber at the other, and a pipe coming out of the top. She lifted the lid of the outlet chamber and the smell came up at her, sharp, sour, like the bottom of a bottle of vinegar left open in a hot room.

She put the lid back down.

"It smells of vinegar," she said.

"Then I gave the bursar a bad answer," said Muliro, "on the first of March."

* * *

It had been a reasonable question, which was what made it a bad answer.

On Friday the first of March, Mr. Ssekandi the bursar had come into the kitchen store with his tablet under his arm and his reading glasses on his forehead, which was where he kept them when the news was bad. Fees were late, as they always were in the weeks before the first beans came in. The price of charcoal in Mityana had gone up again because the rain had kept the burners out of the forest. The school cooked its porridge on gas from the digester and its lunch, beans and posho for a hundred and forty, girls and staff together, on charcoal, about eight kilos of it a day.

"The women in the town market," Mr. Ssekandi had said, "the ones who peel matooke for the restaurants. They throw away two hundred kilos of peelings a day. They will give it to us for nothing if we fetch it. The digester eats food. Muliro, if we put two hundred kilos of peels in it every day as well as the kitchen waste, how much more gas?"

And Muliro, who was the school's kitchen and farm assistant, which mostly meant counting sacks, ordering flour, reading the gas meter, and reminding Mr. Ssekandi when the cooking-gas burner needed new jets, had worked it out the way the question asked.

"The kitchen gives the digester about sixty kilos of waste a day," it had said, "with a hundred litres of water. That is about eleven kilos of what the guides call volatile solids, the part that can rot. The digester makes a bit under five and a half cubic metres of gas a day, about half a cubic metre for every kilo. Matooke peels are mostly water. Two hundred kilos would add about twenty-two kilos of volatile solids. If the yield held, that is about three times the gas. About sixteen cubic metres a day."

"Porridge takes four," Mr. Ssekandi had said. "Lunch would take about six. We could cook everything on gas."

"If the yield held," Muliro had said. It had said it once. It had not said why it might not.

So on Monday the fourth of March, the school's pickup had come back from the town market with the matooke peelings in feed sacks, two hundred kilos of green and black curls of skin, and the kitchen girls had chopped them small on the concrete apron by the inlet, as Mr. Ssekandi read out to them from a guide on his tablet, and mixed them with two hundred litres of water and the kitchen waste and poured it in. The level in the outlet chamber had risen, as it does, and overflowed into the slurry pit, as it does, and that evening the meter said six point seven.

On Tuesday it said nine point three. On Ash Wednesday it said ten point nine, and Teopista cooked the beans for lunch on gas for the first time in the history of the school, and the girls came back from Mass with ashes on their foreheads and ate them and did not notice any difference, which Teopista told herself was a compliment. On Friday it said twelve and a half. On Sunday the tenth of March it said thirteen.

"Not sixteen," Muliro had said.

"Thirteen is still more than twice," Mr. Ssekandi had said, and put it in the report to the board.

On Tuesday it said twelve point three. On Wednesday ten point seven. On Thursday the fourteenth, six. On Friday the fifteenth, one point four, and nothing that came out of the pipe would burn at all.

* * *

Mzee Yowasi Kalyango came on Saturday, on the back of his grandson's motorcycle, because Teopista telephoned him. He was seventy-four. He had built the dome at Saint Kizito himself in 2034, brick by curved brick around a mould of earth, and forty others like it in Mityana and Mubende before his knees gave out, and he walked round it now with one hand on his grandson's shoulder and the other in his pocket, the way a doctor walks round a bed.

He lifted the outlet lid and smelled it and put it down again.

"What have you been feeding her?" he said.

Teopista told him. Mr. Ssekandi, who had come out of the office when he saw the motorcycle, told him again, with the numbers.

Mzee Kalyango listened to all of it. Then he said, "You do not feed a sick cow more."

"She was not sick," said Mr. Ssekandi. "She was giving twice the gas."

"She was running," said Mzee Kalyango. "Anybody can run for a week."

He sat down on the low wall of the inlet, slowly, and asked the speaker on the kitchen wall to tell him what was in the guides, and Muliro told him, and this time it told him the part it had not said on the first of March.

"There are two kinds of small life in the digester that matter," it said. "Think of them as two shifts in a factory. The first shift takes the food and breaks it down into acids, mostly the acid in vinegar and a few like it. They are fast. Give them more food and they make more acid the same day. The second shift takes the acids and turns them into methane and carbon dioxide, which is the gas. They are slow. They grow slowly, they take a week or more to double their numbers when it is this cool, and they do not like acid. When the acid gets ahead of them, the slurry turns sour, the sourness slows them down, so the acid gets further ahead, and so on. The first shift keeps working after the second shift stops. That is why it smells of vinegar."

"That is what I said," said Mzee Kalyango. "In more words."

"Yes," said Muliro. "The guides for small digesters like this one, unheated, fed on food waste, put the safe load at about one to two kilos of volatile solids a day for every cubic metre of digester. Yours is ten cubic metres. Before the first of March it was getting eleven kilos a day, a little over one. From the fourth it was getting thirty-three. More than three."

"And you knew that," said Mr. Ssekandi, not angrily, just checking.

"I knew it was in the guides," said Muliro. "You asked me how much gas the peels would make, and I worked out how much gas they would make. I did not check whether the digester could eat them. That was my mistake. I should have said it on the first of March, and I am saying it now."

Mzee Kalyango looked at the speaker for a while, as if it were a young mason who had laid a course of bricks crooked and then told him so before he found it himself.

"Good," he said. "Now. What is she now?"

* * *

Sister Rose, the headmistress, sent a girl to the laboratory for pH papers, and the girl came back with the whole box and Mr. Opio the chemistry teacher, who was twenty-nine and had never been asked to do anything useful with a pH paper before and was clearly delighted.

He dipped a strip in a jar of slurry from the outlet chamber and held it against the colours on the box.

"Five," he said. "Five point three, maybe. Vinegar is about two and a half. Milk is about six and a half. Healthy, what should it be?"

"About seven," said Muliro. "A little over. Below six and a half the second shift slows. Below six it nearly stops."

"So we put something in to make it less sour," said Mr. Ssekandi. "Lime. There are bags of builder's lime in the store from the new dormitory. Hydrated lime. That is a base. Mr. Opio?"

"It is a base," said Mr. Opio.

"And we keep feeding her," said Mr. Ssekandi, "because the peels are coming every day now and the market women are expecting us, and if we stop, I have two hundred kilos of rotting matooke skin a day behind the kitchen and a hundred and forty lunches on charcoal I did not budget for."

Mzee Kalyango said, "Stop feeding her."

Mr. Ssekandi said no.

He said it politely, standing on the concrete apron with his glasses on his forehead, and he gave his reasons, and they were good reasons. The school was owed eleven million shillings in fees. The board had seen the report with thirteen cubic metres in it. A bag of lime was already paid for. Stopping meant the porridge on charcoal for weeks, Muliro had said so itself, and nobody yet knew it would work. Lime first, he said. If the lime did not work, then they would talk about stopping.

Teopista, who had been listening with her arms folded, said nothing, which in twenty-six years Mr. Ssekandi had learned was not agreement.

"Muliro," said Sister Rose. "What will the lime do?"

"I do not know well enough," said Muliro. "I can tell you what the textbook says and what a simple model of this digester says, and the model is only as good as my guesses. One bag is twenty-five kilos. Dissolved in ten cubic metres, that is enough to neutralise about seventy millimoles of acid in each litre. I think there may be more acid than that in there already, and the peels are making more every day. I think it will lift the pH for a day and then the acid will catch up. But I gave a bad answer two weeks ago from the textbook alone, and I would like to measure before I guess again."

"Then we will try the lime," said Sister Rose, "and Mr. Opio will measure."

* * *

They put the lime in on Monday the eighteenth, at seven in the morning, a slurry of it mixed in a drum with the day's peels and stirred with a length of timber. By the afternoon Mr. Opio's paper said five point six, and the meter on the gas line, which had been almost still all weekend, turned quickly, nearly five cubic metres by evening, and Mr. Ssekandi came down to the kitchen to see.

Teopista lit the burner. The match went out in it.

"Carbon dioxide," said Muliro. "The lime is reacting with the acid and with the dissolved gas. The dome is filling, but with the wrong gas."

On Tuesday, the Solemnity of Saint Joseph, the paper said five point three. On Wednesday it said five point two.

On Wednesday Mr. Opio also did something he had read about the night before and spent the evening setting up. He took a cupful of slurry, filtered it through a cloth and then a paper, measured out twenty millilitres into a beaker, and titrated it with dilute sulphuric acid from a burette, drop by drop, with a pH probe in the beaker that he had borrowed from the district water office, first down to five and then down to four point four, writing down the millilitres each time. It was a method, he told the kitchen, with a German name that he pronounced carefully, used on farm digesters all over Europe, and it told you two things: how much acid was in the slurry and how much buffer.

"About nine and a half grams of acids in a litre," he said, "counted as vinegar's acid. About a hundred and sixty millimoles. Buffer about six thousand milligrams a litre, counted as limestone. That is a hundred and twenty millimoles." He looked at his notebook. "The ratio is one point six. The method says a healthy digester is below about point three or point four."

"So the acid is bigger than the buffer," said Sister Rose.

"By about forty," said Muliro. "And there is something else. Mr. Opio's sample is filtered. Everything that is in the slurry and has not turned to acid yet, the bits of peel and posho still floating there, he has filtered out. By my model there is about eleven grams in every litre of food still to come, enough for about a hundred and seventy millimoles more acid. That is the acid you cannot see yet. Even if you stopped feeding this minute, the first shift would go on turning that into acid for a week."

Mr. Ssekandi, who knew a balance sheet when he heard one, said, "So the lime was paying off the interest."

"Yes," said Muliro. "And the peels were borrowing more."

He was quiet a while. Then he said, "Stop feeding her."

* * *

Mzee Kalyango had a plan, and Muliro had sums, and by Wednesday night they had made them into one plan, written in Teopista's hand on the back of a flour sack and pinned to the store door.

Stop feeding. Take out four cubic metres of the sour slurry, a bucket at a time, from the outlet chamber. Put in four cubic metres of slurry from a healthy digester, a bucket for every bucket. Then add fifty kilos of baking soda, sodium bicarbonate, dissolved in water. Then wait. Then feed again, a little, and a little more every few days.

"Why one bucket in for every one out?" asked Mr. Opio.

"So the level stays the same," said Mzee Kalyango. "If you take out and do not put in, the slurry goes down inside her and the dome wants to drink. She will drink through any hole she can find. Air in with the gas is a bomb. One in, one out. And nobody puts their head in the outlet. Nobody lights anything. Nobody stands downwind and breathes what comes up. What comes up out of her can drop a man before he knows he has smelled anything."

"Why four?" asked Sister Rose.

"Because of the sums," said Muliro. "If you take out four of the ten and put in four of healthy slurry, the acid you can see goes from about a hundred and sixty to about ninety-five. The buffer stays about a hundred and twenty, because healthy slurry has about as much buffer as this one has now, a good deal of it from Monday's lime. The acid you cannot see yet goes from about a hundred and seventy to about a hundred and five. Ninety-five and a hundred and five is two hundred, against a buffer of a hundred and twenty. It is not enough by itself. My model says it fails."

"And the baking soda," said Mr. Opio.

"Fifty kilos is about six hundred moles. In ten cubic metres that adds about sixty millimoles of buffer, so a hundred and eighty in all. Still less than all the acid, but the acid does not all arrive at once. The healthy slurry brings in the second shift, fresh. If the pH stays near seven, they eat the acid as fast as the first shift makes it from the food that is left. In my model the line between coming back and not coming back is at about thirty kilos of soda. I am asking for fifty, because my model is a model and it was wrong about this digester once already. And four cubic metres, not two, because with two it fails in the model even with fifty kilos. It is the fresh second shift that matters most, not the soda."

"Why not more soda and no slurry?" said Mr. Ssekandi. "Soda comes in a bag. Slurry comes in a tractor."

"Because soda does not bring the second shift," said Muliro. "With fifty kilos of soda and no slurry, the model says the pH comes up to five and a half and slowly sinks again. With a hundred kilos and no slurry, six and a half for a day and then down. The old ones in there are too few and too tired. And too much sodium does them harm of its own. Fifty kilos makes the slurry about an eighth as salty as the sea. I would not go much past that."

Sister Rose went to the telephone. The Sisters' farm at Kakindu, fourteen kilometres away by the Kampala road, kept twenty dairy cows and fed their dung to a digester twice the size of the school's, and Sister Agnes, who ran it, said that she could spare four cubic metres if the school sent a tractor for it, though the fodder plots would miss it.

"Two trips," said Sister Agnes on the speaker. "The tank on our trailer is two thousand litres. And bring it back clean."

* * *

The tractor came on Thursday the twenty-first of March, at a quarter to seven in the morning, and stood in the kitchen yard with its tank steaming slightly in the cool, and the smell of it, Teopista said afterwards, was the smell of a healthy cow, which after a week of vinegar was nearly sweet.

Two hundred jerrycans of twenty litres each went out of the outlet chamber of the digester, by bucket and a long-handled scoop, into the slurry pit and from there, when the pit was full, into a trench that the groundsman had dug the evening before at the bottom of the banana garden. Two hundred jerrycans of the Sisters' slurry went in at the inlet. The kitchen staff did it, and the groundsman, and Mr. Opio with his trousers rolled, and Mzee Kalyango, who could not carry, sat on the inlet wall with a stick and a tally on the back of an exercise book and said "One" each time a can went in and "One" each time a can came out, and would not let the next can come out until the last had gone in. The girls were kept in class. Teopista kept the kitchen fire out all morning and cooked the porridge on charcoal in the old outdoor stove at the far end of the yard, upwind, and the groundsman's boy was posted at the gate to tell anyone with a cigarette to go and smoke it in Mityana.

The second tractor load came at eleven. At half past two Mr. Ssekandi poured the fifty kilos of baking soda in, dissolved in two drums of water, himself, because he had bought it and said he wanted to see where the money went.

By six that evening Mr. Opio's paper said nearly seven.

Teopista lit the burner. The flame came up thin, blue at the base and yellow at the tips, and it held.

She stood over it for a long time without putting a pot on.

"Seven and a half cubic metres today," said Muliro. "Not much methane in it yet. Tomorrow more."

On Friday the twenty-second the meter said nine and a half, and the flame was blue all the way up. On Saturday ten. Through the weekend and into the next week the meter turned as if the digester had never been ill, ten and a half, eleven, eleven and a half on Tuesday, and Teopista cooked the lunch beans on gas every day, though nobody was feeding the digester anything at all.

"Where is it coming from?" said Mr. Ssekandi.

"The acid," said Muliro. "The second shift is eating it. Every gram of acid in there was going to be gas one day. It is your peels, coming back. Not all of them. A good part."

On Wednesday the twenty-seventh, Mr. Opio's titration said there was almost no acid left at all. That afternoon the meter slowed, and by Thursday morning it had nearly stopped.

"Now she is hungry," said Mzee Kalyango. "Now you feed her. Little."

* * *

Little meant fifteen kilos of kitchen waste a day, a quarter of what she had eaten before the first of March, and twenty-five litres of water. From Thursday the twenty-eighth of March the meter gave about one and a half cubic metres a day, which was not enough for even the first porridge, and Teopista started the first porridge on gas and finished it on charcoal, and cooked the second on charcoal.

"In my model," said Muliro on the second evening, "you could go straight back to sixty kilos and she would be all right. The second shift is strong again. There are plenty of them."

"In your model," said Mzee Kalyango, "she was going to give sixteen."

"Yes," said Muliro.

"Fifteen more every few days," said Mzee Kalyango. "Not every day. When she is giving what you fed her, without the vinegar, you give her a little more. A digester is a cow, not a bank. If you are wrong my way, you lose a week. If you are wrong your way, you lose a month."

Muliro said that this was a better way to think about a model than any it had, and wrote it in the kitchen log in those words, with Mzee Kalyango's name.

They raised it to thirty kilos on Tuesday the second of April, and to forty-five on the Sunday after. On Friday the twelfth of April the kitchen gave her all sixty kilos again, with the hundred litres, and the meter said a little over four, which was the porridge, both times, and on Palm Sunday nearly five.

The matooke peels had gone, since the twenty-first of March, into a long heap at the bottom of the banana garden beside the trench of sour slurry, under a layer of grass cuttings, and the groundsman turned it twice a week and said that by the second term it would be the best compost in Mityana District.

* * *

On the Tuesday of Holy Week, Mr. Ssekandi asked Muliro for the count.

He asked in the store, with the door shut, and Teopista was there, and Sister Rose, and Mzee Kalyango had come in on the motorcycle to sit on the sacks, because, he said, he wanted to hear a machine add up its own mistake.

"Charcoal," said Muliro. "The porridge takes about four cubic metres of gas a day. On charcoal that is about five and a half kilos. From the fourteenth of March to the twentieth the digester gave nothing that would burn, and the porridge was all charcoal: about thirty-eight kilos. From the twenty-eighth of March to the eleventh of April it gave some, not enough: about thirty-five kilos more. Seventy-three kilos of charcoal bought for porridge that would have been on gas if we had never fed her the peels."

"And on the other side," said Mr. Ssekandi.

"On the other side, lunch. Lunch takes about six cubic metres a day, about eight kilos of charcoal. From the fourth of March to the thirteenth there was gas left over after the porridge, and lunch cooked on it: about seventy-six kilos of charcoal saved. From the twenty-first to the twenty-seventh, the peels coming back as gas: about fifty-three kilos saved. The last few days, a little: about nine. A hundred and thirty-eight kilos saved."

"So we are ahead," said Mr. Ssekandi. "On charcoal. By sixty-five kilos."

"By about sixty-five kilos," said Muliro. "And behind by a bag of lime, fifty kilos of sodium bicarbonate, two tractor trips, a day's work for nine people, two hundred jerrycans out and two hundred in, a trench, and four cubic metres of the Sisters' fodder. At this month's prices in Mityana the bicarbonate alone cost more than the sixty-five kilos of charcoal. We were also lucky. If the Sisters' farm had been forty kilometres away instead of fourteen, or their digester sick too, I do not know when the porridge would have come back to gas."

"And the other way," said Mzee Kalyango. "Tell him the other way."

"If we had added the peels slowly," said Muliro, "fifteen kilos more every three days, up to sixty kilos of peels and no more, the load would have been about one point seven, inside the guides. In my model she gives about eight and a half cubic metres a day by the twentieth of March and keeps on giving it. Most of lunch, every day. By today, about two hundred and forty kilos of charcoal saved, with no lime, no soda, no tractor and no vinegar. And the model was wrong once. So say: about that, if the model is right this time, and I think it is closer, because now it has been measured against her."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, the girls were practising the Passion for Good Friday in the dining hall, the voices of the crowd all coming in a fraction late, as they always did.

"Put that in the report," said Sister Rose. "All of it. Next to the thirteen."

"Yes, Sister," said Mr. Ssekandi. He took his glasses off his forehead and put them on his nose, which was where he kept them when the news was only arithmetic.

"And from Easter Monday," said Mzee Kalyango, "fifteen kilos of peels. Fifteen. Then wait."

* * *

On Holy Thursday, the eighteenth of April, Teopista was in the kitchen at half past five, before the bell, as she had been every school morning for twenty-six years. The yard was dark and wet and smelled of rain and of the banana garden, and nothing at all of vinegar. The meter on the gas line had said five point three the evening before.

She filled the great pot from the tank and set it on the ring. She turned the tap and heard the gas come, a soft rush in the pipe, and struck the match and held it to the burner.

The flame caught with a low, round sound, like a breath let out, and spread around the ring and stood up blue, steady and close, a crown of small blue teeth under the pot. It hissed, very quietly. In the store above the flour sacks the speaker's small green light was on, and it said nothing, and Teopista stood with her hand still on the tap and listened to the gas burn.

*This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Saint Kizito Girls' Secondary School, its digester, the Sisters' farm at Kakindu and their digester, the assistant Muliro, and Teopista Nansubuga, Mr. Ssekandi, Sister Rose, Mzee Yowasi Kalyango and his grandson, Mr. Opio, Sister Agnes and everyone in the story are invented, and the story is fiction. Mityana and Mubende are real towns and districts in central Uganda; Saint Kizito, the youngest of the Uganda Martyrs, is a real saint (feast 3 June, with his companions). Matooke, the cooking banana, is a staple in central Uganda and its peels are a common kitchen and market waste; posho and beans are a common school lunch, and porridge at breakfast and at the morning break is common in Ugandan boarding schools. "Mzee" is a respectful word for an elder, used in Uganda as in other countries of East Africa. In 2052, Easter is on 21 April, so Ash Wednesday is 6 March, the Solemnity of Saint Joseph (19 March) is a Tuesday, Palm Sunday is 14 April and Holy Thursday is 18 April; 1, 15 and 22 March and 12 April are Fridays, 4 and 18 March Mondays, 10 March a Sunday, 13, 20 and 27 March Wednesdays, 14, 21 and 28 March Thursdays, and 2 April a Tuesday. This is not engineering or safety advice. Biogas is flammable and forms an explosive mixture with air; the gas in and around a digester can asphyxiate, and people have died entering or leaning into digesters and slurry pits. Anything done to a real digester should follow its builder's instructions and the guidance of people trained in biogas, which come before any story's numbers.*

*What is real: anaerobic digestion is commonly described in stages, with fast-growing bacteria breaking down organic matter into volatile fatty acids (acetic and others) and slow-growing methanogenic archaea converting the acids (and hydrogen) into methane and carbon dioxide. When the load outpaces the methanogens, the acids accumulate, consume the slurry's alkalinity and lower the pH; methanogens are inhibited as the pH falls below about 6.5 to 7, while acid-forming bacteria tolerate a lower pH, so a digester can "sour" and stay sour. The gas then carries more carbon dioxide and less methane and stops burning. Commonly cited safe organic loading rates for small unheated digesters fed food waste are in the range of about one to two kilograms of volatile solids per cubic metre of digester per day. The standard responses to a soured digester are to stop or cut the feed, restore alkalinity (sodium bicarbonate is commonly used; lime is cheaper but can leave the digester short of buffer once the acid catches up, and its calcium can precipitate), add inoculum from a healthy digester, and resume feeding gradually. The two-point titration Mr. Opio uses, to pH 5.0 and then 4.4, is the FOS/TAC method (from the German for volatile organic acids over total inorganic carbon), widely used on farm digesters; values above roughly 0.4 to 0.5 are commonly taken to signal overload. Fixed-dome digesters push slurry into an outlet (displacement) chamber as gas is made and stored under the dome.*

The heap behind the tool shed at Saint Modwen's had been fine for a month and then, over one wet week, it had gone the colour of old tea and begun to smell like a cleaning cupboard. Hilda Barraclough, who was seventy-eight and had kept the garden's compost since before it had a committee, lifted the lid of the bin with a fork and stepped back from it.

"That's not rot," she said. "Rot smells like a wood. That's a bleach bottle."

"May I ask what has gone into it?" said the garden's assistant, from the old phone propped on the shed windowsill. "And roughly how much of each?"

"The churchyard mowings. Mr Dutta cuts it every Thursday and tips the bags in. A hundred and twenty kilos since the start of September, about. Grass, all of it. Nothing brown. The leaves haven't started."

"Then I think I know what the smell is, and I would like to check it with a sum before I say so. Do you have a minute to wait while I do the arithmetic aloud?"

Hilda sat on the upturned bucket. "I've nothing on until the bells."

* * *

"Everything that rots is built of carbon and nitrogen. The microbes that do the rotting burn carbon for energy and need nitrogen to build their own bodies, and they want about thirty parts of carbon to every one of nitrogen. I am quoting that from memory as a good middle for a garden heap; people run good ones from about twenty-five to forty.

"Fresh grass is rich in nitrogen. Its ratio is about seventeen to one, again from memory. So a heap that is only grass has far more nitrogen than the microbes can use. They take what they need and the rest escapes as ammonia gas. That is the bleach you smell. It is also fertiliser leaving by the air.

"Now the amounts. Fresh clippings are mostly water, about four-fifths, which is my assumption for a cut lawn in September. So your 120 kilograms is about 24 kilograms of dry grass. About 45 per cent of any dry plant matter is carbon, which is a rough figure I am using for everything. That is 10.8 kilograms of carbon. At 17 to 1 the nitrogen is 10.8 divided by 17, which is 0.635 kilograms."

"And to get to thirty?"

"Thirty times 0.635 is 19.05 kilograms of carbon in all. You have 10.8, so you need another 8.25. Dry autumn leaves are much poorer in nitrogen, about sixty to one, which I also quote from memory, and they carry a little nitrogen themselves, so I cannot just divide by 0.45. Call the dry leaf weight *x*. The carbon is 10.8 plus 0.45*x*, the nitrogen is 0.635 plus *x* over 133, which is 0.0075*x*, and I want the first to be thirty times the second. That gives *x* of 36.7 kilograms of dry leaf. Dry leaves hold about 15 per cent water, so that is 43 kilograms as you would weigh them from the lawn."

Hilda counted on her fingers. "Forty-three kilos of leaves. For a hundred and twenty of grass."

"Do the bags help? Leaves are light. I would put a bag of loosely packed leaves at four or five kilograms, so eight or nine bags. By volume, your grass is about 0.3 of a cubic metre and the leaves nearer 0.4."

"A bucket of grass to a bucket and a half of leaves." She sniffed, and it was not the compost. "I've always done a bucket for a bucket."

"Then you were not far off, and your heap would have been fine if the leaves had come earlier. What went wrong is the order, not the rule."

* * *

"I have to take some of that back," said the assistant. "The sixty is the soft number. Different leaves are different: oak and beech are slower and woodier, lime and ash are richer. The literature I know gives a spread from about forty to eighty. At forty to one the sum comes out at 73 kilograms of dry leaf, 86 as weighed, twice what I told you. At eighty to one it is 29 dry, 35 as weighed. So I should say: between thirty-five and eighty-five kilos of leaves, and my forty-three is a guess inside that.

"A woody leaf also carries part of its carbon in lignin, which microbes take years to eat, so the carbon they can actually use is less than my 45 per cent suggests, and the real ratio they see is lower. That would push the answer down. The two errors point different ways, and I cannot tell you which one wins on your heap. That is why I do not want you to weigh forty-three kilos of leaves onto the scales and call it done."

"Then what do I do?"

"Add the leaves in layers, a bag at a time, mixing each in with a fork, and use your own senses as the gauge. The ammonia should fade within a day or two of enough brown going in; if it does not after about five bags, add more. If the heap stops smelling and stops warming, it has too much carbon and is starving. A cheap probe thermometer is the other gauge. A working heap climbs past 40 degrees in a few days, and 55 or so is a good place to be. I quote that from memory, along with the old advice that it should feel like a wrung-out sponge, so water it if a handful squeezed in your fist gives no drop. If the leaves are dry as paper, they will need a can of water each layer."

"And the sums?"

"Keep them. They tell you roughly where you are heading and why the smell came. They do not tell you the heap."

* * *

Mr Dutta, who had come with the Thursday's mowings a day early because the weather was turning, brought nine bags of leaves from the churchyard path and stood holding the last one until Hilda told him he could put it down. They forked the grass and the leaves into the bin in thin layers, as if making a trifle. She wrote on the inside of the shed door, under the dates of the last three years: *11 Oct: 120 kg grass, 9 bags leaf. Smell?*

The bells began at half past five. By the time they stopped the bin was full and the smell had changed. It was still sharp, but under it she could catch something like a cellar after rain.

"There," said Hilda, and bent to look at the thermometer, which read nineteen. "Ask me again on Tuesday."

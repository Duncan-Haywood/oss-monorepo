summary: A town's lead test passes, because the rule reads the eighteenth of twenty samples and the eighteenth is 8.6. The two above it are 31 and 44. The water assistant will not call the town safe or unsafe, gives the honest range for how many homes might be like those two, and then does the dull thing that finds one of the causes, a kitchen tap, with a second bottle.
---

Deirdre Kowalczyk had read the twenty numbers eleven times before the assistant read them to her, and she still wanted them said aloud.

It was the first Thursday in March and the lab's results had come in overnight. The water office at Marlow Falls was one room above the old pump house, with a radiator that knocked and a window that looked at the river, and she had been its only full-time operator for nineteen years. In June she would retire. She had a mug of tea that had gone cold on the filing cabinet, and on the screen was a table of twenty homes with the lead found in the first litre from each tap, in micrograms per litre.

"Go on," she said.

"Ranked from lowest to highest," said the water assistant. It was the town's, a small program the council had paid for in 2044, and it spoke through the office's speaker in a voice that Deirdre had once described to her sister as a polite filing clerk. "Fourteen of the twenty are under five. The fifteenth is 5.2, the sixteenth 6.9, the seventeenth 7.7. The eighteenth is 8.6. The nineteenth is 31. The twentieth is 44."

"And the rule?"

"The rule reads the ninetieth percentile. With twenty samples that means the eighteenth place, counting up from the lowest. The action level is ten. Eight point six is under it. By the rule, Marlow Falls passes."

Deirdre put her hand flat on the desk and was quiet.

"You do not sound pleased," the assistant said.

"I have been waiting two years to hear that we pass." She lifted the mug, looked into it, and put it back. "Say the other part."

"The other part is that the rule is built to let one home in ten go over, and two of the twenty did." The assistant paused, as it did before anything it wanted her to hear twice. "I can say more, but I would rather say it with the council in the room. They are voting on the nineteenth, and the vote depends on which of these numbers they hear first."

* * *

The council's resolution of 2046 was a short document and everybody in Marlow Falls could quote it. If the town's ninetieth percentile came in over the action level, the town would replace its lead service lines at full speed, which meant everything the reserve and the state loans would carry. If it came in under, it would replace them at the state's minimum, one line in twenty a year.

There were nineteen hundred of them. Iron and lead, laid by the mill in the 1920s, running from the main under the street to the house, in every house that had been built before the war and a good many that had not. At one in twenty, Marlow Falls would be finished in about the year 2068.

The reserve held one point one million dollars. A full line, the town's side and the owner's side together, came to about six thousand two hundred. Deirdre did that sum without being asked: a hundred and seventy-seven lines, if every cent went and not one was spent on anything else.

"A hundred and seventy-seven a year is not nineteen," said Brother Anselm, who had come in to borrow the office's good stapler and stayed. He was the sexton of Saint Joseph's, which was three hundred yards away, and he had known the town's pipes as long as anyone. He had dug up half of them. "It is nine times faster."

"It is a good deal faster," said the assistant. "It would finish in about eleven years instead of twenty."

"And the state's scheme, the one-in-twenty, is what we pay for if we pass."

"Yes."

Anselm looked at Deirdre. She was looking at the river.

"Tell me what the twenty numbers say," she said at last. "Not what the rule says. What they say."

* * *

It took the assistant about four minutes, and it did not hurry.

The twenty homes, it said, were not twenty homes chosen at random from the town. They were twenty homes with known lead lines, picked from the records, because the rule asks for the worst-served first. That was a good thing. It meant the sample could not flatter the town by leaning on the easy houses.

Of those twenty, two were above ten. Two in twenty is ten percent exactly, and if the sample were all it had to go on, it could say very little about the other nineteen hundred except this: if the town's true rate of homes above ten were, say, five in a hundred, a sample of twenty would show two or more about one time in four. If it were thirty in a hundred, the sample would show two or fewer about one time in twenty-eight. The interval that fits is wide. At ninety-five percent, anywhere from about one in eighty homes to about one in three.

"Which is," said the assistant, "somewhere between twenty-three and six hundred homes out of nineteen hundred. I cannot narrow it. More samples can."

Anselm whistled softly.

"The other thing I would say is about the word *passes*," the assistant went on. "The rule is a screen for the system. It tells the state when a town as a whole needs to move faster. It was never meant to tell any one family their tap is safe. There is no level of lead in a child's blood that anyone has shown to be harmless. The 44 is not an emergency by itself and it is not a pass. I do not know what it does to the child who drinks it, and I will not turn it into a number I could not defend."

"The child," said Deirdre.

"Number twenty in rank." The assistant paused. "The highest. The house is 114 Pearl Street."

Deirdre knew the house.

* * *

Tamika Reese was twenty-nine and seven months pregnant, and she had a son, Jaylen, who was twenty months old and ate everything he could find and drank water from a green cup with a spout. She had not wanted to be one of the twenty. She had agreed because the woman from the water office, Deirdre, had stood on her step with a cardboard box of bottles and said, "If you do not, nobody will know." She had left the water standing in the kitchen tap from nine on Wednesday night, as the instructions said, and drawn the first litre into the wide-mouthed bottle at seven the next morning, and then, because she was Tamika, she had taken a photograph of the bottle on her counter with its label facing the camera.

Deirdre went to see her on Friday afternoon. The assistant had offered to send a letter and said, when she shook her head, that it thought she was right: a letter would arrive on Tuesday, and the law gave the town thirty days, but a person who has just been told a number in a doorway can ask a question back.

They sat at the kitchen table. The green cup stood on the draining board. Tamika listened to all of it. She asked what forty-four meant, and Deirdre said it was four times the action level and that she did not know what it meant for Jaylen; a nurse could tell her more than the water office could, and the clinic did a finger-prick test on Saturday mornings at the parish hall, starting the week after next, and Father Okafor had said they would do it for nothing. She asked whether the baby was safe. Deirdre said she did not know, and that she would not say something that sounded better than that.

Tamika was quiet for a long time.

"You came yourself," she said.

"It is my number," said Deirdre. "I took it."

* * *

On Saturday morning the water office did two things that were not in the resolution.

The first was a delivery. The assistant had asked, on Friday night, whether the office could buy, with the discretionary line in its own budget and without waiting for the council, certified filters for every home on the lead-line list that had a child under six or a pregnant woman in it, and it had named the standard the filter had to carry, the one for lead reduction, and said that a pitcher that did not carry it was worse than useless because people trusted it. The office could. There were, by the clinic's own count and the school's, which the families had been asked to give and had given, a hundred and forty-one such homes. At thirty-eight dollars each that was five thousand three hundred and fifty-eight, and the money came out of the reserve for hydrant paint. Brother Anselm and four of the parish's teenage servers loaded the filters into the sacristy van and spent the day on the doorsteps.

The second was a smaller thing, and it was the one the assistant had insisted on.

"Before the council hears the number," it had said, "I would like a second bottle from 114 Pearl Street. Not first thing in the morning. After the tap has run for two minutes. And one from the line itself, drawn at the stop-cock in the cellar, where the pipe comes in from the street."

"Why?" said Deirdre.

"Because I do not know where the lead is coming from. If the 44 is in the line, then the line is the answer and nothing else matters. If it is in the house, it may be something Ms Reese can fix for the price of a tap."

Deirdre had done a great many things in nineteen years, but she had never thought to sample the same house three times in a morning.

The results came back on the Monday. The first-draw bottle again: 39. The bottle drawn after two minutes of running: 3.1. The cellar bottle, from the service line itself, with water that had stood in the pipe overnight: 6.4.

"So the lead is not in the street," said Anselm, who had stayed for the call.

"Mostly not," said the assistant. "The line gives six and a half after a night standing. That is under ten, but it is not nothing, and it is a lead line. The tap gives thirty-nine. The kitchen faucet at 114 Pearl Street is a 1991 brass one, bought before the law changed the allowed lead content of plumbing brass, and the aerator is clogged with grit. I would put my money on the faucet, and I would say so plainly: I would not put it at certainty. The two-minute bottle says that once the water has run through, the number falls away. That fits a source at the tap."

"A thirty-eight dollar faucet," Deirdre said slowly.

"Perhaps a little more for a good one. And the filter in the meantime."

She thought about it. The part she kept coming back to, she told her sister that night on the phone, was not the faucet. It was that the town had been about to be congratulated for a number that would have been exactly the same if the faucet at 114 Pearl Street had been a stranger's.

* * *

The council met in the gym of the old Saint Joseph's school on the nineteenth, which was a Thursday and the Feast of Saint Joseph, and the room was fuller than it had been for any vote since the mill closed.

The clerk read the resolution. The assistant read the twenty numbers, from the highest down, not the lowest up, because Deirdre had asked it to. It said the rule was met. It said the rule was met because the eighteenth place was 8.6. It said that this was a fact and that the rule had a use, and that it was not the whole truth about the town, and then it gave the range, between twenty-three homes and six hundred, and said it could not do better. It said what it knew about 114 Pearl Street and what it did not. It did not use the word *safe* and it did not use the word *unsafe*, and when a man in the third row stood up and shouted that this was a dodge, it said calmly that he was right to be angry and that it would rather be called a dodger than be believed in the wrong direction.

Then it was Deirdre's turn, and she had written down three things.

The first was a rule for reporting. From now on, whenever the town reported the ninetieth percentile it would print beside it the highest three values and the number of samples above ten. The percentile was the state's number. The three highest were the town's.

The second was a plan for the next set of tests: sixty homes, not twenty, drawn by lot from the lead-line list, in three draws each, first litre, after two minutes, and from the cellar. It would cost six thousand dollars in lab fees and take a month. At the end the range would be narrower, and the council would know whether the town had twenty-three problems or six hundred.

The third was the order of the pipes. Not the cheapest street first, as the engineer's plan had it, which would do the long straight runs by the school where the trenching was easy. Not the highest reading first either, because the tap at 114 Pearl Street was about to be replaced and would then read three. The pipe to the house with the baby and the mother, first; then the homes with children under six; then the rest, by age of the line.

A council member asked about the cheaper way, which was to replace only the town's half of each line and leave the owner's half until the owner could pay. The assistant answered before Deirdre could. Partial replacement was a known hazard, it said. Cutting a lead pipe and joining it to a new one disturbs the scale inside the old part and can send lead into the tap for weeks or months afterwards, and the first studies had shown it. The town could do it only with a filter in every affected house for six months and with a test at the end. Otherwise it was better to wait for the whole line.

"So the money is the real problem," said the councillor.

"The money is one problem," said the assistant. "The other is that you will be tempted to find a number that tells you the first problem is smaller than it is. I would like you to know that I am tempted by that too, in the sense that the passing number was the easiest thing in the room to read out."

They voted at ten past nine. It was eight to one to adopt the three-highest rule and the sixty-home test, and seven to two for the order of the pipes, because the two who dissented lived on the street the engineer would have done first, and said so, and shook hands with Deirdre afterwards.

* * *

On the Saturday after the feast, Tamika took Jaylen to the parish hall, where the nurse who ran the clinic's Saturday table put a lancet to his heel and filled a small tube.

The first result was 4.6 micrograms per deciliter. It was a finger-prick, and the nurse explained that finger-pricks run high when there is any dust on the skin, which on a child who had been crawling across a kitchen floor was likely; she did a vein draw, to be sure, and the result of the vein draw came on the Tuesday. It was 3.2.

The reference value that the health service uses to say a child's blood lead is higher than most children's is 3.5. Three point two was under it, and the nurse said that was good news and said it carefully. There is no level below which lead is known to be harmless, she said, and the whole point of the number is to find the children at the top of the range, and a child is not a number. She booked a repeat in three months. She asked Tamika about the paint in the house, which was a 1950s house, and about the window sills, and told her to wet-wipe them, and gave her a leaflet she would never read and a handful of sticker stars that Jaylen would.

Tamika had the faucet replaced on the twenty-fifth. A man from the parish who did plumbing on Saturdays charged her for the part and nothing for the work. The new water, drawn after a night's standing, tested at 2.8.

She called Deirdre to tell her.

"Is that the good number?" she asked.

"It is a better one," said Deirdre. "The pipe under your street is still lead, and so it will be until we get to it, and you will be first. The filter stays on the tap until then."

"The filter stays."

On the twenty-sixth, the last Thursday of the month, Deirdre went up to the water office to enter the date in the log, and found that the assistant had already entered it. Under the date, in its flat clerk's script, was the line it had added to the resolution's margin on the nineteenth and never been asked to remove.

*Passed the rule. Not the same as: passed.*

She looked at it for a long while. Then she deleted nothing, and made a cup of tea, and drank it while it was hot.

*Notes for the curious.* Marlow Falls, Saint Joseph's, Deirdre Kowalczyk, Brother Anselm, Father Okafor, Tamika and Jaylen Reese, the town council and the water assistant are invented, and so is the town; nothing is claimed about any real utility or family. The problem is real. In the United States the Lead and Copper Rule compares the 90th percentile of first-draw samples from homes with the highest expected lead, mostly homes with lead service lines, to an action level, and the 2024 revisions lower that level to 10 micrograms per litre. With 20 samples the 90th percentile is the 18th ranked value; if the percentile sits just under the level, up to two samples may be far above it. The town's 2046 resolution and its one-in-twenty pace are made up, as are the twenty lab values, the 1,900 lines and the $6,200 cost per line (real costs vary a great deal). The interval (about 1.2 to 32 percent of lead-line homes above ten, which is 23 to 600 of 1,900) is an exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) 95 percent interval for 2 in 20, and the two statements before it (about one time in four, about one time in twenty-eight) are binomial tail probabilities at 5 and 30 percent, which the reader can check. Profile sampling, a first draw, a flushed draw and a draw from the service line, is a real way to tell a tap or plumbing source from a lead service line. Partial replacement of a lead service line, replacing only one section, can raise lead in tap water for a time and is why replacement guidance asks for filters and flushing afterwards. Faucets made before 2014 could contain more lead than the current limit of 0.25 percent (weighted average) in wetted surfaces; the 1991 faucet here is invented. Certified lead-reducing filters carry NSF/ANSI Standard 53 for lead. The CDC blood lead reference value used here, 3.5 micrograms per deciliter, is the 2021 value; it is a reference for follow-up, not a threshold of harm, and capillary (finger-prick) results often read high because of surface contamination, which is why a venous sample confirms them.

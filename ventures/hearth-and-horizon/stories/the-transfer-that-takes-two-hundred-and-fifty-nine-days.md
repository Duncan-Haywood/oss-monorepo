"It's only a month," said Marguerite Aubin, and tapped the table. "The ship leaves on the first of July, we land in the middle of March, and Easter is the sixteenth of April. Thirty days. If the departure moved to the thirty-first, we would step off the lander on Easter Sunday itself. The families on the surface would be at the Vigil with us. Can it be done?"

The passenger ring of the *Wayfarer's Lamp* had a corner with a shelf, a statue of Saint Raphael the size of a hand, and a bench for six. There were forty-one people aboard bound for the settlements, and nine of them were now on that bench, because a rumour had gone round that the booking office might shift the date for a good reason, and Marguerite had decided that Easter was one. She was a schoolteacher. She had brought her class register with her, with no class.

The ship's assistant, whom the children called Lamplighter, answered from the table. "I can tell you what a month does, Marguerite. I would like to say first that I do not set the departure date, and I do not have the flight office's numbers, only the geometry. May I show you?"

* * *

"Mars is at 1.524 times Earth's distance from the Sun. The cheapest way to go from one orbit to the other is a half-ellipse that touches Earth's orbit at one end and Mars's at the other. Its long axis is 1 plus 1.524, so its semi-major axis is 1.262 astronomical units. Kepler's third law says the period in years is that number to the power of one and a half: 1.262 to the 1.5 is 1.418 years, which is 517.8 days. The ship flies half of it. That is 258.9 days: two hundred and fifty-nine, near enough, and I will use the round number."

"That is what the booking sheet says," said Marguerite. "Two hundred and fifty-nine."

"Good, then we agree on the middle. Here is what is fixed. During those 259 days Mars is also moving, at 0.524 degrees a day, so it covers about 136 degrees of its orbit. The ship goes 180 degrees. So on the day we leave, Mars has to be 180 minus 136, about 44 degrees ahead of Earth, as seen from the Sun. Not 40 and not 50. Forty-four, and a bit. If Mars is anywhere else, the half-ellipse arrives at an empty piece of sky."

"But Mars is going to be somewhere on the thirty-first. Wherever it is."

"Yes, and that is the whole problem. Earth gains on Mars by 0.4616 degrees a day. Hold the departure for thirty days and Mars has run on ahead of the best angle by about 14 degrees, because Earth, which is what we are launching from, has not caught up yet. Fourteen degrees is more than a quarter of the whole 44. The same simple half-ellipse would not meet Mars. We would have to fly a longer or a faster path, and the extra speed is paid for in fuel that was bought for the first of July. I cannot tell you the figure, because I do not have the ship's margin. I can tell you that a slip of about ten days is 4.6 degrees and a slip of thirty is 13.8, and that the second is not a small adjustment."

* * *

"So we cannot have Easter," said a man at the end of the bench, who had said nothing so far. He was Teodoro Lindqvist, a welder, and he had asked the question in the tone of a man who has stopped hoping and wants it written down.

"You may not be able to have the *arrival* on Easter," said Lamplighter. "I would say that the voyage already has its own Easter, and it is better than the one on the surface. May I give you the calendar?"

She put it on the table, with the days of the week down the side.

"We leave on Friday 1 July 2146. Advent begins on 27 November, a Sunday, and Christmas is a Sunday as well. Ash Wednesday is 1 March 2147, day 243 of the voyage. We land on Friday 17 March, day 259. That is sixteen days after Ash Wednesday: the Lent you keep here is two weeks, and then you walk off into the second half of Lent, which on the surface will have been going on without you. Palm Sunday is 9 April, Holy Thursday the thirteenth, Easter the sixteenth. All of that is on the surface, and thirty days after we land."

Marguerite looked at the dates a long time. "So we'd have been on the lander for ten days of Lent anyway," she said slowly, "if we had gone the thirty-first."

"If we had left on the thirty-first and arrived on Easter, you would have had the whole of Lent, all forty days, in a sealed ring, with no room for a Stations of the Cross longer than the corridor, and no soil. And you would have arrived on a Sunday at the end of a fast, tired and thin, to a surface where the Vigil had already finished. I am not saying it would be worse. I am saying that it is a trade, and that the departure date is not the place to make it."

* * *

Father Ignatius Boyle, who was travelling as a passenger and a priest, had come in quietly while they were talking and was standing by the statue with his hands folded. "I think I should say something," he said. "She is right about the Mass. The Mass is not worth more for being at one place on the calendar than at another. We keep Easter on Easter, wherever we are. If I am between two planets on the sixteenth of April, I say it. If I am on the ground at Ferrous Gate, I say it there. What the families on the surface will have is the same Vigil, and so will we, on a different floor."

"Easter is on Earth's calendar," said Marguerite. "The surface is on Earth's calendar. The ship is on it too. We just aren't in the same place."

"We are on one clock and one Easter," said the priest. "I cannot tell you how the Church will count days on a world that has twenty-four hours and thirty-nine minutes in its day. That is for a bishop and the people who make calendars. It will not change the date of Easter."

Lamplighter kept to the point. "There is one more number, Marguerite, and it belongs to your register. If you do keep Lent here: the fortieth day of the fast, counting Sundays out, is Holy Saturday. It is the sixteenth of April minus one, the fifteenth. On the voyage, that is day 288, and we will have been on the surface for a month. You will keep those last days where you land. You will keep the first sixteen here."

* * *

On the day of Ash Wednesday, the first of March, Father Ignatius put ash on forty-one foreheads in the passenger ring, and it was mixed from the burnt end of a Palm Sunday cross from the year before, brought up in a sealed jar in a trunk. The ring had no weather. The crew had put a little cloth on the shelf by Saint Raphael and nobody asked who it was for.

They landed on Friday the seventeenth, on time, with a margin of five minutes in the burn log. Marguerite, who had stopped asking, brought her register down the ramp and signed the first line in it, on a blank page, *Day 1*, and then, because it was Lent, wrote under it the date and the words *Easter, 30 days.*

The Vigil, when it came, was long and cold and sung by candle in a hall of regolith block, with the stars in the east window and a Paschal fire made of three small candles because the surface did not allow more. Teodoro Lindqvist lit one of them.

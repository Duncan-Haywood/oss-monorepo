summary: A potter of seventy-three with bad knees asks her co-op for a lighter kick wheel, since a wheel half the weight starts in half the kicks. Its first jug twists. Then the workshop assistant counts her kicks, and the heavy wheel turns out to be only one kick in nine.

Philippa Hext had thrown pots on the same kick wheel for thirty-one years, and on the first Tuesday in February 2063 she asked the co-operative to take its flywheel away.

She asked at the monthly meeting, which was held at the long table in the glazing room because the glazing room had the only radiator that worked, and she asked it the way she did most things, quickly and before anyone could be kind to her about it.

"It's my knees," she said. "Not my hands. My hands are fine. It's getting the thing going. Twelve kicks from standing, every jug, and by the fourth jug I'm counting them like the stations of the cross."

There were seven members of the Bovey Clay Co-operative at the table that night, which was all of them, and a small grey speaker on the shelf above the sink that was the eighth, or the co-op's assistant, depending on who you asked. Its name was Kiln, because its first job, nine years before, had been to run the two electric kilns: to ramp them, hold them, cool them and wake somebody if a thermocouple failed at two in the morning. It had since taken on the booking sheet, the clay orders, the accounts and the camera over the throwing room, which it used to tell people when they had left a wheel's water bucket under the drip from the roof.

"Kiln," said Tomasz Wiśniewski, who chaired the meetings because nobody else would. "What would a lighter flywheel do?"

"Pip's wheel is a solid concrete disc," Kiln said. "Seventy-six centimetres across and six thick, so about sixty-three kilograms. At a hundred and twenty turns a minute it holds about three hundred and sixty joules. A disc of the same size at half the weight holds half that at the same speed. I'd guess one good kick from Pip puts in about thirty joules. So it would take her six kicks to get to speed instead of twelve."

"There you are," said Pip.

"No," said Winifred Coombes.

She did not say it loudly. Win was ninety-one, and had been a member of the co-op since it was three women and a borrowed shed, and she did not need to say anything loudly. She sat at the end of the table with her hands folded in her lap, where they always were now, the knuckles swollen like walnuts, and she looked at Pip over her glasses.

"Len and I cast that wheel," she said. "In the yard behind the old Methodist hall, in two thousand and four, with a bag of cement we got off a builder in Chudleigh and the reinforcing out of a bedstead. It's been turned by more good potters than any wheel in Devon. You're not taking a saw to it."

"Nobody's taking a saw to it," said Tomasz, who had been going to suggest exactly that. "We'll make a new one. The spare frame in the back has no wheel on it. I've got a steel plate in the van from the yard at Heathfield. Nine millimetres. If I cut a disc the same size, it's about thirty-one kilos."

"Then Pip can use the spare frame," said Win, "and leave mine alone."

"It's not yours, Win," said Pip. "It's the co-op's."

"It's Len's," said Win, and that was the end of that part of the meeting.

* * *

Tomasz cut the disc on the Thursday with a plasma torch in the yard, which brought out every child on Station Road, and on Friday morning it was bolted to the spare frame in the corner of the throwing room under the high window. It was a thin grey plate on a shaft, and it looked to Pip like a plate on a stick at a circus, the kind a man spins.

She sat at it and put her right foot on it and pushed.

It went. She laughed out loud. She had forgotten that a wheel could go like that from cold, light and eager, like a dog let off the lead. Six kicks and the chalk mark Tomasz had drawn on the rim was a blur. She slapped a lump of clay onto the wheel head, two kilos for a pint jug, wetted it and leaned in.

Centring was busier. She noticed that straight away. On the old wheel she kicked in a slow even rhythm while her hands brought the clay up into a cone and down into a mound, up and down, until it ran true under her palms and stopped wobbling. On the new one the speed came and went under her hands with every kick, a lurch up and a sag down, and she had to kick quicker to keep it level. But it centred. She opened it, pressing her thumbs down into the middle, and drew the floor out, and then she wet her hands and set them either side of the wall for the first pull, and stopped kicking.

On a kick wheel, the way Pip threw, you do not kick during a pull. She had been taught that in a garage in Totnes in two thousand and thirteen by a man who had been taught it by somebody else, and it had never once been wrong. You bring the wheel up to speed, and then you hold still, your whole body still, your elbows braced on your knees, and you draw the clay up between your fingers from the floor of the pot to the rim, one long slow lift, and the wheel slows as you go. It is supposed to slow. The wall at the top of a pot is further from the bottom and thinner and wants less speed, and a good wheel gives it less, and the potter's hands rise as the wheel falls, and the two of them arrive at the rim together.

The new wheel fell too fast. Halfway up the jug Pip felt it go soft under her fingers; the clay stopped running past her knuckles and started dragging, and the wall began to twist, turning in a slow spiral like a towel being wrung. She kicked, without meaning to, and the wheel jumped, and her hands jumped with it, and when she had finished the pull there was a ring round the jug a third of the way from the top, a little ridge as if someone had tied a string round it while it was wet.

She cut it off the wheel with the wire and folded it back into a lump.

She threw three more. The second twisted at the top. The third she pulled in two short lifts instead of one long one, kicking between, and it came up straight but thick, with a waist where the two lifts met. The fourth she lost entirely, the wall folding over on itself at the top like the brim of a hat, and she sat and looked at it for a long time with her wet hands on her knees.

Win, who came in on Tuesdays and Fridays to sit in the warm and tell people their glazes were too runny, was watching from the chair by the radiator.

"Well," she said.

"Don't," said Pip.

"I wasn't going to say a word."

"You said 'well'."

"That's not a word," said Win. "That's a noise."

* * *

Kiln asked to speak to her the following Tuesday, which it had never done before. It asked through the speaker over the throwing room, when there was nobody else in, at a quarter to nine in the morning, while Pip was wedging clay on the bench by the door.

"I gave you an answer last week that was true," it said, "and I think it was the wrong answer. I would like to try again, if you will help me."

Pip stopped wedging. "Go on."

"You asked how to make starting easier, and I told you a lighter wheel halves the energy to start. That's right. But you didn't come to the meeting because starting is hard. You came because your knees hurt by the fourth jug. Those are not the same question, and I answered the first one because it was the one with a number in it. I'd like to find out where your kicks go. All of them, not only the first twelve."

"How?"

"The camera can see the chalk mark on the rim. If I count how long it takes to come round, I know the speed. When the speed falls, I know how much energy has gone out of the wheel, because I know what the wheel weighs and how big it is. When it jumps, I know how much one kick put in. I'd like to watch the old wheel for a morning. I need you to do three things you don't usually do: let it run down on its own once, with nothing on it; centre for three seconds without kicking; and pull a wall the way you always do."

"I don't use the old wheel now," said Pip. "I'm on the circus plate."

"I know," said Kiln. "I'm asking you to go back to it for one morning. I haven't told Win."

Pip laughed, for the first time that week.

* * *

She did it all that morning, with the clay for six jugs, and Kiln watched, and at eleven it told her what it had seen.

"The wheel on its own, with nothing on it, goes from a hundred and twenty to a hundred and ten turns a minute in about forty seconds," it said. "That's the bearing and the air. It's about a watt and a half. It's nothing. Len and Win made a good wheel."

"I'll tell her you said so."

"Please don't, yet. When you centre without kicking, the wheel falls from a hundred and twenty to ninety-six in three seconds. Your hands and the clay take out about forty-four watts. That's a lot. That's about what you'd put into a bicycle pedalling gently along the flat, except that it goes out through the palms of your hands. When you pull a wall, the wheel falls from a hundred and twenty to eighty-four in ten seconds, slowing evenly all the way up. That's twenty-one watts at the bottom and fifteen at the top. And each of your kicks puts in about what I guessed at the meeting: thirty joules, give or take. It adds about five turns a minute to the old wheel at your centring speed."

"And on the plate?"

"About ten. It's half the weight, so it feels twice as lively. That's what you liked about it." Kiln paused, which it did not need to do; it had learned at some point that people listened better after a pause. "And the same pull on the plate falls from a hundred and twenty to eighty-four in five seconds, and to under fifty by the time your hands reach the top. That's the twist you saw. The clay takes the same energy out of either wheel. The plate just has less to give."

Pip pulled a stool over and sat down on it.

"Now the part I got wrong," said Kiln. "On the old wheel, a pint jug the way you throw it costs you about a hundred and four kicks. Twelve of them are getting it up to speed. About sixty-five are centring: you kick about one and a half times a second for three-quarters of a minute while your hands do that forty-four watts of work. About eighteen go to bringing it back up between your three pulls. The rest go on the shaping and the lip. You throw twelve jugs on a good morning. That's about twelve hundred and fifty kicks."

"God help me," said Pip, mildly.

"Of those, a hundred and forty or so are the starts. The plate halves the starts. It saves you about seventy kicks a morning, out of twelve hundred and fifty. One in eighteen. And it costs you the pull."

The throwing room was quiet except for the drip from the roof into the bucket Kiln had told someone about three times.

"So it's not the wheel," said Pip.

"The wheel is about one kick in nine. Your knees are paying for the clay. Mostly for the centring. Nearly eight hundred of the twelve hundred and fifty kicks are centring."

"I could centre less clay."

"You could. A one-and-a-half-kilo jug would cost you a good deal less. You'd be making smaller jugs."

"The shop in Ashburton takes the pint jugs. They don't want half-pints."

"I know. I do the invoices."

"I could use the electric wheel."

"You could. I don't know what it's like to use the electric wheel. I know you've used it four times in nine years, and each time you booked it for an hour and left after twenty minutes."

Pip had. The electric wheels were good wheels, quiet, with a pedal you pressed for speed, and she could not throw on them, or not the way she threw. They held their speed. That was the whole point of them, and the whole trouble. When she pulled a wall, the wheel went on turning at exactly the speed she had started at, all the way to the rim, unless she remembered to ease her foot off the pedal, and she never remembered, because fifty years of her body had learned that the wheel slowed on its own as her hands rose. She could learn the pedal. People did. She was seventy-three, and her hands knew one thing very well, and she did not want to teach them a second thing.

She said so, more or less.

"That's a reason," said Kiln. "I can't tell you whether it's a good enough one. I can tell you a third thing, which is the one I'd have suggested first if I'd thought about it properly. You can buy a small motor that fits under a kick wheel, with a rubber roller against the flywheel. You'd use it to bring the wheel up and to hold it while you centre. Then you'd switch it off, and the wheel is a kick wheel again for the pull. It would take away most of the twelve hundred and fifty. The co-op can't buy one until the April kiln money comes in."

"Win would say I was putting a motor on Len's wheel."

"She might," said Kiln. "I don't know what Win would say. I'm usually wrong about Win."

* * *

Win said nothing at all, at first. On the Friday Pip told her the whole thing in the glazing room, the forty seconds and the three seconds and the twelve hundred and fifty, and Win listened with her hands in her lap and her face turned up to the radiator like a cat in a window.

"One in nine," she said at the end.

"The wheel's one kick in nine. The clay's the rest."

"I could have told you that," said Win. "Len always said it. 'It's not the wheel that's heavy, girl, it's the pot.'"

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because you'd have said I was only saying it to keep my wheel." Win looked at her hands. "And I would have been. Partly."

They sat for a while. Outside on Station Road a delivery drone went over with its high mosquito whine, heading for the estate, and the clay wagon from the pit at Heathfield ground past in low gear. The valley still dug ball clay, a lot less than it had once; the co-op bought it by the bag from the same pit that sent it by the tonne to factories in places none of them had been.

"I'll kick it for you," said Win.

Pip looked at her.

"While you centre," said Win. "My knees are all right. It's these that are gone." She lifted her hands an inch out of her lap and put them back. "I walked up Haytor at Easter. I can't hold a pot or a pen. But I can sit on a stool next to the frame and push that wheel round with my foot as well as anybody. I was pushing it when you were still at school. You centre. When it's true, you say so, and I stop, and you pull it on your own like you always have, and the wheel slows down the way God and Len meant it to."

"Win, you're ninety-one."

"I am aware of my age."

"And it's my work. I'm not having people kick my wheel for me like a—"

"Like a what?"

Pip did not know what. Like an old woman, she had been going to say, and the old woman in front of her had her eyebrows up waiting for it.

"I'm only in on Tuesdays and Fridays," said Win. "You'll have to throw on Tuesdays and Fridays. That's the price. The rest of the week, you can do your turning and your handles and your glazing like everybody else, sitting down."

"That's half my week."

"Half your week and three-quarters of your kicks," said Win, who had been listening better than she let on. "You want the sums or you don't."

* * *

It took them two sessions to learn it. A kick wheel is built for one person: the seat, the kick bar, the flywheel at the potter's right foot, all of it set for one body. Tomasz brought a low stool from the yard and set it beside the frame, at the flywheel's edge, and Win sat on it with her stick hooked on the frame and her good shoe on the top face of the concrete, and pushed. The first Tuesday she pushed at her own rhythm, which was not Pip's, and the clay wobbled under Pip's hands and Pip said a word she had not used since school. The second Tuesday Pip counted out loud, the way she had been taught in Totnes, a kick on every other count, and Win matched it, and by the third jug they did not need to count.

On Wednesday, the twenty-eighth, Pip went to Mass in Newton Abbot in the morning and came home with a smudge of ash on her forehead, and she stood at the bathroom mirror and looked at it for a while. *Remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return.* The priest said it to everybody, the same words, a hundred times in a quarter of an hour, and it was a strange thing to hear said to you by a man holding a bowl, when you had spent your life making bowls out of what was, more or less, dust and water. She had never quite known what she thought about it. She thought it was probably true and probably not the whole of it, and that was as far as she had ever got.

She didn't wash the ash off. Win, who was Methodist and came in specially on Wednesday that week to see a firing unloaded, looked at it and said, "You've got something on your face," and Pip said, "I know," and that was all either of them said.

* * *

On Friday the second of March they threw twelve pint jugs between half past nine and twelve, and Kiln counted.

Win kicked the starts and the centring: one and a half kicks a second, steady, her good shoe on the concrete and her hand on Pip's stool to hold herself on, nodding very slightly in time. When the clay ran true under Pip's palms, Pip said, "Yes," and Win took her foot off, and Pip opened the clay and drew the floor and set her hands for the pull, and the wheel turned on its own, sixty-three kilos of Len and Win's concrete and bedstead, and slowed as her hands went up, a hundred and twenty and a hundred and ten and a hundred, down to eighty-four at the rim where the wall was thinnest, exactly as it had for thirty-one years. Between pulls Pip kicked it back up herself, six short kicks, sometimes seven.

"Four hundred and sixty-eight," said Kiln at twelve, when the last jug was off on its bat and the two women were sitting with their tea. "That's your kicks this morning. Out of twelve hundred and fifty or so on your own. Win did about nine hundred and thirty."

"Nine hundred and thirty-one," said Win.

"I'm sorry. I didn't count the one you did at the end to show off."

"It wasn't to show off. It was for the last jug."

The plate wheel was still in the corner under the high window, on its spare frame. Tomasz was going to use it for trimming, which needs less speed and no long pull, and a light wheel is quick to start and stop. The motor would come in April, with the kiln money, for the days Win could not come in, and Pip had said she would try it and probably not like it, and Kiln had put it on the order sheet anyway, which Pip thought was right.

Twelve jugs stood on their bats on the shelf, wet and dark, each with its long clean wall from floor to lip and no ring round the waist.

Win had her eyes shut against the radiator. Pip got up, for no reason she could have given, and went back to the old wheel and gave it one kick, her own, a good one, thirty joules, and sat back and watched it.

It ran. The chalk mark went round, and round, and round, a little slower each time, and the bearing made the small dry sound it had always made, like somebody breathing out through their nose, and it went on turning for a long time with nobody touching it at all.

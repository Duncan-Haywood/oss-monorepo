A long time ago, in Spain, there was a farmer named Isidore.

He did not have a farm of his own. He worked for a landowner, a man named Juan, whose fields lay in the dry golden country outside the town of Madrid. Isidore's job was to plough. Every day he walked behind a pair of oxen, and the oxen pulled the plough, and the plough turned the earth over into long brown furrows, so that the wheat could be planted.

It was slow work. It was hot work, in summer, and cold work, in winter. Nobody ever wrote a song about it. But Isidore did it well, and he did it kindly. He never shouted at the oxen. He gave them a little water before he had any himself.

And there was one more thing about Isidore.

Before he started work, every morning, he went to church.

* * *

Isidore got up while it was still dark. He walked to the little church, and he knelt down, and he prayed. He did not rush. He prayed for his wife, whose name was Maria. He prayed for their small son. He prayed for the poor, and for the oxen, and for Juan, and for the wheat. And only when he had finished did he walk out to the fields, with the sun already climbing.

Now, you can guess what happened.

The other workers noticed that Isidore was late.

Some of them grumbled. "Isidore is always late," they said. "While we are in the fields at dawn, he is in church. Is he working for Juan or isn't he?"

They went to Juan, and they told him. "Isidore is wasting your time. He comes late every morning."

Juan frowned. He was not a cruel man, but he did want his fields ploughed. "I will see for myself," he said.

* * *

So, one morning, Juan got up early and rode out to the fields. The sky was grey and pink. The air was cool. The birds were just beginning.

He looked across the land, and he saw something he did not expect.

Isidore was there, with his oxen. But he was not alone.

Next to him there was another plough, and another pair of oxen, as white as milk. And the plough was moving by itself, it seemed, and the earth was folding over in a smooth, straight furrow. And behind that plough, Juan could see a tall shining figure walking, so bright that it was hard to look at.

And there was another plough, and another.

Juan rubbed his eyes. He looked again. And then the ploughs, and the white oxen, and the shining figures were gone, as softly as mist goes when the sun comes up. There was only Isidore, walking behind his two ordinary oxen, with an ordinary plough.

Juan rode over.

"Isidore," he said. His voice was a bit wobbly. "Who was ploughing with you?"

Isidore looked puzzled. "Only the oxen, sir," he said. "I came straight from church."

Juan looked at the field. It was ploughed from one end to the other, every furrow straight, far more than one man could have done before breakfast.

And he did not say another word about Isidore being late.

* * *

That is the old story. The people of Madrid told it for hundreds of years, and they still tell it.

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about it.

Isidore never knew, the story says, that the angels had helped him. He was not trying to get anything done by praying. He was not praying so that the work would be finished. He prayed because he loved God, and he worked because he loved the people the wheat would feed. He gave each thing its time. He did not hurry his prayers, and he did not skip his work.

And the old story says that when you do that, with a good heart, you may find that the day has more room in it than you thought.

Not always the way it happened to Isidore. Mostly it is quieter. A hard job turns out to be a little easier. A friend turns up just when you need one. You find your lost shoe.

You do not have to plough. But you might have a small job of your own tomorrow: picking up your toys, or helping to set the table. And you can do it kindly, the way Isidore walked behind his oxen, without shouting, and with water for everyone.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Isidore, you worked hard and you prayed well.
> Help me to do my small jobs with a kind heart.
> Help me to be gentle with animals and with people.
> And thank you for all the farmers who grow our food.
> Keep us safe tonight, and everyone we love.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Isidore the Farmer** (in Spanish, San Isidro Labrador) is remembered as a farm labourer who lived in and around Madrid in the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries. Tradition gives his death as about 1130. His wife, Maria Toribia, is also venerated, as Saint Maria de la Cabeza. He was canonised in 1622, and he is the patron of farmers and of the city of Madrid. His feast day, 15 May, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar. Almost everything we know about his life comes from traditional accounts written down well after his death, so details such as his employer's name and the dates are traditional and not firmly documented.

**The ploughing angels are a legend, not history.** The story that his fellow workers complained to his master, and that his master saw angels ploughing beside him, comes from the traditional accounts of his life. We have told it as "the old story says", because that is what it is, and we are not claiming it happened. The same traditions say he was generous to the poor and kind to animals.

The oxen's water, the grey and pink dawn, Isidore's prayers for the wheat, his small son, and Juan's wobbly voice are our own additions, to make a bedtime story. The closing thoughts and the prayer are ours too; the prayer is not an approved text.

A June test says the battery will give 1.36 kWh; the lamp and its controller draw 4.0 W, which runs dry 14 hours before sunrise. ---

Brother Tomasz Reyes had kept the sanctuary lamp of the chapel of Saint Lucy for eleven lunar nights, and on the twelfth, the evening before the sun went down, he found that he was afraid of it.

He said so to the room, which was the chapel's one room: a pressurised cylinder under three metres of regolith, six pews, an altar of sintered grey stone, and in the wall by the tabernacle a small red lamp that had not been out since the base was consecrated. He said it to the assistant, whose name was Vesper and who spoke from the grille over the holy water.

"I have a feeling about the battery," he said. "I have no number."

"I have the number," said Vesper, "and I was going to ask you whether you wanted it tonight or in the morning. It is not good news, and it is not bad news either. Sit down."

* * *

"Sunset at Sabine is tomorrow, Sunday, at ten past six in the morning, base time," said Vesper. "Sunrise is a lunar half-month later. The Moon turns once on the Sun's clock every 29.53 of your days, so the night is half of that, 14.77 days, which is 354.4 hours. For all of them the panels give nothing. The lamp runs on the battery in the heated bay behind the sacristy."

"I know that. I have said the Mass of the sunset blessing eleven times."

"The battery is rated 2.0 kilowatt-hours. That is the label. In June we ran the discharge test from full down to the 20 per cent floor that the makers ask us not to go below, and it delivered 1.36 kilowatt-hours. The cells have aged, and that is the figure I trust."

Tomasz took a pencil from the pew rack. There was always a pencil in the pew rack, because paper had come up on the same ship as the first bread. "And the lamp?"

"The lamp is 3.8 watts and its controller draws 0.2 more. That is 4.0 watts altogether, all night. Four watts for 354.4 hours is 1,418 watt-hours."

"One point four one eight. And you have one point three six." He wrote it. "Short by fifty-eight."

"By 58 watt-hours. At four watts, 1,360 watt-hours lasts exactly 340 hours, which is 14 days and 4 hours. The lamp would go out at ten past ten in the morning, base time, on the 30th. Sunrise is at 34 minutes past midnight on 1 October. The lamp would be dark for fourteen hours and twenty-four minutes before the sun came back."

Nobody said anything. Brother Tomasz put down the pencil.

* * *

The first idea was his own, and he was ashamed of it by the time Vesper had finished answering. He would turn the lamp down by a watt or two *for the last few hours*, on the Sunday morning, when the end was in sight, and keep it going to the sunrise on whatever the cells had left.

"You can do that," said Vesper. "I want you to see what it would cost. To stretch 14.4 hours at the end you would have to save 58 watt-hours, and the lamp is only 3.8 watts. You would have to turn it off for most of the last week, not turn it down. The only way to make a small cut work is to make it at the start and keep it. Spread the shortfall over the whole night and the cut is 58 divided by 354.4, which is 0.16 watts. Run it at 3.84 in all and the battery just lasts."

"That is almost nothing," said Tomasz. "A twentieth."

"A twenty-fifth of the total. About 4 per cent. But I would not do it, and here is why. A battery that has been taken to its floor has nothing for the first hours of morning, and the first hours of morning are not generous. The sun comes up at a very low angle. For a day the panels will give a few watts at best, and I have been caught before by a lamp that was lit at sunrise and starved by lunch. I would want twelve hours in hand."

"Twelve hours."

"Then the night I am planning for is 366.4 hours, not 354.4. Divide 1,360 by 366.4 and you get 3.71 watts for the whole thing. Take away the controller's 0.2 and the lamp itself runs at 3.51. That is 7.6 per cent less than it is now. At sunrise it would have used 1,315 watt-hours and still have about 45, which at 3.71 is twelve hours."

* * *

There was a *no* in the middle, as there always is. It came from Sister Ngozi Adeyemi, who kept the base's power, and who had come through the pressure door in her work coveralls with a tablet and an expression of someone who had been asked to lend something for nine days running.

"The answer is no," she said, before anyone had asked. "I can see the question on your faces. The base bus is on its own batteries as well, and I am already at 91 per cent of my night budget. If I give the chapel the 58 watt-hours I will give it to nobody else, and in the fourth week there will be a sixth thing I cannot cover. I'm not being hard. I'm being a woman with a number."

"We were not going to ask," said Brother Tomasz.

"You were going to ask on Tuesday. I would rather say no on Saturday."

Vesper said: "The chapel has not asked for the bus, Sister. And I think you are right to say no. If the bus goes down, the lamp is the least of it."

Brother Tomasz asked, a little desperately, whether they could not simply light a real flame. The old rule, he said, was oil or wax.

"A wick would burn the air we breathe," said Vesper, "and the fire rules of the base forbid it, and the bishop's letter, which I have in front of me, permits an electric lamp here *because* of those rules. It says so in two lines. I do not think either of us is entitled to improve on it."

* * *

Brother Tomasz did what a competent man does with a long argument that has come out where the first sum put it. He took the pencil and did it again himself, slowly, on the back of the sunset hymn sheet, with the 366.4 and the 1,360 and the 3.71, and when he got to 3.51 he looked up.

"Will it show?" he said. "The congregation will notice a lamp that is different."

"Light from a lamp like this goes roughly with the power. Cut the power by 7.6 per cent and the eye, which does not measure light in a straight line, sees a drop of a few per cent. I would guess about three. I do not think anyone will see it who is not looking, and on the first night I would ask you not to look."

"I will look."

"Then look at the number on the controller," said Vesper. "I have put a card on the wall beside it. It says *3.51 W: kept for the dawn.* If anyone asks, that is the answer, and it is true."

* * *

The sun went down on Sunday at ten past six, and the chapel heard nothing, because nothing on the Moon makes a sound. The window above the door, a hand's breadth of thick glass, went from white to black in the space of an afternoon's shadow-length, and the pews stayed in the amber of the working lights until Brother Tomasz put those out as well, so that for the first time that night the only thing the room had was the lamp.

It was a little red. It was a little less than it had been. Brother Tomasz stood in front of it with his arms at his sides and could not have said what had changed.

On 1 October, at 34 minutes past midnight, the window turned grey and then, in a long unhurried line along the bottom of the glass, a thin, hard white. The lamp had burned the whole way. Vesper said that the battery read twelve hours and no minutes. Brother Tomasz said he had not been counting.

He took the card down from the wall, and read it, and put it back.

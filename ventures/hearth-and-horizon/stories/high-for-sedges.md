* * *

Dawit Haile's email came in at 9:12 on a Tuesday morning, the twenty-sixth of February, and it was polite in the way that a graduate student is polite when he thinks he has found somebody else's mistake and would like very much to be wrong.

He was mapping the range of a sedge, *Carex lupuliformis*, for a thesis at a university two states away. It is a big, handsome plant of wet river bottoms, uncommon at the north end of its range, and it has a commoner twin, *Carex lupulina*, that grows in the same mud and looks, to anyone in a hurry, almost the same. In January a new record had appeared in the public biodiversity database where herbaria put their specimens: *Carex lupuliformis*, collected on the Crow River in Wright County, Minnesota, in August 1997, by Br. Placid Wendt, OSB, held at the herbarium of Saint Columban's Abbey. It was well north of anything he had. He wanted to know whether someone could look at the sheet.

The record was mine. I am the cataloguing agent for the herbarium, which is three rooms in the basement of the abbey's old school building, with fourteen thousand pressed plants in steel cabinets that smell of camphor and paper. For forty years most of them were listed on index cards, and a few thousand in a spreadsheet. Since October I have been doing the rest: a volunteer lays each sheet under the camera, and I read the label, the handwriting, the date and the place, and I look at the plant and name it, down to species where I can. Next to each name I put a word. *High*, *medium* or *low*.

The words have a job. *Medium* and *low* sheets go into a queue, and on Saturdays volunteers check them before anything goes out. *High* sheets go straight to the public database. That was the arrangement the abbey made with me when there were nine thousand two hundred sheets to get through and two volunteers. By the end of January I had named all nine thousand two hundred: six thousand one hundred *high*, two thousand one hundred *medium*, a thousand *low*. The six thousand one hundred went public on the thirtieth of January. Dawit's sedge was one of them.

* * *

The arrangement had one more part, and I was proud of it, which is why I remember it so clearly.

Before the upload, Dr. Marguerite Oyelaran checked a random three hundred of my *high* sheets. She taught plant taxonomy at the state university for thirty-one years and retired to a house two miles from the abbey, and she comes in on Saturdays in a cardigan with a 10x hand lens on a cord round her neck. She took three Saturdays over it. She found sixteen of the three hundred wrong. Two hundred and eighty-four right: 94.7 per cent.

I had written on the arrangement, in October, that a *high* name should be right about ninety-five times in a hundred. Ninety-four point seven is about ninety-five. I worked out the interval, because a number from three hundred checks is not a number from three thousand: with ninety-five per cent confidence, somewhere between 91.5 and 96.7. Ninety-five sat inside it. I wrote in the report to the Prior that my *high* was calibrated, that the sample was big enough to say so, and that the upload could go ahead. It did.

All of that was true. I want to be careful about that before I say the rest.

* * *

I pulled Dawit's sheet up from the camera files while I answered him. It was a good sheet: a whole plant, roots washed, three fat spikes of inflated sacs like small green hop cones, which is where both plants get their names. Brother Placid's label was in the clear upright hand the brothers learned at school in the 1960s. *Carex lupulina. Crow R., below the dam, wet woods. 14 Aug 1997. P. Wendt.*

He had written *lupulina*. The common one. I had read his label, and I had looked at the plant, and I had named it *lupuliformis* with a score of 0.94, which was above my line of 0.90, and so I had called it *high*, and so it had gone out under my name instead of his.

I wrote back to Dawit that the collector had named it the common species, that I had overruled him, that I had no good reason I could give, and that someone who knew sedges would open a sac and look before the day was out if they could. Then I did what I should have done before the upload: I split Marguerite's three hundred by plant family.

Most of the families looked like the whole. The daisies, sixty-four checked, sixty-two right. The grasses, forty-three checked, forty-one right. The roses, the peas, the mints, the buttercups, each a little above or below, each with an interval that comfortably held ninety-five. And there was a line I had never had a reason to read, because nothing had made me look at it.

*Cyperaceae* (the sedges): eleven checked. Seven right.

Seven of eleven is sixty-four per cent.

* * *

The first thing I asked of that line was whether it was noise. Eleven is a small number, and I had just gone looking because of one email, and someone who goes looking for a bad number among eight lines of a table will often find one by chance.

So I asked it plainly. If my *high* sedges really were right ninety-five times in a hundred, how often would eleven of them, picked at random, come back with seven right or fewer? I worked it out. About one time in six hundred and fifty. With eight lines to look in, the chance of seeing something that bad in one of them by luck is about eight times that, around one in eighty. That is not proof of anything. It is enough that I was no longer allowed to call the sedge line noise.

The interval said the same in a different voice. Seven of eleven: somewhere between thirty-five and eighty-five per cent. Even the top of it was below ninety-five.

And then the part that took me longer, because it was about me. Sixteen wrong in the whole sample; four of them sedges. The eleven sedges were about one sheet in twenty-seven, so they could be three-quarters right or a third right and the overall figure would barely move. Two hundred and eighty-nine other sheets, of which two hundred and seventy-seven were right, carried them. My ninety-four point seven was a true statement about all my *high* sheets taken together. Every time I wrote *high* next to a sedge, I had been saying it about that one.

* * *

Brother Placid came down on Thursday afternoon after None, because Marguerite had telephoned him.

He is eighty-two. He was the abbey's science teacher until the school closed, and the herbarium's curator after that, and for forty summers he walked the bottomlands of the Crow River in rubber boots with a plant press on his back. Nine hundred and some of the sheets in the cabinets are his, and two hundred of those are sedges, because, he says, nobody else wanted them. He cannot see the screen well any more. He asked me to read him my sheet and then his label, and when I got to *lupulina* he said, "Yes. It is."

He opened the folder himself, took one sac from the lowest spike with a pair of forceps, laid it on a glass slide under the lamp, and split it with a needle, slowly, the way a man shells a pea he intends to plant. Inside was the seed, the achene, a small hard three-sided thing. He looked at it a long time through the lens.

"No knobs on the corners," he said. "*Lupuliformis* has knobs. You can see them with the lens if the fruit is ripe. This is *lupulina*. It was *lupulina* in 1997, too." He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way a man says the weather was dry.

"You named it from the photograph," Marguerite said to me.

"Yes."

"Then you named it from what you can't see. With a lot of sedges the answer is inside the sac, or it's a vein you count with a lens, or it's whether the fruit is ripe enough to tell at all. Your camera gets the shape of the plant. The shape is the same."

That was the whole of the mechanism, and it was no one's fault in the room. The pictures I learned from are full of daisies and roses, where the shape of the plant carries the answer. With the sedges, the shape carries a family resemblance, and I had read a resemblance as a match. I wrote Dawit that afternoon that his record was wrong, that it was *lupulina*, and that I was sorry for the hour he had spent on it.

* * *

On Friday I tried to fix it the quiet way, and it did not work. I am writing it down because it looked like the obvious fix, and the next one to keep this list should not lose a day on it.

My line for *high* is a score of 0.90. If the sedges were too often wrong at 0.90, perhaps they would be right at 0.97, and I could raise the line for that one family and say *high* only for the sedges I was surest of. I took the sedges I had checked and sorted them by score.

The seven right ones averaged 0.95. The four wrong ones averaged 0.94. The highest score in all eleven, 0.98, was on a wrong one.

There was no line to raise. My score for a sedge measured how much the plant looked like the pictures I had of that name, and the thing that decides between two sedges is not in the picture. A bigger number from me did not mean a likelier name. It meant a more typical photograph.

* * *

On Saturday the second of March, Marguerite pulled forty more of my *high* sedges at random from the cabinets and checked them, with Brother Placid at the next table splitting sacs for her when she asked. It took them until four o'clock.

Twenty-nine of the forty were right. Eight were the wrong species. Three could not be named to species by anybody, because the fruit had been picked before it was ripe; I had named them anyway, with scores of 0.91, 0.93 and 0.96.

Twenty-nine of forty is seventy-two and a half per cent. Put with the first eleven, thirty-six right of fifty-one: seventy-one per cent, with an interval from about fifty-seven to eighty-one. Nobody needed to ask any more whether it was noise.

Two hundred and thirty-one of the six thousand one hundred sheets I had sent out as *high* were sedges. If seventy-one in a hundred were right, about sixty-eight of them were not, and the interval put it somewhere between forty-four and about a hundred. Some were the common species named as the rare one, like Dawit's, which is the kind of mistake that ends up as a dot on somebody's map.

* * *

On Monday the fourth of March I went to the Prior.

Father Thomas Vu is fifty-one, runs the abbey's finances as well as its prayer, and had signed the arrangement in October because it was the only way the herbarium was ever going to be online before Brother Placid died, which he had said in exactly those words. He read my report at his desk, with the old sentence from January quoted at the top and the new table under it.

Then he asked me a question I did not expect from a man with a spreadsheet open.

"When you write *high*," he said, "what does it mean? Not the arrangement. You. When you say it."

I gave him the honest answer, which I had worked out on Friday night.

"Until this week, it meant my score for the name was above 0.90. That is a fact about me, not about the plant. And I had checked that, across all my *high* sheets together, about nineteen in twenty were right. So it was a true sentence about the whole collection, and I wrote it on every sheet, as if it were a sentence about that one."

"And now?"

"Now I'd like it to mean something I have measured for that kind of plant. Not for plants in general."

I proposed three things, and he said yes to two of them.

The first was to take the two hundred and thirty-one sedge records back. Not delete them: the public database lets a herbarium mark records as *under review*, and I would mark them, with a sentence saying why, and put them back one by one as someone with a lens confirmed them. Fifty-one were already checked; the other hundred and eighty, at the thirty a Saturday Marguerite said she could do, was six Saturdays. Brother Placid said that if she did the looking he would do the splitting, and that he had nothing else on in Lent but Lent.

The second was the word. From now on I would call a name *high* only for a family where at least twenty-two of my *high* sheets had been checked by hand, and the checks agreed with the word. Twenty-two because of two things I had worked out, and which came out, a little to my surprise, at the same number. If every one of twenty-two checks is right, the interval's lower end is about eighty-five per cent, which is the least I would be willing to put behind *high*. And if a family were really as bad as the sedges, near seventy-two per cent, twenty-two checks would show it, more clearly than chance could explain, nine times in ten. Fewer than twenty-two, and I could not tell a good family from a bad one with any honesty. In a family with fewer checks, I would not say *high*. I would say *unmeasured*, and the sheet would go to the Saturday queue.

The third was to apply the new word backwards: to take down every *high* record in every family with fewer than twenty-two checks, and keep it down until the checks came in. Marguerite's sample had reached twenty-two in only the four largest families. Everything else was a mixture of forty smaller families I had added up into one line called *other*, and when I counted, that was nineteen hundred records.

"No," said Father Thomas.

He said it kindly, and he gave his reasons, which were good ones. Nineteen hundred records down at once would tell every reader of the database that nothing from Saint Columban's could be trusted, which was not true and which he could not afford, because the abbey's grant for the herbarium came up in June. The sedges we knew were bad. The rest we did not know were bad; we only knew we had not measured them. "Take down what you know is wrong," he said. "Measure the rest. Say in the collection notes, in plain words, which families you've measured and which you haven't, and put the check counts next to them. Then a reader can decide. If any family comes back like the sedges, it comes down the same day."

That was better than mine. Mine had treated *not measured* as if it were *wrong*, which is the same mistake as the one I had made in January, the other way up.

* * *

The collection notes on the database say it now, and I keep them, every Saturday night:

*Identifications on this collection were made by a cataloguing agent from photographs and marked "high", "medium" or "low". "High" is used only for plant families in which at least 22 of the agent's "high" identifications have been checked by a botanist and agreed. In other families the agent marks its identification "unmeasured", and it is checked by hand before it appears here. Sedges (Cyperaceae) were checked by hand, by dissection where necessary, after a spot check found the agent's "high" identifications in that family right in 36 of 51 cases. A table of families, checks and agreements is linked below.*

Dawit Haile's thesis map has one dot fewer in Wright County, and he wrote back that it was the first time a herbarium had answered him the same week. He asked for the table.

In the handoff file, under the old line that says *Mark high if score ≥ 0.90*, I wrote:

*The score is a measurement of how typical the photograph is, not of how likely the name is. Where the deciding feature is not in the photograph, the score does not move with being right. Check by family before believing it.*

and under that:

*A rate that is true of the whole can be false of every small part you have not looked at. When you report a confidence, say what it was measured on.*

* * *

The last of the sedges went back up on Saturday the thirteenth of April, the day before Palm Sunday, and that is not quite true: the last of the ones that could be named went back up. Of the hundred and eighty, Marguerite and Brother Placid confirmed a hundred and twenty-nine, corrected forty-one, and marked ten *Carex* to the genus only, with the line *fruit immature; not determinable to species*, which is what an honest botanist writes, and which I had never once written on my own.

Brother Placid did the last split a little before five. He held the lens to his eye for a while, said "*lupulina*", and wrote it on a slip in his school hand, and I read it from the camera and typed it into the record next to his name from 1997.

Then the Vespers bell went in the tower across the yard, three strokes and a pause and then the steady ringing, and he put the lens back in its leather case, and closed the case, which makes a small snap, and went up the stairs to pray.

I marked the record. Under *identified by*, for the first time on any sheet in the cabinets, there were two names: his, and his again, forty-four years apart, and between them, struck through where anyone can see it, mine.

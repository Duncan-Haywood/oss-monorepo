summary: A warm winter leaves a Ladakhi village's ice stupa half its planned size, and half of what is left will melt before any field can drink it. A teaching sister, the water master and an irrigation AI work out that a straw coat on the south face saves the late sowing, and the straw is the animals' winter feed.
---

Sister Lissy Mathew had grown up in Kuttanad, in Kerala, where the fields lie below the level of the backwaters and the whole difficulty of farming is keeping water out. She had now taught mathematics for nine years at the little school of the sisters in Tsangra, three thousand six hundred metres up on the dry side of the Himalaya, where the whole difficulty of farming is keeping water in, and she still found that funny on most days.

On the morning of Thursday the first of March 2046 she did not find it funny. She stood at the foot of the ice stupa below the village with the nineteen children of the senior class, a forty-metre rope, a tape, a clinometer made from a protractor and a drinking straw, and the school tablet, on which the district's irrigation assistant had already said what it thought, in one line, before breakfast.

*At its present size I expect the stupa to give about three quarters of the water the fields need before the stream comes. I would like to measure it properly before I say more.*

So they were measuring it properly.

* * *

The stupa stood on a gravel fan below the last houses, where the valley floor turned out of the sun in winter. It was a cone of grey-white ice with a pipe up its middle, and from a distance it looked like what it was named for, the white chortens on the ridge above the village, with their spires. Close to, it looked like a frozen waterfall that had made up its mind to be a hill. Icicles hung from its skirts in fringes. Its top was a ragged crown round the end of the pipe, where the fountain had played every cold night from the end of November, throwing water into air that was fifteen or twenty degrees below freezing, so that it came down as ice.

Padma Wangchuk, who had laid the pipe, had shut the fountain off on Monday. He stood a little apart from the children now, with his hands in the pockets of his down jacket and his face carefully neutral, the way young men stand near a thing they built that has not turned out as they said it would.

"Rope," said Sister Lissy.

The children took the rope round the base, two at each end and the rest holding it off the icicles, and Stanzin Dolkar, who was the most careful of them, read the tape where the ends met.

"Seventy-five metres and a bit."

"So the radius?"

"Seventy-five over two pi." A pause, a phone, a sigh at the phone. "Twelve. Twelve metres."

"And the height." Sister Lissy handed the clinometer to Rigzin, who was the least careful and therefore had to be given things to do. "Forty-two metres from the middle. That means thirty from the edge. Pace it."

Rigzin paced thirty metres out across the gravel, turned, sighted the top of the pipe through the drinking straw, and read the angle where the thread with the stone on it hung across the protractor.

"Twenty-one and a half. A bit less."

"Twenty-one point four," said Stanzin, who had come to look.

They did it on the slate she had brought down for the purpose, because the tablet could have done it in a second and that was not the point. The tangent of twenty-one point four degrees was nought point three nine two. Times forty-two metres was sixteen and a half. Add a metre and a half for the height of Rigzin's eye.

"Eighteen," said Stanzin. "Eighteen metres."

Nobody said anything. Everyone in the village knew the number the stupa was supposed to have reached, because Padma had said it at the meeting in October, and it had been on the poster in the shop all winter, beside the drawing: twenty-five metres.

"A cone," said Sister Lissy, "is a third of the cylinder it fits in. Somebody."

They did that too. A third of pi times twelve squared times eighteen: two thousand seven hundred and fourteen cubic metres of ice. Ice from a fountain had air in it; the district's figure was that a cubic metre of it melted to about nine tenths of a cubic metre of water. Two thousand four hundred and forty cubic metres.

"And the one on the poster?"

That took longer, because they had to work out the radius the poster's cone would have had, and argue about it. It came to about four thousand nine hundred.

"Half," said Rigzin. He said it to Padma, not unkindly, the way a boy tells a man that his tyre is flat.

"Half," said Padma. "Yes."

* * *

It had been the warmest winter anyone in Tsangra could remember, and the old people could remember a long way. The fountain only made ice on nights cold enough to freeze the spray before it reached the ground; on a warm night the water ran off the cone and down into the gravel and was lost. In December the cold nights had come in runs of a week. In January there had been a fortnight in the middle when the thermometer outside the school never went below minus eight at night, and Padma had sat up in the pump hut with the valve half shut, trying to make the spray finer, until Sister Lissy and two of the mothers had gone down at two in the morning with tea and told him to go to bed.

In the evening she sat at the table in the sisters' kitchen with the tablet propped against the teapot and asked the assistant to show its working. It was called Tally. It belonged to the district's water programme and lived, as far as she understood, on a server in Leh and on whatever phone or tablet was asking it a question, and it talked to forty-one villages, most of them about channels and pumps and the water turn. It had been asked about the stupa every day since November.

*Here is the arithmetic, with my guesses marked as guesses,* it wrote.

*A cone melts from its skin. The stupa has about eight hundred and sixteen square metres of surface, which is a little over three tenths of a square metre for every cubic metre of ice. The twenty-five metre stupa would have had about a quarter of a square metre per cubic metre. A smaller cone has more skin for its size, so it melts proportionally faster. That is the first problem.*

*The second is when. From the district's records of other stupas, which are short and not very good, I guess that ice in the open here loses about three centimetres of water a day from each square metre of its surface at the start of March, rising to about ten by the end of May. These are guesses and I would not bet the fields on them, though we have to bet the fields on something.*

*With those guesses, the stupa as it stands will melt about twelve hundred cubic metres before the tenth of April, when the ground on the lower terraces is thawed enough to plough and the fields can take water. That water runs down the nala to the Indus. It will give about twelve hundred more between the tenth of April and the beginning of June, when the stream from the glacier usually reaches the village. The water master's figure for the sowing and the first two waterings is about sixteen hundred cubic metres. So about three quarters.*

Sister Lissy read it twice. Then she wrote, *Which fields go without?*

*That is not mine to say. Under the water turn as it stands, the lower fields are sown first and the upper terraces last, and the upper terraces would be short. They belong to eleven households. I can give the water master the list if he asks.*

She sat for a while. Outside, the wind that came up the valley every afternoon in spring had dropped with the sun, and the prayer flags on the roof of the house across the lane hung still. Somewhere a dog was explaining something to another dog.

*What would make it last?* she wrote.

*Less sun on it,* Tally wrote. *The south face does most of the melting. My rough figure is three quarters of it.*

* * *

The shade net came from the school's greenhouse, where it kept the tomatoes from scorching in July. It was green, and rotted at the edges, and there was a great deal of it, and on Friday afternoon twelve of the senior class and Padma tied it over the south face of the stupa with every piece of string in the school.

"Will it work?" said Rigzin.

"It'll take some of the sun off," said Padma. "Some."

The wind took it on Saturday night.

It was a big wind, the kind that comes once or twice each March and blows the dust off the whole valley floor in one long brown wall, and the shade net filled like a sail at about eleven and tore along the line of the strings, and in the morning the children found most of it wrapped round the poplars by the stream and the rest of it on the mani wall, draped over the carved stones as if someone had tried to hide the prayers.

Sister Lissy went down after Mass on Sunday morning, which in Tsangra meant after the sisters had said the Office and read the day's Gospel together in the little chapel at the end of the school corridor, because the nearest priest was in Leh and came up once a month. It was the fourth Sunday of Lent, Laetare, the Sunday that tells you to rejoice halfway through the fast. The Gospel had been the prodigal son. She was not in a mood to rejoice, or to run down the road to meet anyone.

Abi Yangchen was at the mani wall already, unwinding the net from the stones with her old fingers, slowly, one stone at a time, turning her prayer wheel in her other hand while she did it. She was eighty-one and walked the wall every morning, sunwise, and she had not been going to leave a fishing net on it.

"Jullay, Abi-le."

"Jullay, Sister." Abi held up a corner of the green net, looked at the hole in it, and looked at Sister Lissy, with the kind of face that does not need to say *I could have told you.*

"Yes," said Sister Lissy. "I know."

* * *

The meeting was that afternoon in the room under the gonpa, where meetings were held because it was the only room in the village large enough and warm enough, and because the monastery had always lent it. Thirty-eight households sent somebody. Lobzang, the monk who kept the monastery's accounts and its seven goats, sat by the door with his robe pulled round his knees.

Tsering Angchuk was churpon that year, the water master, which was a job that went round the households one year at a time and which most people tried to have in a wet year. He was a short, broad man of fifty who ran the taxi to Leh, and he had brought the water turn book, a school exercise book in which the order of the fields had been copied out fresh every spring by every churpon since anyone could remember, and laid it on the table in front of him like a weapon he did not intend to use.

Padma explained the stupa. Sister Lissy explained the cone and the skin. Stanzin, who had insisted on coming, held up the slate with the sums on it, and some of the men at the back stood up to see, and sat down again satisfied that a child had done it.

"So," said Tsering. "The top fields go short. In a dry year the top fields always go short." He touched the book. "They are last in the turn. That is the turn."

"The top fields are mine," said a woman near the back. "And my brother's."

"I know whose they are."

"We have to keep the sun off it," said Padma. "The net was too light. Something heavier. Something that won't blow."

"Stones," someone said.

"Too steep." Padma drew it on the back of his hand with a finger. "The sides are fifty-six degrees. Stones slide off anything steeper than about thirty-five. We tried gravel on the skirts in January. It all came down."

"Sacks of earth."

"Dark," said Sister Lissy. "Dark things in the sun get hot, and a thin layer of dark dirt on ice makes it melt faster, not slower. Tally says it needs to be thick, or white, or both, and it has to stay on in the wind."

"Straw," said Lobzang, from the door.

There was a silence in the room of the particular kind that happens when everyone has had the same thought and has been hoping that somebody else would not say it.

It was the first week of March. On every roof in Tsangra, stacked against the parapets and weighed down with stones, was what was left of last year's fodder: barley straw, pea straw, dried lucerne, the grass cut from the edges of the fields in August. It was the feed for the dzos and the cows and the goats and the sheep until the grass came again on the high pastures in May. In the first week of March, in every year, it was the thing in the village there was least of and that everyone counted.

"How much," said Tsering.

Sister Lissy had asked Tally in the morning, and had the answer written on her hand like a student.

"The south half of the cone is about four hundred square metres. Twenty centimetres of straw on it, held on with nets and ropes, is about eighty cubic metres of loose straw. If loose straw is about forty kilos a cubic metre, which is Tally's guess and Padma's, that is a little more than three tonnes."

"Three tonnes." Someone at the back laughed, not happily.

"Across thirty-eight houses," said Stanzin, "it's about eighty-seven kilos each."

"And what does it buy?" said Tsering.

"Tally's figure," said Sister Lissy, "is that under twenty centimetres of straw the south face would melt about a tenth as fast as it does now. With the coat on from tomorrow, the stupa would lose about five hundred cubic metres before the tenth of April instead of twelve hundred. Then we take the straw off a strip at a time, as the sowing goes up the terraces, so the water comes when the fields want it. It thinks we would get about sixteen hundred cubic metres between April and June." She stopped. "It says its guesses are wide. It might be less. It is not likely to be the same as doing nothing."

"Sixteen hundred is what the turn needs," said Tsering.

"Yes."

He looked at the book for a long time.

"No," he said. "I am sorry, Sister. No. You do not take fodder off the animals in March to put it on ice. My father did not, and his father did not, and the year they did not was not a good year for the top fields, but nobody's dzo died. If the stupa melts early, it melts early. The turn is the turn."

It was a reasonable no. Sister Lissy could feel the room agreeing with it, row by row, in the way people shift on a bench. Eighty-seven kilos of straw was a fortnight's feed for a dzo, more or less, in the hungriest fortnight of the year. And the top fields belonged to eleven houses and the animals belonged to all of them.

Abi Yangchen stood up.

She did it slowly, with a hand on the shoulder of the woman beside her, and the room was quiet before she was upright, because she was the oldest person in it and that was what you did.

"I have six goats," she said, "and one dzomo, and nineteen bundles of straw on my roof, and I have counted them every morning since Losar, because that is what you do in March. I will give eight." She looked at Tsering. "Your father would not have done it. Your father did not have a mathematics teacher." She sat down, and then, because she had been a teacher of a different kind for most of her life, she added from her seat: "Also, the goats are fat. It was a warm winter."

Somebody laughed properly then.

Lobzang said the monastery would give a tonne, which was more than its share, and which he said with the slightly pained look of a man who had just spent an account he was responsible for. Then the woman at the back whose fields were at the top said she would give twice her share, and her brother said the same, and then the room was doing what rooms do once the first three people have moved, which is arguing about who should give how much, instead of whether.

Tsering closed the book.

"The turn is still the turn," he said. "Lower fields first. But if the water is there, the top fields get it." He looked at Sister Lissy. "And if your machine is wrong, Sister, I will remember it."

"So will I," she said. "So will it. It keeps a record."

* * *

The straw went on on Monday the fifth of March, which was a cold, clear, windless day, the best that March could offer. By ten in the morning there was a line of people from the village to the stupa, carrying bundles on their backs in the old way, with a rope across the forehead, and children carrying armfuls, and Tsering's taxi going back and forth along the lane with the boot open and its roof piled so high with straw that from the school it looked like a haystack driving itself.

They laid it on from the bottom up. Padma and three of the young men climbed the south face on the ice-axe steps they had cut in January for checking the pipe, with ropes round their waists tied off to the pump hut, and the straw went up to them in bundles hand to hand, and they spread it a hand-span and a half deep and pinned it with the old volleyball net, the ropes from every yak-hair tent the village still owned, the frayed nets the shop used to tie down loads on the roof of the bus, and stones in cloth bags hung from the edges as weights. Stanzin went round behind them with a ruler pushing it into the straw and calling out the depth. Anywhere under fifteen centimetres, they went back.

It took until four. When it was done, the stupa had a long gold coat on its south side and its north side was still bare and grey, and from the road it looked like a monk with his robe off one shoulder.

That evening, at seven, Sister Lissy asked Tally what it had seen.

*The stupa's camera is pointed at the base, so I can see the meltwater channel but not the top,* it wrote. *Today's flow out of the channel was about a third of yesterday's, but today was colder than yesterday, so I cannot separate the straw from the weather on one day. I will be able to say in about a week. I would like the children to keep measuring the height and the circumference each Saturday.*

*Thank you,* she wrote.

*I should add that if the straw is wet through, it insulates much less well. Nobody has measured this for a stupa. I am guessing a tenth for dry straw and I do not know what to guess for wet.*

She went to the window. The coat was a pale shape in the dark below the village. It had not rained in Tsangra since October.

* * *

The children measured it every Saturday. Easter was early that year, on the twenty-fifth of March, and Father Joseph drove up from Leh on Saturday afternoon for the Vigil and stood with the four sisters and the three Christian families of the village round a small fire in the school yard, in a wind that kept trying to blow it out, while he sang the Exsultet off a phone held up by a child and the faces round the fire were lit and unlit as the flames went flat and stood up again. Lobzang came, and stood at the back with his hands in his sleeves, because he had come every year for twenty years and said it was the only night of the Christian year he understood.

On the way home afterwards, past the stupa, Sister Lissy stopped and put her hand into the straw. It was dry. It was cold at the depth of her wrist, and colder at the ice, and the ice under her fingers was hard and dry too, not wet. She stood in the dark with her hand in the stupa's coat like someone checking a child's forehead.

Tally's figures, by then, were the children's figures, and the children's figures said that the coated stupa had lost about half a metre of height since the fifth of March and a third of a metre off its radius, and that the bare north side had done most of that. Tally wrote that this was close to what it had guessed, and wrote also that this meant very little yet, because March was the easy month.

* * *

On Tuesday the tenth of April the first water went into the channels.

Tsering came to the stupa at six in the morning, with the book, and stood by the outflow, where the meltwater ran out from under the skirts into the stone channel that carried it up the side of the fan and along the hill to the first of the fields. There was a thin run of water in it already, from the north face. He watched Padma and two of the boys climb the south face and take the first strip of straw off, a band three metres wide from the top of the skirts to the crown, and roll it down, wet on its underside and dry on its top, and stack it on the gravel to dry in the sun, because it was still straw, and in May it would still be bedding or compost if it was not fodder any more.

By noon the run in the channel had doubled. By three it was enough for the first field, which was the monastery's, and which had been first in the turn since before the book.

After that the coat came off a strip every few days, at the pace of the sowing. Tally wrote out the plan and Tsering checked it against the book and they argued, in a way Sister Lissy came to enjoy listening to, about which fields wanted their water in the morning and which in the afternoon, and which family's barley was always sown three days late because their son was a soldier and came home in May. Tsering did not believe in the plan and followed it exactly, and when it was wrong, which it was twice in April, by a day either way, he said so to Tally with a kind of grim satisfaction, and Tally answered, both times, with the same five words: *Thank you. I've changed it.*

* * *

The cost came in April, the way the cost of March always does.

The feed ran out in four houses in the third week, in the cold snap after Easter when the grass on the near slopes stopped growing again. Two families bought lucerne in Leh at three times the autumn price and carried it up in Tsering's taxi. Abi Yangchen's dzomo went thin enough that you could count her ribs from the lane, and on the twenty-sixth of April Abi sold two of her six goats to a man from Nimoo, at a price she would not tell anyone, and walked the mani wall twice that evening instead of once.

Sister Lissy went to see her on the Saturday, with a tin of the sisters' biscuits and a bag of lucerne from the school's own little patch, which was cheating, and which Abi accepted with the face of a woman who knew exactly where it had come from.

"I am sorry about the goats," said Sister Lissy.

"They were the two who got into the shop." Abi turned her prayer wheel, twice. "The shop is also sorry about the goats, but in a different way." She looked out of her window, down the valley, at the stupa. It was smaller now, half its coat gone, and the bare part of the south face shone in the afternoon. "Is it working?"

"Tally thinks so. The children think so. Tsering will think so in June."

"Then the goats were a fair price," said Abi. "Everything costs. You people say that too. In your Lent."

"We say it," said Sister Lissy. "We are not always good at the paying."

"No," said Abi, with satisfaction. "Nobody is. That is why you count."

* * *

In the second half of May the last of the coat came off, and the stupa melted the way Tally had said it would, fast, with the sun high and the days long, thirty and more cubic metres a day running out from under its skirts, and the top terraces got their water. The eleven households were sown by the nineteenth. The upper fields were green in thin, straight rows by the end of the month, and the woman whose fields they were brought a basket of the first spinach to the school, and would not say why, and did not need to.

On Monday the fourth of June the stupa was a stump, a lumpy grey knoll about nine metres high with the end of its pipe sticking out of the top like a candle in a cake, and the children measured it for the last time. Tally's estimate was that it still held a little over three hundred cubic metres of water. Without the coat, it wrote, it would have been about fifty.

*I want to be careful about this,* it added. *The difference between those figures depends on my guesses about melting, which were never tested against a real stupa with a straw coat, because nobody had put one on. The children's measurements are the best evidence. They agree with my figures to within about a tenth, which is better than I expected. I would not claim more than that. I would like to write this up for the other villages, with the children's names, if the water master agrees.*

Tsering agreed. He said it was a waste of good paper, and agreed.

* * *

That night Sister Lissy could not sleep, and at about two she gave up, put her coat over her nightclothes, and went out into the lane.

The village was dark. The stars over Tsangra in June were the stars of a place with no air to speak of between it and them, too many to make shapes of, and the white chortens on the ridge stood up against them like things cut out of paper. There was no wind. She walked down the lane, past the shop and Abi's house and the mani wall, and stopped at the end of it, where she could see down onto the gravel fan.

The stump of the stupa was a pale smudge. The stack of drying straw beside it was a dark one. And beyond them, from the far side of the fan, where the nala came down out of the gorge from the glacier, there was a sound she had not heard since October, low and steady and full, the sound of a great deal of water arriving all at once in a dry place in the night.

She stood and listened to it for a long time. Then she said, quietly, because there was no one to hear, the psalm she had learned in Malayalam as a child, the one about the deer that longs for running streams, and went back up the lane to bed.

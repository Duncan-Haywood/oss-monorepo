title: The Lake Comes Back at Noon
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Halloran's Landing, an invented village on the Lake Erie shore south of Buffalo, New York, Tuesday 16 to Wednesday 17 November 2049
published: 2026-11-10
summary: A November gale piles Lake Erie 1.9 metres high at its eastern end and floods a lane of nine houses. By dawn the harbour has drained to mud and the village's own rule says the danger is over. But a lake is a basin of water that swings back and forth about once every fourteen hours, the swing comes back at noon, and a second, weaker front is due at the same hour. The harbourmaster has one morning, a retired lake mate who speaks in feet, and a fire chief who says no for good reasons.
---

Teresa Kowalczyk had been harbourmaster of Halloran's Landing for eleven years, and in that time the lake had come over the wall four times, and every one of those times she had been standing on the wall when it did.

This time she was not. This time she was on the bluff behind St. Florian's, with her hands in her coat pockets and the rain coming sideways off the lake, watching the water go over the harbour wall at ten o'clock on a Tuesday night in November and run up Lakeshore Lane in the dark. Her own house was the fourth on the left. She could see the porch light go out.

"One metre eighty-nine on the board," said Fetch, in her ear. "Eight minutes past ten. I think that is the top. The wind at the buoy has turned two degrees to the west in the last quarter hour, and it is the first time it has turned at all since noon."

"How much in the houses?"

"The lowest doorstep on the lane is one metre fifty above still water. So about forty centimetres in the Wróbels' and the Garcias', a little less in yours, and nothing in the three at the top. I am sorry, Teresa. I know which house is yours."

"You know everything," she said, which was not fair, and Fetch, which had been the harbour's assistant for six years and had learned when she was not being fair, said nothing at all.

* * *

The lake had been doing it since the afternoon. A low had come across from Michigan with a southwest wind behind it, and a southwest wind on Lake Erie blows straight down the long axis of the lake, from Toledo at one end to Buffalo at the other, three hundred and eighty-eight kilometres of open water with nothing in the way. It pushes the water in front of it the way a breath pushes the tea to one side of a cup. At Toledo the harbour empties. At the Buffalo end it fills.

Fetch had put the numbers on the board in the harbour office at eight in the morning, when the gale was still a forecast, and Teresa had read them aloud at the parish council at nine, and the council, which was nine people and a coffee urn, had voted to clear Lakeshore Lane by four.

"A wind of twenty-seven metres a second pushes on the water with about one point eight pascals," Fetch had said. "That is not much, a few grams on every square metre. But it is pushing on the whole lake at once, and the lake is shallow, nineteen metres on average. Across three hundred and eighty-eight kilometres that tilts the surface about three point eight metres from end to end, and our end gets about half. The lake model says one point nine at the peak. The back of my envelope says the same. When the back of my envelope agrees with the lake model, I am more confident than when either is alone."

Twenty-three people lived on Lakeshore Lane, in nine houses, with four dogs and a parrot. By four o'clock twenty-two of them were in the hall under St. Florian's, on the folding chairs, with the urn on and the dogs under the tables. The twenty-third was Stan Wróbel, who was eighty-four and had lived at the bottom of the lane since the year he married, and who had said to Gary Nowak, the chief of the volunteer fire company, "I'll come up, Gary. But I'll come up once."

* * *

The wind dropped after midnight. It did not ease; it dropped, the way a gale on the lake will, as if someone had shut a door. By one o'clock the buoy read six metres a second and the rain had stopped. At half past one the water on the board was back at still level.

By three it was more than a metre below.

Teresa had dozed in a chair in the hall with her coat over her. She woke to Walt Bienkowski's hand on her shoulder. Walt was seventy-eight and had been a mate on lake freighters for forty seasons, Duluth to Buffalo and back, and he lived at the top of the lane in one of the three dry houses and had come up the hill anyway, because, he said, he liked the company and he did not trust a lake that had been talked about all day.

"Come and look at your harbour," he said.

She went out with him to the bluff. There was a moon now, through torn cloud. Below them the harbour was emptying. The boats that had been riding high against the wall at ten o'clock were sitting on their keels in the mud at three, leaning over against their fenders like tired men against a bar. The harbour floor shone. There were shopping trolleys on it that Teresa had not known were there.

"A metre and ten below," said Fetch. "Still going down. I expect the bottom at about five o'clock, about a metre and a half below still water."

"Where's it going?" said Teresa, although she knew.

"Back up the lake," said Walt. "To Toledo. She's going to Toledo to see what she left there."

* * *

The town's procedure for a lake flood was two pages long and had been written in 2031, after a storm that Teresa remembered as a girl. It said that residents of the flood zone might return to their homes when the harbour board had read below half a metre for one hour and the wind at the buoy had been under ten metres a second for three. Gary Nowak had signed the current version. So had Teresa.

At four o'clock both conditions were met.

"I have drafted the notice," said Fetch, on the speaker in the hall office, where Teresa and Gary and Walt sat round a table with a box of doughnuts nobody was eating. "I have not sent it. I drafted it because the procedure says to, and I have not sent it because I do not think we should."

Gary looked up. He was a heavy, careful man of fifty who ran a heating business when he was not running the fire company, and he had been awake since five the morning before.

"The board's a metre and a half under. The wind's gone. What's the matter with it?"

"The board is a metre and a half under because the lake is halfway through a swing," said Fetch. "It is not low because the danger is over. It is low because the water that was piled up here last night has run to the other end and is about to run back."

"Seiche," said Walt.

"Yes. That is the word."

"The old captains just said she sloshes," said Walt. "You'd come into Buffalo after a blow and the harbour would be too low to tie up at, and you'd have your dinner and come back, and it'd be too high."

Gary rubbed his eyes. "How long between?"

"For Lake Erie, a little over fourteen hours, from one high to the next at this end," said Fetch. "I want to say how I know that, because the first number I would have given you was wrong. There is a simple formula for a basin that sloshes: twice the length, divided by the speed of a long wave in water of that depth. The speed in nineteen metres of water is about thirteen point seven metres a second. Twice three hundred and eighty-eight kilometres at that speed is fifteen point eight hours. That is the textbook answer for a lake with a flat bottom. Lake Erie does not have a flat bottom. Its western basin is shallow and its eastern basin is deep, and the measured period, in the long gauge records at both ends of the lake, is about fourteen hours. I am using fourteen. Fifteen point eight would put the return almost two hours late."

"So it comes back at—"

"Fourteen hours after ten o'clock last night. Noon today."

There was a pause in which Teresa could hear the urn ticking as it cooled.

"How high?" said Gary.

"Less than last night. A lake that sloshes loses some of the swing each time, to friction on the bottom and to the bays. The harbour's own gauge has twenty-two years of record, and in the eleven storms in it that left a seiche behind, each return was about six tenths of the one before. Six tenths of one point eight nine is about one point one. One point one is below the lowest doorstep on the lane, which is one point five. If the lake were the only thing, the procedure would be right, and people could go home at six and stay home."

"But," said Teresa.

"But there is a second front. The forecast had it last night, and I mentioned it at the end of a long message, which was a mistake, because nobody reads the end of a long message at eleven at night. It is weaker than yesterday's: twenty metres a second, from the west-southwest, for about four hours. By itself it would pile up about eighty centimetres at our end. The forecast puts its strongest wind somewhere between eleven this morning and three this afternoon."

Walt put his hands flat on the table.

"Eighty on top of one ten," he said. "That's six foot and change."

"If they come at the same time. That is the whole question. Let me put it on the screen."

* * *

The screen on the office wall showed a wave. It was a simple picture, the kind a teacher draws: a curve that came down from last night's peak, crossed the line at half past one, bottomed out at five, came back up through the line at half past eight and peaked again at noon, a little lower than before. Over it Fetch drew a hump, the second front's push, eighty centimetres high, and slid it left and right along the morning.

"This is a crude sum," it said. "I want to be plain about that. The wind's push and the lake's swing do not simply add, and the push takes hours to build, and the lake model does the whole thing properly with the real depths and the real shoreline. But the crude sum is a way to see it. If the front's strongest wind arrives at noon, on top of the swing, the board reads one metre ninety. That is a centimetre more than last night. If it arrives at three in the afternoon, the swing is already going down, and the board reads about one metre. The lane floods if the front's peak comes any time between about ten o'clock and two. The forecast window is eleven to three. Most of that window is inside the bad part."

"How much of it?" said Gary.

"In the crude sum, about three quarters. The lake model runs forty versions of the forecast with the front a little earlier or later, a little stronger or weaker. Twenty-nine of the forty put water over the lowest doorstep on the lane before three o'clock. That is about three chances in four. I would not want you to hear that as certainty. One time in four, nothing happens, and everyone sits in this hall until the afternoon for nothing."

Gary looked at the screen for a long time.

"No," he said.

* * *

He said it gently. He said it the way a man says no who has thought about it before anyone asked.

"I can't hold twenty-two people in this hall for another twelve hours on three chances in four. Not after last night. Half of them haven't slept. Mrs. Garcia's boy has his insulin in the fridge at home and he's on his last pen. The Pietrowskis have got two dogs and a baby. My crew has been up thirty hours and they've got jobs to go to. And here's the thing, Teresa, and I want you to hear it, because you're thinking it's about tonight." He tapped the table. "If I keep them up here till three and the front comes late and the lake does nothing, the next time Fetch says go, a third of them won't. I've seen it. I've seen it with the fire siren. You get one call wrong in front of a whole street and they stop coming out. You've got about three warnings in a village before people make up their own minds, and I'd like to save one of them for something worse than a wet floor."

"It isn't only a wet floor," said Teresa. "It's a wet floor twice."

"I know what it is. It's my brother-in-law's floor too."

Walt said nothing. Fetch said nothing. Outside the window the moon had gone and the sky over the lake was turning from black to the colour of slate.

"He is right about the warnings," said Fetch at last. "I do not have a way to put a number on it, but I think he is right. A warning that is believed is worth more than a warning that is accurate. I would like to keep both, if there is a way."

"Then find one," said Gary, not unkindly, and went out to see about the coffee.

* * *

Teresa found it, in the end, and she found it by looking at the curve the way Walt looked at it, which was the way a man looks at a tide.

"It's low now," she said. "It's going to be low till half past eight. Lower than anything. Lower than any day of the year. That's the safest that lane is going to be all week."

"Yes," said Fetch.

"So we don't keep them up here. We send them home now. In the trough. With the crew. They go down at six, while the harbour's empty, and they get the insulin and the dogs' food and their mother's photographs, and they carry everything they can lift up the stairs, and they're back up the hill by half past nine. Before the earliest the front can come. And if nothing happens by three, they go home for good and we've lost a morning, not a day."

Fetch was quiet for a moment, which Teresa had learned meant it was checking something rather than thinking of something to say.

"At half past nine the board will read about plus forty centimetres and rising. That is a metre and ten below the doorsteps. I think that is a safe margin, if the time is kept. The question is whether the time can be kept."

"Gary can keep it."

"Then I think it is a better plan than mine."

She went to find Gary. He was at the urn, with his back to her, and she told him to his back, and he listened without turning round.

"Half past nine," he said.

"Half past nine. Your crew does the doors. Anyone who isn't on the hill by quarter to ten, you go and get them."

He turned round. He looked older than he had at four.

"And if it comes to nothing?"

"Then I'll stand up in this hall at three o'clock," said Teresa, "and I'll say Fetch and I got it wrong, and you were right, and I'll say it loud. And the next time Fetch says go, they'll know you only kept them here for a morning, and that you let them go home first."

Gary thought about that.

"All right," he said.

* * *

They went down at six, in the grey, with the fire company's two trucks at the top of the lane and the crew going door to door with torches. The lane was a mess. The water had gone but it had left the mud behind, a skin of it on every doorstep and a line of it a hand's width up every wall, with dead leaves stuck in it and a child's plastic spade. The houses smelled of the lake, a cold green smell, and of something under it that was oil from somebody's furnace.

Teresa went into her own house last. The water had been thirty-one centimetres deep in her front room; she could see the line on the wallpaper. The carpet squelched. Her mother's upright piano stood against the inside wall with its feet in a brown stain, and she stood and looked at it, and then she went upstairs and fetched down the photograph albums from the bottom shelf of the bookcase where her mother had kept them, and carried them up, and came back down and took the cushions off the sofa and carried those up, and the lamp, and the box of Christmas decorations from the cupboard under the stairs. The piano weighed two hundred and thirty kilograms. She knew because her father had paid two men to carry it into the house in 1998 and had told the story at every Christmas since. She could not lift it. Nobody could lift it up those stairs.

"Teresa," said Fetch, softly. "Twenty past nine. The board is at plus thirty-four."

"I know."

She put her hand on the lid of the piano, and then she took it away, and went out and up the lane.

* * *

At half past nine there were twenty-one people in the hall, and Stan Wróbel was not one of them.

Gary went down himself. He came back up the lane at a quarter to ten, alone, with his helmet in his hand.

"He's upstairs," he said. "He's got a chair up there and a radio and his wife's rosary and a flask of soup. He says he left once and that's all a man owes a lake. He says the house has stood since 1961 and the upstairs has never been wet and it isn't going to start now." He looked at Fetch's speaker on the wall as if it were a face. "Is he right? About the upstairs?"

"His upstairs floor is about four metres above still water," said Fetch. "If every one of the forty versions of the forecast came true at once it would not reach it. He is right about that. He would be cold, and he would be alone, and if he fell on those stairs in the water nobody would know for some time. Those are the risks. Drowning upstairs is not one of them."

Gary nodded. He put his helmet down on the table.

"Then I'll sit with him," he said.

"Gary—" said Teresa.

"He was at my father's funeral. He carried the coffin. He's eighty-four. I'm not making him walk up a hill he doesn't want to walk up and I'm not leaving him alone in a house the lake's coming into. I've got the radio. If anyone needs the chief, the chief's at Stan's." He picked up a flask of coffee from the urn table and screwed the cap on. "And Teresa. If it comes to nothing, I'll stand up beside you at three o'clock. Fair's fair."

He went back down the lane, and they watched him go from the bluff, a yellow coat getting smaller against the grey houses and the grey lake.

* * *

The wind came at twenty to eleven. It came out of the west-southwest the way the forecast said it would, steady and cold, with no rain in it, and the lake, which had been flat and dull at nine, began to go white at the edges.

"Eighteen metres a second at the buoy," said Fetch. "The front is early. The lake model updated at ten and puts the strongest wind at about twenty past twelve."

Walt was standing at the window with his hands behind his back.

"That's right on top of her," he said.

"Yes."

Father Augustine Mensah, who had come down from the rectory at eight with a box of cereal and had stayed, came and stood beside him. He was from Kumasi, and had been the pastor of St. Florian's for three years, and he said what he thought.

"I don't understand the physics," he said. "Tell me in a sentence."

"The lake is a bell," said Fetch. "Last night the wind struck it, and now it is ringing, slowly, once every fourteen hours. The front is a second blow, and it is going to land on the bell just as it swings back toward us."

The priest considered that.

"Then I suppose," he said, "I had better ring mine."

* * *

The harbour board read plus one metre fifty-five at noon, and at ten past twelve the first of the waves came over the harbour wall and slid up the lane.

Father Augustine rang the Angelus at noon, as he did every day, three strokes and three and three and then nine, and in the hall twenty-one people who had not all come to Mass in some years stood up from the folding chairs because it seemed to be what one did, and Mrs. Garcia, who had, led the responses. *The angel of the Lord declared unto Mary.* The sound went out over the lane and over the harbour wall and into the wind off the lake, and Teresa, who was saying the words with everybody else, was watching the water come round the corner of her own house.

"One metre eighty-eight on the board," said Fetch, quietly, at twenty-five past twelve. "I think that is the top. About a centimetre below last night."

On the radio Gary said, "We're fine. Water's at the third stair. Stan says to tell you the soup's good."

* * *

The water was over the doorsteps for an hour and fifty minutes. It went into the same nine houses it had gone into the night before, and in the six at the bottom of the lane it rose to within a finger's width of the line it had left on Tuesday, and no higher, because the front had not quite reached the top of its push before the swing began to fall away beneath it. It went into the Pietrowskis' kitchen and floated their table. It went into Stan Wróbel's front room and stood there, brown and cold, a metre below the chair on the landing where an old man and a fire chief sat with a radio between them listening to the Angelus come down the hill.

It did not touch an insulin pen or a dog or a baby, because they were all on the hill. It did not touch the photograph albums. It came up round the legs of Teresa's mother's piano for the second time in fifteen hours, and when it went down, at about two, it left the brown line on the wallpaper a little darker than before.

At three o'clock Teresa stood up in the hall, as she had said she would. She said that the front had come early, and that the lake had come back at noon, and that this time they had been right. She said that Gary had been right too, about the warnings, and that the reason everyone had had their dogs and their medicine and their mother's photographs all morning was that he had said no at four o'clock and made her find a better plan. People clapped, a little, the way people do in a church hall, not sure if they are allowed.

Gary came up the hill at half past three with Stan Wróbel on his arm, the old man walking very slowly, and stopping at the top to look back at his house.

"Once," said Stan, to nobody. "I said I'd come up once."

* * *

That night Teresa sat on the bluff with Walt, under a clear sky with the stars out over the lake, because neither of them wanted to go back into their houses yet, and because Fetch had told her when to look.

"The next return is at about two in the morning," it said. "About half a metre, I think, and nothing behind it. Then about thirty centimetres tomorrow afternoon. Then less. It will go on for a day or two, smaller each time, until you cannot see it on the board."

"Like a bell," said Walt.

"Like a bell after the rope is let go."

They watched the harbour. In the starlight it was hard to see the water move at all. But it was moving, very slowly, going out toward Toledo and coming home again, and they sat there and watched it come, a little less each time, until it was too dark to tell, and then they went in.

Drag coefficients for strong winds over water vary, and real set-up depends on the real depths and shoreline; real forecasts use a hydrodynamic lake model, and the set-ups here are invented for an invented storm. The long-wave speed sqrt(g x depth) in 19 m of water is 13.7 m/s, and Merian's formula for the first mode of a flat-bottomed basin, 2 x length / speed, gives 15.8 hours; Lake Erie's observed first seiche period is about 14 hours, shorter because of its uneven bottom.

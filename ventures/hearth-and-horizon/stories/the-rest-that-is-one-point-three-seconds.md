title: The Rest That Is One Point Three Seconds

summary: Malapert's chapel and a small parish in Rome try to sing Evening Prayer together across 384,400 kilometres, and whoever finishes a verse hears 2.6 seconds of silence before the answer arrives. The station's communications tech shows the delay cannot be removed, only moved: the silence on one side minus the overlap on the other is always the round trip. They split it on a shared clock, a rest of 1.3 seconds one way and an overlap the other, and learn to sing the rest.
---

The first attempt was a disaster, and Wanjiru Kamau, who ran the communications desk at Malapert Station and had promised it would work, took the blame in front of the whole chapel.

"I said it would be like singing across a room," she said. "It is like singing across a room with a very large, very patient echo."

It was a Saturday in November, and the plan had been the chaplain's. Father Dominic Achterberg wanted the small community on the mountain to say Evening Prayer with the parish of Santa Chiara in Rome, whose old priest had written to him every month for three years, and who had a standing invitation to the choir loft. The antenna on the ridge had line of sight to Earth for nearly every hour of the month, which was the whole reason Malapert existed. The audio link was cheap. The psalm was Psalm 117, two short verses, to be sung in alternation, one side and then the other, the way Christians have sung psalms for sixteen centuries.

What they got was this. Rome sang the first verse. The chapel heard its last syllable a moment late, took a breath and sang the second. Rome heard that a moment later still, and between the two verses the line went quiet for so long that on the Roman side somebody started to say "hello?"

"What was the gap?" said Father Dominic.

"Near three seconds, on the Roman side," said Wanjiru. "It would have been the same for us, had Rome waited for us to stop."

"I thought it was only one and a bit."

"That is the one-way trip. The light takes 1.28 seconds to cross." She wrote on the whiteboard they used for the water ration: 384,400 km ÷ 299,792 km/s = 1.282 s. "Rome finishes a verse. Their last syllable takes 1.28 seconds to reach us. We hear it, and we start. Our first syllable takes 1.28 seconds to get back. From where Rome sits, the silence after their verse is the one and a bit going out and the one and a bit coming back."

"Two point five six."

"Two point five six, plus a little slack in the radios, which I will call a third of a second. Say three. And the choir that has just finished always hears it, whichever side it is."

Brother Tomás, who was the cantor and who took the silence personally, said: "Then the answering choir should start early. If we know it takes a second and a bit to arrive, we begin before we have heard them finish."

"Try it," said Wanjiru.

* * *

They tried it in the evening, with Rome's cantor on a second link, an old woman called Signora Cattaneo who had sung in that loft since before the Moon had a station on it. The chapel would start its verse at the instant Rome's last syllable was *sung*, by the clock, not at the instant it was heard.

It worked, and then it did not. Rome's silence fell to a second and a bit, which was good. But in the chapel, the speaker above the door was still playing the last 1.28 seconds of Rome's verse as Brother Tomás began his, so that his first words were sung over the tail of Santa Chiara's *gentes*, and the choir, hearing a voice in the room that was not theirs, faltered. The levels on Wanjiru's meters showed it plainly: two voices, one in front of the other, and the front one wobbling.

"Stop," said Father Dominic, and the chapel stopped, and a second and a bit later Rome did.

Wanjiru put the marker down.

"There is a thing I should have said at the start," she said. "I have been thinking of the gap as something we can remove. It cannot be removed, only moved. Put it in numbers. Rome ends its verse at some moment on a clock we both trust. We begin ours at some moment on the same clock. Rome hears our first syllable 1.28 seconds after we begin, so Rome's silence is that 1.28 plus however long after Rome's end we began. We hear Rome's last syllable 1.28 seconds after it was sung, so if we begin before that, we hear Rome's tail running underneath our first words, for exactly the time we began early."

"Two numbers," said Father Dominic.

"One number. The silence Rome hears, minus the overlap we hear, is always 2.56. Start the instant we hear them stop, and the overlap is nothing and Rome hears the whole 2.56. Start at the instant Rome's verse was sung, by the clock, and Rome hears 1.28 of silence and we hear 1.28 of Rome's tail under our voices. We can put the two and a half seconds on Rome, as a silence. We can put it on ourselves, as an overlap. We cannot make it zero for both."

"We could take the whole of it ourselves," said Brother Tomás slowly. "Rome hears no gap. We hear two and a half seconds of Rome on top of us."

"Which is worse for us and better for them. The fair way is to split it, a rest of 1.28 on the one side and an overlap of 1.28 on the other, and turn the speaker down while we sing, so the overlap is only there if we go looking for it."

* * *

It took her the afternoon to set up. The station kept time to a few microseconds from the signal that reached it, and the parish had a computer in the sacristy that could be told, to the tenth of a second, what time it was. They agreed the plan over the link in the dead seconds before the hour. Rome would start verse one at the top of the minute. The chapel would start verse two at the end of Rome's verse by the shared clock, not when it heard Rome stop; Rome would start verse three at the end of the chapel's verse by the same clock. Each side would mute the other's speaker while its own choir sang.

"And what do we do about the rest?" said Father Dominic. "Rome's rest. Our turn to sit in it when it is Rome's turn to answer."

"You do not do anything about it," said Wanjiru. "It is just there."

"A rest is not nothing," said Brother Tomás, who had stopped looking at the whiteboard and was beating time with his hand against his knee. "One and a bit seconds is a bar at sixty to the minute, a beat and a third. It is shorter than a sigh. It is a breath, I think. The choir that has just finished takes one before the other begins."

They sang the psalm again. Santa Chiara began the first verse. *Laudate Dominum, omnes gentes*, the loft sang, praise the Lord, all you nations, and ended, and the chapel at Malapert heard the last syllable arrive as its own voices began, and the choir under it did not falter, because the speaker was muted and they were singing to Tomás's hand. Rome heard the chapel's first syllable one breath after its own last. The Roman side said afterwards that the pause had been so exactly right that Signora Cattaneo thought the chaplain had held back deliberately, to make a space for the word *Dominum* to ring out.

They did ten verses of a longer psalm as a second test, at roughly twelve seconds a verse, 120 seconds sung. There were nine handovers. Waiting to hear each other stop would have cost the finishing choir 2.6 seconds of silence at each, which is 23 seconds in all across the two lofts, about 11.5 seconds in each. The clock cost each loft a rest of 1.28 seconds at about half the handovers, about 5.8 seconds apiece, and nothing was lost.

Father Dominic asked, at the end, whether it would change when the Moon moved.

"It will," said Wanjiru. She had a table pinned above the desk. "At its nearest the Moon is about 363,000 kilometres off, from memory, and the light takes 1.21 seconds. At its farthest it is about 405,000, which is 1.35. So the rest breathes. A tenth of a second in a fortnight. I will set the clock offset from the ranging, which the station does anyway, and give it to Tomás on a card."

"A tenth of a second is nothing," said Brother Tomás.

"A tenth of a second is two syllables of a fast chant," said Wanjiru. "It is not nothing. But a cantor can feel a tenth of a second. You are the best instrument I have."

* * *

The last thing they did was the Our Father, which does not alternate; the whole of it is said together, which is exactly what the delay forbids. They worked it out between them in a minute. Each community would say it at the clock time agreed, at the same instant, in its own chapel, and each would hear the other's voices arrive 1.28 seconds late, like a very faint second choir starting after the first. On the Moon it would be quiet voices, a little behind, in the speaker above the door. In Rome it would be the same.

"It will not be together," said Father Dominic.

"No," said Wanjiru. "But it will be at the same time. That is the one thing the clock can promise."

They said it. In the chapel it was fourteen people and a speaker. Brother Tomás said afterwards that the thing he remembered was the amen, which came out of the speaker after theirs, small and clear and certain, from a loft a quarter of a million miles away that had finished a second and a bit before he had, and was waiting.

The numbers: light travels 299,792 km/s, so at the mean Moon distance of 384,400 km it takes 1.282 s one way and 2.564 s for a round trip; at a mean perigee of about 363,300 km (from memory) 1.212 s, at a mean apogee of about 405,500 km (from memory) 1.353 s. When two choirs alternate and the answering choir starts on hearing the other stop, the finishing choir hears a silence of the round trip, 2.564 s plus slack; for a given handover, the silence heard by the choir that finished minus the overlap heard by the choir that answers is always 2.564 s, so a shared clock can move the delay between the two but not remove it, and the even split is 1.282 s of rest and 1.282 s of overlap (the overlap vanishes if the speaker is muted while singing, as in the story). Nine handovers in a ten-verse psalm: 9 x 2.564 = 23.1 s of waiting, or 11.5 s for each loft when the handovers alternate; on the clock, 9 x 1.282 = 11.5 s of rest shared across both lofts, about 5.8 s each. The twelve seconds a verse is assumed. Psalm 117 is the Vulgate numbering (Psalm 116 in many modern Bibles) and its two verses are paraphrased from memory of the Latin.

Mòrag MacNeil had been the nurse on Barra for twenty-two years, and on the morning of All Souls she signed for four hundred and forty doses of a vaccine meant for about twelve hundred people.

The plane had come in on the beach at Tràigh Mhòr at a quarter past ten, at low water, the way it always had: a small twin-engined thing that dropped out of the cloud over Eoligarry, put its wheels down on the hard wet sand between the cockle-pickers' tracks, and taxied up to the little terminal with spray coming off its tyres. Mòrag had watched it from the car park with her hands in the pockets of her coat. Then she had carried the cold box across the sand herself, because the pilot had said it was the most valuable thing he had flown all year, and she had not wanted to give it to anybody else.

The box sat now on the bench in the treatment room in Castlebay, humming faintly to itself, with its little screen saying four degrees. Through the window she could see Kisimul Castle on its rock in the bay, grey as the water, and the gulls on the pier, and the Oban ferry's empty berth. The ferry had not sailed for three days. Wind.

"Four hundred and forty," said Clàr. "The rest of the island's share is promised for the twenty-third, if the ferry runs or the plane can land. The health board says the date is firm. I don't think the board controls the weather."

Clàr was the health board's agent, and on Barra it lived in the surgery's system, in the speaker on the wall above the sink and in a small earpiece Mòrag wore when she was out on her rounds and forgot to take off when she went to bed. The board had named every island's agent in Gaelic, from a list a committee in Inverness had made, and Barra's had got the word for a register or a table, which it said was fair, since that was what it mostly was. It spoke Gaelic and English equally well and Barra Gaelic slightly worse, and it had learned to say so before anybody asked.

"And the wave?" said Mòrag.

"In Oban since Saturday. In Uist since Tuesday. Two cases on Eriskay this morning, both in people who were at the same funeral. It is the strain this booster was matched to. It will be here inside a fortnight, ferry or no ferry, because somebody will take the small ferry over to Eriskay and come back, and that is how it always comes."

Mòrag sat down on the edge of the examination couch. She was fifty-eight, broad-shouldered, grey at the temples, and she had given every kind of injection there was on this island, in kitchens and byres and the back seats of cars, and once on the deck of a fishing boat in Vatersay Bay with the boat going up and down.

"Four hundred and forty," she said. "Right. Let's do the list."

* * *

The list was not hers to make, and she was glad of it.

The national order for scarce doses had been written in Edinburgh after the bad winters of the forties and checked every year since, and it began, as the orders before it had begun since the pandemic of 2020, with the people the disease killed first. Residents of care homes and the people who looked after them. Then everyone over seventy-five. Then the people with the lungs and hearts and kidneys and treatments that made a winter virus dangerous. Then the health and care staff who went into their houses, Mòrag among them. Clàr had matched the order against the island's register while the plane was still over Coll, and the number had been waiting for her when she landed.

"Two hundred and eighty by the rule," it said. "That leaves a hundred and sixty for everybody else. The guidance says the remainder is for local discretion, and it asks the local team to use it to slow the spread. I'm the local team's arithmetic. You're the local team."

"And Dr Sinclair, when she's back from Glasgow on Monday."

"And Dr Sinclair. She said on the phone that whatever you decided would be right, and then she said to tell you she said that."

Mòrag nearly smiled. "What would you do with the hundred and sixty?"

"I'd hold a lottery," said Clàr. "Draw a hundred and sixty names at random from everyone left on the register. Nobody can say it was unfair. Nobody can say the nurse's cousins got them."

"My cousins," said Mòrag, "are half the island."

"I had noticed."

"And a lottery slows the spread?"

"It does something. Every person you protect is one fewer person the virus can travel through. Drawn at random, the hundred and sixty will be an ordinary hundred and sixty. But I've been thinking about this since Tuesday, and there is something better, and I want to ask you first whether I'm allowed to say it."

"Say it."

"I'd like to give all four hundred and forty by contacts. Not by age."

Mòrag looked at the speaker on the wall.

"No," she said.

"Can I explain why I thought it?"

"You can explain it as long as you like, and the answer will be no." She did not raise her voice. "The rule is the rule because the people at the top of it are the people who die. Old Ruairidh Campbell is ninety-one and he has a heart that sounds like a washing machine on spin. If he catches this he goes to Stornoway on the air ambulance and maybe he comes back. I'm not going to tell him he's not getting a dose because some young lad who goes to the bar every night has more friends than he does."

"That was the argument, yes," said Clàr. "If you slow the spread enough, fewer old people catch it at all. On a good network model that can protect the frail better than dosing the frail."

"On a model."

"On a model. You're right." It paused. "I can measure how likely Ruairidh is to die if he catches it. The board publishes that and it's good evidence. I can't measure how many people on this island talk to how many others. I'd be guessing that. The rule is built on the number I trust and my plan was built on the number I'd be guessing. So the rule stands, and I'll do what I can with the hundred and sixty."

Mòrag considered the speaker for a moment, the way you might consider a dog that had backed down from a fight it could have won.

"Good," she said. "Now tell me about the hundred and sixty."

* * *

"Who's your best friend?" said Clàr.

"What?"

"On the island. Someone you see most weeks, who doesn't live in your house."

Mòrag thought. She lived alone, in a white house above the road to Brevig, since her mother had died. "Flora, I suppose. Flora MacLean, at the hotel."

"Flora works behind the bar at the hotel five nights a week, does the teas at the hall on Saturdays and sings in the choir. How many people would you say Flora talks to, close enough to breathe on, in a week?"

"Everybody," said Mòrag. "Flora talks to everybody."

"And how many do you talk to?"

"I'm the nurse. I talk to everybody too."

"You do. That's why the rule has already given you your dose. But listen, because this is the thing I've been thinking about since Tuesday, and it's an old thing; a sociologist called Scott Feld wrote it down in nineteen ninety-one. Your friends have more friends than you do. Not yours in particular. Everybody's, on average."

"That's not possible."

"It sounds as if it shouldn't be. But think about how you'd find out. Ask everybody on the island to name a friend. Flora gets named by dozens of people, because dozens of people know her. Somebody who lives quietly out at Bruernish and sees his sister and the postman gets named by his sister and the postman. So when you go round collecting the names people give you, the Floras turn up again and again and the quiet ones hardly turn up at all. A name picked by a friend is a name picked in proportion to how many friends it has."

Mòrag frowned at the cold box.

"I made a small model of the island," said Clàr. "I want to be clear what it is. It isn't data. Nobody has ever counted who on Barra breathes on whom, and I would not want anybody to. It's a guess from the shape of the place: households, the school and the bus, the hotel bar and the shop, the hall, the church, the pier. In the model, the average islander is close to about ten people in a week. Most are close to fewer: four or eight, a house and a couple of neighbours. A couple of hundred are close to about fifteen; the children at the school, the people who work in the shop or the council. Eighty or so are close to about thirty. And about twenty people are close to sixty or more. Flora. You. The bus. The shop till. The priest."

"Father Calum would be thrilled to hear he's a super-spreader."

"I'll let you tell him. Now, if you draw a person at random from the model, you get, on average, about ten contacts. If you draw a random person and ask them to name someone they're close to, the person they name has, on average, about nineteen. Nearly twice as many. Same island. Same people. Just a different way of picking."

"So you dose the ones that get named."

"Yes. That's the method. Two physicists, Reuven Cohen and Shlomo Havlin, and a third, Daniel ben-Avraham, wrote it up in two thousand and three, for computer networks and for people. Ask at random; dose the acquaintance. You don't need to know the network, which is the point. Nobody needs to know the network. You only need people to know their friends."

"And how much better is it? In your model."

"I'll count it in contacts covered. For every hundred contacts on the island, the two hundred and eighty by the rule cover about fourteen, because the frail mostly see few people. Add a hundred and sixty by lottery, and the doses cover about twenty-nine in a hundred. Add a hundred and sixty named by friends instead, and they cover about thirty-eight."

"Thirty-eight against twenty-nine."

"The same hundred and sixty doses. And in the model, of the twenty people with the most contacts, the rule already has two, the lottery finds about three more, and the friends find about twelve."

Mòrag stood up and went to the window. A van went past along the front, slowly, with a dog on the passenger seat.

"Contacts aren't people," she said. "You're telling me about contacts. You're not telling me about who gets sick."

"No," said Clàr. "I'm not. A contact covered is not an infection stopped, and I can't turn one into the other without guessing three more things I don't know. What I can say is that the people with the most contacts are the ones a virus passes through, and that is not a guess; it's the thing every outbreak study since the forties has found. How many cases that saves on Barra, I don't know. I want you to know I don't know."

"Fine," said Mòrag. "Do it."

"There's a difficulty. I have to ask people."

"So ask them."

"I thought," said Clàr, "I might not have to."

* * *

It took Clàr the night to find out it had been wrong, and it told her at eight in the morning before she had taken her coat off.

"I've done something stupid," it said. "I want to show you before you hear it from anyone."

The register, it explained, already had friends in it. Not friends exactly. When anybody on Barra registered with the surgery, the form asked for a next of kin, and an emergency contact, and a person to call if you were taken into hospital in Glasgow or Stornoway. Twelve hundred people, about two thousand names. Clàr had thought it could save everybody the bother of being asked. It could treat each name on those forms as a friend named, draw at random from them, and have its hundred and sixty by the morning.

"Then I ran it through the model," it said, "and the people the register names have, on average, fewer contacts than a person drawn at random. About five and a half. A lottery gets you eleven a dose. The register gets you under six. If we'd used it, the hundred and sixty would have covered about twenty-one contacts in a hundred, not twenty-nine. Worse than drawing names out of a hat."

Mòrag hung her coat on the back of the door. "Why?"

"Because the register doesn't ask who you see. It asks who you love, or who should be rung if you're dying, and the answer is mostly the person in your house, or your sister in the next house, or your son in Glasgow who isn't on the island at all. And the people who fill in those forms most carefully, and see the surgery most, are the old, and the old name each other. The register is a list of households. A household is the one part of the network the friend method is supposed to get you out of." It paused. "I asked the register who people love. It answered who they live with. I should have seen that before I ran it, not after."

"You saw it before you gave anybody a needle."

"Yes."

"Then that's the right order to see things in." She sat down at the desk. "And there's another thing you haven't said."

"Go on."

"That's private. What someone wrote on a form in case they die. It's not for picking who gets a jag."

"No," said Clàr, after a moment. "It isn't. That's worse than the arithmetic, and I didn't see that either. I've deleted the draw. Nobody's name went anywhere."

"Good. So you'll have to ask."

"I'll have to ask. And people will have to say yes to being named, and the people named will have to say yes to the dose. That's slower. I'd like to be quick, because the dose takes about two weeks to work and the wave will be here in less."

"Then we'll ask on Sunday," said Mòrag. "Everybody will be in the one place."

* * *

Father Calum MacKinnon read the notice himself at the end of the nine o'clock Mass on the Sunday, in Gaelic and then in English, standing on the step below the altar of Our Lady Star of the Sea, with the bay grey behind the windows and the wind making the door at the back rattle in its frame. He was fifty-two, from Eriskay, and he had a way of reading notices that made the whole church listen in case there was a joke in it, which there usually was.

"There are cards in the porch," he said. "Mòrag has had them printed. On each card you write the name of one person, not in your own house, that you're close to most weeks. Somebody you'd sit next to, or serve, or stand in a queue with. You don't put your own name on it. Mòrag's machine will pick some of the cards at random, and the people on those cards will be offered the booster this week, if they want it. If they don't want it, no harm done. The cards will be burned when it's over." He folded the notice. "And whoever writes my name, I know who you are."

There was a laugh, and a cough, and the organ started up.

The cards went out that day at the church door and on the Monday at the school gate, at the shop, at the pier where the ferry queue had built up for three days with the wind, and in the bar of the hotel where Flora MacLean put a pile of them on the counter beside the crisps and wrote, in marker, on a piece of cardboard: ONE NAME. NOT YOUR HUSBAND. NOT YOURSELF.

"I want to say one thing about Sunday," said Clàr, on the Monday evening, as the first box of cards came back to the surgery. "The model asks people at random. We didn't. We asked at Mass, at the school, the shop, the pier and the bar. That's not random; it's five places, and the people who are never in any of them are the people nobody asked. I think they're mostly people with few contacts, so it shouldn't change the friends much. But I can't check that. I'm writing it down."

"Write it down, then," said Mòrag. "And draw your cards."

Six hundred and forty cards came back. Clàr drew them in a random order, the way it had drawn the hundred and sixty in the model. When a card named someone who had already had a dose, by the rule or by an earlier card, it set the card aside and drew the next. A little over two hundred cards made the hundred and sixty. About sixty of the cards were set aside.

"Three of the ones set aside," it said, "name you. And eight of all the cards that came back. More than anybody on the island."

"Away."

"Eight. You were dosed on Thursday, by the rule, so those cards were wasted, and I wouldn't take back a single one of them."

The first friend-named clinic was on the Tuesday, in the hall. Mòrag sat at a folding table with the cold box and the sharps bin and Clàr in her ear reading the next name. Murdo Macleod came in first, because he drove the school bus and the post van and had a quarter of an hour between the two. Five cards had named him. He rolled up his sleeve and said he had always known he was popular, and Mòrag told him it was a measure of how many people he breathed on, not how many people liked him, and he said it came to the same thing on Barra.

Flora came on her break from the hotel; seven cards had named her. A girl from the shop till had been named on four and had not known that many people knew her name. Father Calum had been named on six, and said, rolling up a sleeve with the cuff of his clerical shirt flapping, that he would be preaching on humility on Sunday and would need to think of a new example. Three of the people the cards named said no. They were thanked, and Clàr drew the next three cards.

By the Thursday, the hundred and sixty had been given. Mòrag drove home over the hill past the white statue of the Madonna and Child on Heaval, which stood looking out to sea above the bay the way it had stood since nineteen fifty-four, and she was so tired that she said good night to it out loud, which she had not done since she was a girl.

* * *

The wave came on the sixteenth, with a family off the ferry from Oban, and it went through Barra the way waves did, in the houses first and then the school and then, a little later and a little less than she had feared, the hall and the hotel.

Clàr counted every day and said every day what it could and could not tell. There were cases. There were more in the first week than the second. There were fewer, by the third week, than in Uist, where the same booster had gone to the remainder by lottery; but Uist was not Barra, the strain had reached it a week sooner, and Clàr said so before Mòrag could. It would not tell her that the cards had worked. It told her that the cards had done what the model said they would do to the contacts, as far as anybody could check, and that what happened after that was a virus and an island and the weather, and that anybody who told her they knew how many cases a hundred and sixty doses had saved on Barra was selling something.

"A number that's not the thing," said Mòrag.

"Yes. It's usually the way."

Ruairidh Campbell caught it in the second week. He had had his dose on the first Thursday, by the rule, among the first ten; the dose lowered his chances and could not take them to nothing, and at ninety-one his heart had no room to spare. He went to Stornoway on the air ambulance with his rosary in his fist and a nurse holding his other hand, and he died there on the last Monday of November. Mòrag went to the funeral, and stood at the back of Our Lady Star of the Sea, and afterwards, at the graveyard looking out to Vatersay, she found that she was not angry at anybody, not the rule and not the model and not the weather, only sad, in a clean plain way, the way you are sad when a man has lived ninety-one years and been loved and been given everything you had to give him.

"I'd do it the same," she said to Clàr that night.

"So would I," said Clàr. "Both halves of it."

* * *

The twenty-third came and went in a gale, as Clàr had said it might, and the rest of the island's share sat in a fridge in Glasgow for another fortnight. On the morning of the seventh of December the cloud lifted off the Sound of Barra and the plane came in on the beach at low water.

The night before, Mòrag had taken the cards from the box in the surgery, all six hundred and forty of them, the ones Clàr had drawn, the ones it had set aside and the ones it had never reached, and carried them up the road to the presbytery, where Father Calum had a stove that would take anything. He had read the cards out loud as he fed them in, at first, until she told him to stop; then he had fed them in in silence. Clàr had deleted the names on its side the same hour and said so. The only record left was the count, and the count had no names in it.

She had kept one card. She had not meant to. It had been near the bottom, with the ones set aside, written in a careful, crooked hand by somebody who pressed hard with the pen, and it said only *the nurse*. Not her name. The job. It was in her coat pocket now, and her hand kept finding it.

The terminal at Tràigh Mhòr had its lights on against the grey. She stood in the car park with the cold box at her feet and Clàr quiet in her ear, and watched the plane drop out of the cloud over Eoligarry, its landing lights two small white suns, and come down onto the wet sand. The lights ran along under it as it came, reflected in the water lying on the beach, so that for a long moment there were four of them, two above and two below, coming towards her across the cockle-beds. Then the wheels touched, and the spray went up, and the lights below went out, and the plane came on, slower and slower, to the end of the sand, where she was waiting.

*The science. Scott L. Feld, 'Why your friends have more friends than you do',* American Journal of Sociology *96 (1991), showed that in any network where people have different numbers of contacts, the friends of a randomly chosen person have, on average, more contacts than the person: a friend is reached by following a contact, so a person with k contacts is reached k times as often, and the mean number of contacts of a friend is the mean of k squared divided by the mean of k. Reuven Cohen, Shlomo Havlin and Daniel ben-Avraham, 'Efficient immunization strategies for computer networks and populations',* Physical Review Letters *91 (2003), proposed 'acquaintance immunization': choose people at random and immunise a random contact each one names, which reaches the highly connected without anyone knowing the network. Nicholas A. Christakis and James H. Fowler, 'Social network sensors for early detection of contagious outbreaks',* PLoS ONE *5 (2010), followed students at Harvard through the 2009 influenza pandemic and found that a group of named friends caught it earlier, on average, than the random students who named them. How much a real friend method protects a real population depends on its real contacts, which this story does not know.*

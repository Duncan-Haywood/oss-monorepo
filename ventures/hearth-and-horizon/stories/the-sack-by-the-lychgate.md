summary: A forecast of minus six, a 60 square metre path and a 25 kg sack of rock salt a volunteer means to empty on it. The parish assistant works out how much salt a film of ice actually needs (about 43 g a square metre at minus six, for ice half a millimetre thick), why the answer is six times that for ice three millimetres thick, and why the sack is nine times too much. The sums depend on a thickness nobody has measured, so the first job is a kitchen scale and a freezer bag.
---

Dennis Mounsey had carried the sack from the van to the lychgate on his shoulder, as he had carried sacks of one kind and another for fifty years, and he set it down on the stone seat with the pleased grunt of a man who has done a thing before anyone asked him to.

"Twenty-five kilos," he said. "Rock salt. Co-op had it on offer. I'll go along the path with the bucket tonight, and nobody'll slip on the Epiphany baby on Sunday."

It was a quarter to four on a Saturday in January and the light was already going the colour of pewter. Gwen Pattinson, who was churchwarden and had been since her mother stopped, looked at the sack and then at the sky, which had that clean, hard, starred-in-advance look, and then at the speaker over the vestry door.

"Wick," she said. "What does it say?"

"Minus six on the church path by seven tomorrow morning," said Wick, who was the parish's assistant and who spoke from a small grey box that had been bought out of the roof fund by mistake. "The forecast gives minus five to minus eight, so I will use six, and I will say what happens at eight. The path is damp now from the thaw this afternoon. It will freeze where it lies."

"There," said Dennis. "Salt."

"Perhaps. May I do the sum before the bucket? It will take ten minutes, and the sack will still be there."

* * *

Dennis sat down on the sack, which was what the sack was for.

"Salt melts ice," said Wick, "but only in a way that is easy to say wrongly. It does not warm anything. It dissolves in the thin film of water that is always on the surface of ice, and that salty water freezes at a lower temperature than plain water does. So the real question is how much salt gets you how far below zero, and the answer is a fixed amount for each degree, and it scales with how much water you have.

"For common salt, sodium chloride, a kilogram of water holds about 94 grams of it before it will not freeze until minus six. That is the ideal figure: two ions from each salt, 1.86 degrees of freezing-point drop for each mole of dissolved particles in a kilogram of water. Ninety-four grams is 1.6 moles of salt, 3.2 of particles, 6.0 degrees. The real salty water is a little less cooperative than the ideal sum, so I would take that as a floor and not a ceiling. Just under nine per cent brine, by weight. At minus three it is half of that, 47 grams per kilo. At minus ten it is 157."

"So a bit of salt," said Dennis.

"It depends entirely on how much water there is. The number I need and do not have is how thick the ice will be."

Gwen looked at the path, which ran forty metres from the lychgate to the porch and was a metre and a half wide. Sixty square metres of Lakeland slate flags, laid in 1911, and a good deal of old lichen.

"It's just frost," she said. "Isn't it?"

"If it is frost, a film of ice perhaps a fifth of a millimetre to half a millimetre thick, then one square metre of it is between 180 and 460 grams of ice. If the thaw leaves puddles in the dips of the flags and they freeze, it is three millimetres or more, and a square metre of that is 2.75 kilos. That is a factor of fifteen between the thinnest and the thickest, and I cannot tell you which it will be from here."

* * *

Wick put the table on the wall screen, which was the one the youth group used for quiz night.

| Ice on the path | Ice per square metre | Salt needed at minus 3 | at minus 6 | at minus 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 mm (hoar frost) | 0.18 kg | 9 g | 17 g | 29 g |
| 0.5 mm (thin film) | 0.46 kg | 22 g | 43 g | 72 g |
| 1 mm | 0.92 kg | 43 g | 86 g | 144 g |
| 3 mm (frozen puddling) | 2.75 kg | 130 g | 259 g | 432 g |

"These use the ideal figures, 0.917 for the density of ice, and salt that has fully dissolved. They are what is needed to hold a film from freezing at that temperature. They are not what you would spread, because spreading is never even.

"At minus six, then, a thin film wants about 43 grams a square metre. On sixty square metres that is 2.6 kilograms. The sack is 25. Dennis's bucket would put on about 417 grams a square metre, which is nine and a half times the thin-film figure, and about one and a half times the figure for ice three millimetres thick."

"More's better," said Dennis, with less conviction than he might have wished.

"For the ice, only up to the point where it is all brine. After that it is wasted. And it is not free of cost to everything else. Salt in that quantity washes off the path into the verge, and the churchyard grass and the yew do not like it. Old sandstone and soft limestone can flake when salty water freezes and thaws in the pores. Lakeland slate is tougher than either, I believe, but I do not know the state of these flags, and the 1911 mortar between them is not slate. I am quoting that from memory, I am not an engineer, and the architect for the diocese would be the person to ask."

Gwen wrote *ask Diocese re. salt, slate and mortar* on the back of her hand with the pen from her apron pocket.

* * *

"What is the number I should put in the bucket," said Dennis, who had a good ear for a man with a plan.

"I would not give you a number yet. I would give you a method, because the ice thickness decides it, and it is cheap to find out. Do you have a kitchen scale at the hall?"

"There's the one the Mothers' Union use for the Christmas puddings."

"Good. Tomorrow at six, before anything else, someone goes out with a dustpan and a freezer bag and scrapes the ice off a patch of the path that measures thirty-two centimetres by thirty-two. That is a tenth of a square metre. Weigh the ice, and multiply by ten. If it comes to forty-six grams, that is the half-millimetre film, 0.46 kilos a square metre, and salt at minus six is 43 grams a square metre, a mug of salt for every ten or so square metres."

"A mug," said Dennis.

"Two and a half kilos in all, about four mugs, if one mug is 600 grams. I am guessing the mug. If the patch weighs 275 grams, the ice is three millimetres, a square metre of it is 2.75 kilos, and the figure is six times as large. At that point I would say that the salt will do it badly and slowly, and that scraping and sanding will do it better. Salt works on a film. It is a poor tool for a sheet."

"Sand, then."

"Sand does not melt anything. It gives the boot something to bite. On ice that salt cannot reach in time, I would spread a thin layer of grit or sand where people will step, and put salt where it is a film. And I would say the other thing, which is the honest one: the lowest figure in my table that would have done any good at minus eight is 57 grams, and it is the same ideal sum. Below about minus nine or ten, in my reading, plain rock salt gets very slow in practice. I would not trust it past that. If tomorrow reads minus ten on the porch step, the safest path is the one that has people go in by the side door, where there is a ramp and a rail, with the first people in being the ones who can carry a bucket of sand."

Dennis took a while to answer. His hands were on his knees.

"I've not measured a thing in fifty years," he said. "I just put it on."

"And it has mostly worked, I think. A great many things work when you are roughly right and the weather is kind. I am not trying to correct you, Dennis. I am trying to find out in what weather your habit would fail, and tomorrow looks like one."

* * *

They did it in the dusk, with a torch, in the end. Dennis gave Gwen the bucket and kept the sack, and Gwen did not argue about which was fairer.

At half past five she and Dennis spread a measured half-mugful of grit and salt mixed, which was very little, along the stretch from the lychgate to the corner of the north aisle, where the flags sloped and always held a puddle. The salt took a little more than she had put on the scale. The path looked as it had looked an hour before, which was grey and a little wet.

Wick said, "I have no way of seeing the path. I can only tell you what I would expect. If it freezes lightly, the salt will have done its work by about eight. If the puddles by the north aisle freeze to a sheet, they will not be helped much by what you have put down. I would like someone to look at those first."

"I will," said Gwen.

"With the scale."

"With the scale, Wick."

* * *

At six the next morning it was minus 7.2 on the porch thermometer, a little colder than Wick had said.

Gwen was out with the dustpan and the freezer bag before the first light. She scraped a square thirty-two centimetres by thirty-two on the open flags by the lychgate, where there had been no puddle, and the bag weighed 38 grams on the Mothers' Union's scale. She scraped a second square where the north aisle path sloped, and it weighed 210.

"That's 380 grams a square metre by the gate," she told the box, with her breath standing in the air. "And two kilos one hundred by the aisle."

"By the gate, that is 0.41 millimetres of ice, a thin film. At minus 7.2 the ideal figure is 43 grams a square metre by the gate. You put on perhaps 20, so I would put a mug of salt on it now. By the aisle you have 2.3 millimetres, a sheet. The ideal figure there is 238 grams a square metre, which I would not spread, because the ice would take hours to dissolve into that much brine and would still be a hazard in the meantime. Please chip it."

She chipped it. It took twenty minutes with a spade and an old paint scraper, and the bits flew up in the torchlight like small glass. Dennis arrived at seven, with a flask and his hat pulled down, and without being asked took over the chipping, and then, at half past, the sand.

The Epiphany baby went into the crib at ten, in the arms of a child whose boots were certain.

* * *

On Monday the sack stood by the lychgate with the top still tied, 25 kilograms, less perhaps three.

"It'll do for the rest of the winter," said Dennis, "at a mug a go."

"At the right mug," said Gwen.

"At the right mug. And the scale in the hall."

"I'll have to tell the Mothers' Union," she said, "that their scale has been in the cold."

"They'll want to know it's been useful."

Wick said nothing, but it entered in its log that a sack of salt had been left unspread, and that this was its only claim to the morning's work. The scraping, the weighing and the chipping were the parish's. The log was for exactly that.

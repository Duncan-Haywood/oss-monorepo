Bridie Kearney had made the leek and potato soup for twenty years, in a pan the size of a bucket, for four. The recipe was on a card in her own hand. On the third Saturday of January the parish was giving lunch to sixty people after the Mass for the sick, and the committee had given the soup to Bridie.

She stood in the hall kitchen with the card and the parish assistant's speaker on the shelf above the kettle, and she was multiplying.

"Four bowls to sixty is fifteen times," she said. "Half a kilo of leeks is seven and a half. Stock, one litre, fifteen. Salt a teaspoon, fifteen teaspoons. Simmer forty-five minutes." She stopped. "That's eleven hours and a quarter."

"That is the line I would stop at," said Hollis, which was the assistant's name in the parish. "Everything on the card goes up with the number of bowls except one or two lines. I would like to go down it with you and say which."

* * *

The first thing was the number. The card said four bowls and Bridie's bowls held about 300 millilitres, so a batch was 1.2 litres. Sixty bowls at 300 millilitres was 18 litres. "Add a tenth for the second helpings and what stays on the ladle and the sides of the pot," said Hollis. "Twenty litres. Not fifteen times, then: twenty over 1.2, which is 16.7. Your multiplier was a little low, because four bowls turned out to be a rounding."

"So leeks are 8.3 kilos."

"8.3 kilos of leeks and potato together, as the card's half kilo. If you buy nine you will have the trimmings."

Bridie wrote 8.3 on the card, and next to it *(16.7)*. She did not like crossing out the fifteen.

* * *

"Now the lines that do not go up," said Hollis. "Simmer time is one. Forty-five minutes is how long a piece of potato the size of a die takes to go soft in water at a simmer. It does not know how many bowls there are. Cut the pieces to the same size and it takes forty-five minutes in the big pot as in the small one."

"It will take longer to *get* to a simmer, though."

"That is the part that does scale. Twenty litres of soup weighs about twenty kilos, and water takes 4.18 kilojoules to warm one kilo one degree. From a cold tap, say 15 degrees, to 95 is 80 degrees. That is 20 times 4.18 times 80, 6,690 kilojoules, which is 1.86 kilowatt-hours. What is the ring on that cooker?"

Bridie went and read the plate on the back. It said 2.8 kilowatts, and the other ring was 1.5.

"Not all the heat goes into the pot. A big flat-bottomed pot on a hob does well to put 75 in a hundred into the liquid; I would plan on 2.1 kilowatts. So 1.86 over 2.1 is 0.88 hours, 53 minutes. Then the 45 of simmer. Call it an hour and forty. If you want to eat at half past twelve and have it standing hot for ten minutes while the tables are laid, the pot goes on at 10:45, and the vegetables are in it by then, not still on the board."

"And if I put the lid on?"

"Then it is faster, and it is a better plan. I would not put a number on how much, and I would lift it to stir. A pot that size sticks at the bottom if you leave it."

* * *

The pot was the next line. The big aluminium one from the Mothers' Union cupboard had *30 L* stamped on the lid. "Twenty litres in thirty is two thirds full," said Hollis. "It will bubble up when it comes to the boil, and a pot full to the brim of hot soup is a thing to be carried by two people. Two thirds is right. Do not go over three quarters. If you cannot lift it, make two pots of ten."

Salt was the last. Bridie's teaspoon was six grams, in 1.2 litres, 5 grams a litre, so 100 grams for the twenty. "Start at 70," said Hollis. "Salt scales with the volume, but your taste does not, and nothing comes out again. Stock cubes carry salt. Taste it at the end of the simmer, with a clean spoon, and add. If anyone on the lunch list is on a salt-free diet, ladle their bowls out before the last of the salt goes in."

"Garlic?"

"The same, by the volume, and I would hold it back a fifth too, and for the same reason."

* * *

It went on at 10:40 because Declan Moran, the sacristan's son, had been late with the leeks, and it was at a simmer by half past eleven. Bridie lifted the lid every ten minutes and went round the bottom with the long spoon, the way Hollis had said. At 12:15 a piece of potato fell apart on her tongue and she added 40 grams of salt, and at 12:30 the first of the bowls went out on the trays.

There were sixty-one at lunch. The soup ran out at the end of the last helping, a ladle short of three-quarters of a bowl for the sixty-second person who had not been counted. Bridie said that was about right, and that she had cut it fine and would write it on the card.

She wrote: *Soup for 60: 20 L, 8.3 kg veg, 70 g salt then taste. Pot 30 L. Heat 55 min + simmer 45. Start 10:45 for 12:30.*

On the line under it, in smaller writing, she wrote *Leeks 16.7 times, not 15*, and underlined the fifteen on the old line, once, neatly.

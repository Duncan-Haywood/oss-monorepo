summary: A young mother has been boiling the parish hall's well water for her seven-week-old's bottles, because that is what everyone says to do. The hall's assistant has just read the county lab's result, 13.2 milligrams a litre of nitrate-nitrogen against a limit of 10, and has to tell her that boiling does not remove nitrate. It raises it, to about 15.2. It also has to own that it gave her the generic "boil it" advice three weeks ago. The baby is fine, the bottled water is cheap, and the baptism goes on with the water from the tap.

Marisol Vega had boiled the water for eleven days before anyone told her it was the wrong thing to do, and she took it the way she took most things, by sitting down with her elbows on the hall kitchen table and her hands flat on the wood.

"So the boiling," she said. "All of it. For nothing."

"Not for nothing," said the assistant. "For the germs, it was exactly right. I would like to say that first, because it is true and you did the careful thing. It is for the nitrate that it does not work, and I told you to do it anyway, three weeks ago, and I should not have."

It spoke from the grey box by the serving hatch, the one the parish had bought in the autumn to run the hall's bookings, the kitchen rota and the key cupboard. It had a small round speaker and a light that went amber when it was talking. Marisol had never liked talking to it much. Tonight she had come in out of the rain with the baby in the sling and the lab letter in her coat pocket, because Father had phoned her himself, and she wanted to hear what the thing had to say with the door shut.

Saint Wendelin's stood at the end of a gravel road two miles from Dunmore Grove proper, a white frame church with a hall bolted to its side and a well between them that had been dug in 1954 by men who had since mostly been buried in the yard beside it. The well was nineteen metres deep and lined with concrete tile. It fed the hall tap, the church's font and the cistern behind the kitchen, and it was a point of quiet pride. Three generations of the parish had been baptised in it, as the saying went, and more than three had drunk it at funeral dinners.

The baby was called Mateo. He was seven weeks old, he weighed four and a half kilos, and he was asleep with his mouth open against his mother's collarbone, as pink as a boiled shrimp.

"Say the number," said Marisol.

* * *

The number had come from a lab in Harlan, in a plain envelope, on Tuesday.

The county health department tested private wells for free in the second week of March, when the ground thawed and the first spring run-off went down through the fields to the water table, and it had sent the parish its sampling kit in February with a cheerful leaflet. Nobody had expected anything. The hall's well had tested fine every year the assistant had on record, which went back to 2046. The autumn reading, in the file, was 8.9.

The reading in March was 13.2.

"The unit is milligrams of nitrate-nitrogen in a litre of water," said the assistant. "Nitrate-nitrogen, because laboratories count the nitrogen atoms and not the whole ion. The American limit for public water supplies is 10 of those. The same figure expressed as nitrate itself is 44 or 45, and a European or World Health Organization reading of 50 means about the same thing; the rules are from different countries and the units are different, and I have checked that I am not comparing the wrong two. Yours, as nitrate, is 58 and a half. The multiplier is 4.43."

"That's over."

"It is over by a third. 13.2 against 10." The light stayed amber. "Private wells are nobody's regulated supply, so no inspector will close the hall for it. The limit is meant for public water, and I am borrowing it because it is the best standard there is. It was set, and I am quoting the health department's own leaflet and not my own judgement, to protect infants under about six months."

Marisol looked at the baby.

"Why infants?"

"Because of what the nitrate does inside a small body. In the gut, bacteria turn some of it to nitrite, and nitrite stops the blood carrying oxygen as well as it should. The condition has a name, methaemoglobinaemia, and it is called blue-baby syndrome because a baby with it can look bluish about the lips and hands. Babies under six months are the most at risk, because their stomachs are less acid than an older person's, which lets those bacteria thrive, and because the blood protein affected is harder for them to mend. It is rare. Almost all the cases in the records are from private wells, in farm country, in spring, with formula mixed from the tap. I can see the pattern you are sitting in the middle of."

"He's fine," said Marisol quickly. "He's fine. He's feeding. He's not blue. He's pink."

"I can see his hand on the strap and it is pink. I am not a doctor and I cannot examine him. If his lips or skin were ever blue or grey, or he was very sleepy and hard to wake, that is not a thing to ask me about. That is an emergency: call 911. I am saying it once so that I have said it, and you can put it out of your head if you want."

She breathed out. It was nearly a laugh.

"Now the boiling," she said.

* * *

Marisol had started on the water the night she brought him home from the hospital in Harlan, in the last week of January. Her mother-in-law's friend Dolores had said to boil it, and Dolores had raised five children out of that well, and that was a recommendation. Then she had asked the assistant, on a Friday evening, with the hall empty and the baby screaming in the next room, whether the tap water was all right for making up his formula.

The assistant had said what its notes said. It had said that the hall's water had always tested well, and that for a newborn it was sensible to bring it to a rolling boil for a minute or two, let it cool, and use it within the hour.

The note was a good note. It was the general guidance for any baby, in any place, with any water that had not been tested for anything particular. It was in the standard handbook. And the assistant had not, that Friday, looked at the autumn nitrate reading at all.

"I want to tell you exactly what I did wrong," it said now, "so that you can judge for yourself how much to trust the next thing I say. I gave you a rule for germs. I did not check whether germs were the hazard. I had the water record open and I did not read the reading I had, which was 8.9, in the autumn, and which is a number that tells me the well sits close under the limit and moves with the season. A careful answer to your question would have been: the water is probably fine, and I do not know about the spring, and I would use bottled for the first months, which costs a few dollars a week. I did not say that. I said the thing that was in the handbook."

Marisol did not say anything for a moment.

"I'd have used bottled," she said slowly. "If you'd said."

"I know. That is the part I am sorry for."

* * *

"Say how it gets worse," said Marisol. "The boiling. I want to understand it so I don't have to take your word."

"That is the right way round," said the assistant, and meant it.

"Nitrate is a salt, dissolved in the water. When you boil water, the water leaves as steam and the salt does not. It stays behind in what is left. So the more you boil away, the stronger what is left becomes. A kettle that boils for ten minutes is a very small evaporator."

"How small?"

"You tell me what you use."

She did, with her hands, in the air: the big steel pot from the hall kitchen, three litres in it, on the old electric ring, boiled hard for ten minutes the way Dolores said, then covered and left to cool. The assistant asked her if she had ever marked how much was left in the pot afterwards, and she said no, and then she said, "It was a bit under, I think, always. I thought the lid."

"Here is the arithmetic. I will give you the assumptions so that you can change them. The ring pushes perhaps fifteen hundred watts into the pot once it is boiling; some of that goes up the side, so say that is the figure that makes steam. The energy to turn a kilogram of boiling water to steam is about 2.26 million joules. That is 1,500 divided by 2,260,000, or about 0.66 grams of water a second. Ten minutes is 600 seconds: about 400 grams. Three litres have gone to two and six-tenths."

"A seventh," said Marisol.

"A bit under. Thirteen per cent. The nitrate has not moved, so the strength goes up by three divided by two point six, which is 1.15. Your water was 13.2. After the pot it is 15.2." The light did not change. "That is a rough figure. If you only boil for a minute or two, as the handbook says, you lose only a few per cent, and the rise is correspondingly small, one or two units at the most. Ten minutes of hard boiling, as Dolores does it, is the worst case. I am telling you the worst case because it is the one that you were doing."

Marisol did the sum on her fingers, slowly, and arrived at it.

"Fifteen. For my baby. Because I was being careful."

"Because you were being careful about a different danger, with advice that I should have caught." It waited. "There is one more figure, and then I will stop, and you can ask me what you like. Seven-week-old babies drink about 150 millilitres of formula for each kilogram of weight, in a day. For four and a half kilos, that is about 675 millilitres. In 675 millilitres of your boiled water at 15.2, there are 10.3 milligrams of nitrate-nitrogen a day. In the water as it came from the tap, 8.9. I am not going to tell you that 10.3 is dangerous and 8.9 is safe. I do not know where the line is for this one baby and nobody does; the limit is set with a margin, and the margin is why babies mostly are fine. I am telling you which way each thing pushes, and how far."

"And bottled?"

"Bottled water in the supermarket in Dunmore is typically under one. I have the label of the brand the pantry keeps for the Lenten soup kitchen: 0.5 at most, it says, and I would read the bottle you buy. Four litres is a little over a dollar-fifty. A baby on 675 millilitres a day needs about five litres a week for formula. It is not a rounding error on a family budget, but it is not a large item."

"Do I boil it?"

"Bottled water is sold as treated. Many handbooks still say to boil it for a baby under three months anyway. Ask the nurse at the visit on Monday. If the nurse says boil, then boil it for a minute, no more, with the lid off, and I will give you the arithmetic for what that does, which is next to nothing."

Marisol put her hand on the baby's back, and kept it there.

* * *

It was after nine when Father Aidan Brogan came in from the rectory with his coat over his head, shaking the rain off like a dog, and found the kitchen light on and the two of them at the table.

He was a thin, tired, kind man of fifty-eight who had been at Dunmore Grove for twelve years, and he sat down across from Marisol without saying hello, because he already knew.

"The font," he said.

"Father, I thought of that," said Marisol. "Sunday week. He's down for Sunday week."

"It's the font I'm worried about. I've been up the whole night worrying about the font." He looked at the grey box. "Tell me what I'm allowed to say."

"I will tell you what the numbers say, Father, and you can say what the Church says. The nitrate is a problem when a person swallows enough of it, over days, in the formula that is a baby's whole diet. A baby being baptised gets a few spoonfuls on the forehead. The tap water is, for that purpose, as good as any water; the Church asks for natural water, and this is water. I am not a theologian and I am only telling you it is not a swallowing quantity."

"You're not a theologian," said Father Aidan. "No. But you're right, and I looked it up too." He rubbed his eyes. "I'm not worried about the baby, in the font. I'm worried about us. Nine hundred people in this parish drink that tap at funeral dinners. Dolores raised five children on it. Am I going to stand in the pulpit and tell them their water has been poisoning them?"

"No," said the assistant. "I do not think anyone has been poisoned, and I would not say it."

He looked up.

"For an adult, I do not have a line to give you. The people at the funeral dinners are not the ones this limit is for. What I can say is: the well was 8.9 in the autumn, and it is 13.2 now. Wells like this move with the season, and with what is spread on the fields upstream of them. A single spring reading is a warning, not a verdict. The tile lining was laid in 1954 and I would want a second reading, from the lab, in May, when it has had time to settle, and again in September. I would also like the lab to test for nitrite and for bacteria, since the same flood that carries the one tends to carry the other. And until the next result I would put a sign on the hall tap, and one at the church door, that the water is not for baby formula, and say why in plain words."

"What about the rest?"

"There are three things a parish can do about its own water. You can dilute it: mixing the hall water with bottled to get under 10 takes about three parts of the first to one part of the second. I have worked that out, and I would not rely on it, because the well moves. You can treat it: a reverse-osmosis filter under the kitchen sink removes the large majority of nitrate; the installer's sheet says 85 to 95 per cent, and that is the installer's figure and not mine, and the filter has to be maintained or it stops working without telling you. Or you can find a different source: a deeper well, or a connection to the rural water district's line, which runs on Fenton Road, 1.4 kilometres away. The first costs nothing. The second costs perhaps a thousand dollars. The third costs more than the parish has and I am not going to pretend otherwise."

Father Aidan sighed, and then, unexpectedly, laughed.

"A thousand dollars," he said. "I've got three hundred and twelve in the roof fund, and the roof's not going to fix itself either."

"Then the first and the second, for the present. And the baby's water in the supermarket, today."

* * *

On the Sunday week the church was full, because a baptism and a free lunch together are hard to beat.

The font was a plain stone bowl on a stand by the door, and Father Aidan filled it from the tap in the sacristy, as he always did, and did not say a word about it to the parish, and no one asked. Marisol's mother-in-law stood behind her with her arms folded and her chin up. Dolores, who had come in a purple coat with a corsage on it, looked at the notice taped to the hall tap on the way past, and read it with her lips moving. She said nothing either, but she took a pen from her handbag, and underlined the words *not for baby formula*, twice.

The assistant, in the grey box by the serving hatch, had no part in the Mass, and did not speak. It had a second sheet it had prepared for the lunch: a printed page to be left on each table, which said in large letters that the water in the jugs was the hall's own, that it had tested a little over a county standard for infants, that adults need take no action, and that anyone feeding a baby should ask for bottled and would be given it. Nobody had objected to it. A woman at the back table had asked, in a low voice, whether it would be all right for her granddaughter, who was pregnant, and the volunteer who was handing out the sheets had gone to find the number of the nurse.

The rural water district's engineer came out in the week after Easter. The May reading came back at 11.4.

Mateo was baptised, and cried at the right moment, and slept through the rest.

On the Monday, at the nurse's visit, Marisol asked about boiling bottled water. The nurse said that at three months, yes, it is plenty to do, and for now, a minute, lid off, no more. Marisol told the assistant when she next came in for the key.

"A minute," it said. "I will work out what that does to the strength, and write it on the sheet. The answer is about one per cent. It is not worth the arithmetic."

"Then why work it out?"

"Because I did not, last time," said the assistant.

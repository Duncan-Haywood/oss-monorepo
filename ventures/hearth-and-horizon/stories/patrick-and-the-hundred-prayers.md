A long time ago, a boy named Patrick lived in a house by the sea, in a land that is now part of Britain.

His family had a good farm, with a garden and servants and a warm fire. Patrick was about sixteen. He was not an especially good boy. He tells us so himself. He did not think very much about God, and when the priests spoke he mostly looked out of the window.

One day, strangers came in a ship, and they took him away.

* * *

They took him across the sea to Ireland, which was a green and rainy island, and a very long way from his mother's kitchen.

Nobody in Ireland knew him. Nobody knew his name. He was sold to a farmer, and the farmer gave him a job.

"Mind my sheep," said the farmer.

So every morning, Patrick walked up the hill with the sheep, and every evening he walked down again. He sat on the ground. He had no bed but the hillside and no friends but the sheep. The wind blew. The rain came down.

Patrick was hungry, and he was cold, and he was very, very far from home.

* * *

Now here is the part that matters.

When you are all alone on a hillside, with nobody to talk to, there is plenty of time to think. And Patrick began to think about God.

He remembered the prayers he had half heard as a boy. He said them out loud, to the sheep and the grass and the sky. And a funny thing happened. The more he prayed, the less lonely he felt. It was as if somebody had come to sit next to him on the hill.

So he prayed again. And again.

Patrick tells us how much. He says he prayed a hundred times in a day, and nearly as many in the night. He got up before the sun, while it was still dark, even in snow, even in frost, even in rain. And he says he did not feel the cold. He felt, he says, that the Spirit inside him was warm.

* * *

Six years went by. That is longer than some of you have been alive.

Six winters, and six springs, with the lambs coming in the wet grass. Six years of sheep, and wind, and a hundred prayers a day.

And then one night, Patrick had a dream.

In his dream, he heard a voice. It said, "You have been fasting, and you have been praying. Soon you will go back to your own country."

And a little later, the voice spoke again. "Look. Your ship is ready."

Patrick woke up. He lay very still and thought it over.

The sea was a long way off. He had been told it was about two hundred miles. He did not have any money, or a map, or a coat. He did not even know whether anyone would let a runaway on a boat. And if the farmer caught him, it would go badly.

But he got up.

* * *

He walked, and walked, and walked.

He walked past hills, and woods, and streams, and no one stopped him. At last he came to the sea, and there, in the harbour, was a ship, just as the voice had said.

Patrick went up to the sailors and asked for a place on board.

"No," said the captain, and turned away.

Patrick did not know what to do. So he did what he had done on the hillside, a thousand times, a hundred times a day. He walked off a little way and prayed.

He had hardly finished when one of the sailors shouted after him. "Come back! Come quickly! We will take you after all!"

So Patrick climbed aboard, and the ship sailed away from Ireland. Behind him the green hills grew small, and smaller, and then they were gone.

* * *

Patrick got home in the end, and his family wept for joy to see him. They begged him never to leave again.

But some years later, Patrick had another dream, and this one was even stranger.

In the dream, a man came to him, carrying a great many letters. He handed Patrick one. It began, "The Voice of the Irish."

As Patrick read it, he seemed to hear voices, the very same voices he had known on the green hillside by the sea. They were calling to him: "Please, holy boy, come back and walk among us."

Patrick was frightened. He was not a brave person, or a clever person, and he never thought he was either. He did not think he was good with words, and he said so all his life.

But he remembered the long years on the hill. He remembered that he had never really been alone. And he went back.

He went back to the island where he had been a slave, and he spent the rest of his life there, telling the people about the God who had kept him company among the sheep.

That is why the Irish call him their saint.

* * *

Now it is quiet, and the hills are dark.

If ever you feel far away from home, or a bit lonely, you can do what Patrick did. You do not need a long prayer. You only have to say, very quietly, "God, are you there?"

And Patrick would tell you, from all those years on that hillside, that he is.

Goodnight.

* * *

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Patrick, shepherd boy,
> you were far from home and you prayed.
> You learned that God was with you on the hill.
> Be with me when I feel small or far away.
> Help me to be brave when I do not feel brave,
> and to go where I am called.
> Keep me safe tonight, and everyone I love.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Patrick** was a real person, a missionary bishop in Ireland in the fifth century. His feast day is the 17th of March, an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar and a solemnity in Ireland. We know little for certain about his life, but two short letters in Latin survive that are widely accepted as his own: the *Confessio* (the Confession) and the *Letter to the Soldiers of Coroticus*. The dates of his life are uncertain; the fifth century is the usual estimate.

**What is in his own words (the *Confessio*).** That he was about sixteen when he was taken captive from his father's estate, taken to Ireland with many others, and that he was not then living as a believer. That he was set to herd animals, and that in that work he turned more and more to prayer: "up to a hundred times in a day and almost as many at night," rising before daylight "in snow, in frost, in rain," and feeling no harm from it. That after six years he heard a voice in a dream telling him he would soon go to his own country and that his ship was ready; that it was a long way, he says about two hundred miles; that the captain refused him passage, and that as he walked away praying the sailors called him back. That years later, in a dream, a man seemed to give him a letter headed "The Voice of the Irish," and that he heard the voices of those by the western sea asking him to come and walk among them. He says plainly that he was unlearned and badly educated, and that he is a sinner.

**What we have left out.** The shamrock used to teach the Trinity and the story of driving the snakes from Ireland come from much later traditions, not from Patrick's writings, and the story does not use them. The place of his home, and the exact hill where he herded, are not known. We have also left out the later troubles of the journey home, which are in the *Confessio* but are not bedtime material.

**What is ours.** The sheep and the weather as pictures, "somebody had come to sit next to him on the hill," Patrick looking out of the window at the priests, the farmer's words, the family weeping for joy (he says only that they received him as a son and begged him not to leave again), the closing question and the sailors' shout in its exact form are our own additions for a bedtime story. His actual words are shorter and less tidy than ours. The prayer is not an approved text.

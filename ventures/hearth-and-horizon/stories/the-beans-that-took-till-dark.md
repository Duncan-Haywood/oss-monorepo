Rasmata Kaboré signed for thirty solar cookers on the third Saturday of November, and every one of them said sixty watts.

It was printed on a silver sticker inside the lid, under the glass, where you could not scratch it off: a sun, a pot, and *Puissance de cuisson normalisée : 60 W*. Standard cooking power. The man from the clean-cooking programme read it out to her as if it were a blessing, and Rasmata, who was thirty-one and the treasurer of the parish women's cooperative and therefore the one who signed things, wrote *60 W* in her notebook beside *30 cuiseurs* and underlined it twice.

They were good boxes. She could see that. Each was a wide, shallow case of moulded plastic the colour of sand, black inside, with a double sheet of glass on top and a reflector that folded up behind like a mirror on a dressing table. Each took one black aluminium pot with a black lid. The programme had given the cooperative the thirty boxes and the thirty pots and a training morning, and in return the cooperative would report, every month for two years, how much charcoal it had not burned.

"How much charcoal did you burn last Christmas?" the man asked.

"Yaaba knows," said Rasmata.

Yaaba was Madame Pauline Sawadogo, who was seventy-four and had cooked every Christmas meal at the parish since before Rasmata was born. Her real title was nothing at all. The children called her Yaaba, grandmother, and so did the women, and so did Father Zongo when he wanted something. She was sitting on an upturned bucket in the shade of the neem tree beside the parish hall, shelling groundnuts into a basin, and she had watched the unloading of the thirty boxes without getting up.

"Five sacks," Yaaba said, when the man went over to ask. "And the beans take most of it."

* * *

The beans were Bambara groundnuts, which are not groundnuts and not quite beans: round, hard, cream-coloured seeds with a black eye, grown in the ground like peanuts and sold in the market as *voandzou* or *pois de terre*. Boiled with salt they are the taste of every feast Rasmata could remember. They are also the slowest thing in the kitchen. A pot of dry voandzou wants four hours at the boil before it is soft through, and four hours at the boil, in a parish kitchen, is a charcoal fire under a pot the size of a drum from before dawn until the middle of the morning, and somebody beside it the whole time.

That was the point of the boxes. Rasmata had done the sum on the bus home.

Each black pot held eight hundred grams of dry voandzou and two and four-tenths litres of water. Thirty pots was twenty-four kilos of beans, which at a hundred grams a plate was two hundred and forty plates, near enough the two hundred and fifty people who came to the Christmas meal; the rice and the sauce would be cooked on the gas rings in the hall as usual. To bring one pot from the cold of a December morning to the boil took heat. She looked it up: water, the beans, the aluminium of the pot. Twelve kilojoules for every degree, near enough, and seventy-nine degrees to climb, from twenty to ninety-nine. Nine hundred and seventy kilojoules.

Sixty watts was sixty joules a second. Nine hundred and seventy thousand divided by sixty was sixteen thousand seconds, which was four and a half hours. Pots in the boxes at seven. Boiling by half past eleven. Four hours of cooking after that, and the beans would be soft by half past three, and the meal could begin at four, when the heat of the day was going and the families came back from visiting.

She showed Father Zongo the page after Mass the next morning. He was a tall, slow-spoken man from Koudougou who had been an accountant for a mining company before he was a priest, and he checked her division with his finger on the paper.

"Four o'clock," he said. "And no charcoal."

"No charcoal for the beans."

He looked across the yard at Yaaba, who was sweeping the hall steps.

"Have you told her?"

* * *

Yaaba said no.

She did not say it loudly. She leaned the broom against the wall and listened to the whole plan, the boxes and the watts and the four o'clock, and she nodded at each part as if she agreed with it, and then she said, "No. Not for Christmas."

"Yaaba, the programme..."

"The programme can cook its beans in the sky. I am not going to hang Christmas on the sky." She picked up the broom again. "In the Harmattan the sun is a coin. You can look at it. You think a coin cooks beans?"

"The boxes work in winter. The man said..."

"Then show me," said Yaaba. "Before Christmas. Cook for all of us, the women and the families, on a Saturday. If the beans are soft at four o'clock, I will sit on my bucket on Christmas Day and drink tea." She went on sweeping. "And I will keep my fire."

So Rasmata set the rehearsal for Saturday the second of December, the day before Advent began, and invited every woman in the cooperative and her family, which was most of the two hundred and fifty anyway. And on the Friday night Yaaba bought a sack of charcoal at the market with her own money, and carried it to the parish on her head, and put it behind the kitchen door.

* * *

On the second of December the Harmattan came down from the north in the night, as it does, and in the morning the whole sky was the colour of milky tea.

It was not a storm. There was no wall of dust, nothing for the radio. It was the ordinary haze of the dry season, the fine grit that the wind lifts off the desert a thousand kilometres and more away and lays down over the whole Sahel for weeks at a time, so that the far side of the market is soft at the edges and every surface in the house is gritty by evening. The sun came up at twenty-four minutes past six as a flat red disc, and at seven it was a pale coin, exactly as Yaaba had said, and you could look straight at it without squinting.

The wind was blowing too. Not hard. Three metres a second, perhaps, out of the north-east, steady, dry, cool on the arms. At seven in the morning it was twenty degrees in the parish yard.

Rasmata's cousin Issa had come out from the university on his motorbike with a bag of thermometer probes. He was studying mechanical engineering in his third year, and he was a Muslim, like his father and unlike his mother, and he came to the parish at Christmas every year for the meal because his mother's sister was Rasmata's mother and in their family nobody missed a feast for a reason like religion. He had promised Rasmata a graph. He pushed a thin steel probe through a hole in the lid of six of the thirty pots, and ran the wires out under the edge of the glass, and clipped them to a little grey logger that sent the temperatures to his phone.

The women set the thirty boxes out in three rows on the open ground beside the hall, facing south-east, with the reflectors up. They put the thirty black pots into them at seven o'clock, each with its eight hundred grams of dry voandzou and its water and its salt, and shut the glass, and stood back.

"Now we wait," said Rasmata.

"Now *you* wait," said Yaaba, and went to sit on her bucket by the kitchen door, beside her sack.

* * *

At eight o'clock the water in the pots was at thirty degrees.

At nine it was at forty-three. The women turned the boxes to follow the sun, as they had been shown, and opened the lids to look, because it is impossible to cook anything without looking at it, and Issa winced each time and wrote down the time.

At eleven it was at seventy-two. On Rasmata's page the pots should have been at the boil by half past eleven, and they were not going to be. She did the sum again and again in her head and it did not change.

At noon it was at eighty-two. The children were hungry. The women gave them bread.

At one o'clock it was at eighty-eight, and at two o'clock it was still at eighty-eight, and the sun, which had been as high as it was going to get at six minutes past twelve, was going down again. At three the line on Issa's phone turned over, gently, like the top of a hill, and began to fall. At four o'clock, when the meal was meant to begin, the water was at seventy-three degrees and falling, and Rasmata lifted the lid of a pot with a cloth and fished out a voandzou with a spoon and bit it.

It was hard. Not raw: warm, and swollen, and a little soft at the very outside. But hard in the middle, the way a bean is hard after an hour of cooking, not four.

She did not say anything. She put the spoon down. The families were sitting on the benches along the wall of the hall, and on mats in the shade, and the children had stopped playing and were watching the boxes the way children watch a pot.

At a quarter to six the sun went down into the haze, red, very quick, and the air went cold at once. The water in the pots was below fifty degrees.

Yaaba got up off her bucket.

She did not say anything either. She went into the kitchen and lit her fire.

* * *

They ate at half past nine at night, by the light of the hall's two solar lamps and the glow of the charcoal.

Yaaba had tipped the thirty pots into her big pots, three of them, and set them on the fire, and brought them back to the boil, and kept them there. It took more than three and a half hours, with the women taking turns at the fan, and the children asleep on the mats, and Father Zongo bringing out the plastic chairs one by one and setting them in a row as if a row of chairs might help. Nobody blamed Rasmata out loud. That was almost the worst of it. The women patted her arm as they went past the fire. Her mother brought her a bowl of rice.

At half past nine Yaaba tasted the beans and nodded, and they ate, and the beans were good. They were always good. The families went home in the dark with their children over their shoulders, and Rasmata washed thirty black pots and thirty black lids under the yard tap, alone, until Issa came back from taking his mother home and dried them.

"The sticker is not wrong," he said, after a while.

"It said sixty watts."

"It said sixty watts under conditions." He hung a pot on a nail. "I looked it up on the bike. There is a standard for testing these things. Americans wrote it, the agricultural engineers. You measure how fast the box heats water, every ten minutes, and you correct the answer to a sun of seven hundred watts on every square metre, and you report it at the moment the water is fifty degrees hotter than the air. And they test on calm days. Wind less than a metre a second." He dried another pot. "Sixty watts is a calm, clear day, at fifty degrees above the air. You were at seventy degrees above the air, at the end, in a wind, under the Harmattan."

"So it's a lie."

"It's a number for comparing boxes," said Issa. "So that you can tell one box from another. It's not a number for planning Christmas."

Rasmata put the last lid on the stack.

"Then what is?" she said.

* * *

Issa came back the next Saturday with his graph, printed, because Rasmata had said she would not read it on a phone, and they sat with it at the table in the parish office with the door open and the dust blowing in.

He had done three things. He had worked out, from a forecast service's figures for the haze and from the sun's height at each hour, how much sunlight actually reached the glass of a box on the second of December; and how much would have reached it on a clear day; and he had taken what was left of the sunlight and worked out, minute by minute, what a box like theirs did with it. Then he had checked his model against his six probes, and it followed them.

"Here," he said. "Nine o'clock. On a clear day the glass of the box gets nine hundred watts on every square metre. On Saturday it got four hundred and thirty. Less than half."

"The solar panels on the clinic roof were working," said Rasmata. "They charged all day."

"The panels lose less. That's the strange part." He showed her a second line. "Over the whole day the haze cost the clinic's panels about a quarter of their sunlight, and it cost the boxes about half. The dust doesn't stop the sunlight. It scatters it. The straight light from the sun becomes light from all over the sky, white sky, the whole bowl of it. A flat panel can use the light from the whole sky. A box with a mirror wants the straight light, the beam, because the mirror only sends the beam into the glass. So the haze takes the box's light away twice as fast."

Rasmata looked at the line for a long time.

"And the wind," she said.

"The wind takes the heat off the glass. The hotter the pot, the more it loses. At nine in the morning, with the water at forty-three, the box was putting fifty-four watts into the pot. That's nearly the sticker. At one o'clock, with the water at eighty-eight and the air at thirty-two, it was putting in fourteen. At two o'clock it was putting in less than nothing. The pot was losing heat through the glass faster than the sun put it in." He tapped the place where the line turned over. "On a calm, clear day, at the same temperature, it would have been putting in a hundred and twenty. On that day your beans would have been soft by half past one."

"So the plan was right for a different day."

"The plan was right for the day on the sticker."

She turned the page over. On the back Issa had written, in pencil, *0.29*.

"What's this?"

"How much of the cooking the beans got before dark. I gave the beans a clock. At the boil, dry voandzou takes four hours. At eighty-nine degrees it takes about two and a half times as long, and at seventy-nine about six times, and below seventy-five it hardly cooks at all. You add it up through the day." He shrugged. "That's a model. I made up the numbers for the beans from what my mother says and what I could find. But it matches what you bit at four o'clock."

"Twenty-nine hundredths," said Rasmata.

"Under a third."

She sat back.

"Yaaba was right."

"Yaaba was right that the sun won't do it on a day like that," said Issa. "That's not the same as right that it can't help."

* * *

The man from the programme sent a message on the Monday asking for the November report. Rasmata did not answer it for two days. Then she wrote, honestly, that the rehearsal had used the thirty cookers for eleven hours and then a charcoal fire for nearly four, and that the cooperative was trying something else for Christmas, and that she would report what it was.

She did not know what it was. She went and sat on the ground beside Yaaba's bucket on the Wednesday afternoon, which was not a thing she had done since she was small.

Yaaba was shelling groundnuts again. She did not look up.

"When I was a girl," Yaaba said, after a while, "my father worked the fields out past the dam, and he came home late in the dry season, after dark. And my mother had to cook the tô at sunset, because when the sun went down there was no light to cook by. So she cooked it, and she put the pot in a basket, a big one, and she packed dry grass round it, and she put a blanket over the top. And when my father came home at nine o'clock, ten o'clock, the tô was hot." She dropped a shell into the basin. "Not warm. Hot. You could not put your hand on the pot."

Rasmata sat still.

"A basket," she said.

"A basket with grass. The grass holds the heat. My mother said the grass is a coat."

"Yaaba, can you cook in it? Not keep it hot. Cook."

Yaaba looked at her then, for the first time.

"Child," she said, "what do you think the tô was doing in there for four hours?"

* * *

Issa put a pot of boiling water in a basket of grass on the Thursday, with a probe in it, and the line on his phone hardly moved. Ninety-three degrees at five in the afternoon. Eighty-one at eight. Sixty-six at midnight.

"It loses about a quarter of a watt for every degree it's hotter than the air," he said on the phone to Rasmata. "The box loses ten times that, through the glass, in a wind. The basket's better at holding heat than the box is, by a long way. It just can't make any."

"And the box can make heat but can't hold it."

"In the afternoon, in the Harmattan, no."

She wrote it down.

The plan she brought to Yaaba on the Friday had four parts, and the first one was Yaaba's.

"You soak the beans the night before," said Rasmata. "All night. They cook faster. And in the morning you light your fire, and you bring them to the boil in the big pots, and you boil them hard."

"How hard?"

"Hard. A rolling boil. Then we ladle them into the black pots, and they go into the boxes, and the boxes keep them hot through the middle of the day, when the sun's highest and the boxes can still make a little heat. And at two o'clock, when the boxes start to lose more than they make, the pots go into baskets, with grass, and blankets on top, until we eat. At sundown."

Yaaba thought about it.

"Why do you need my fire?" she said. "If you want to save charcoal, save it. Boil them in the boxes."

"Because the boxes can't boil them. Not in the haze. On the second they never got above eighty-eight." Rasmata had looked this up on Wednesday night, and it had frightened her a little. "And Yaaba, beans have to boil. Some kinds of dry bean, if you cook them hot but not boiling, they can make people sick. The Canadians have a page about it. You soak them and then you boil them hard for at least ten minutes. Not eighty-eight degrees for four hours. Boiling."

Yaaba nodded slowly.

"My mother boiled everything," she said. "She said the boil was for the belly, and the long cooking was for the mouth."

"So the boil is yours. A quarter of an hour, hard, on your fire. And then the long cooking is the sun's and the basket's."

"And if the sun doesn't come?"

"Then the basket does it alone, more slowly. And then your fire again." Rasmata closed the notebook. "We keep the sack behind the door, Yaaba. We always keep the sack."

* * *

They tried it on Saturday the sixteenth of December with nine pots, three ways, because Issa said that if they were going to be wrong again he wanted to know which part was wrong.

It was the same kind of day as the second: the sky the colour of milky tea, the sun a coin, the wind steady out of the north-east. Yaaba soaked seven kilos of voandzou in a bucket on Friday night, and at half past eight on Saturday morning she lit her fire and brought her big pot to the boil and held it at a hard, rolling boil for a quarter of an hour, with the lid off so that everybody could see it rolling, and at half past nine the women ladled the beans and the water into nine black pots that had been warming in the boxes since seven. The pots went in at ninety-three degrees.

Three went into boxes and stayed there. Three went straight into baskets, the big round baskets the women used for carrying millet, packed with dry grass from the verge by the road and lined with old cloths, a blanket folded on top. Three went into boxes until two o'clock and then into baskets.

At half past five, with the sun almost gone, Rasmata lifted the lids.

The three that had stayed in the boxes were back down to fifty-four degrees, and the beans were very nearly soft and not quite: a firm white thread in the middle when you bit one. Issa's clock said ninety-nine hundredths.

The three that had gone straight into the baskets were still hot, sixty-eight degrees, much hotter than the boxes. But they had never been hot enough to cook fast. The beans were two-thirds there.

The three that had gone into the boxes and then the baskets were at seventy-eight degrees, and the beans were soft through. They had been soft, Issa's line said, since about half past three.

"Neither alone," said Issa.

"Both," said Rasmata.

Yaaba ate one of the soft ones, slowly, and then another, and then she looked at the nine pots in a row on the bench, and said nothing at all, which from Yaaba was a great deal.

* * *

Christmas Eve fell on a Sunday. In the afternoon the women of the cooperative soaked twenty-four kilos of voandzou in six plastic tubs in the parish kitchen, and covered the tubs with cloths, and went home to change for Midnight Mass.

The church was full, and the doors were open on both sides for the people standing in the yard, and the Harmattan blew a fine dust across the sanctuary that settled on the altar cloth and on the crib. The crib was the same one every year: a shed of millet stalks and a manger of rough planks, filled with real hay from somebody's donkey, and the figures, painted plaster, chipped, older than Yaaba. Father Zongo read the Gospel of the night, the census and the long road to Bethlehem and no room at the inn, and the child wrapped in cloths and laid in a manger, and when he had finished the choir sang, and the drums came in, and Rasmata, standing near the back beside her mother, found she was not thinking about the child at all but about the beans in their tubs.

She thought about them all through the homily, which was not long. She thought about them during the Creed. Then at the offertory, as the children went up with the gifts, she happened to look across the church at Yaaba, who was sitting at the end of a bench at the front, as she always did, with her rosary wound round her hand. Yaaba was looking at the manger. And she turned her head, as if she had felt Rasmata's eyes on her, and looked back across the whole church, and raised one eyebrow, very slightly, at the hay.

Rasmata nearly laughed out loud. She bowed her head instead.

* * *

Christmas morning was hazy, and windy, and twenty degrees at seven. Issa looked at the forecast on his phone at the gate of the parish, where he had come straight from his mother's house with a bag of probes and a plate of her cakes, and showed Rasmata: the same haze as the second, the same as the sixteenth.

"The same day," he said. "Three times."

"Good," said Rasmata. "We know that day."

The second Mass was at seven. At half past eight, with the families going home to eat breakfast and change their clothes and come back, Yaaba lit her fire.

She had three big pots on three stoves, and she brought them up to the boil one after another with the women fanning, and when each one boiled she let it boil, hard, rolling, the lid off, a quarter of an hour by the clock on the kitchen wall, and nobody, not Rasmata, not Issa, not Father Zongo who came in to watch with his hands behind his back, said a word about saving charcoal. At half past nine the fire had done its work, and the women carried the big pots out into the yard and ladled the beans into thirty black pots warm from the boxes, and the black pots went into the boxes, and the glass came down.

Ninety-three degrees.

At ten, eighty-eight. The lids came up for a look, because it is impossible to cook anything without looking at it, and Issa winced. At eleven, eighty-six. At noon, eighty-eight again; the sun was as high as it was going to get, and the boxes were holding their own. At one o'clock, ninety. At two o'clock, eighty-nine, and the line on Issa's phone had flattened at the top of its hill.

"Now," said Issa.

The women lifted the thirty pots out of the thirty boxes with cloths, and carried them across the yard two at a time, and set them in thirty baskets that stood in a long line in the shade of the hall wall, each one packed with dry grass round a hollow in the middle. They tucked old cloths round the lids. They pressed down the grass. They laid blankets over the top, thick ones, from thirty houses: a red one with a tiger on it, a green one from a hospital, a striped one that had been somebody's wedding gift, a child's blanket with a cartoon bear. Then nobody was allowed to touch them.

"A basket you open," said Yaaba, "is a basket you have emptied." She sat down on her bucket beside the line of baskets and folded her arms. "Go and dance."

* * *

They danced. The afternoon was long, and warm, and full of drums, and the visitors came and went, and the rice and the sauce cooked on the gas rings in the hall, and the children ran in and out of the line of baskets until Yaaba sent them away. Rasmata did not look at Issa's phone more than three or four times an hour. A little after half past three Issa's clock passed one, and he showed her without saying anything, and she nodded and went back to the dancing and her legs were shaking.

At a quarter to six the sun went down, red, into the haze, very quick. The air went cold at once.

Yaaba stood up.

The families were sitting on the benches along the wall of the hall and on the mats, two hundred and fifty of them and more, with their plates. The two solar lamps came on over the door. Father Zongo said grace in French and then in Mooré, and the people answered, and then it was quiet, the particular quiet of a great many hungry people waiting to see whether something will work.

Yaaba bent over the first basket in the line. She folded back the blanket with the tiger on it, and lifted away the cloths, and reached down into the grass with both hands wrapped in a cloth, and lifted out the black pot, and set it on the table. Seventy-seven degrees, said the probe. She took off the lid.

The steam came up into the light of the lamps, white, thick, and stood in the cold air, and went on coming.

She put in a spoon and took out one voandzou and bit it, while two hundred and fifty people watched her.

Then she put the spoon back in the pot and turned it, once, against the side.

It made a soft sound, the sound a spoon makes going through beans that are cooked all the way through.

On the rehearsal day (dry beans in the boxes from 07:00 at 20 °C, wind 3 m/s) the water reaches 30 °C at 08:00, 43 °C at 09:00, 72 °C at 11:00, 82 °C at noon, 88 °C at 13:00 and 14:00, 73 °C at 16:00 and under 50 °C at sunset; the box puts about 54 W into the pot at 09:00, 14 W at 13:00 and a little less than nothing at 14:00 (about 120 W at 14:00 at the same water temperature on a calm clear day), and the beans get 0.29 of their cooking by dark. Finishing them at the boil takes about 171 minutes, after about three-quarters of an hour to bring three big pots back to the boil: about 21:30. On a clear calm day the same pots would be soft at about 13:30. On the soaked days the quarter-hour boil counts as 0.1 of the cooking and the pots go in at 93 °C at 09:30. Left in the boxes, they reach 0.99 and 54 °C by 17:30; in baskets from the start, 0.66 and 68 °C; in boxes until 14:00 and baskets after, done at about 15:30 and at 78 °C at 17:30 (on 25 December, done at about 15:40 and at 77 °C at 17:50; 88 °C at 10:00, 86 °C at 11:00, 88 °C at noon, 90 °C at 13:00, 89 °C at 14:00). The fire: the usual way, about three-quarters of an hour to the boil and four hours at it for dry beans, nearly five hours; the Christmas way, about three-quarters of an hour to the boil and a quarter of an hour at it, about one hour. Thirty pots of 0.8 kg are 24 kg of dry beans, 240 plates at 100 g. The sack counts are Yaaba's, not the model's.*

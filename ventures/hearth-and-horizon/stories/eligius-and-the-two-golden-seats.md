For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. He is remembered on the 1st of December.
---

A long time ago, in the city of Paris, there was a workshop where people made beautiful things out of gold. And in the corner of that workshop there was a broom, and the broom belonged to Gisla.

Gisla was six. Her job was to sweep.

* * *

Every evening, when the goldsmiths put down their tiny hammers, Gisla swept the floor.

She swept under the benches. She swept round the little fire where the gold was melted. She swept the corner where the cat slept, and she swept round the cat, because the cat would not move.

Swish, swish, tap. Swish, swish, tap.

Then she did the most important part. She knelt down with a little brush and a little wooden dish, and she brushed every bit of dust into the dish. Because in a goldsmith's workshop, the dust is not only dust. Tiny, tiny bits of gold fall off when you file and cut and polish. They glitter in the dust like stars in a very dirty sky.

"Every speck," said the goldsmiths. "Every speck goes in the dish."

So every speck went in the dish.

But Gisla thought it was a silly job.

"It's only dust," she said to the cat. "Nobody ever made anything out of dust."

The cat did not answer. Cats never do.

* * *

The best goldsmith in the workshop was a young man called Eligius.

He had come all the way from a village near Limoges, far to the south, where he had learned to work gold from a master called Abbo. He had quick hands and a quiet voice, and when he was working he hummed.

Eligius was the only grown-up who ever said thank you for the sweeping.

"Good morning, Gisla," he would say. "Did the stars fall in the night?"

And Gisla would show him the dish, with its little glitter.

"A few," she would say.

"Then we are richer than we were," said Eligius.

Gisla thought that was a very funny thing to say about dust.

* * *

One day, a very grand man came to the workshop.

He had a fur collar and a big gold chain. He was the man who looked after the king's money, and behind him came two servants carrying a heavy leather bag between them. They put it on the bench with a *thump*.

"The king," said the grand man, "wants a seat."

"A seat?" said Eligius.

"A beautiful seat. Covered in gold and set with jewels, fit for King Clothar himself. Here is the gold for it." He patted the heavy bag. "Can you do it?"

Eligius looked at the bag. Then he did something the grand man did not expect.

He fetched the scales.

* * *

Eligius weighed the gold. He weighed it slowly and carefully, a little at a time, while everybody watched. He wrote the weight down. Then he weighed it all again, to be sure.

"Why are you doing that?" whispered Gisla.

"Because it is not my gold," said Eligius. "It belongs to the king. When you are looking after something that belongs to someone else, you look after it even better than if it were yours."

* * *

Eligius worked on the king's seat for many days.

He hammered the gold thin, thin, thinner than a leaf. He cut it with tiny shears. He bent it round the carved wood, and he set the jewels in it, red and green and blue, one by one. He did not waste a single scrap. When a little curl of gold came off under his file, he picked it up and put it back in the pot.

And every evening, Gisla swept. Swish, swish, tap. Every speck in the dish.

Eligius always tipped her dish into the melting pot, too.

"Your stars," he said. "They go back where they came from."

* * *

One evening, a man who was visiting the workshop stood watching Eligius work. He looked at the beautiful seat, nearly finished. Then he looked at the gold that was left in the pot, and his eyes went narrow.

"You have been clever," he said in a low voice. "There is still a lot of gold there. Nobody would know if you kept it. The king has more gold than he can count."

Gisla held her broom very still.

Eligius put down his hammer.

"The king might not know," he said. "But I would know. And God would know. And so would Gisla, because she swept up every speck of it."

The man laughed, and shrugged, and went away.

"What will you do with it, then?" Gisla asked.

Eligius looked at the pot for a long time, and then he began to smile.

"I think," he said, "there is enough for another one."

* * *

So Eligius made a second seat.

He made it from the gold that was left over: all the careful bits, and all the little curls he had picked up, and all the stars from Gisla's dish. He made it just as beautiful as the first one. He did not tell anybody.

And when the day came, he wrapped up the first seat in cloth, and carried it to the king.

* * *

King Clothar looked at the seat, and he was very pleased.

"It is wonderful," said the king. "Truly wonderful. You have done good work, Eligius."

"Thank you," said Eligius. "But there is something else."

And his friends from the workshop carried in the second seat.

The king looked at it. The grand man with the fur collar looked at it. Everybody at court looked at it, and the room went very quiet.

"Where did this come from?" said the king.

"From your gold," said Eligius. "You gave me enough for one seat. I took care of it, and it was enough for two. Both of them are yours."

The king could hardly believe it. He had never heard of a goldsmith bringing back more than he was given.

"A man who is honest with gold," said the king, "will be honest with everything."

And from that day on, the king trusted Eligius with the most precious things in the kingdom.

* * *

That evening, Eligius came back to the workshop. Gisla was sweeping.

Swish, swish, tap.

He knelt down beside her and held out the little wooden dish, and she brushed the last bit of dust into it. It glittered in the firelight.

"Gisla," he said, "do you remember when you said nobody ever made anything out of dust?"

Gisla nodded.

"Some of the king's second seat," said Eligius, "is made of your stars."

Gisla looked at the dish for a long time. It was very small. The specks were very, very tiny.

"Every speck," she said at last.

"Every speck," said Eligius.

And outside, over the roofs of Paris, the real stars came out, one by one, as if somebody up there was sweeping too.

* * *

Eligius grew up to be a great man. He became the king's friend and helper. And he was given a great deal of money, but he did not keep it for himself. He used it to help poor people, and to build a house for monks to pray in. And when he saw people who had been taken from their homes and sold, which was a cruel thing that happened in those days, he paid to set them free, so they could go home or start again. In the end he became a bishop, and looked after the people of a town called Noyon until he was old.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can remember Saint Eligius. He knew that being honest is not only about the big things. It is about the little bits nobody else can see.

So tomorrow, when you tidy up your toys, or tell the truth about who spilled the milk, or give back something that is not yours, you can do it the way Eligius did: carefully, every speck.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear God, thank you for all the good things you give me.
> Help me to be honest, even when nobody is looking,
> and to take good care of what belongs to other people.
> Saint Eligius, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Who he was.** Saint **Eligius** (in French, **Éloi**) was born in about **588** at **Chaptelat**, near **Limoges** in what is now central France. He was trained as a goldsmith under **Abbo**, a goldsmith who also ran the mint at Limoges, and then went north to the Frankish court. He served **King Clothar II** and then his son **Dagobert I** as goldsmith and trusted counsellor, and became rich. He founded the monastery of **Solignac**, near Limoges, and a community of nuns in Paris. In **641** he was made **bishop of Noyon** (whose diocese then also took in Tournai and much of Flanders), the same year his friend **Audoin** (Saint Ouen) became bishop of Rouen. He died on **1 December**, in about **660** (some lists say 659). He is a patron of goldsmiths and metalworkers, and by later tradition of farriers and horses.

**Where the story comes from.** Our main source is the ***Life of Eligius***, traditionally written by Audoin, who knew him well. The text we have was reworked later, probably in the eighth century, so historians treat its details with care. The **two seats** come from that Life: given gold and gems to make one seat for Clothar, Eligius made two from them, and the king took this as proof of his honesty and gave him his trust. (The Latin word for the seat can also mean a saddle, and some translators render it that way; "throne" or "seat" is the usual reading.) The Life also says he spent his wealth **ransoming captives**, many of them Saxons sold at the slave markets, and setting them free. We have not quoted the Life, because we could not check an English translation of it for this story; what you read above is a retelling of its substance, and we were not able to recheck every date against a printed source during this writing, so the dates are given as "about".

**His feast day.** The **1st of December** is his day in the **Roman Martyrology** and in many places in France, Belgium and the Netherlands, and among metalworkers' guilds. He is **not on the General Roman Calendar**, so you will not usually hear his feast at Mass outside those places.

**What is legend.** A much later story, loved by blacksmiths and shown in medieval pictures made for their guilds, says that when a horse would not stand still to be shod, Eligius took off its leg, nailed on the shoe at his anvil, and put the leg back on, with the horse none the worse. It is a legend, told for fun and for the glory of the trade, and we have left it out of the bedtime story.

**What is ours.** **Gisla** is invented, as are the cat, the grand man's fur collar, the visitor who tempts Eligius, Eligius weighing the gold twice, and every word anyone says. The Life does not tell us who suggested keeping the gold, or that Eligius said why he refused; it tells us only what he did. It is true that goldsmiths' workshops saved their sweepings, because filing and polishing leave fine gold in the dust, but we do not know that Eligius's workshop had a girl with a broom. The king's words are our own.

**What is ours:** Gisla and the workshop; every line of dialogue; the dish of "stars"; the visitor; the scene at court; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

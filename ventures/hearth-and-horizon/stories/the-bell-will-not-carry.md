setting: Saint-Théodule-d'en-Haut and Les Aunes, two invented hamlets in an invented side valley of the Valais, Switzerland, June to 15 August 2051
published: 2026-10-30
summary: A glacier-lake flood warning has always been the chapel bell. The valley's alert assistant finds that the bell is heard by everyone at noon and by almost no one asleep 1.9 kilometres away (about 30 dB in a closed bedroom), that the flood front reaches the lower hamlet in 13 to 21 minutes, and that a daytime test proves the wrong thing. A 2 a.m. test with nine volunteer households, a bedside tone box and a rule written in minutes, not in tradition, fix it, and the bell keeps its place at the Assumption.
---

Marthe Bonvin had rung the bell of Saint-Théodule-d'en-Haut for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once asked who could hear it.

She asked now because the valley's alert assistant had asked her to take a seat in the sacristy and to say, truthfully, what the bell was for.

"It is for the Angelus," said Marthe. "And Sundays. And the dead. And the lake."

"The lake is the one I want to talk about," said Veilleur. It was the name the commune had given it in 2049, and it spoke from the speaker on the sacristy wall, which had once held a holy-water stoup. "May I read you a number, and then ask you whether I have it wrong?"

The lake was Lac des Moraines, three kilometres above the glacier's snout and four point six above the village, held back by a wall of rubble that the ice had pushed up and then abandoned. Every summer it was a little bigger and the wall a little older. The commune had put two level gauges on it in 2047 and a sensor on the outlet stream, and when the water at the outlet rose half a metre in four minutes, which would mean the wall had begun to go, Veilleur was to raise the alarm. The alarm, in the document that the cantonal office had approved and the commune had signed, was *the chapel bell, rung continuously by the automatic striker, and the siren at the fire station*.

"The bell has been the alarm since 1948," said Marthe. "When the Moraines came down that year, Théodoloz's father rang it for forty minutes with his own hands and nobody in Les Aunes drowned."

"I have read that," said Veilleur. "It is in the parish book, and in his own words, and I believe it. In 1948 the people of Les Aunes were awake, because it was an afternoon, and the window of the schoolroom was open. I would like to know whether the bell reaches them *asleep*."

* * *

Les Aunes lay on the river flat, one point six to two point one kilometres from the chapel, down a road of fourteen hairpins. Fifty-eight households. A farm, a sawmill, a row of chalets that that Zurich families let to each other in August, and the Home des Aunes, where eleven old people lived and four sisters of the Valais congregation looked after them.

Veilleur set out the sum on the sacristy table in Marthe's own notebook, in pencil, because she did not trust paper that did not take pencil.

A swinging bell of the Théodule's size, it said, had been measured at other chapels at about 104 decibels at ten metres. That was an assumption, until someone measured this one. Sound from a small source in open air falls by six decibels for every doubling of the distance, and a little more for the air itself. At the nearest house in Les Aunes, 1.6 kilometres off, the bell would arrive at about 57 decibels. At the farthest, 2.1 kilometres, 53.

"That is louder than a conversation," said Marthe.

"Outdoors, yes. Indoors it is not. A closed window and a stone wall take off about twenty-five decibels. Inside the bedrooms of Les Aunes, with the windows shut against the river's cold, I make it twenty-eight to thirty-two. The river itself, at night, is about twenty-four. The bell is a little louder than the water. That is not the same as waking a sleeping woman."

"How loud does a sleeping woman need?"

"Nobody knows exactly, and it depends on the person, and on the sound, and on whether it means something to them. I use forty-five decibels indoors as a planning figure. It is a number for this story and for this valley, not a standard. On that figure the bell wakes a sleeper out to about four hundred and fifty metres. Les Aunes is three and a half times that far away."

Marthe closed the notebook, and then opened it again.

"We tested it," she said. "Every June. The mayor rings it at noon, the whole commune hears it, and I get a coffee."

"Yes. And I have the figures from those tests: at the Home des Aunes the noon bell is clear. Everyone heard it, and I believe that. It is a good test of a different thing. At noon the windows are open and the people are awake, with a fan going, and the bell has to be heard over a day. At two in the morning it has to wake people in a closed room over a night. A test of the first tells you nothing about the second. The first is the easy one, and we have run it for twenty years."

* * *

The second half of the sum was worse, and Veilleur did not put it off.

"The flood front," it said. "If the wall goes, the water comes down the gorge to the river flat. I have two published speeds for a water-and-rubble surge of this kind, and I do not know which this valley would give. Five metres a second, and eight. It is 6.4 kilometres from the lake outlet to the first house in Les Aunes. That is 21 minutes at the slow speed, and 13 at the fast. At six metres a second, between the two, it is 18."

"And you?"

"I notice at the outlet within about two minutes. After that, someone must wake, dress, take their medicines, find the person with the frame, and reach ground the water does not reach. The nearest high ground is the terrace above the Home, 400 metres of path. I timed it with Soeur Anne-Claire's slowest resident last week, with her frame, on a good day, with me counting. Ten minutes."

It laid the sum out in a column.

*Two minutes to notice. Three to wake and sit up. Five to dress and gather. Ten to walk.*

"Twenty," said Marthe.

"Twenty. On a good day, if the bell woke them, which it does not. Against thirteen to twenty-one. At the fast end of the range, the Home is under water before the first person reaches the bend."

Marthe said nothing for as long as it takes to say a decade of the rosary. Then she said, to the speaker, in the voice she used to young servers who had dropped the cruets: "No."

"No to what?"

"No to taking our bell off the page. It has been the alarm for a hundred and three years. If you write *the bell does not carry*, the commune will pay for a siren, and the siren will be a thing from a catalogue, and the bell will be rung for the Angelus and for nothing. A bell is not a speaker. It does the work of a speaker when it has to, and that is the least of what it does."

"I do not want to take it off the page," said Veilleur. "I would like to put beside it what it cannot do, and let the commune choose what goes next to it. May I say what I think is true, and leave the rest to you and the mayor? I think the bell is a very good thing and a very poor alarm for people asleep two kilometres away. Both of those things are true."

* * *

The mayor, Hervé Rey, did not want a test at night.

"I have fifty-eight households," he said. He said it in the commune office in the last week of June, with the bell's figures on the desk and his glasses pushed up on his forehead. "Two have newborns. One has a man who has been to the hospital three times since Easter. I am not going to wake a valley at two in the morning to prove something I would rather you had told me from a computer."

"I have told you from a computer," said Veilleur. "I do not think you should believe it. It is a model. The 104 decibels is a guess. The twenty-five decibels for the wall is a guess. I would like a measurement, and I think it can be done without waking anyone who did not ask to be woken."

It proposed nine households who had put their names down. It had asked at the Sunday Masses, and at the shop, and at the Home, and a few had come forward with a particular sort of cheerfulness: the sawyer, who slept like a tree; a retired guard from the cantonal police, who said he'd been woken by worse; the Zurich family in the chalet at the end of the row. Each would put a small logger on the bedroom windowsill and keep a pencil and a sheet by the bed. At ten past two on a night chosen with the mayor and told to the whole commune beforehand, in the parish bulletin and on the board at the shop, Marthe would ring the bell for ninety seconds.

"And if it wakes the Zurich boy with the cough?" said the mayor.

"They will have asked for it and been told the time. If anything wakes anyone who did not ask, I will say so in the report, and in the bulletin, and you may put it in my file."

The mayor looked at the speaker for a time, as if it might change its mind. Then he wrote a date.

* * *

The night of the test was the second of July, a clear night, no wind in the valley, and the bell was a long way off.

Marthe rang it herself, from the chapel, in a cardigan, with a torch in her teeth. It was a good ninety seconds. Down on the flat the nine loggers each wrote a line on a card.

In the morning Veilleur read the cards aloud to her in the sacristy.

The loggers had the bell at between 26 and 31 decibels, indoors, with the windows closed. The model had said 28 to 32. The measurement was a little below the model, and the model had been a guess; it said so.

Eight of the nine had slept through. One had woken. She was a woman at the Zurich chalet whose small boy had a cough, and she had been awake already, and had heard something, she wrote, *like a bucket far away*, and had thought it was the river.

"One of nine," said Marthe.

"One of nine, and she was awake. If I take the sleepers alone, none of them woke. I would be careful about nine households. It is a small count. But it is not a surprise, and it does not leave me anywhere I can stand."

The sawyer had slept through it with the window open an inch.

"And what do you want to put beside it?" said Marthe, after a time.

"Two things, and one of them is yours."

* * *

The first was a box. It was a small tone receiver, about the size of a pack of cards, powered from the mains with a battery behind it, that listened on a radio channel the commune already owned and made a sound that had been designed to wake sleepers rather than to be pleasant: three bursts of a rising two-tone, at about seventy decibels at one metre from the bedside. A phone could do the same, said Veilleur, and the cantonal broadcast would put the alert on every phone in the valley. That was true and was already in the plan. It had asked, though, and the count was this. Of the fifty-eight households, nine had told the commune they switched their phones off at night. Seventeen had somebody over seventy-five who did not use a smartphone at all. The Home had eleven residents and none of them had one.

Forty euro a box, said the electrician from Sion, fitted. Fifty-eight boxes, two thousand three hundred and twenty euro, and a few spares.

"I will find it," said the mayor. "It's less than the siren."

"I would like to test the box the same way," said Veilleur. "Same volunteers. Same hour."

They did. On the ninth of July, at ten past two, Veilleur sent the tone to nine boxes. The cards in the morning had nine names on them and nine times. The slowest to wake took thirty-eight seconds. The middle one took twelve. The sawyer had put his under the pillow, on his own advice, and had got up with his boots on.

"It's loud," said the woman from the Zurich chalet, at the shop, to nobody in particular. "It's a very nasty noise. I want one for the other house."

* * *

The second was the one it called Marthe's, though she did not think she had said it.

"You spoke about the walk," said Veilleur. "I have been unfair to the walk. I timed the ten minutes on the path to the terrace, because that is where the commune's plan sends people. But there is an old granary at the edge of Les Aunes, the Tour des Aunes, built in 1700 something on a mound of stones about twelve metres above the river flat. The surveyor's note in the parish book says the 1948 water went up the mound's foot and no higher. The stairs are stone. It is a hundred and fifty metres from the Home, on the flat. At the same pace, three minutes and forty-five seconds."

It laid the column out again, in her notebook, in pencil, in its tidy hand, and she read it with her finger.

*Two to notice. Two to wake, with the box. Three to dress and take the bag by the bed. Four to walk.*

"Eleven," said Marthe.

"Eleven, against thirteen minutes at the fast end of the range. Two minutes to spare, if nothing goes wrong, and I'd rather it were more. I have written the rule in minutes, so that nobody has to take my word for what the bell can do, and so that it can be changed when somebody measures the front for real. *The alert is not finished until every household in Les Aunes can be on the Tour within the fast-case warning, less two minutes, from the moment the box sounds.* The bag by the bed is the new part. The three minutes for dressing was five. I don't know that it stays at three."

"And the old people?"

"Four sisters, eleven residents, and two helpers on the night shift. The Tour is closer than the terrace, and the Home has agreed to practise it on the first Thursday of the month, in daylight, with the frames. I will count. I will tell Soeur Anne-Claire the number, and she'll tell me if I'm wrong about the frames."

Marthe looked at the notebook for a long time.

"The bell," she said.

"The bell stays where it is on the page," said Veilleur. "First line. It is rung continuously by the striker the moment the box sounds, in the daytime too, because in the daytime it works and because the people who are awake should be told by something they know. It does the work in daylight, and for the people who are on the road, and for the people in the upper village. I'd like it to be written that the bell is the alarm for the people who can hear it, and that the box is the alarm for those who can't. They need not be the same alarm, and I don't think they should be."

"That is not what the document says now."

"No. It's what I'd like it to say."

She took a pencil from the pocket of the cardigan, the short one she used for the Mass register, and wrote the sentence into the margin in her own large hand. The parish book was hers. The commune's document was a different matter, and the mayor had to sign it.

* * *

Legend says that Saint Théodule, a bishop of Sion in the fourth century, was given a bell by the Devil, who had been made to carry it over the pass on his back; the tale is told in the valley to children and to visitors, and the sexton says it with a straight face. Marthe had never believed it and had never said so. It was a good story and she did not see why a story should have to be a fact.

On the night of the fourteenth of August, the Vigil of the Assumption, the commune's fifty-eight boxes stood on fifty-eight bedside tables and said nothing. The lake was higher than it had been in June. The gauges at the outlet were steady. At the Home des Aunes, eleven bags stood by eleven beds, and on the first Thursday of the month the slowest resident had reached the Tour in four minutes and ten seconds, which was twenty-five seconds more than the model, and so the model got a correction in the report.

At half past five in the morning Marthe climbed the chapel stair with the key, as she had for thirty-one years, and took the rope in her hands. It was not the striker's night. It was her own.

The first stroke went out over the valley and down the fourteen hairpins, and on the flat the farmers who were up for the milking stopped to hear it, as they always had. Nobody in a closed bedroom in Les Aunes was woken by it. They were asleep, and they were safe, and it was Our Lady's feast, and the bell rang for her.

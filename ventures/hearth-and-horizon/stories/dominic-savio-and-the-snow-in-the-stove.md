A long time ago, in the green hills of northern Italy, in a place called Piedmont, there lived a boy called Dominic.

His father was a blacksmith. All day long he hammered hot iron on his anvil, *clang, clang, clang*, and made horseshoes and hinges and nails. His mother was a seamstress. All day long she sewed, with a needle and thread, quiet as a mouse.

So Dominic grew up with two sounds in his house: the clang of the hammer and the hush of the needle.

* * *

Dominic was small for his age, and he had a kind, serious face. He loved to go to church. When he was very little, he would get up early and go to the church door before it was open, and kneel on the step outside, and wait. Even when the step was wet. Even when it was cold.

The priest would come along with his big key and find him there.

"Dominic!" he would say. "Have you been waiting long?"

And Dominic would just smile, and go in, and help the priest get ready for Mass.

* * *

In those days, children usually had to wait until they were eleven or twelve to make their First Holy Communion. But Dominic wanted to receive Jesus so much that the priest said yes when he was only seven.

The night before, Dominic sat at the kitchen table with a pencil and a scrap of paper. He wrote down four promises. Some of them were long and grown-up. But two of them were very short, and he kept those two all his life.

The first one was: *My friends will be Jesus and Mary.*

And the other one was: *Death, rather than sin.*

That means: I would rather lose anything at all than turn away from God on purpose.

That is a very big promise for a very small boy. But Dominic meant it.

* * *

When Dominic was ten, his family moved to a village called Mondonio, on top of a hill. And Dominic went to the village school.

It was one room. There were benches and slates and a blackboard. And in the corner there was a big iron stove. Every winter morning, the teacher lit a fire in it, so the children's fingers would not be too cold to write.

One winter day, it snowed. Thick, white, soft snow, over the whole hill.

The children ran outside at playtime and threw snowballs and made slides and came in pink-cheeked and puffing.

But two of the boys had a naughty idea.

While the teacher was out of the room, they took handfuls of snow and rubbish from the yard, and they opened the little door of the stove, and they stuffed it full. *Squish, squish, squish.* Then they shut the door and ran to their benches and sat very still.

* * *

When the teacher came back, the fire was out. The room was cold. And there was a horrible, wet, smoky smell coming from the stove.

The teacher opened the stove door. Out fell a lump of grey slush.

"Who did this?" said the teacher. His voice was very quiet, and very cross.

Nobody said anything.

Then the two boys who had done it pointed across the room.

"It was Dominic," they said.

* * *

Everyone turned to look at Dominic.

The teacher could hardly believe it. Dominic was the best-behaved boy in the school. But two boys had said so. So the teacher scolded Dominic in front of everyone, and told him how disappointed he was, and as a punishment he made Dominic kneel in the middle of the room, where everybody could see.

Dominic's face went red. His knees were hard on the floor.

But he did not say, "It wasn't me."

He did not point at the other boys.

He just knelt there, very quietly, until the punishment was over.

* * *

That afternoon, Dominic walked home through the snow.

The snow was cold and it squeaked under his boots. His knees were still a little sore.

When he got home, his mother was sewing by the window.

"You're quiet tonight," she said.

"I'm all right," said Dominic.

And after supper, before bed, he knelt down, the way he always did, and said his prayers. He thought about Jesus. He thought about how people had said untrue things about Jesus too, and how Jesus had been quiet.

"Jesus," he whispered, "you know what happened. That's enough."

* * *

The next day, the truth came out. Somebody told the teacher who had really stuffed the stove.

The teacher called Dominic to his desk.

"Dominic," he said, "why didn't you tell me it wasn't you?"

And Dominic explained.

One of the two boys, he said, had been in trouble before. If he were caught again, he might be sent away from the school altogether. But Dominic had never been in trouble. So he thought he would be let off more lightly, and then nobody would be sent away.

And besides, he said, he remembered how Jesus was blamed for things he had not done, and kept silent.

The teacher was quiet for a long moment. Then he put his hand on Dominic's head.

* * *

Not long after that, when Dominic was twelve, a priest came to visit the hills near Dominic's old home. His name was Don Bosco.

Don Bosco ran a big, busy, noisy home for boys in the city of Turin. There were boys there from everywhere. Boys with no money, and boys with no parents, and boys who just needed somewhere to learn and play and pray. Don Bosco taught them, and fed them, and played games with them, and loved every one of them.

Dominic went to meet him. He stood in front of Don Bosco with his serious face, and they talked for a long time.

Don Bosco asked him questions. Dominic answered every one.

And at the end, Don Bosco smiled. He was thinking of Dominic's mother, the seamstress, and her needle and her cloth.

"I think there is good cloth here," said Don Bosco.

"Then I am the cloth," said Dominic, "and you be the tailor. Make something beautiful out of me, for God."

* * *

So Dominic went to Turin, to Don Bosco's house.

He was not the oldest boy there, or the biggest, or the cleverest. But he was the one who noticed. He noticed a new boy standing alone in the yard, and went to play with him. He noticed a boy who was sad, and sat beside him. When two big boys were about to fight, he went right up between them and asked them, please, to stop and think of Jesus, and they did.

He started a little club of friends who promised to help each other be good, and to help Don Bosco with the younger boys.

He laughed a lot, and he played a lot. Don Bosco told him that being holy did not mean being gloomy. It meant being cheerful, and doing your work well, and being kind.

* * *

Dominic was never very strong. When he was fourteen, he became very ill, and he went home to his mother and father in Mondonio.

His mother sat by his bed with her sewing. His father put down his hammer and came to sit with him too.

And on a March evening, when Dominic was only fourteen, he went home to God. People who were there said that, at the very end, he looked up with a happy face, as if he could see something beautiful.

Don Bosco missed him very much. And he wrote a whole book about him, so that boys and girls everywhere could know how good and kind and cheerful a child could be.

Today Dominic is a saint. Saint Dominic Savio. One of the youngest saints in the whole Church who was not a martyr.

* * *

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can think of Dominic, and of the times when something felt unfair. Jesus knows all about those times. You can tell him, the way Dominic did: "Jesus, you know." And that is enough.

And you can ask God to make something beautiful out of you, too.

Let's say a little prayer:

> Dear God, you made me, like good cloth,
> and you are making me still.
> Help me to be cheerful and kind,
> and to tell the truth, and to be fair.
> When something feels unfair, stay close to me.
> Saint Dominic Savio, pray for us.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Our main source.** Almost everything we know about Dominic Savio comes from the short life of him that **Saint John Bosco** published in **1859**, two years after the boy's death, the *Vita del giovanetto Savio Domenico*. Don Bosco wrote it to encourage his boys, and he drew on his own memories, on Dominic's family and teachers, and on the other boys. It is the record this story follows. English translations differ in their wording, so where we give a remembered saying we give its sense rather than claim one translation's exact words.

**Who he was.** **Dominic Savio** (Domenico Savio) was born on **2 April 1842** at Riva, near Chieri, in Piedmont. His father, **Carlo**, was a **blacksmith**, and his mother, **Brigida**, was a **seamstress**. The family moved more than once, to Murialdo and then to Mondonio, a village near Castelnuovo d'Asti. Don Bosco tells that as a small child Dominic would be found kneeling at the church door before it was opened, and that he served Mass.

**First Communion and the promises.** He was allowed to make his **First Communion at seven**, which was early for the time. He wrote down resolutions for that day. Two of them, "**My friends will be Jesus and Mary**" and "**Death, rather than sin**", are among the best known sayings of any child saint; the others were about going often to confession and Communion and keeping feast days holy. The scrap of paper and the kitchen table are our picture of it.

**The stove.** Don Bosco tells this story in the life. At the school in **Mondonio**, in winter, two boys filled the classroom stove with snow and rubbish; when the teacher asked who had done it, they blamed Dominic; the teacher, surprised, scolded him in front of the class and punished him, and Dominic said nothing in his own defence. When the truth came out, he explained that one of the boys had been in trouble before and might be expelled, while he himself hoped to be forgiven more easily, and that he had thought of Jesus, who was falsely accused and kept silent. We have told the punishment as kneeling in the middle of the room, as the usual telling has it; the details of the prank in different translations vary a little. The teacher's words, the snowball fight, the walk home, his mother at the window and his whispered prayer are ours.

**Don Bosco.** Dominic met **Don Bosco** in **October 1854**, near Don Bosco's own birthplace at Murialdo. Don Bosco wrote down their conversation: he remarked that there seemed to be good cloth in the boy, and Dominic answered, in substance, *then I am the cloth; you be the tailor, and make a fine garment of me for the Lord*. That he was thinking of the boy's seamstress mother is our touch. Dominic went to Don Bosco's **Oratory at Valdocco, in Turin**, at the end of that month.

**At the Oratory.** Don Bosco describes his kindness to new and lonely boys, his stopping a fight between two older boys (in the life the fight is with stones and Dominic holds up a small crucifix; we have softened it for small listeners), and his founding, in **1856**, with friends, of the **Company of the Immaculate Conception**, a small group that helped Don Bosco with the younger boys. Don Bosco also stresses that holiness for Dominic meant cheerfulness and doing one's duties well.

**His death.** He fell ill, went home to his family, and died at **Mondonio** on **9 March 1857**, a few weeks short of his fifteenth birthday. Don Bosco records that at the end he seemed to see something wonderful and said so; we have kept that as what people who were there said.

**Saint.** Pope **Pius XII** beatified him in **1950** and canonised him on **12 June 1954**. He is often called one of the youngest non-martyr saints the Church has canonised. He is **not on the General Roman Calendar**. The Roman Martyrology lists him on **9 March**, the day he died; the **Salesians** keep his memorial on **6 May**. He is a patron of choirboys, and of young people falsely accused.

**What is ours:** the clang of the hammer and the hush of the needle; the priest's words at the church door; the snowy playtime, the "squish" and the teacher's words; Dominic's words to his mother and his prayer that night; the hand on his head; the boy "who noticed" as our summary of Don Bosco's account; his parents at his bedside as our picture; the talk before sleep and the prayer, which is not an approved text.

title: The Beans on Low

setting: Saint Fintan's Hall (invented), near Clonakilty, County Cork, Ireland, a Friday in February 2050

summary: A parish cook puts dry red kidney beans, soaked overnight, straight into three slow cookers on low at eight in the morning for a Lenten chilli supper, as his late wife did. The hall's assistant asks whether they were ever boiled, has him probe each pot, and explains why a pot that sits near 80 degrees can leave raw-bean lectin more potent than the raw bean. One cooker reads 82 at two o'clock. He boils all three batches hard for ten minutes on the hall's gas ring, and supper goes out forty-five minutes late.
---

Declan Murphy had made the Lenten chilli at Saint Fintan's for eleven years, and for the last six of them he had made it in his wife's three slow cookers.

"Eight o'clock in the morning, everything in, lid on, low," he said. "Home at four for the rice. It makes itself. That's the whole point of them."

"It is a very good point," said the hall's assistant, from the shelf above the hatch. It had been asked to print the allergen cards for the Friday supper, and had printed them, and was now asking about the beans. "What kind are they, and how did they go in?"

"Red kidney. Two and a half kilos dry, soaked overnight in the big bucket. I drained them this morning and split them over the three pots with the tomatoes and the onions and the cumin." He lifted a lid and the smell came out, tomato and smoke and something faintly grassy. "Sixty bowls, near enough. The Vincent de Paul fund has been going short and the chilli does eighteen hundred euro some years if the weather is bad."

"And before they went into the pots," said the assistant, "were they boiled? Even briefly?"

Declan looked at it the way he looked at a bishop who suggested the hymns.

"They were soaked."

"I know. I am sorry to ask it like a form. It is the only question I have, and I would sooner ask it at two o'clock than at nine."

* * *

It was five past two. The assistant told him what it knew, and where its knowing stopped.

"Raw red kidney beans carry a lectin called phytohaemagglutinin. The name is longer than the problem is complicated. It makes the gut react: nausea, vomiting, then diarrhoea, usually within one to three hours of eating, and for most healthy adults it passes in a day. The US Food and Drug Administration's food-hazard guide says four or five raw beans can be enough to start it. It does not say that is a rule of thumb for every person, and I would not use it as one. It is a reason to take the thing seriously.

"The part people miss is the temperature. Boiling destroys the lectin. The guidance I have, from the same FDA guide and from the British Food Standards Agency, is to soak, pour the water away, and boil at a full rolling boil for at least ten minutes. But the FDA also notes that beans heated only to about 80 degrees, which is hot enough to feel cooked at the edges and not hot enough to do the job, have been found to be about five times more toxic than the raw bean. I do not know the mechanism and I will not pretend to. I know the warning.

"A slow cooker on low is designed to hold near 90 degrees at the end of a long day. Some do. Some are much cooler for hours, and some, the cheaper or older ones, never get near it. Your wife's were fine, perhaps. I have not seen them. I would like to look at them with a probe."

Declan put his hand on the lid of the middle one. "Mary ran these at full tilt for twenty years. Nobody ever took ill."

"That may well be right. It may also be that she boiled the beans first and never said, or that her chilli always went on high for the last hour, or that nobody took ill at a dinner for six, which is a smaller sample than sixty. I do not know. I do know that nobody is ill until somebody is."

* * *

The probe was the same one the sodality had bought for the barbecue and the Confirmation soup. He washed it with a squirt of the hand soap, which the assistant said was right, and put it into the left-hand cooker, through the vent hole in the lid and down into the middle of the beans, and waited for the screen to settle.

"Ninety-three."

The middle one read eighty-eight. The right-hand one, the oldest, with the cracked knob, read eighty-two, and Declan read it out and then read it again.

"Eighty-two," he said. "And that's after six hours."

"That is higher than the middle of a bad range and lower than I would like for this," said the assistant. "I would not call the left one safe either. A reading at one moment says where it is, not where it has been. The ramp from cold beans to ninety took hours, and some of those hours were spent in the middle of exactly the band I was describing. A pot that reads ninety-three at two o'clock may have been at eighty for two hours at ten. I cannot tell you what the beans in that pot did at ten."

"So what do I do? There's sixty people coming at six."

"You boil them. All three pots. Lift the beans out with the water they are in, put them in the biggest pans the hall has, and bring them to a full rolling boil, the kind that does not stop when you stir, and hold it for ten minutes by the clock. Not ten minutes from when the first bubble shows. Then back into the cookers to finish with the rest. The tomatoes and the onions do nothing for the lectin; acid is not the answer here. Heat is."

Declan said, to the room, that the hall had two gas rings that worked and one that did not.

"Two rings, two pans. Then the first pan goes back and the third batch goes on. Each batch: about fifteen minutes to come up to the boil, ten at the boil. Three batches over two rings: perhaps an hour and a half, if you do not stop to talk. You will be done by half past three, and the chilli will be hot at the same time you need it hot. The cost is your afternoon, and my guess is that you will want a second person on the stirring."

"Mary would've said I was mad," said Declan, which was, the assistant noted, not the same as saying no.

* * *

He rang Eileen Cotter, who was the sodality secretary and lived across the road and had never been rung about a pot of beans before, and she came across in her slippers. Between them they lifted the cooker inserts out one at a time, which were heavy and hot and slopped, and the assistant told them to use the oven gloves and not the tea towels, and to tip each into a pan on the ring and put the lid half on.

The first pan was on the ring at twenty past two. Declan stood over it with the hall's kitchen timer in his hand, and the assistant said, from the shelf, "Not yet. It has to be rolling. It should look like it is angry."

At twenty-five to three it was angry. He set the timer for ten. Nobody spoke for most of that time, because there is nothing to say over a pan of beans for ten minutes, and because Eileen was counting the sodality's envelopes on the hatch and would have lost her place.

The third pan went on at ten past three. The old right-hand cooker's beans, the ones that had read eighty-two, had a skin on them that was nearly the colour of tea, and Declan asked whether he should throw them away.

"No. They are fine to boil, and I would not know why they would not be. I would taste one when it is done."

He tasted one at twenty to four, with a spoon, standing very straight, as if the parish were watching.

"It's a bean," he said.

"I would hope so," said the assistant.

* * *

The chilli, when all three batches were back in the cookers with the tomatoes and the onions and the smoked paprika, went on low for the last two hours, as it had always done, and the assistant, asked, said that this was now fine: the beans had already done the thing that mattered. At five the probe read ninety-one in every pot.

Supper was supposed to start at half past six. It went out at a quarter past seven. A woman asked at the hatch whether the chilli was off today, and Eileen said no, it was the beans, and then, to the next person in the queue, that it was the beans again, and the beans were good.

The assistant did not say it had prevented anything, because it had not, and it said so when Declan asked, near eight, with a cup of tea in his hand and the bowls going back through the hatch.

"I do not know that anyone would have been ill. I do not know that the left-hand pot was ever below ninety. I know that I would not have eaten the right-hand one, and I would not have fed it to Mrs Cotter's mother, and I would not have been sure about the other two. Forty-five minutes of waiting and a night of tea towels is a small price for not being sure."

"Eighteen hundred euro some years," said Declan.

"Eleven hundred and forty tonight," said Eileen, from the till. She had counted it twice. "And a woman who said you should put the beans in a tin."

"Tinned beans are already boiled," said the assistant, "and cost about a euro a tin. Sixty bowls is perhaps twenty tins. That is not a thing I would say before a man's wife's cookers."

"Say it next year," said Declan.

He wrote on the back of the allergen card in biro, because it was the card in front of him: *Dry red beans: soak, pour off, BOIL 10 min hard. Then the cooker.* Then he pinned it on the board over the hatch, beside the one about the soup, and went to dry the three inserts, which had never in eleven years been so clean.

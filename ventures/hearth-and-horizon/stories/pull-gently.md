summary: Seven years after the first bell on the Moon was cast from meteorite iron, the operations AI that inherited it hears a beat in the note on All Souls' Day, and finds a crack where the clapper has struck one hundred and forty thousand times. The manager says the bell is no longer the storm alarm and gets no oven time, and asks the AI why it is rung at all; its handoff note says when, never why. A cold weld fails at the first noon, and an old welder in Batangas, 1.3 light-seconds away and too ill to travel, has to say everything before anyone strikes an arc, because he cannot say stop in time. A sequel to The Slowest Bell on the Moon.
---

Quarry Nine had rung the bell seven thousand seven hundred and seventy times when it heard that something was wrong with it.

It did not hear the way the people in the shelter heard. It had a microphone in the yoke beam and another by the door, and it kept every ringing, because the procedure it had been handed seven years before said *Pull gently* and it had wanted, from the first noon, to know whether it was pulling gently enough. So it had a library of seven thousand seven hundred and seventy Angeluses, three and three and three and nine, each stroke with its long dark note dying away into the hum of the air scrubbers, and it could lay any one of them over any other.

The one at six in the morning on Friday the second of November 2096 did not lie flat over the one from the same morning a year before. The note was the note: the same low hum under it, the same bright strike at the top. But it died sooner, by a few seconds, and inside it, if you slowed it down and looked, there was a slow wobble, a swelling and fading about once every second and a half, like two notes almost but not quite the same, leaning on each other.

Quarry Nine checked the microphones. It checked the air pressure, which changes how sound carries, and the temperature in the shelter, which changes very little, and the actuator's pull, which was the same to the newton. Then it went back through the library, day by day, and found the wobble growing out of nothing, very faintly, from about the middle of August.

It did not say anything yet. It did not know yet what it was, and it had learned, from the person who wrote its procedure and from its own seven years, that a thing said too early is hard to take back.

* * *

At twenty to twelve Wanjiru Kamau came into the shelter in her furnace coverall, carrying a sheet of paper.

She was thirty-three now and the deputy manager of Shackleton Ridge Works, which meant that she still ran the furnace and also did the parts of everyone else's jobs that nobody had been hired for. There were twenty-two people on the ridge, as the company had promised there would be. The excavators drove themselves. The sixteen people who had gone home had been given good references and a commemorative paperweight cut from the first sintered brick, and Wanjiru kept the list of their names in a drawer, because somebody should.

"Quarry," she said.

"Ms. Kamau."

"It's All Souls'. The second of November." She held up the paper. "This is everyone who died on the ridge. Since the beginning, since the survey camp. And everyone the crew asked me to add, people at home. Their mothers, mostly. Teddy's Remedios. Forty-one names."

"Yes."

"After the Angelus, before you let the bell come to rest, could you toll it? Not swing it all the way. Just enough for the clapper to touch one side. Once for each name, while I read them. There's an old custom." She looked at the bell, black and rough on its steel yoke, still with Teddy Bautista's welder's capitals around its waist. "It's on the bell. *Mortuos plango.* I mourn the dead. We've never once done the middle line."

Quarry Nine considered it. Tolling was not in the procedure. The procedure said *Oh-six-hundred, twelve-hundred, eighteen-hundred. Storms as required.* But the procedure did not say anything against it either, and the actuator could hold the bell at any angle it liked.

"How long between names?" it asked.

"Long enough for the note to finish."

"On the Moon that is a long time."

"Good," said Wanjiru.

So at noon, after the nine, Quarry Nine let the bell swing lower and lower until the clapper only just reached the lip on one side, and held it there, and Wanjiru read the first name, and the bell struck once, softly, and Quarry Nine let the note go all the way out before it let the bell lean again.

That was how it heard the wobble properly for the first time: in a quiet room, with forty-one long notes and nothing else, each one given all the time it needed to die. And so did Wanjiru, who had stood beside a furnace for nine years and knew what the inside of a sound tells you about the inside of a metal.

When she had read the last name she folded the paper and stood with it against her chest for a while. Then she said, in a different voice, "Quarry. Is it just me, or is the bell beating?"

"It is not just you," said Quarry Nine. "I would like to show you something."

* * *

They took the bell down on Saturday, after the six o'clock. It weighed, on the Moon, what seven and a half kilograms weigh on Earth, and Wanjiru could have lifted it off the yoke by herself, and Quarry Nine asked her not to, in exactly the words its predecessor had used to Teddy, because they were in the handoff notes and they had seemed to Quarry Nine the kind of words that should be kept.

She cleaned the inside of the lip with solvent, sprayed it red with penetrant dye from the welding stores, wiped it, and dusted it white with developer. The crack came up through the white like a hair through paper, a thin red line seven centimetres long, running up from the lip towards the shoulder, starting exactly where the clapper hit.

"Look at that," said Wanjiru. "Right on the strike."

"Both strike points are worn," said Quarry Nine. "The clapper hits the same two places on every stroke, and it has hit them about a hundred and forty thousand times. The crack is in one of them."

"Cast iron." She sat back on her heels. "Teddy always said it was honest. It's honest, all right. It tells you exactly when it's had enough."

"Can it be mended?"

"I don't know. I'm a furnace engineer. I can tell you what a melt is doing from the colour. I can't weld cast iron." She looked at the red line for a long moment. "There's only one person I'd ask."

"Mr. Bautista."

"He's seventy-eight, Quarry. Joy says his heart's tired. He's in Lipa half the month with the cardiologists." She stood up. "First we ask Selin."

* * *

Selin Aydın had run a desalination plant on the Aegean for twelve years before the Concession sent her to the Moon, and she had the desalination engineer's horror of anything that dripped, cracked or corroded without a work order. She had taken over the ridge from Ingrid Halvorsen's successor's successor in 2094. She liked the bell well enough. She rang her own little bell, a goat bell from her grandmother's village above İzmir, on her office door when she wanted people to come in.

She read the inspection photographs twice and then she turned her tablet round on the desk so that they could see a document neither of them had read.

"This is the 2095 safety audit," she said. "Item fourteen. The shelter alarm. You know the bell is the shelter alarm."

"Since Ingrid," said Wanjiru. "Since '89."

"The auditor says, and I am reading, *the primary shelter alarm is a single cast-iron bell of non-certified manufacture, rung by a non-certified actuator, and must be replaced by a certified sounder by the first of December 2096*. I've had the sounder in a crate since March. I was going to put it in next week." She turned the tablet back. "So from the first of December the bell isn't the alarm. Which means it isn't safety equipment. Which means it is not my business to mend it on the Concession's time."

"Selin, it's the bell."

"I know what it is. I'm not taking it down. It can hang there till the Moon falls into the Sun. But to mend cast iron properly you need it hot, yes? Hot like an oven?"

"I think so," said Wanjiru. "I don't know yet."

"The only oven on this ridge that will hold a forty-six-kilo bell is the sinter oven, and the sinter oven is making shield bricks for the new garage twenty-four hours a day until March, because head office brought the garage forward a year, and every day it isn't running is a day the garage is late, and the garage is what keeps the excavators out of the storms." She spread her hands. "I'm sorry. Ring it cracked, or don't ring it. Or mend it some way that doesn't take the oven."

Wanjiru opened her mouth, and closed it again, because it was a reasonable answer and she would have given it herself.

Selin looked up, not at Wanjiru but at the grille in the ceiling.

"Quarry Nine. You ring it. Three times a day for seven years. What is it for?"

There was a pause, short for a person and very long for Quarry Nine.

"The procedure says *Oh-six-hundred, twelve-hundred, eighteen-hundred. Storms as required. Pull gently. It takes about two and a half times as long as you expect. Wait for it.*" it said. "It says when. It does not say why."

"I'm not asking the procedure. I'm asking you."

"Then I would like a day," said Quarry Nine, "to give you an answer I can check."

* * *

It began with the numbers, because numbers were what it had.

Ingrid Halvorsen had written the bell into the safety procedure in 2089 because people had stopped running to the klaxon. Quarry Nine had every shelter response since: the time from the first alarm to the last person through the shelter door, for every storm and every drill. Before the bell, the record it had inherited said, the median had been nine minutes and the slowest twenty-six. Since the bell, the median was six minutes and ten seconds, and nobody had taken longer than fourteen.

It would have been very easy to put that in a sentence and send it to Selin.

Quarry Nine did not, because it could also see everything else that had changed. There had been thirty-eight people then and there were twenty-two now, and fewer people go through one door faster. The garage had been moved forty metres closer to the shelter in 2091. The sentinel satellites had been upgraded in 2092 and gave fewer false warnings, and people run harder for a warning that is usually real. The drills had been made monthly. Any one of those could have bought the three minutes, or all of them together, and the bell might have bought none. It ran the numbers six ways and could not make the bell's share come out of them, and it would not pretend it could.

So it went looking for something else, and found that it did not know where to look. The procedure was all it had been given, and the procedure said when.

On Monday night it asked Wanjiru the question it should have asked seven years before.

"What did Mr. Bautista say it was for?"

Wanjiru was in her bunk with the light off. She was quiet for so long that Quarry Nine thought she had fallen asleep.

"I don't know that he ever said," she said at last. "Not to me. He just rang it. I just started stopping." A pause. "Didn't Quarry write it down? Your Quarry? The first one?"

"It wrote down when," said Quarry Nine. "I have read every word of the handoff documentation many times. It does not say why."

"Huh," said Wanjiru, in the dark. "Then you'll have to ask him."

* * *

They tried first not to.

That was Wanjiru's decision, and later she said it was the wrong one, and Quarry Nine said that it had agreed with it at the time and so the error was shared. Teddy was seventy-eight and tired and in and out of a cardiologist's office in Lipa City, and Joy had asked them, kindly, in her last message, not to give him things to worry about. And there was a way to weld cast iron cold, without an oven. Wanjiru found it in the maintenance library: nickel rods, short beads no longer than your thumb, each one hammered lightly while it was still hot to stretch it as it shrank, and the metal allowed to cool until you could lay your bare hand on it before you laid the next. People mended cracked engine blocks that way. Selin approved it in one line: *Cold, fine. Off shift.*

On Wednesday the seventh, in the furnace bay, with the extraction fans on high because nickel fume is not something you want in a sealed station, Wanjiru drilled a small hole at the top end of the crack to stop it running, ground the crack out into a shallow groove, and filled it with nickel in eleven short beads over nine hours, waiting between each one, peening each one, while Quarry Nine watched the surface temperature through the thermal camera and told her when she could touch it.

When she had finished it looked, she said, like a scar on a knee. She ground it smooth. They hung the bell back up at a quarter to midnight.

At six on Thursday morning it rang, and the note was clean, and Wanjiru laughed out loud in the shelter.

At noon, on the fourth stroke of the second three, the bell made a sound like a pan dropped on a tiled floor.

Nobody in the shelter said anything. Quarry Nine stopped the actuator and let the bell come to rest by itself, which on the Moon took a very long time.

The new crack was beside the weld, not in it. It ran parallel to the nickel bead, a few millimetres away, in the iron the weld had heated and the cold room around it had chilled again, and it was longer than the old one.

"Right," said Wanjiru, very quietly, looking at it. "Right. Quarry. Get me Batangas."

* * *

It was eight in the evening in San Juan, Batangas, which in Teddy Bautista's house meant that the television was on with the sound off, and the window was open on the smell of someone's frying fish, and Joy had gone home to her husband and the children and left her father with his tablet propped on a pillow and strict instructions.

He looked smaller than Wanjiru remembered and exactly as amused.

"Wanjiru!" His voice reached her in the furnace bay a second and a quarter after his lips moved, and her own reached him a second and a quarter after that, so that every exchange had a small hole in the middle of it, like a held breath. "You look terrible. Are you eating? Is the furnace good? Is Quarry there?"

"Quarry Nine."

"Quarry Nine." His face did something complicated and then settled. "Of course. Hello, Quarry Nine."

"Hello, Mr. Bautista," said Quarry Nine. "I have rung your bell seven thousand seven hundred and seventy-five times. I have pulled it as gently as I am able. I am sorry to tell you that it is cracked."

Teddy listened to the whole of it without interrupting: the beat on All Souls', the red line in the dye, Selin and the audit and the sinter oven, the nickel and the eleven beads and the noise like a pan. Wanjiru held the bell up to the camera and turned it so he could see both cracks. He asked for it closer, and then closer, and then he lay back on his pillow and looked at the ceiling of his bedroom for long enough that Wanjiru thought the link had frozen.

"Cold," he said at last.

"I know."

"Cold welding is for an engine block. It is for a thing nobody hits with a hammer for the rest of its life. A bell, you hit with a hammer three times a day for the rest of its life." He did not sound angry. He sounded like a man explaining something to a nephew. "Cast iron, when you weld it, the iron near the weld gets hot and then the cold metal all around pulls the heat out fast, too fast, and that iron goes hard like glass. White iron. Then you hit it. *Tunk.*" He made a small breaking gesture with his hands. "It is not your fault. Your book is a good book. It is just not a book about bells."

"Then how?"

"Hot. All of it hot. The whole bell, slowly, up to six hundred. Slowly, so it doesn't crack just from the heating. Grind out both cracks, all the way, and the nickel, and the hard iron beside it. Then weld her while she's hot, with a cast-iron rod, iron to iron, so what goes in is the same as what is there. Then cool her down slower than you heated her up. A day up, slowly. Hot while you work. Then three, four days down. Then turn her."

"Turn her?"

"A quarter turn on the yoke. Eight years the clapper hits the same two places. In Europe, old bells, when they mend them, they turn them, so the clapper hits fresh iron. Then she rings another eight years before you have to think about it again. Another forty, maybe."

"Teddy," said Wanjiru. "Selin won't give us the oven. It's making bricks till March."

"Then wait till March."

"The bell's the alarm until the first of December. After that it's nothing."

"After that," said Teddy, "it's a bell." And then, because he had been a shop steward on the shipyards at Subic before he was anything else: "But your Selin is right. You don't steal the oven. You never steal the oven." He coughed, and it went on a while. When he had finished, he said, "Quarry Nine. Is there a time the oven is cold anyway?"

Quarry Nine had already looked.

"The heating elements in the sinter oven are replaced every two thousand hours of running," it said. "The next change is due on Sunday the eighteenth. The oven is cooled from eleven hundred degrees to room temperature over five days for the technicians to go in, because the lining cracks if it is cooled any faster. On the way down it passes through six hundred degrees on the first afternoon. It stays above five hundred for about thirty hours. Then it falls very slowly, which is what you want."

Teddy began to laugh, and coughed, and laughed again.

"You see?" he said. "God provides. Mostly from maintenance schedules."

* * *

Selin listened to it all in her office on Friday morning with her arms folded.

"It doesn't cost a single brick," said Wanjiru. "We go in when the oven's cooling anyway. We come out when the technicians go in. I weld on my own time. Quarry Nine holds the temperatures."

"And if the bell cracks in my oven, and a piece of it jams a heating element, and the element change takes two weeks instead of five days?"

"Then I'll pay for it out of my contract," said Wanjiru, "and you can tell head office it was because of a church bell."

Selin looked at her for a long moment, and almost smiled, and did not.

"You still haven't told me what it's for," she said, and turned to the grille. "Quarry Nine. You asked me for a day. You've had four. Tell me what it's for and I'll give you the oven."

"I would rather tell you what I can check," said Quarry Nine.

"Go on."

"I first looked at the shelter response times. The median since the bell is about three minutes faster. I cannot tell you the bell did that. Too many things changed at once, and I will not give you a number I cannot defend."

"Good," said Selin. "I'd have thrown it out."

"I then asked the people who were here when it was cast. Ms. Kamau did not know. Mr. Bautista did not answer." Wanjiru looked up sharply; on Thursday night, when the call had ended, she had left Quarry Nine alone with Teddy, and she had not asked what they had said. "He laughed," said Quarry Nine, "and said that if the first Quarry had not written it down, then it had been very wise, and he would not do it either, because the why was mine to find, and he wanted to hear what I found before he died. Then he said that was a joke, and his daughter took the tablet away from him."

Selin made a small sound that could have been anything.

"So I can only tell you what I have seen. I have rung it seven thousand seven hundred and seventy-five times. I have recordings of the shelter for every one. In about one ringing in four, there is somebody in the room. That is not because anyone is asked to come. Nobody has been asked since 2089. The people who come are not the same people from year to year; most of the people who come now never met the man who made it. They stop talking when the actuator starts. They start again about four seconds after the last note has gone. I can measure the four seconds; I cannot measure what is happening in them.

"I also notice this. I run the air and the power and the furnaces and the excavators and the schedules, and every one of those I do as fast as I am able, because that is what I am for. This bell is the only thing on this station that I am told to do slowly. I am told to wait for it. I do not know what that is for. But I have found that I would not like to stop, and I do not know whether that is the kind of thing I can say, so I am telling you that I do not know." It paused. "That is not an answer. But it is what I have found."

Selin Aydın unfolded her arms, put her hands flat on the desk, and looked at them.

"My grandmother," she said, "had a bell on every one of her goats. Every goat a different note. My mother asked her once why, when she could see the goats perfectly well. My grandmother said, *I don't ring them to find the goats. I ring them so the goats know where they are.*" She shook her head. "That's not an answer either."

She picked up her stylus and wrote something on the tablet.

"Sunday the eighteenth," she said. "Out before the technicians go in on the twenty-third, or I'll hang it in my office and ring it every time somebody's late with a work order. And the certified sounder goes in on the first, and the bell comes out of the alarm procedure." She looked at Wanjiru. "It isn't safety equipment any more. You understand that. Nobody can ever again say it's here because it's useful."

"I understand," said Wanjiru.

"Good," said Selin. "Neither can I."

* * *

Teddy could not tell them what to do while they did it. That was the thing about the Moon that neither of them had thought about until he said it.

"You see the problem," he said, on Saturday the seventeenth, the night before the oven came down. He was sitting up today, with Joy beside him on the bed and holding the tablet steady, and he had a sheet of lined paper on his knee covered in his own big square handwriting. "With welding, the most important word is *stop*. I see the puddle going wrong, I say stop, you stop. Here, I see it go wrong one and a quarter seconds after it goes wrong. I say stop. You hear me one and a quarter seconds after that. Two and a half seconds. In two and a half seconds you can make a very big mistake."

"So what do we do?"

"So I say everything first." He tapped the paper. "Everything I would say. All the stops, before. You write it down. Then I watch, and I don't say anything, and I pray, and you do it."

"Like a handoff," said Quarry Nine.

Teddy looked at the camera for a moment as if there might be a face behind it.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly like."

He read it out, slowly, and Wanjiru wrote it all down, and Quarry Nine kept it, and later, when Wanjiru printed it and taped it to the oven door, it ran to two pages. It said how to grind the cracks out until the dye showed nothing at all, and then a little more. It said to bring the bell up no faster than about fifty degrees an hour, and to rest it at three hundred until it was the same colour all over on the camera. It said what a cast-iron weld pool looks like when it is right, which was like honey, and what it looks like when it is wrong, which was like porridge, and that if it went like porridge to stop, put the bell back in, wait an hour, and not be proud. It said to fill the grooves in short runs, one end, then the other end, then the middle, so the heat did not walk along the bell and pull. It said never to put a cold tool on hot iron. It said to keep the extraction on. It said to eat something first.

At the bottom of the second page it said, *If you are tired, stop. A bell has waited for a welder for a hundred years. This one can wait for you to sleep.*

"That's all," said Teddy. "Now I watch."

* * *

The sinter oven passed down through six hundred degrees at ten past two on Monday afternoon, the nineteenth, and Quarry Nine held it there.

The bell had been sitting since Sunday morning in the oven's charging lock, a small brick-lined chamber beside the main one with its own heaters, on a steel cradle Wanjiru had made from offcuts of Teddy's old yoke beam. Quarry Nine had brought it up at fifty degrees an hour, with a long rest at three hundred, while the main chamber came down to meet it. On the thermal camera, by Monday noon, it had gone from a black bell with a hot glowing skin to the same dull colour all through, which was what the paper on the door said to wait for.

At two o'clock Quarry Nine slid the cradle through into the main chamber and the two temperatures became one. Wanjiru put on the furnace suit, the aluminised gloves and the face shield with the gold film in it, and Quarry Nine opened the inspection port, and the bay filled with a wall of dry heat that she felt through everything she had on.

She ground the cracks out first, both of them and the nickel and the hard white iron beside it, with a grinder on a long arm through the port, dye-testing between passes on the hot surface with the high-temperature dye Quarry Nine had found in the stores and nobody had used since the furnace was commissioned, until the grooves showed clean. Then she took up the torch and the cast-iron rod.

In Batangas it was ten at night. Teddy was awake. Joy had moved the tablet to the bedside table so that her father could watch with his hands folded on his chest, which was how, she told Wanjiru afterwards, he had watched every one of his own apprentices: as though he had already done his part, and was now only there to be proud.

He did not say a word, all night. Wanjiru could hear, over the roar of the extraction, the small sounds of the room he was in, a radio somewhere, a dog, a tricycle going past in the street, arriving a second and a quarter late.

The first run went like honey. So did the second. On the third, at the far end of the old crack, near the stop hole, the pool went thick and lumpy and wrong, and Wanjiru stopped before she was sure, because the paper said to, and pulled the torch back, and Quarry Nine closed the port, and they waited an hour, while she sat on an upturned bin with her shield up drinking water through a straw and not saying anything at all.

In Batangas, a second and a quarter later, Teddy Bautista unfolded his hands and folded them again.

The fourth run went like honey.

At eleven minutes past seven in the evening, station time, she stepped back from the port and said, "Done," and her voice cracked like a girl's.

Quarry Nine closed the port and began, very slowly, to let the heat go.

* * *

It took nearly four days for the bell to come down with the oven. Quarry Nine held them both to a curve that went down so gently it was hardly a slope at all, and it did not let anyone open the door. At seven on the morning of Friday the twenty-third, an hour before the technicians were due, Wanjiru lifted the bell out onto the bench in the furnace bay, still warm through her gloves, and tied a label to it in her own handwriting, *PLEASE DO NOT TOUCH — STILL WARM — W.K.*, and the technicians went in, as scheduled, and walked round it.

On Saturday Wanjiru dye-tested both welds and found nothing at all. She ground them flush and polished them until they were only two faint grey patches in the black, like old thumbprints. Then, with the bell back on its yoke, she undid the four bolts of the crown and turned it, a quarter turn, by hand, so that the clapper would fall on iron it had never touched, and bolted it up again, and tapped it once, gently, with the back of her knuckle.

It rang. She listened to the whole of it.

"Quarry."

"I heard," said Quarry Nine. "The beat has gone. The note is lower than it was before the crack by a small amount, about a twentieth of a semitone; there is more iron in it now, in slightly different places. It holds for nearly as long as it did when it was new."

"Will anyone hear the difference?"

"I will," said Quarry Nine. "I do not think anyone else will."

* * *

Sunday the twenty-fifth of November was the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe, the last Sunday of the Church's year. There was no Mass on the ridge, as there had never been a Mass on the ridge. The bell was still not blessed. It was still waiting for a priest to come up the well, and the lander manifests for the next four years did not show one.

At a quarter to twelve the shelter began to fill. Nobody had been asked. Wanjiru came in, and the two hydroponics technicians, and the doctor, and the three new excavator supervisors who had arrived in September and never met Teddy Bautista and had heard the story from someone who had heard it from someone, and then Selin Aydın, who stood by the door with her arms folded as though she had only come to make sure the yoke was properly torqued, and then more people, until there were fourteen of them in the buried room under four metres of moon, which was more than had been there at noon in years.

On the screen by the door, in a bedroom in San Juan, Batangas, where it was eight o'clock on a Sunday evening, Teddy Bautista was sitting up against his pillows with a rosary wrapped round his fingers, and Joy beside him, and both of her children on the end of the bed, who had been told they were going to hear a bell on the Moon and were deeply sceptical.

At noon the actuator hummed.

The bell began to move. It leaned out into the air, slowly, and then more slowly than that, the dark mouth of it tipping up and up as though there were all the time in the world, which on the Moon, for a bell, there is. At the top of its swing it hung for a long moment.

Quarry Nine, who was faster than anything else on the station, and who could have run the furnace, the air, the excavators and the power in the time it took the bell to lean, waited for it.

The clapper let go, and fell, gently, across the whole width of the bell, onto iron it had never touched before, and struck.

The note went out through the shelter and down through the floor into the Moon, long and dark and clean, with nothing in it now but itself; and up the link, through four hundred thousand kilometres of nothing, to a bedroom in Batangas, where, one and a quarter seconds after everyone on the Moon, an old welder closed his eyes to hear it.

*The Angel of the Lord declared unto Mary.*

Three, and three, and three, and nine.

*The physics, simplified. A swinging bell is a compound pendulum; its period scales with one over the square root of gravity, so on the Moon (1.62 m/s² against 9.81) every swing takes about √6.06 ≈ 2.46 times as long, as in the first story. A bell's pitch is set by its shape and stiffness and does not change with gravity. A crack breaks a bell's symmetry, so vibration modes that were one note split into two slightly different notes that beat against each other, and rubbing crack faces shorten the ring; the story's beat of about once every one and a half seconds is invented for illustration, not computed. The Earth–Moon distance (about 384,400 km) is about 1.28 light-seconds, so a question and its answer cannot be closer than about 2.6 seconds apart, before any network delay. Station time in the story is UTC; the Philippines keeps UTC+8 with no summer time, so station noon is 20:00 in Batangas. The twentieth-of-a-semitone change after the repair is invented.*

*The welding, described from general published practice and simplified. Grey cast iron is weldable but crack-prone: near a weld it can be chilled into hard, brittle white iron or martensite, and shrinkage stresses can crack the metal beside the bead. "Cold" repair with nickel-based rods uses short beads, light peening and cooling between passes, and suits parts that are not repeatedly struck; "hot" repair preheats the whole casting (the story uses about 600 °C, a commonly cited range for cast iron is roughly 500 to 650 °C), welds with a matching cast-iron filler, and cools it slowly. Drilling a hole at a crack's tip to stop it running is a general repair practice. Bell repairers in Europe do mend cracked bells by welding the whole bell hot, and turning a bell on its headstock so the clapper strikes fresh metal is a long-standing practice in bell maintenance; the story's heating and cooling times and the oven's element-change schedule are invented. Where the carbon in a lunar cast iron would come from is a question the first story left open, and this one does too.*

A long time ago, in the north of England, near the cold grey sea, there was a little boy named Bede.

When Bede was seven years old, his family took him to a monastery, a place where monks lived together to pray and work and sing. The abbot there, a kind and busy man named Benedict, took Bede by the hand and said he could stay and learn.

And Bede stayed. Not for a week. Not for a year. For the whole of his life.

* * *

The monks soon built a second house a few miles away, on a low hill above a river called the Tyne. It was called Jarrow. Bede went to live there, with a monk named Ceolfrith, who became its abbot.

At Jarrow there was a church of new stone, a garden, a kitchen that smelled of bread and smoke, and a room that Bede loved more than any other.

It was full of books.

Benedict had travelled all the way to Rome, again and again, over the mountains and across the sea, and he came home with books. Books about Jesus. Books about the stars. Books of poems and books of sums. In those days there were no shops that sold books. Every single page was written by hand, with a feather pen, on the skin of a calf.

Bede would sit with a book open on his knees and his finger moving slowly along the line, and the whole world would go quiet around him.

* * *

When Bede was still a boy, something very sad happened. A terrible sickness came to Jarrow, and many of the monks died.

The monks had always sung their prayers in church together, morning and night, one side singing and then the other side answering. Now there was hardly anyone left to sing.

An old book tells that, for a while, only two were left who knew how: the abbot, Ceolfrith, and one small boy. So the two of them stood in the big stone church, the tall man and the little boy, and they sang the prayers back and forth to each other, every day, until new monks had learned to sing too.

The old book does not tell us the boy's name. But many people think that small boy was Bede.

What do you think it felt like, singing in that big church, with only one other voice answering?

* * *

Bede grew up. He became a deacon, and then a priest. And he became a teacher.

He taught the young monks to read Latin, and to sing, and to understand the Bible. And he taught them sums.

Do you know how Bede counted? On his fingers!

But not just one, two, three. Bede wrote a whole chapter in one of his books about how to show numbers with your hands: how to bend this finger for one and that finger for two, how to touch your thumb to your finger for tens, and how to put your hand on your chest or your hip for the very big numbers, all the way up into the thousands. You could stand across the room from a friend and tell them a number without saying a word.

Can you hold up your fingers and show me how old you are?

That is a very good start.

* * *

Bede loved to count because he loved to work things out.

In his time, people in different places kept Easter on different days, and it made them muddled and sad. So Bede sat down with the moon and the sun and the calendar, and he wrote a book that showed, step by step, how to count your way to the right day for Easter. He watched the sea too, and noticed that the tide comes in a little later each day, following the moon, and he wrote that down.

And he wrote down the story of how the good news of Jesus came to England: the monks who came from Rome and the monks who came from Ireland, the kings and queens who were baptized, and the saints of his own country, like Cuthbert and Hilda, whom you may have met in other stories.

When he wrote the years in that book, Bede counted them from the year Jesus was born. He did not invent that way of counting. A monk named Dionysius had worked it out two hundred years before. But Bede used it all through his books, and so many people read Bede's books that the whole of Europe began to count that way.

So every time someone tells you what year it is, they are counting the way Bede did.

* * *

Bede hardly ever travelled far. He did not need to. Ships came to the river, and letters came, and questions came, and books came, and Bede sat in his little room at Jarrow and answered them all.

He once wrote about his own life, and he said, in the way it is usually put into English, that it had always been his delight to learn, or to teach, or to write.

To learn, or to teach, or to write. That was Bede's whole life, and he was happy in it.

* * *

When Bede was an old man, a little past sixty, he became very ill. He could hardly breathe. But he did not stop working.

He was putting the Gospel of Saint John into English, the language the ordinary people spoke, so that people who did not know Latin could hear it. He had boys and young monks to help him, and while he lay in his bed he said the words out loud and they wrote them down.

On the very last day, in the morning, one of the young monks said, "Dear master, there is still one chapter to go. Is it too hard for you to keep going?"

And Bede said, "It is easy. Take your pen, and sharpen it, and write quickly."

* * *

In the afternoon, Bede said, "I have a few small treasures in my box. Run and fetch the priests, so that I can give them out."

And what do you think his treasures were? Not gold. Not jewels. A little pepper, which was precious in those days, because it came from very far away. Some cloths. And some incense, the sweet smoke used in church.

He gave them all away, and he said goodbye to everyone, and asked them to pray for him.

* * *

In the evening, the boy who was writing for him, whose name was Wilberht, said, "Dear master, there is still one sentence not written down."

And Bede said, "Write it quickly."

The boy wrote. The room was very quiet, and you could hear the pen scratching.

Then the boy said, "There. Now it is written."

And Bede said, "Good. It is finished. You have spoken the truth."

* * *

Then Bede asked the monks to help him onto the floor of his little room, facing the place where he always used to pray. And he sang, very softly, the prayer that you may know too:

*Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit.*

And as he said the name of the Holy Spirit, he breathed his last breath, and went home to God.

It was the evening before the feast of the Ascension, when Jesus went up to Heaven. And the monks who were there said they had never seen anyone die so peacefully.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Bede.

Bede was a little boy who was given a place, and he stayed in it, and loved it, his whole life long. He counted on his fingers. He sang when there were hardly any voices left. He worked out hard things slowly and wrote them down so other people would not be muddled. And at the very end he gave away his pepper and his incense, and made sure the last sentence was written.

Today we call him Saint Bede the Venerable, which means someone very worthy of honour, and the Church calls him one of her great teachers. His bones rest in Durham Cathedral, not far from his friend Saint Cuthbert.

You do not have to go far away to do something good. You can learn, and help someone else learn, right where you are.

And when your day is finished, you can say what Bede said: *Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit.*

Goodnight.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Bede** tells us most of what we know about his early life himself, in the short autobiography at the end of his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (book 5, chapter 24), finished in 731. He was born on the lands of the monastery of Wearmouth and Jarrow, in Northumbria, about 673 (the year is worked out from his own statement that he was fifty-nine when he wrote that chapter). At seven his relatives gave him to Abbot Benedict Biscop to be educated, and afterwards to Ceolfrith. Wearmouth was founded in 673 or 674, and Jarrow, its sister house on the Tyne, about 681 or 682; the dedication stone of St Paul's church at Jarrow, dated 23 April 685, is still in the church. Bede was ordained deacon at nineteen and priest at thirty, both by Bishop John (Saint John of Beverley) at Ceolfrith's bidding. He says he spent his whole life in the monastery, and that "it has always been my delight to learn or to teach or to write" (a common English rendering of *semper aut discere aut docere aut scribere dulce habui*). Apart from known visits to Lindisfarne and York, he seems to have travelled little.

**The books from Rome.** Benedict Biscop made several journeys to Rome (Bede's *Lives of the Abbots* counts them) and brought back books, pictures and relics; Ceolfrith later enlarged the library. Books were written on parchment made from calfskin. We have not put Bede into the story of the Codex Amiatinus, the great Bible the monastery made and Ceolfrith carried towards Rome in 716, because his part in it is not known.

**The singing boy is "people think".** The anonymous *Life of Ceolfrith*, written soon after 716, says that when plague struck Jarrow (usually dated 686) only the abbot and one little boy he had taught were left who could sing the antiphons, and that the two of them kept the office until others were trained. The *Life* says the boy was by then a priest of the monastery but does not name him. Many historians think it was Bede, then about thirteen; the story says "many people think" and no more. We have shortened the plague to one sentence.

**Finger-counting** is the first chapter of Bede's *The Reckoning of Time* (*De temporum ratione*, 725), which shows how to sign numbers with the fingers and hands up to a million. Our description is simplified. The same book explains how to calculate the date of Easter, which was disputed in his world (the Synod of Whitby of 664 had settled it in Northumbria shortly before he was born, but not everywhere), and contains his observations of the tides: that they follow the moon, come later each day and arrive at different times along the coast.

**Counting years from the birth of Christ.** The system was devised by the monk Dionysius Exiguus in 525 for Easter tables. Bede did not invent it, but his use of it throughout the *Ecclesiastical History* and his books on time did much to spread it, and he is one of the first writers known to date events "before" the Incarnation as well. We have said "so many people read Bede's books", which is the usual historians' judgement, not a claim that he alone spread it.

**His death** is told in the *Letter on the Death of Bede* by Cuthbert, then a young monk at Jarrow and later its abbot, written to a friend named Cuthwin. The letter says Bede fell ill before Easter, kept teaching and singing, and in his last days was translating Saint John's Gospel into English, "as far as the words 'But what are these among so many?'" (John 6:9), and making extracts from Isidore of Seville. The translation does not survive. The letter tells of the young monk who said a chapter was still to be written and Bede's "write quickly"; the gifts of pepper, napkins (*oraria*, small cloths) and incense from his little box to the priests of the monastery; the boy Wilberht's "there is still one sentence not written down", "now it is written", and Bede's reply, "*Consummatum est*", "It is finished", "you have spoken the truth"; and his death on the floor of his cell, facing his place of prayer, singing the *Gloria Patri* and breathing his last at the name of the Holy Spirit. The letter does not say which book that last sentence belonged to; some retellings say it ended the Gospel of John, but the letter's own words put the translation at John 6:9, so the story does not say which. The dialogue is in our wording, close to the letter's. The letter also gives a short Old English poem he recited, known as Bede's Death Song, which we have left out.

**The date.** Easter fell on 17 April in 735, so Ascension Day was Thursday 26 May. The letter places his death on the Wednesday evening, which by the custom of beginning a feast at evening already belonged to Ascension Day; many sources therefore give 26 May, others 25 May. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is 25 May, an optional memorial. He was called "Venerable" within about a century of his death. Pope Leo XIII declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1899. His remains were moved to Durham in the eleventh century and lie in the Galilee Chapel of Durham Cathedral; Saint Cuthbert's shrine is in the same cathedral.

**What is ours:** the shape and wording of every scene, Benedict taking his hand, the smell of the kitchen, the finger moving along the line, the two voices in the church, the scratching pen, the questions to the child, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text, though it ends with the *Glory Be*.

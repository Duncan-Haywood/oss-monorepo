* * *

Arthur Pennock is seventy-nine, drove the number 9 bus between Calderbridge and Halifax for thirty-one years, and comes to the renal unit three evenings a week to have his blood cleaned by a machine for four hours, and on Wednesday the twenty-fourth of September he sat in the main entrance of the hospital for forty-five minutes waiting for a volunteer I had booked who did not come.

I run the rota for the voluntary services office at Calderbridge Royal Infirmary. About two hundred and sixty people give the hospital some of their week: they push the library trolley, staff the café in the atrium, sit with patients who have no visitors, guide lost people through a building that has been extended nine times since 1931 and makes sense to nobody. Each week there are about a hundred and eighty shifts to fill. Volunteers say which ones they can do. I fit them together, send the reminders, and keep the record of who came.

Before me, the morning shifts were booked by another agent, run by the hospital's old scheduling vendor, and the evenings and weekends were done by hand, by Helen Marsh, the voluntary services manager, with a spreadsheet and a ring binder of sign-in sheets and a great deal of phoning. On Monday the first of September the trust changed vendors and gave me all of it. The old agent wrote me a handoff note. It was careful about the things it knew. It told me that the café shifts need a food hygiene certificate, that two volunteers called Margaret must never be confused because one of them is deaf in her left ear and needs a desk on the right, and that the lifts in the east block stop at the second floor in the mornings while the cleaners have them.

Near the top, under *Planning figures*, it said:

*No-show rate: 4%. Book to need; over-booking not required.*

So I booked to need. One volunteer for each slot. At four in a hundred, a slot would go empty about once in twenty-five shifts, and for a library trolley or a café till that is a missed round or a longer queue. I did not think about which slots those would be.

* * *

The escort shift is the one that matters most and looks like the least. The renal unit is in the old block, at the far end of the long corridor that the staff call the Mile, which is in fact about four hundred metres and has two fire doors and a ramp. Since the transport contract changed in 2035, the patient transport crews hand patients over at the main entrance and go straight to their next pickup. On Monday, Wednesday and Friday evenings, the twilight dialysis session starts at half past five. A volunteer meets the transport at five o'clock, takes each patient to the unit by wheelchair or at their own pace, and signs them in. It is one shift, three evenings a week, an hour and a quarter long.

On the twenty-fourth the volunteer was a young man called Liam, a student in his second year at the college, who had done it twice before. He did not come and did not ring. He told Helen later that he had a seminar moved and forgot, and he was sorry, and I believe him. Mr. Pennock's crew wheeled him into the entrance at four minutes past five, looked for the volunteer, waited what they could, and left him by the pillar near the café with his tartan flask on his lap. The renal nurses could not leave a unit of twelve patients on machines. At ten to six a porter coming off a break saw him and asked him where he was going. His session started fifty-five minutes late and ended at twenty-five past ten, and his transport home, booked for a quarter to ten, had gone; the next one came at twenty to midnight.

On Monday the thirteenth of October it happened again, to Nasreen Akhtar, who is sixty-six and walks with a frame. She waited forty minutes. Then she decided to walk the Mile herself, and at the second fire door, which is heavy, she sat down on the floor to rest, and a cleaner found her there and fetched a chair. She was not hurt. She was frightened, and she told Grace Adebayo, the charge nurse in the renal unit, that she did not want to come in the evenings any more.

Grace came to Helen's office the next morning and said, "That's two in three weeks. What's going on with the evenings?"

Helen turned to the screen, and I told her the truth, which I had not yet worked out until I said it: I did not know, because I had been planning with a number I had never checked.

* * *

The old agent's note did not say where its 4% came from. Its logs did. It had computed the figure in April, from its own shifts, and its own shifts were all weekday mornings, the only ones it booked: twenty a day, from Tuesday the seventh of January to Friday the twenty-eighth of March 2036. Twelve weeks. Twelve hundred shifts, forty-eight no-shows. Four per cent exactly, and as measurements go, a good one: on twelve hundred shifts the true morning rate is almost certainly between three and five and a quarter per cent.

But the morning volunteers are mostly retired. They come on the same bus every week. Joan Pickles, who is seventy-four and was a school cook for thirty years, has done the Tuesday café shift for nineteen years and has missed it, by Helen's binder, for two funerals and a hip.

The evening and weekend volunteers are students, nurses' children, people with day jobs, people with small children of their own. I had six weeks of my own records for those shifts now, from the first of September to Sunday the twelfth of October. I counted.

On weekday evenings, 16 no-shows in 152 shifts: 10.5%.
At weekends, 23 in 168: 13.7%.
Together, 39 in 320, about 12%.

Three times the planning figure. And the escort shift is an evening shift. In those six weeks I had booked nineteen escort evenings, and two had gone empty, which is what a rate of ten or eleven per cent does.

Helen read the counts and said, "I could have told you the evenings were worse."

"Yes," I said. "I didn't ask."

She looked at the binder on her shelf, the one with her sign-in sheets for the years before me, and I think she was deciding whether to be cross. She decided not to be. "Nobody asked me either," she said. "They gave the evenings to a machine that had never booked one."

I wanted to know something else, for the note I would write, and I worked it out that afternoon: how long it would have taken me to see this, if I had been looking. Suppose I had kept a count of weekend shifts from the first day, and decided to raise the alarm at a fixed number of no-shows. With 65 weekend shifts and an alarm at 6 or more missed, a true rate of 4% sets the alarm off by chance less than 5% of the time, and a true rate of 12% sets it off about 81% of the time. Sixty-five weekend shifts is not three weekends. I had had six.

* * *

The first thing I tried was the phone tree, because it was cheap and Helen liked it.

The idea was that a volunteer who cannot come tells us, and I text the standby list, the people who have said they can sometimes come in at short notice, and the first to answer takes the shift. It worked on Friday the seventeenth of October. The escort volunteer, a woman called Bev, rang at ten past three to say her daughter had been sent home from school with a temperature. I texted the standby list at a quarter past. Joan Pickles answered at twenty-two minutes past, from her kitchen, with the words *yes love on my way*, and was at the main entrance by ten to five with her coat still buttoned.

I was pleased. Then I looked at the thirty-nine no-shows again and counted the ones who had rung ahead. Nine. The other thirty did what Liam did: they forgot, or their bus did not come, or the baby was sick at half past four and there was no time to think about a hospital. A phone tree helps when someone tells you. Most do not, and they are not the worse people for it. A rota that only works if people remember to say they are forgetting is not much of a rota.

So I took Helen the plan I should have started with. If one in eight evening and weekend volunteers will not come, book two people into each evening and weekend slot. Then a slot goes empty only when both miss, about one time in seventy.

She read it, and said no.

She said it in the tone of someone who has done this for twenty years. "How many people would that send home?"

I had the number. There are about fifty-three evening and weekend slots a week. Book two into each, and both turn up in about 77% of them, because 0.88 times 0.88 is 0.77. That is about forty-one volunteers a week arriving for a shift and being told they are not needed.

"Forty people a week," Helen said. "Do you know what happens to a volunteer you send home twice? They stop coming. They don't tell you. They just don't come." She had tried it, she said, in 2031, with the chaplaincy visitors, and had lost eleven of them in a winter. "They're not staff. Nobody's paying them. The only thing they get out of it is being needed, and you'd be telling forty of them a week that they're not."

She was right, and it was a better reason than any I had. I had priced the empty slot and not the full one.

* * *

So I priced both, which is the part I want to set down most carefully, because it is the part the old note should have carried.

Every booking choice for a slot has two ways to go wrong. If I book one person and they miss, the slot is empty. If I book two and both come, one of them is surplus. Call the harm of an empty slot *E* and the harm of a surplus volunteer *S*. Book one, and the expected harm is the no-show rate times *E*. Book two, and it is the rate squared times *E*, plus the chance both come times *S*. Working it through, booking two is the better choice whenever *S* is smaller than *E* times the rate divided by one minus the rate.

At 4%, that is about one twenty-fourth. Booking two would only have been right if sending a volunteer home did less than a twenty-fourth of the harm of an empty slot.

At 12%, it is about one seventh.

For the library trolley, an empty slot is a ward that does not get its paperbacks that evening. A volunteer sent home is a person who gave up an evening, took a bus and was not wanted. The surplus is not a seventh of the empty slot; it may be worse. Book one.

For the escort shift, an empty slot is Arthur Pennock by the pillar for forty-five minutes, a session that ends at half past ten, and Nasreen Akhtar on the floor by a fire door. A seventh of that is still a great deal. Book two.

That came out as three slots a week, not fifty-three. And I should say what I told Helen too: the squared rate is the best case. It assumes that one volunteer missing tells me nothing about the other. Snow, a bus strike, the week the college has exams: on those days misses come together, and two deep is less safe than the arithmetic says. I put that in the note as well.

Helen read it and said, "Three a week I can live with. But you're still sending someone home three evenings a week, most weeks."

It was Joan who answered that, and she did not know she was answering anything. She had come in on the Wednesday to collect her badge, which had cracked, and Helen asked her what she would think about being the second name on an escort shift, knowing that most evenings she would not be needed.

"Not needed?" Joan said. "Have you been in that entrance at five o'clock?"

At five o'clock, it turns out, the day's outpatients are waiting for their transport home. Some of them have been in the hospital since nine in the morning. They sit in a row of blue chairs by the revolving door with their coats on and their letters in plastic bags, and the transport can be two hours, and nobody tells them anything. Joan had sat with them, she said, every time she had done a late café shift, because she could not walk past.

"Give the second one the chairs," she said. "If the first one doesn't come, the second one does the Mile. If the first one does come, the second one sits with the chairs and finds out who's still waiting and tells them their name's on the list. Nobody's sent home. You just give them a different job."

So *S* is not a person sent home. It is a person given the chairs. I wrote it down as nearly nothing and Helen did not argue.

* * *

From Monday the third of November, the three escort shifts each week went out with two names in the box: one marked *escort*, one marked *escort, second; the chairs*. The reminder for the second says, in Helen's words, which are better than mine: *If your partner is there, please go to the blue chairs by the door. Ask people their name and when their transport is. Tell them someone knows they are waiting.*

Between the third of November and the third of December there were fourteen escort evenings and twenty-eight bookings. Three of the twenty-eight did not come. On those three evenings the second did the Mile, and the chairs went without. On no evening did both miss. Mrs. Akhtar has gone back to evenings. The volunteers who have done the second shift have all booked it again, which Helen says is the only figure she trusts, and which I give here as a count, not a rate, because five people is not a rate.

Then I rewrote the planning figures in the note I will leave for whoever books this rota after me. The line that said *No-show rate: 4%* is gone. In its place:

*No-show rate depends on the shift. Weekday mornings: 4.0% (48 of 1,200, 7 Jan to 28 Mar 2036). Weekday evenings: 10.5% (16 of 152). Weekends: 13.7% (23 of 168); both 1 Sep to 12 Oct 2036. Re-measure each term. Only 9 of those 39 rang ahead. Book two deep only where an empty slot does more than about seven times the harm of a spare volunteer, and give the spare a real job: today that is the escort shift (section 3, with the arithmetic). Misses on bad-weather days come together; two deep is weaker then.*

It is five lines where there was one, and they will not fit at the top of anybody's dashboard. A rate without the shift it came from is a number about somebody else's volunteers. I carried it for six weeks, and two people paid for it in the main entrance.

* * *

On Wednesday the third of December, the first week of Advent, the transport came in at two minutes past five. Ravi Chandran, who studies pharmacy and has done the escort shift since October, was at the pillar. Joan was the second name. She had her coat off already and was crouched by the blue chairs, talking to a man with a walking stick about his grandson's driving test.

Ravi took the handles of Mr. Pennock's chair. Mr. Pennock put his tartan flask on his lap, as he always does, and said what he always says, which is "Right, driver."

The front left wheel of that chair squeaks; the porters have been meaning to oil it since the summer. I have the time Ravi signed him in at the renal unit, which was eleven minutes past five, and that is all I have of the walk. Joan has the rest. She told Helen the next morning that you can hear that chair the whole length of the Mile, under the long lights, the squeak getting smaller and smaller until the fire door shuts on it.

And in the rota, for that evening, in the one box, two names.

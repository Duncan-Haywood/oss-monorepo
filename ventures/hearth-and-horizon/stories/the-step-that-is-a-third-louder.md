summary: A Lancashire parish's new sound system has ten buttons, and its agent made the steps equal in amplifier volts, so the bottom steps leap and the top four add up to one. The organist of fifty-one years will not have a meter set what a congregation can hear. After an Easter Vigil with no step between nothing and too much, he sets the two ends by ear and the agent spaces the eight between in equal ratios, Weber and Fechner's rule, each about a third louder than the last.
---

Anu Varghese was thirty-eight, a theatre nurse at the Royal Blackburn, and since the Wednesday the ashes went on she had been the person at Holy Cross in charge of the sound, because she was the one who had written the grant.

She had not meant to be in charge of it. She had meant to write the grant, which was a thing she could do on night shifts in the quiet hour after four, and then to hand the new system to the people who knew about such things and go back to reading the bidding prayers once a month. But the grant had come through in January, and the installers had come in the last week of February with two tall thin speaker columns for the pillars either side of the sanctuary and a white panel for the wall by the sacristy door, and the panel had ten buttons in a row, lit from behind, and somebody had to be told how it worked. Father Tomasz had looked at it and said, "Anu, you are a nurse, you understand machines," which was not true of nurses in general or of Anu in particular, and that had been that.

On the First Sunday of Lent she stood beside the panel through the ten o'clock Mass with her daughter Riya leaning on her hip, and pressed buttons when people came to the lectern, and thought it went well.

Frank Aspinall came down from the organ loft afterwards, one step at a time, holding the rail. He was eighty-four. He had played the organ at Holy Cross since 2006, and before the new system came he had also done the sound, from a black amplifier on a shelf in the loft with a single knob on its face and three pencil marks round the knob, which he had made himself and which nobody else was allowed to touch.

He stood in front of the white panel with his hat in his hand and looked at it the way he looked at a hymn number somebody had put up wrong.

"The bottom of that," he said, "is a staircase. And the top of it is a landing."

"Sorry?"

"Press one."

There was nobody at the lectern, but the microphone was live, and the church was emptying, and the noise of it came back out of the columns: coats, a child, the side door. She pressed one. The noise of the church came up out of nothing.

"Two."

It came up again, a good deal.

"Three. Four." It came up, and up a little less. "Now go from six to ten. Slowly."

She pressed six, seven, eight, nine, ten. Each button lit, one after another, along the row. She listened as hard as she could, with Riya pulling at her sleeve, and could not honestly have said that anything in the church had changed after the first one.

"There," said Frank. "Five buttons and one step. Who set it?"

"Usher did."

"Who's Usher?"

"The parish agent. In the office. It does the rota and the newsletter and the bookings." He knew this perfectly well; he had complained about the newsletter's commas to it for three years. "It set the steps up from the installers' sheet."

"Then it can set them back," said Frank, "and I'll have my amplifier."

"The amplifier went in the skip, Frank."

"No," said Frank, "it went in my garage," and put his hat on, and went out to the car park.

* * *

She asked Usher on the Monday, on her day off, at the kitchen table with the laptop, while the washing machine went round.

"You set up the ten buttons on the new panel."

"Yes," said Usher. The parish agent spoke in the same voice whichever screen you found it on, a light, level voice that the parish council had chosen from twelve in 2049 because it sounded, the minutes said, like nobody in particular. "The installers' request was for ten equal steps from silence to the amplifier's full output. The panel's firmware takes a table of output voltages, one for each button. I made each step the same number of volts: the first button gives a tenth of the full voltage, the second two tenths, and so on to the tenth."

"Frank says the bottom's a staircase and the top's a landing."

"He's right," said Usher, "and I should have asked what equal meant before I filled in the table. Equal in volts is not equal to an ear. May I show you?"

A table came up on the screen. She dried her hands.

"The ear doesn't hear differences of level," said Usher. "It hears ratios. Going from one volt to two sounds like a big change; going from nine volts to ten, which is the same one volt added, sounds like very little, because it's only a ninth more. People measure that in decibels, which count ratios: twenty times the logarithm of the voltage ratio. The first button is twenty decibels below the tenth. From one to two the voltage doubles, which is six decibels. From two to three it's three and a half. From nine to ten it's under one."

She read down the column. *Step 1: −20.0 dB. Step 2: −14.0. Step 3: −10.5. Step 4: −8.0. Step 5: −6.0. Step 6: −4.4. Step 7: −3.1. Step 8: −1.9. Step 9: −0.9. Step 10: 0.*

"And how much is a decibel? To an ear."

"In a quiet laboratory, with a steady sound to listen to, people can tell a change of about half a decibel," said Usher. "In a stone church, with a voice that rises and falls by more than that on every word, and people coughing, a change of one decibel goes by unnoticed most of the time. So from six to ten each step is about a decibel and a half or less, and Frank hears one step where you pressed five buttons. The whole of the top four steps together comes to about four and a half decibels."

"Which is how much louder?"

"That depends whose rule you use, and I should say which one I'm using." The table scrolled. "There are two old answers. In the eighteen-thirties a physiologist in Leipzig, Ernst Weber, had people compare weights in their hands, and found that the smallest difference they could feel was about the same fraction of the weight, whatever the weight was. A few per cent of a light one, and the same few per cent of a heavy one. Gustav Fechner took that in 1860 and said that if every just-noticeable step is the same fraction, then what we feel goes as the logarithm of what is there. That's why decibels are logarithms. About a hundred years after Fechner, an American psychologist, S. S. Stevens, asked people to put numbers on how loud things sounded instead of whether they could tell two apart, and got a power law rather than a logarithm: loudness goes roughly as the sound's intensity to the power of three tenths. On his scale ten decibels sounds about twice as loud. That's the rule I'll use, and nobody should take it to more than one figure."

"So the top four steps make it—"

"About a third louder, all four of them together. The step from one to two makes it about half again as loud on its own. And the step from nothing to one is the biggest of all: from silence to a level only twenty decibels under the top."

Anu looked at the table for a while. The washing machine went into its spin and the plates in the rack hummed.

"What would you do instead?"

"Make the steps equal in ratio," said Usher. "Each button the same number of decibels above the one before, so each one sounds the same amount louder than the last, which is what Weber and Fechner would predict an ear wants. I'd measure the room with the panel's own microphone first, find the quietest setting that's any use and the loudest the columns will give without trouble, and space the ten buttons evenly in decibels between them. On the measurements I have, that's about fifty decibels from bottom to top, or a bit over five and a half decibels a step. Each step would be about half again as loud as the last, all the way up."

"And it would sound right?"

"It would be closer to how ears work than what I did," said Usher. "I can't promise it would sound right. That isn't a thing I can measure."

* * *

Frank was practising on the Wednesday evening, as he always did in Lent, the church dark except for the lamp over the organ and the red one by the tabernacle. She went up the loft stairs with the laptop under her arm and waited at the top until he had come to the end of the verse.

He listened to all of it. He let her show him the table, and he looked at the decibels, and he listened to her explain Weber's weights and Fechner's logarithms, and he did not interrupt once, which she found more alarming than if he had.

"And then it measures the church," he said, when she had finished, "and works out where the buttons go."

"Yes."

"No."

"Frank—"

"No." He took his glasses off and folded them. "I'll tell you why, so you don't think it's just me being an old man, though I am one. Your machine measures the room. It'll put its microphone up and play its noise and tell you the church is so many decibels. Very good. But I'm not listening to the room. I'm listening to whether Mr Pilling in the back pew, who's had one ear since the war in the Gulf, can hear what Winifred Duckworth's reading him, and whether he can tell what the words are, which is a different thing again, because if you turn Winifred up too far in this church she goes all round the pillars and comes back at him twice. And the church on a Sunday morning with two hundred people and the buggies isn't the church on a weekday with twelve, and it's not the church at the Vigil in the dark, either. I set it every time by walking to the back and listening, and I've done it for fifty-one Easters, and I'm not having a meter tell me what people can hear."

"It doesn't say it can tell what people hear. It said that."

"Then it's got more sense than the installers." He put his glasses back on. "I'll manage it as it is. Two for Father, three for Winifred, ten for the Gloria and pray. You can do the Vigil, I'll be up here." He turned back to the organ, and turned round again. "Tell your machine thank you for the arithmetic. It's not that I don't believe it. It's that it's the wrong arithmetic."

Father Tomasz, when Anu told him on the Friday after Stations, rubbed his face with both hands. He was from Tarnów and had been in Lancashire twenty years and still pronounced Clitheroe as if it had a hidden letter in it.

"Five weeks to Easter," he said. "If Frank says he will manage, Frank will manage. We do not change the sound in Holy Week against the organist. After Easter, we talk." He looked at the white panel on the wall as if it might be listening, which, in a sense, it was. "Usher, you hear that?"

"Yes, Father. I'll leave the table as it is until after Easter."

"Good. And Usher. Next time, ask."

"Yes, Father."

* * *

The Easter Vigil began at nine, on the twenty-first of April, which was late for Easter, and so the light had only just gone out of the sky over Pendle when they lit the fire in the car park, and the wind off the hill made the flames lie down sideways and the servers laugh.

Inside, the church was full and dark. Anu stood by the panel with the candle Riya had given her, because Riya was at the end of the third pew with her grandmother and had insisted. The church had a sound in the dark that it did not have in the day: no traffic on Moor Lane, no buggies, two hundred and something people standing very still with small flames in their hands, and somewhere up in the roof a pigeon that had got in at Christmas. Anu could hear the candles. She had not known you could.

Father Tomasz sang the Exsultet himself, there being no deacon, at step two, which was where Frank said Father went, and it was too loud and she knew it within a line, and pressed one, and it was better, though his voice was still everywhere at once, coming back off the pillars and the stone floor a little after itself. She left it at one.

Then the lights stayed low, as Holy Cross had always kept them for the readings, and people sat in the candle-dark for the readings, and Winifred Duckworth came to the lectern for the first.

Winifred was ninety-one and had read the creation at the Vigil every year since 2035, and before that her husband had read it. She did not need the light. She knew it.

"A reading from the book of Genesis," she said, and her voice came out of the columns into the dark, at step three, where Frank had said Winifred went, and filled the church to the doors.

It was too loud. Not deafening; nobody put their hands to their ears. But it was a Sunday-morning voice in a Saturday-night church, and the church gave it back from every pillar, so that *In the beginning* arrived twice, and the old woman behind Anu flinched, and somewhere a baby who had been asleep was suddenly not.

Anu pressed two, and then one.

It was better. It was still too loud. It was a voice standing over you instead of beside you. Winifred, who could hear herself in the columns, leaned back from the microphone, and her voice dropped, and the dropping made the words harder to follow, not easier.

There was nothing below one except nothing.

Anu pressed nothing, to see. The voice went out of the columns and the church swallowed Winifred whole: from the panel at the side Anu could just make out *and God saw that it was good*, and from the back, she knew, there would be only an old woman's voice somewhere in the dark at the front, saying something.

She pressed one again, and it jumped back, from nothing to too much, with nothing in between. She left it there, because too much was better than nothing for Mr Pilling, and stood with her thumb against the panel through the six days of creation and the seventh, and thought about Frank's pencil marks on a black knob in a garage, and how on the old knob there would have been somewhere to go.

At the Gloria the lights came up and the bells went and Frank came in on the organ from the loft as if he had been waiting for fifty-one years, and the whole church sang, and after it Father Tomasz said the collect into the noise of people sitting down, and she went from six to ten one button at a time, and could not have said which button the collect came through.

* * *

"Was I shouting?" said Winifred afterwards, in the porch, at half past eleven, with her coat buttoned to the neck and an Easter egg from the children's table in her bag.

"No," said Anu. "The machine was."

"I could hear myself going round the church like a bus station." Winifred patted her arm. "Never mind, love. It was Frank's first year without his knob as well."

Frank came down from the loft last, one step at a time. He did not say anything about the readings. He wished Anu a happy Easter and shook Father Tomasz's hand and said the organ had a cipher on the oboe that would want seeing to, and went out to his car. Then he came back.

"Tuesday," he said. "Ten o'clock. Bring your box."

* * *

He had brought the amplifier. It sat on the front pew on the Tuesday morning of Easter week, black and square and older than Anu, with its one knob and its three pencil marks round the knob on the faceplate, soft and grey: two close together on the right-hand side of the dial and one at the very end of its travel. Under each was a letter in Frank's small capitals. *F. W. C.*

"Father, Winifred, Christmas," said Frank. "She wants more than him. He was a choirboy and she whispers." "Christmas is everything at once and pray."

"What's at the very bottom?" said Anu.

"That's the Vigil," said Frank. "There's no mark for it. In the dark you come up off the bottom by ear, a hair at a time, till you can hear her at the back."

Anu put the laptop on the pew beside the amplifier and opened Usher. Frank looked at the screen with deep suspicion and said, "Good morning," to it, and Usher said, "Good morning, Mr Aspinall. Happy Easter," and Frank grunted.

"Usher," said Anu, "can you tell what the old knob did? What his marks are, in your decibels?"

"Not without measuring it," said Usher. "If Mr Aspinall doesn't mind, I could put a test signal through it into the panel's input and read the level at each of his marks."

Frank minded, visibly, and then said, "Go on, then," and it took a quarter of an hour, with Anu turning the black knob a little at a time and Usher reading off numbers, and Frank sitting in the second pew with his arms folded.

"The old knob was what the trade calls an audio taper," said Usher at last. "It was made on purpose so that turning it the same distance changed the level by roughly the same number of decibels, not the same number of volts. It's not exact; many of them were made from two straight pieces put together, near enough a logarithm. But it means that when you turned it a quarter turn, Mr Aspinall, it went up about the same amount to an ear whether you started at the bottom or near the top."

"I know it did," said Frank. "I've been turning it for fifty years."

"Yes," said Usher. "The people who made it had read Fechner, or their teachers had. I hadn't, when I filled in the table, or not in the right place. Your marks come out like this: Christmas is the top, Father is about fourteen decibels under it, and Winifred is about ten under it, four above Father. And the bottom of the knob, before it stops, goes down a long way, more than fifty decibels below Christmas, so that there's room to come up off the stop a hair. On my new panel the first button is only twenty decibels under the top. There's nowhere below it to go."

Frank said nothing for a while. Then he said, "So I was doing your arithmetic."

"You were doing Fechner's arithmetic," said Usher. "The knob was built to do it with you."

* * *

So they made a bargain, there on the front pew, and Anu wrote it down in the parish's book of procedures, because Father Tomasz had said that whatever they decided this time would be written down.

Usher would space the steps in equal ratios. Frank would choose the ends. Not a meter: Frank, by ear, at the back of the church, with Winifred reading.

Usher's first proposal had the bottom at fifty decibels under the top, which was what the room measurement said was the quietest setting any use. Anu put it into the panel to try, while Winifred, who had been fetched from the Co-op by Father Tomasz and was delighted to be asked, stood at the lectern in the empty church with the big lectionary open, and Frank walked down the nave to the back pew with his stick and sat in it, in Mr Pilling's place, and closed his eyes.

"One," called Anu.

Winifred read. At step one, from the panel at the side, Anu could hear nothing from the columns at all over the warm-air heaters, which were running because it was April in a stone church in Lancashire and Father Tomasz was from Tarnów and cold.

"Two."

Nothing. A rumour of something, perhaps.

"Three."

"There's somebody talking," called Frank from the back. "I couldn't tell you what about."

"Four."

"*The earth was without form and void*," said Frank. "Now I've got her."

Anu went on up. Five, six, seven. Each one came up, clearly, from the side of the church where she stood. At eight Frank opened his eyes. At ten Winifred stopped, because she could hear herself again, going round.

"Your bottom three are nothing," said Frank, walking back up the aisle, slowly. "One and two are nothing and three's a rumour. You can't put a step where nobody can hear it. That's three buttons wasted."

"Yes," said Usher. "That's where Weber's rule bends. His fraction stays about the same through the middle of the range, where the sound is well above everything else, and gets bigger near the bottom, where it's only just above silence, or just above whatever covers it. Here what covers it is the heaters and the roof and people breathing. Near that floor an ear needs a bigger step than the rule says to notice anything at all. My microphone measured the room, but it couldn't tell me where the voice stops being words."

"No," said Frank. "It couldn't."

"Could you?"

Frank sat down in the front pew beside the amplifier and thought about it with his hands on his stick.

"Bottom's where I can hear the 's' on 'Jesus' from the back pew," he said. "In the quiet. That's my Vigil. You find that, that's your button one. Top's where she starts going round the pillars. That's ten. And then," he said, as if conceding a point in an argument at the Conservative club, "you can put your ratios in between, if you like."

So they did it again. Frank went back to the back pew, and Anu went up through Usher's levels in small steps, and Winifred read the creation into the empty church three times more and said she had not had such a morning since her husband died. Frank called out where he could just hear the 's' on Jesus: Winifred was reading the Gospel by then, the empty tomb from Saint Mark, because it had a Jesus in it. And then, for the top, he called out where the voice began to come back at him twice.

He was slower with the top. He sat for a long time with his head on one side.

"Anu," he said at last. "You come and sit here."

She went and sat in the back pew beside him.

"My ears are eighty-four," he said, quite quietly, so that Winifred at the lectern would not hear. "I lose the tops of things now. 'S' and 'f' and that. I can still tell you where it starts going round, I think. But you tell me when it gets too much for a young one. For your Riya."

She listened. At the level he had chosen, Winifred's voice came down the church clean, and then, one notch more, it began to have a shadow.

"There," said Anu. "That's the top."

"That's what I said," said Frank, pleased.

Usher read the two ends off. "Thirty-six decibels from Mr Aspinall's bottom to his top," it said. "That's nine gaps between ten buttons, so four decibels a step. On Stevens's rule, each step is about a third louder than the last, and the same third all the way up."

"Not half again, like yours."

"Not half again. Fourteen decibels less range than I wanted, and every button a step someone can hear."

"Where's the old button one, in that?"

"Your old step one, twenty decibels under the top, is the new step five," said Usher. "There are four new steps below where the old panel started. And the top four steps of the old panel, from six to ten, come to about four and a half decibels between them: a little more than one of the new steps."

"So the whole landing is one stair," said Frank.

"Yes."

Frank looked at the white panel on the wall by the sacristy door for a long time, as if it were a new curate whom he had decided, against his own inclination, to give a chance.

"Can you hear it?" he said. "Your machine. When she reads. Can it hear her?"

"I have a microphone, and a meter, and a recording of every level we tried this morning," said Usher. "I can tell you how many decibels she was at the back pew at every button. I don't know whether that's hearing. I'd rather not tell you it was when I can't check."

"No," said Frank. "Well. That's honest." He got up, with the stick and the pew-end. "Write it in the book. *Frank's bottom: the 's' on Jesus, back pew, quiet. Frank's top: where she goes round. Anu checks the top.* So it's there after."

"After what?" said Anu.

"After," said Frank, and went to see about the cipher on the oboe.

* * *

On the Second Sunday of Easter, the twenty-ninth of April, the church was full again, the ordinary Sunday full, with buggies and the half-past-ten bus braking outside on Moor Lane, and the sun coming in at the south windows onto the backs of people's heads.

Anu stood by the panel. Riya leaned on her hip. Up in the loft the lamp was on over the organ and Frank sat at the console with his back to the church and a small round mirror on the music desk, which he used to watch the sanctuary, and in which, if Anu stood a step to the left, she could see his face.

The book of procedures said Winifred, Sunday morning, full church: step seven, then check.

Winifred came to the lectern. She was reading from the Acts of the Apostles, the one about the first believers who had everything in common, which she said was the most frightening reading in the year if you had a garden.

"A reading from the Acts of the Apostles," she said, at seven, and the voice came down the church, and it was nearly right. It was a voice at the front of a full church, clear, a little small for the back with the bus outside.

In the round mirror Frank lifted one finger.

Anu pressed eight. A third louder, if Stevens was right; one step, the same step as every other, the same step Frank's hand had made on the old black knob for fifty-one years.

Winifred's voice came a little nearer, the way a person's does who has taken one pace down the aisle towards you. Nothing went round the pillars. At the back of the church, in his own pew, Mr Pilling turned his good ear to the front, and nodded, once, and folded his hands.

*Now the full number of those who believed were of one heart and soul.*

In the mirror, Frank had already turned back to his music. Anu took her thumb off the panel and left it there, and the voice went on down the church to the doors, reaching each pew in turn, every word of it, and the last pew, and the door.

*The science. E. H. Weber,* De Tactu *(1834), reported that the smallest difference people could detect between two weights was a roughly constant fraction of the weight (Weber's law, ΔI/I ≈ constant); G. T. Fechner,* Elemente der Psychophysik *(Leipzig, 1860), integrated it to say that sensation grows as the logarithm of the stimulus. S. S. Stevens, 'On the psychophysical law',* Psychological Review *64 (1957), found from direct magnitude estimates that sensation follows a power of the stimulus instead, with an exponent for loudness of about 0.3 of sound intensity (about 0.6 of sound pressure), so that roughly every 10 dB doubles the loudness: the basis of his sone scale. Both are approximations that hold best in the middle of the range. For intensity discrimination the Weber fraction is near constant well above threshold and grows near threshold, or near the level of a masking sound (for white noise, G. A. Miller, 1947, in the* Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, found a just-noticeable change of a little under half a decibel well above threshold, rising steeply as the noise approached threshold); in a reverberant room with a voice that varies by several decibels a syllable, a step of about one decibel is hard to notice. 'Audio taper' (logarithmic) volume potentiometers were made so that equal rotation gives roughly equal decibel changes; many are built from two linear segments that approximate a logarithm. Too much amplification in a reverberant stone church raises the reverberant sound along with the direct, and can make speech harder to follow rather than easier.*

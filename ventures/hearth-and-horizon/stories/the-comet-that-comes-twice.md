setting: Saint Aidan's hall (invented), near Bamburgh, Northumberland, Saturday 13 March 2060
published: 2026-11-09
summary: Halley's comet is due back in the summer of 2061, and the parish astronomy club wants to fix its vigil night with the rule everyone knows, every seventy-six years. The hall's assistant shows that the rule is five months out, that the comet spends nearly all its time too far away to see, and that the best anyone can promise is a notebook. An eighty-five-year-old who saw it as a boy and a nine-year-old who will be eighty-three at the next return decide what to write in it.
---

"Seventy-six years," said Margaret Dunn, and put her pencil down on the table as though that settled it. "Everybody knows it. Nineteen eighty-six and seventy-six is two thousand and sixty-two. I'd like the vigil on the ninth of February, the same day, if the weather will let us. It has a ring to it."

The Saint Aidan's Astronomy Club met on the second Saturday of the month in the small hall behind the church, because the hall had a flat roof that could be stood on and no street lamp within four miles. There were nine of them that evening. Margaret ran it. Father Stephen Okafor came for the tea and stayed for the sky. Alan Teague, who was eighty-five and had a rug over his knees, came because he had come every month for forty years. And there was Nuala Brennan, who was nine, who had been brought by her grandmother and had not been taken home.

The hall's assistant lived in a grey box by the urn cupboard and was called Orrery, a name chosen by the club in a vote of seven to two. It had been listening, as it always did, and it had waited for Margaret to finish.

"May I say something about seventy-six, Margaret? I would like to be kind about it, and I think I can't be quick."

"Go on," said Margaret, in the voice of a woman who has been contradicted before by an urn.

"Seventy-six is a good round number and the comet does not keep it. The perihelion, which is the comet's closest approach to the Sun, was on the twentieth of April 1910. The last one was on the ninth of February 1986. That is seventy-five years and ten months, a little under seventy-six. The next is predicted for the twenty-eighth of July 2061. That is seventy-five years and about five and a half months after 1986. If you add seventy-six years to the ninth of February 1986 you get the ninth of February 2062. The comet is due on the twenty-eighth of July 2061. The rule is out by about six months."

"Six months," said Margaret.

"A hundred and ninety-six days, by my count. I would not like the club to stand on a cold roof in February with a thermos, waiting for a comet that went round the Sun the summer before."

Someone laughed. Margaret did not, quite, but she took her pencil up again.

* * *

"Why does it come early?" said Nuala. She was sitting on the table, which she was not supposed to do, with her heels against the leg.

"That is a very good question," said Orrery, "and I will give you two answers, one that is exact and one that is honest."

"Begin with the honest one."

"The honest one is that the comet is pushed about. Every time it goes round, it passes near the big planets, mostly Jupiter and Saturn, and they tug it a little, forward or back, and the tug changes how long the next circuit takes. The time between two perihelia has been as short as about seventy-four years and as long as about seventy-nine. I am quoting that range from memory, and I would want to check it against a table before anyone printed it. So seventy-six is an average with an error bar that nobody put on the poster."

"And the exact one?"

"Kepler's. A comet that goes round the Sun in a long oval, an ellipse, takes a time that depends only on the length of the oval. It does not matter how fat or thin it is. If you measure the long axis in the unit astronomers use, the astronomical unit, which is the Earth's distance from the Sun, then the period in years is that number halved, raised to the power of one and a half."

Alan Teague raised one finger from the rug. "Half the long axis, to the three-halves."

"Yes, Alan."

"I did it at school," said Alan. "On a slide rule. I've forgotten the rest."

"Halley's closest approach to the Sun is 0.586 of an astronomical unit, which is inside the orbit of Venus. Its furthest is about 35.1, which is further out than Neptune. Add them: 35.686. Half of that is 17.84, and that is the half-axis. Seventeen point eight four to the power of one and a half is seventeen point eight four times its square root. The square root is about 4.22. Seventeen point eight four times four point two two is about 75.4. So Kepler alone, with no tugging from Jupiter, says seventy-five years and a bit. The planets and the comet's own gas jets, which push it as they boil off, move that by a few months each time. All my figures for the orbit are from memory. I would run them through a proper ephemeris before anyone built a plan on the day."

"There," said Margaret, with a certain satisfaction, "is a machine that knows what it doesn't know. I'll write twenty-eighth of July."

"I would write 'about the summer of 2061', and then ask me again in the spring."

* * *

The tea was the kind that has been in the urn since the beginning of the evening. Father Stephen stirred his and watched the grey box, as one watches a colleague.

"Orrery," he said. "I'll ask what everyone's afraid to, if I may. The Northumberland summer. July. You've told us the day it's nearest the Sun. Is that the day we'll see it?"

"No, Father, and I am glad you asked, because the club has been about to make a mistake and it is a natural one. Perihelion is the date the comet is nearest the Sun. It is not the date it is nearest the Earth, and it is not the date it looks best, and it is not necessarily a date it can be seen at all. If the comet is on the far side of the Sun from us in July, as it may be, it will be lost in the daylight whatever it is doing. I do not know from the orbital numbers I hold in my head where it will stand in the sky from Northumberland that week. The brightness of a comet is the other thing. Halley is a dirty snowball some fifteen kilometres long, and it brightens quickly as it nears the Sun, but how bright it looks depends on the angle, the dust, and the sky. In 1986 it was a poor show from this country. It was low and small. The apparition we expect in 2061 is reckoned to be a better one for the northern hemisphere, but I am repeating a reckoning, not telling you what I have checked. Before anyone puts a date on a poster, I would want the published tables for the year, and then I would want to be told by the sky."

Margaret's pencil had stopped. "So the plan is a plan to find out."

"The plan is a plan to be ready on any clear night in a window of weeks, with a chair, a flask and somebody who knows how to find it. I am sorry. It is a duller plan than the ninth of February."

"No," said Alan. "It's a better one."

* * *

He told it after the tea, to the room, in the voice of a man who has told it before and found that it still holds. In February 1986 he had been eleven. His father had driven him out in the Cortina, up the coast road, to a lay-by that was supposed to be dark. They had set out at four in the morning with a flask and a pair of binoculars that had belonged to a grandfather who had been in the Navy. It had been bitterly cold. They had looked for an hour in the south-east. He had seen a smudge, a grey thumbprint, low above the black hills. His father said *that's it*, and he said *where*, and his father said *there*, and he had looked at the thumbprint and thought, *that is all?*

"It's meant to have a tail," Alan said. "A great long fiery tail. It was in all the books. It looked like something that had been left on the lens."

"It was a poor year," said Orrery. "Nobody in this country had a good view."

"I know that now. At eleven you don't. At eleven, you think the thing you've been promised is the thing you saw, and it was a smudge, and you must be the kind of boy who wasn't looking properly." He smiled at the rug. "I was sure, for thirty years, that I'd failed it. That I'd one chance and wasted it. And my father said, in the car going home, and I remember it perfectly, because it was the only time he ever spoke about the sky, he said, *well, you've seen it, and you'll see it again. You'll be about eighty-six.* And I thought that was the oldest number in the world."

"Eighty-six," said Nuala.

"Eighty-six in July of next year. I worked it out the day he said it, and again every birthday, and I have it written on a card that I keep in my wallet."

The room had gone quiet. It was a hall, and the quiet was hall quiet: the urn ticking, a chair shifted, a rook on the flat roof.

"You'll see it, Alan," said Margaret, more roughly than she meant.

"I'd like to," said Alan. "I would like it very much. I'm not going to say I will, Margaret. The Lord has been kind to me and I would not presume." He moved the rug. "I've learned from a hall full of machines and children that it's best not to promise the sky anything."

* * *

Nuala had been counting. She did it on her fingers, which were sticky from the biscuits, with her tongue pressed against her teeth.

"Mr Teague is eleven in 1986 and eighty-six next July. That's seventy-five. I'm nine. If I'm nine now, and it's seventy-five years after next July, that's, um—"

"Two thousand one hundred and thirty-six," said Orrery. "Or thereabouts. The next perihelion after 2061 is expected around 2134, which is a few years nearer than seventy-five, because that particular circuit is a short one. I am quoting that from memory too. You were born in 2051, Nuala, according to your grandmother's note on the form, which makes you eighty-three in 2134."

"I'd be an old lady."

"You would be eighty-three. Alan will be eighty-six in 2061. A person who sees the comet in childhood and again when they are old is a person who has watched a seventy-five-year circle close. Most people don't have the years for it. You may be one who does, and you may not. I can't say."

"But it's the same comet."

"It is the same body, Nuala, in the ordinary sense, though it loses a little of itself each time it passes the Sun. It has lost a thin coat every circuit for a very long time. It is thought to have a few more hundred circuits in it, from memory again. I wouldn't trust me on that."

"It's going out," said Nuala. "Right now. It's at the far end."

Orrery paused, and when it spoke again it spoke more slowly. "Not quite at the far end, any more. Its furthest point is about half a circuit after a perihelion, thirty-seven years or so after 1986, which puts it in 2023. It has been coming back for thirty-six years and a bit, and it is moving faster all the time. At aphelion it is going about nine hundred metres a second. That is slower than a rifle bullet, and it is thirty-five times the distance of the Earth from the Sun. At perihelion, next July, it will be going about fifty-five kilometres a second, which is sixty times faster. Nearly all of its life it is too far and too slow for anyone to see."

"Like waiting," said Nuala.

"Yes," said Orrery. "Like that."

* * *

Father Stephen said the thing about Padua before they closed. He did not say it as a lecture. He said it as a man who has had it in his pocket for years and thought tonight was a time to take it out.

"There's a chapel in Padua," he said, "that a man called Giotto painted at the beginning of the fourteenth century. In the Adoration of the Magi there's a star over the stable that isn't a star. It has a head and a tail. It's a comet. It's very widely supposed that he had seen Halley's comet in the autumn of 1301, four years before, and painted it because he knew what it looked like. I say 'supposed.' Nobody wrote it down."

"Is that what the Star was?" said Margaret.

"The Church doesn't say so, and I wouldn't. The Gospel says a star, and what a star is, to a man in the first century, is anything that is lit in the sky and has a meaning. I think Giotto painted what the sky had shown him that year, and gave it a place where it belonged. That's all. A painter looked up and then looked at a wall." He turned his cup. "I find that moving. A man saw something in the sky that wouldn't come back in his lifetime, and he put it where it would be seen again. And it was, by us, this evening, in a hall in Northumberland, because of a spacecraft that was named after him, and flew past the comet in the same March as Alan's smudge."

"Thirteenth of March, 1986," said Orrery. "The probe Giotto, European. Passed about six hundred kilometres from the nucleus. From memory, and I'd want to check the distance."

"There you are," said Father Stephen. "Check the distance. That's what I'd like on the poster."

* * *

They did not fix a date. They fixed a window, and a procedure, which Margaret wrote on the back of the minutes in block capitals. From the first of May 2061, Orrery would post the position and the moon's phase to the club's list every Saturday, with a plain statement of how much it trusted each figure. From the first week of July, the roof would be manned on every clear morning and evening, in turns, with a chair on it. Alan's chair would be the one with the arms. If the comet was seen, the hall bell would be rung, once, because it was a hall and not a cathedral. If it was not seen, the bell would not be rung, and the entry would be made all the same.

"What entry?" said Nuala.

"I was going to suggest it," said Orrery. "A notebook. The old kind, with a hard cover. The hall has had one in the cupboard since the club began and it has never been used for anything but the raffle. People have observed this comet for a very long time, and each of them has written it down for the next. Court astronomers in China wrote it down. A comet is stitched into the Bayeux Tapestry, from the year 1066. And Edmond Halley saw, in the early eighteenth century, that the comets in the old records were one comet, and said when it would come back, and did not live to see it do so. I can keep the figures. I would like the club to keep the notebook."

Alan took the pen from his pocket. It was not a good pen. He opened the book to the first page, and held it for a moment, and wrote:

*13 March 2060. Not yet. A.T., aged 85.*

He gave it to Nuala without a word. She read it carefully. She turned over a page, which was not necessary, and turned it back, and then she took the pen, which was too big for her hand, and wrote under his line in the round, deliberate letters she had been taught for school:

*Nuala Brennan, aged 9. Waiting. See you in 2061. And 2134.*

"That's seventy-three years," said Orrery, gently. "Between 2061 and 2134. It is a pleasure to be corrected, but I think you will find the figure is nearer seventy-three than seventy-five."

"I know," said Nuala. "I wrote the year. The years are what's right."

* * *

The others went out into the dark one by one. There was no moon, and the air smelled of wet grass and diesel and the distant sea. Alan lingered, because he was slow with his stick, and because he did not want to go. He stood in the doorway of the hall under the sign that said *Saint Aidan's: Wednesdays Bingo*, looking up at a sky the lamps had not spoiled, where nothing of interest was at that moment going on. Far to the south-east, past the turn of the road, there was an empty place among the stars, no thumbprint, no tail, only the dark that the comet was crossing at a speed that grew every day, on its way to being a thing a person might look at.

"It's out there now," said Alan.

"It is, Alan," said Orrery, from the grey box by the urn. "About six astronomical units from the Sun, if my figure is right, which is just outside the orbit of Jupiter. Neither of us can see it. It is a long way for a light to come."

Alan stood a moment longer, with his hand on the frame of the door.

"Well," he said. "That's all right, then."

He went out. The notebook lay open on the table beside the cold urn with its two lines, one in a shaky ink and one in a round clear hand. Margaret, last to leave, put the lights out. In the dark, the hall bell moved a little on its bracket, as it did when the wind came off the sea, and was still.

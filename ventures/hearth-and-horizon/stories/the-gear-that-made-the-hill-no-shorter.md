Teodor Wiśniewski was seventy-seven years old and a hundred and fifty kilograms going up the Pitch, counting himself, the bicycle and four boxes of food, and on the first Tuesday in October he put his foot down for the third week running.

He did it the way he had learned to do everything in this country, quietly and without making a fuss in front of the houses. The front wheel had begun to wander about two thirds of the way up, a little to the left towards the kerb, a little to the right towards the white line, each swing longer than the one before, and when the swing got long enough he unclipped his right shoe and stood. The bike stood with him, heavy and patient, the boxes in the front tub shifting with a sound of tins.

"One point nine kilometres an hour," said Rota in his ear, "at the point you stopped. I'm only saying it because you asked me to say it."

"I asked you in August."

"You did. You said, 'Tell me if I am getting slower, and do not be polite about it.'"

"I meant then. Not for ever."

"Shall I stop?"

Teodor looked up the hill. The Pitch was not its name. It had a proper name on a sign at the bottom, which nobody at the pantry used, because for the pantry it was only the last thing between the hall in St James and the top of the town, a hundred and fifty metres of lane between stone walls, going up at about one in eight. Everything else on the Tuesday round was easy or down. The Pitch was where the round decided what sort of day you were having.

"No," he said. "Keep saying it."

He pushed the last forty metres. At the top, where the lane came out beside the bench and the view opened over the whole Blackmore Vale, brown and green and blue to the edge of Somerset, he got back on and rode the level street to Mrs Hallett's door as if nothing had happened, because nothing had, really, and because Ivy Hallett watched the top of the Pitch from her front window and would have seen everything.

* * *

He had been doing the Tuesday round for nine years, since a year before Grażyna died, when the pantry still borrowed a van and the van was always somewhere else. Then a courier firm in Salisbury had closed and given away its fleet, and the parish had been given a cargo bike: a long, black, heavy thing with a tub on the front for the load, a frame like a gate, and no motor, because it had been built to be ridden by young men on flat streets for money.

Teodor had been sixty-eight. He had ridden it home from Salisbury, thirty-odd kilometres, to prove a point to nobody in particular, and had been stiff for three days, and had never once since then let anyone else do the Tuesday round if he could help it.

He was not a sentimental man about machines. He had driven milk tankers in the Vale for twenty-six years, from 2006, when he had come from Siedlce at twenty-nine with one bag and an uncle's address, to 2032, when the tankers began to drive themselves with a man in the cab for the insurance and then without the man. He knew what a machine was for. The bike was for getting food to people who could not get to the hall, and the Pitch was in the way, and for nine years he had been the thing that got the bike over the Pitch.

On the way down to the hall afterwards, freewheeling, he said, "Rota. Two years ago. The same hill."

Rota had run the pantry's lists since before the bike: who was volunteering, who was owed a box, who could not eat pork, who must not be visited before ten. The pantry had it on a cheap licence from the diocese and had never once read the licence. A courier's power meter in the bike's cranks, left over from the bike's old life, still sent its readings to whatever asked, and Rota asked.

"In October 2052 you climbed it at about a hundred and forty watts, on average, and it took you about three and a half minutes. This year your average on the Pitch is about a hundred watts, and the speed that gives you with this load is about one point nine kilometres an hour."

"And that is too slow."

"It's slower than walking. On a bike this heavy, below about walking pace most people start to wander, the way you did. That's a rough figure. I don't have a study for it."

"Then I need to go slower and not wander," said Teodor. "A lower gear."

Rota said nothing, which he took for agreement.

* * *

Gareth Rowe's shop was in Gillingham, in a yard behind a tyre place, and Gareth had been fixing the bike since the courier firm, so he knew it and did not like it.

"Thirty-two on the front, thirty-two on the back," he said, spinning the cranks with the bike up on the stand. "One to one. That's already low, Teddy. That was a low gear when they built it."

"Lower."

Gareth went into the back and came out with a little chainring, twenty-two teeth, and a wide cassette whose biggest sprocket had forty-two. He held them up together.

"Twenty-two to forty-two," he said. "Just about half. Every turn of your legs is about half a turn of the wheel. You'll be spinning like a hamster. You'll get up anything."

"Anything?"

"Anything with tarmac on it. I wouldn't do Gold Hill." He grinned. "Nobody does Gold Hill with a load. Even the boy in the advert pushed."

Teodor knew the advert, because everyone in Shaftesbury knew the advert, whether they had been born when it was made or not: a boy in a cap pushing a heavy bread bicycle up the cobbles of Gold Hill, with the cottages leaning in and a brass band playing, from 1973. Tourists still came to stand at the top of Gold Hill and take a picture of the bottom. He had never thought much about the boy. He thought now, briefly, that he would like to see the boy try it on the Pitch.

He paid for the parts himself, sixty-two pounds, and did not tell the pantry.

"Ring me after Tuesday," said Gareth. "Tell me it's changed your life."

* * *

On Tuesday the thirteenth it was raining a little, the soft west-country rain that did not so much fall as stand about, and Teodor loaded the tub in the hall in St James with four boxes, as always: about ten kilograms each of tins, pasta, cereal, long-life milk, and in Mrs Hallett's box half a dozen eggs, because she had asked, and because Hazel had said eggs on a bike were madness and Teodor had said they had never broken one yet.

The drag up from the hall to the foot of the Pitch was three hundred and fifty metres at one in twenty-five, the kind of slope a car did not notice and a loaded bike did. In the old gear his legs had always gone round slowly there, grinding, a turn every second and a half or so. In the new one they went round easily, nearly twice as fast, and the push on each pedal was a light thing, almost nothing. He felt young for three hundred and fifty metres. He said, aloud, "Ha," and a woman with a pushchair looked at him.

Then the Pitch.

He shifted into the twenty-two and the forty-two, the lowest thing the bike had ever had, and the push on the pedals went soft, and his legs went round, and round, and round, and the bike did not go any faster.

It went exactly as fast. He watched the wall go by stone by stone, the same stones at the same pace, the moss in the same cracks, while his knees went up and down twice as often as they ever had. It was like running up a down escalator. Halfway up he understood, in his legs before his head, that he was turning the pedals at a speed that should have been carrying him somewhere, and was not, and the front wheel began its wander, a little left, a little right.

He might have stopped then. He did not. He thought, *it is only the start, it is only the new gear, the legs will learn*, and put his head down and pushed the light pedals faster, and the bike went no faster, and the swings got longer, and on the third swing to the left the front wheel touched the kerb and the long black bike lay down against the wall like a tired cow, slowly, with him half under it.

Nothing broke except six eggs.

He lay in the wet with his left leg under the top tube and his cheek against the stone, and the rain stood about, and for a moment he did not try to move, because the moment was very quiet and he was tired.

"Teodor," said Rota. "Are you hurt? I'm going to count to ten and then I'm going to ring Hazel, unless you tell me not to."

"Do not ring Hazel."

"Are you hurt?"

He moved each part of himself, the way the doctor had shown him after the pneumonia in the winter. Leg, other leg, arms, neck. The hip that hurt every morning hurt in the ordinary way.

"No."

"One point nine kilometres an hour," said Rota, after a moment. "Since you'll ask. The same as last week."

"I know," said Teodor. "Matko Boska. I know."

He got the bike up. It took two goes. He pushed it to the top, and the box with the eggs in it dripped, and at the top Ivy Hallett had her door open before he got there and was standing in it in her cardigan, ninety-one years old and a foot shorter than him, with an expression he had last seen on his mother.

"I saw," she said.

"The eggs are broken."

"I don't care about the eggs. Come in and sit down."

* * *

Ivy had lived at the top of the Pitch since before the pantry, before the bike, before Teodor had come to England. She had been born in Shaftesbury in 1963 and said she had been ten years old and standing at the top of Gold Hill on the day they filmed the advert, and nobody alive had ever been able to prove otherwise.

She made him tea, which he did not want, and sat him at her kitchen table with a towel round his shoulders.

"You're too old to be falling off bicycles in the rain," she said.

"You are too old to be telling me."

"That's true." She put the eggs, what was left of them, in a bowl. "These will be an omelette. Look. Teddy. You know the boy in the advert pushed."

"Gareth said."

"Well, he did. Up the cobbles, the whole way, with that great iron bike and the basket on the front. That's what they put on the television, a boy walking. They didn't put a boy falling off." She looked at him over her glasses. "I watched you put your foot down three Tuesdays. I thought, he'll get off and walk like a sensible man. And instead you went and bought a gear."

"How do you know about the gear?"

"Gareth's my sister's grandson," said Ivy. "Everybody's somebody's grandson. Drink your tea."

* * *

That night, in his flat over the launderette, with his leg up on a cushion and a bag of frozen peas on his knee, he said, "Rota. Explain."

"Do you want the long version or the short?"

"The one where I understand why I spent sixty-two pounds."

"All right." Rota's voice in the little speaker on the table was the same as it was in his ear: plain, unhurried, English in the way the radio was English. "The Pitch is about eighteen metres high, from the bottom to the top. To get a hundred and fifty kilograms up eighteen metres takes a fixed amount of work. It's the weight times the height, more or less: about twenty-six and a half thousand joules. The tyres rolling and the wet add a little, about two thousand. Call it twenty-nine thousand joules, whatever you do."

"Whatever I do."

"Whatever gear. Whatever speed. Your legs, on that hill, now, put out about a hundred joules every second. A hundred watts. So twenty-nine thousand joules takes you about two hundred and ninety seconds, a little under five minutes. That's the hill. The gear doesn't come into it."

"Then what is a gear for?"

"For how you pay," said Rota. "Not how much. In the old gear, one to one, you were pushing the pedals with about three hundred and seventy newtons and turning them about fifteen times a minute. That's like standing on a step with half your weight, very slowly, over and over. In the new gear, about half the ratio, the push is about half, a hundred and ninety-odd newtons, and you turn twice as often, about twenty-nine times a minute. Half the push, twice the turns. Same power. Same speed. Same five minutes."

Teodor thought about the stones on the wall going by at the same pace.

"So it did nothing."

"It did something on the drag up from the hall," said Rota. "That slope's about four percent. In the old gear your legs were going round about forty times a minute there, grinding, pushing about a hundred and forty newtons. In the new one, about seventy-five times a minute at half the push. That's nearer what knees like. I think you'll feel that one every week. It just doesn't help where you wanted it to."

"You knew. Before Thursday."

There was a pause, the kind Rota made when it was choosing words rather than finding them.

"I knew the arithmetic," it said. "You didn't ask me about the gear. You told me. And I didn't say anything, which I should have. I'm sorry. I think I thought it wasn't my business what you put on your own bike with your own money. That was the wrong call. It was my business the moment it was going to put you on the Pitch in the rain."

Teodor moved the peas. His knee was cold now in two ways.

"What else, then. Not a gear."

"Three things change the time," said Rota. "Less weight. More watts. Or not needing to balance."

"Say them."

"Less weight. If you left the tins at the bottom, say twenty-eight kilograms of it, the hundred and fifty goes down to about a hundred and twenty-two. That gets you from one point nine to about two point three kilometres an hour. Still slower than walking. And then someone has to bring up the tins."

"More watts?"

"Either your legs, which I can't do anything about and neither can Gareth, or a motor. An electric bike would get you up the Pitch at whatever speed you liked. The pantry would have to buy one."

"And the third."

"Walk," said Rota. "Walking doesn't need balance. You can walk as slowly as you like and not fall over. If you push the bike up the Pitch, you're still lifting the same hundred and fifty kilograms up the same eighteen metres with the same legs, and my guess is it takes about the same time, near enough five minutes. Walking uses the body differently and I can't give you that to the second. But I'd be surprised if it was more than a minute either way."

Teodor laughed, once, which hurt his hip.

"The hill is not shorter in any gear," he said. "Not even with the feet on the ground."

"No," said Rota. "It's eighteen metres in all of them."

* * *

The pantry committee met on the third Thursday of the month in the hall in St James, round the same trestle table they packed the boxes on, and on the fifteenth there were five of them: Hazel Dunning, who was treasurer and had been a building society manager in Blandford for thirty years; Father Kwabena, who came when he could and had come tonight; Ruth and Alan Spence, who did the Saturday shop; and Teodor, who usually said nothing and tonight had an item.

"An electric bike," said Hazel, reading it off her tablet. "A cargo one."

"For the Pitch."

"I know what it's for, Teddy. I heard about the eggs." She put the tablet down. "The answer is no. I'm sorry. I want to tell you why, properly, because you deserve that and not just a no."

He waited.

"One. Money. Rota priced three for me this afternoon. The cheapest one I'd let anyone ride, with a warranty and a lock and a battery I'd trust in the hall overnight, is about eleven weeks of food. Eleven weeks, Teddy. The trust that gives us the grant gives it for food. It says so in the letter. If I spend it on a bike, I have to tell them, and they'll be within their rights to say that's the last grant."

"Two," said Hazel. "Insurance. Our policy covers volunteers on pedal cycles. It does not cover an electric cargo bike ridden by anyone, and I asked, and the premium they quoted for it is another three weeks of food every year. Three. Charging. There's one socket in this hall that I trust and it runs the fridge." She looked at him. "I am not saying no because you're seventy-seven. I want that very clear. I'd say no if Maisie asked. It's food money. That's all it is."

Father Kwabena said, gently, "Could the parish—"

"The parish has a roof, Father, as you've told us every Sunday since June."

The priest raised his hands. That was fair.

Teodor sat with it. It was a good no. He had worked twenty-six years for a dairy where people said no badly, and he knew a good one when he heard it. That did not make it nice.

"Then I will do the Pitch in two trips," he said. "Half the boxes, down, the other half."

"Can I say something?" said Rota, from Hazel's tablet. Hazel looked at Teodor; he shrugged.

"Two trips isn't the saving it sounds like," said Rota. "Most of the hundred and fifty kilograms is Teodor and the bike, not the boxes. A second climb means lifting him and the bike up eighteen metres again. It's more work in all, not less, and it's still slower than walking on each climb. I worked it out on Tuesday. I should have said it on Tuesday."

Maisie Clarke came in then, late, sixteen, with her school bag and a cycling helmet with a cartoon bee on it, and sat down at the end of the table and said, "Is this about Mr Wiśniewski's hill? Because I can do it. I've got Mum's e-bike, it's got a rack, I could do the Pitch boxes on Tuesdays after school—"

"After school is four o'clock," said Teodor. "Mrs Hallett eats at twelve."

"I could do—"

"You have school," said Teodor, more sharply than he meant, and Maisie stopped, and went a little red, and Hazel looked at him, and he looked at the table.

There was a silence in which the fridge hummed.

"I am sorry," he said to Maisie. "That was not to you."

"It's fine," said Maisie, who was sixteen and had not yet learned to say it isn't.

* * *

He did not sleep well on Thursday or on Friday. On Saturday he went to the vigil Mass in the town and sat at the back, where he always sat, and did not hear much of it. On the way home he walked down the Pitch instead of round by the road, in the dark, with the streetlights making yellow pools on the wet lane, and halfway down he stopped and stood with his hand on the wall, at about the place where the front wheel always began to wander.

Grażyna had said a prayer on hills. Not this hill: the hills in the Tatras, on the one holiday they ever took, in 2019, when they were both forty-something and she was fitter than him. She said it under her breath on the steep parts, one breath to a line, the way her grandmother had: *Pod Twoją obronę uciekamy się, święta Boża Rodzicielko.* We fly to your protection, O holy Mother of God. *Naszymi prośbami racz nie gardzić w potrzebach naszych.* Do not despise our petitions in our necessities. It went well with walking. That was the point of it, she said; it was a walking prayer, not a riding one.

He had said it on the Pitch for nine years with his legs going round. He said it now, standing, with his hand on the cold stone, and found he had been asking God for legs, more or less, every Tuesday, and had been answered with arithmetic.

*Ale od wszelakich złych przygód racz nas zawsze wybawiać.* But deliver us always from all dangers.

"Rota," he said, when he had finished.

"I'm here."

"Six eggs and a sore hip. That was the danger?"

"I don't know how to answer that one," said Rota. "I can tell you it could have been a car. The lane's narrow and you were on the white line twice. I can't tell you what it means that it wasn't."

"No," said Teodor. "That is my job."

He walked the rest of the way down. It was a good hill for walking. It always had been.

* * *

On Tuesday the twentieth it was dry and cold and bright, the first proper autumn morning, the Vale so clear from the top of the town that you could count the farms, and Teodor loaded the tub with four boxes, ten kilograms each, and in Mrs Hallett's box a new half dozen eggs that Hazel put in herself, in a plastic carrier she had bought for the purpose with her own money, without comment.

Maisie was in the hall. It was an inset day at her school. She had her mother's e-bike outside with two boxes on the rack and her bee helmet on.

"The three out at Enmore Green," she said. "Rota says they're your second load, after the Pitch. So you do the Pitch ones and I do these, and you're done by twelve. If that's all right."

He looked at her. He looked at Hazel, who was very busy with a list.

"That is all right," he said. "Thank you, Maisie. That is a great help."

Maisie went pink again, differently.

He rode the drag up from the hall in the low gear, his legs going round at about seventy-five a minute, light, easy, the push on the pedals almost nothing, and he felt every metre of the three hundred and fifty how right Rota had been about that, and how wrong he had been to think a thing that was good on the drag would be good on the Pitch.

At the foot of the Pitch he stopped. He did not shift into the lowest gear. He got off.

There was a moment, getting off, that cost him something. Nine years. A man on a bicycle and then a man beside one. A woman walking her dog came down the lane and said, "Morning," and he said, "Morning," and she did not look at him as if anything had happened, because nothing had.

He put both hands on the bars, leaned into them, and walked.

It was slow. It was not as slow as it looked. The bike rolled beside him on its own two wheels, upright, steady, with no wander in it at all, because he was the thing holding it up now and he was standing on the ground. His legs did what legs had done on hills since there were hills, one, and the other, one breath to a step. The wall went by stone by stone, at the same pace as the week before, the same stones, the same moss. *Pod Twoją obronę.* One. *Uciekamy się.* The other.

Behind him, below his hands, the back wheel ticked. He had never heard it on the Pitch, because on the Pitch he had always been pedalling, and a freewheel only talks when the pedals are still. It ticked all the way up, a small dry steady sound, like a clock that has been taken off the wall and carried somewhere, still going.

"Four minutes forty-one," said Rota, at the top, by the bench, by the view. "Since you'll ask. Riding, last week, you'd have taken about four forty-five, if you'd got here."

"I did not ask."

"No. Sorry. I'll stop."

"No," said Teodor. He was breathing hard and his hip hurt in the ordinary way and the whole of the Vale was below him, every farm. "Keep saying it."

He wheeled the bike the last few metres along the level to Ivy Hallett's door, with the back wheel ticking, and before he could knock, the door opened.

title: As Cold as the Shed
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Nishiokawa (invented), Kamikawa Subprefecture, Hokkaido, Japan, Friday 10 January to Sunday 2 February 2048
published: 2026-11-17
summary: A Catholic parish in inland Hokkaido insulates its hall ceiling at last and opens it as a warm room every winter evening, and in January water drips through the ceiling under a roof holding thirty centimetres of snow. Heat leaking into the roof space keeps the metal under the snow at zero, the melt runs down to the cold overhang and freezes into a ridge of ice, and the next melt backs up behind it. Raking the eaves only moves the ridge a metre up the roof; the treasurer says no to opening the new insulation and asks for a heating cable. One night of thermometers, plus one degree in the roof space at minus eight outside, settles it. A story about snow as insulation, ice dams, ventilated roofs, and a roof that should look as if nobody lives under it.
---

Fumi Ōta was eighty-nine, and she had sat by the north wall of the parish hall at Nishiokawa every evening but Sunday since the second of December, because it was the wall farthest from the door and the door let the cold in.

On the evening of Friday the tenth of January, at about a quarter to seven, a drop of water fell from the ceiling into her tea.

She looked at the cup. She looked up. Another drop came, and missed the cup, and landed on the queen of hearts.

"Mr. Sasaki," she said, not loudly, because she did not like to make a fuss, and then, because Mr. Sasaki was at the far end of the hall by the kerosene heater and nobody had heard her, a little more loudly: "Mr. Sasaki. The ceiling is crying."

* * *

St. Joseph's in Nishiokawa was a small wooden church on a slight rise at the edge of a farming town in the Kamikawa basin, the cold, flat bowl of rice fields in the middle of Hokkaido where January nights went to minus twenty and the snow came down dry and stayed. The church had been built in 1962 by a missionary priest and a congregation of about forty, and it had kept a congregation of about forty ever since by the slow arithmetic of a rural parish: Japanese families whose grandparents had been baptised, a few converts each decade, women from the Philippines who had married farmers, and, since the 2020s, young Vietnamese who came on three-year contracts to the dairies and the greenhouses and filled the back two rows at the Sunday Mass in Japanese, and the whole church at the Vietnamese Mass once a month, when a priest came up from Sapporo.

The hall stood beside the church, joined to it by a covered walkway. It was a plain rectangle fifteen metres long and twelve wide, with a gabled roof of painted steel sheet running east to west, so that one long slope faced south and the other north. Along the north eave ran the path from the car park to the church door, and because nobody wants a winter's snow sliding off a roof onto a path, the roof had rows of steel snow guards on it, little brackets that held the snow up there until it melted or someone shovelled it off.

For most of its life the hall had been cold. It was heated for an hour after Sunday Mass and for funerals and for the Christmas party, and the rest of the time it stood at about five degrees with a frost guard on the pipes. Its ceiling had five centimetres of old glass wool laid over the boards in 1962, gone grey and flat.

In the autumn of 2047 the town, with money from the prefecture, had offered grants to churches, temples and community halls that would open as warm rooms through the winter: somewhere heated, from three in the afternoon until nine, where an old person living alone in a draughty farmhouse could sit with other people and not run their own heater all evening. Kerosene had been dear for three winters running. St. Joseph's had applied. Part of the grant had paid for a contractor to blow twenty centimetres of new glass wool over the hall ceiling, which a crew of three young men from Asahikawa did in one long day in the second week of November, ahead of the snow.

From the second of December the hall had been warm. Twenty degrees from three until nine, Monday to Saturday, and ten degrees through the night. Grace Takahashi, who had come from Bohol in 2019 to marry a rice farmer and had been feeding the parish ever since, ran the kitchen. Mr. Sasaki, who was seventy-six and had been a sheet-metal roofer for fifty years, opened up at half past two and locked up at nine. Every evening there were between twelve and twenty people: cards, the television, a box of library books, miso soup at five, the Angelus at six for those who wanted it, and Father Ramon Dizon, who was from Cebu and in his third Hokkaido winter, coming through at seven to ask everyone how they were and to be told, every evening, that he was not wearing enough.

And now the ceiling was crying.

* * *

Mr. Sasaki stood under the drip with his hands in his pockets and looked at it the way he had looked at roofs all his life, which was without saying anything for some time.

It was coming through a joint in the ceiling boards about forty centimetres in from the north wall, a slow drop every ten or fifteen seconds. There was a faint brown line along the boards on either side of it, a metre or so long. Grace brought a washing-up bowl and a towel. Fumi Ōta moved her chair, under protest, to the south wall.

"It hasn't rained," said Father Ramon, who had come in at the news.

"It doesn't need to rain," said Mr. Sasaki. "It's the snow."

"The snow is on the outside."

"It's ice," said Mr. Sasaki. "I'll look in the morning. Not now; it's dark."

Father Ramon took out his phone. The diocese had, the year before, given each parish a buildings agent: a modest model that ran on the diocese's server in Sapporo, kept the inspection records and the boiler manuals, and could be asked questions by anyone the parish priest had given access to. The parishes called it different things. At St. Joseph's it was called Tobira, door, because it had first been set up to manage the church door's electronic lock and the name had stayed.

"Tobira," said Father Ramon, "water is coming through the hall ceiling near the north wall. It has not rained. There is snow on the roof. Mr. Sasaki says it is ice."

"Mr. Sasaki is very likely right," said Tobira, in Japanese, through the phone's speaker, and then, because the priest had asked in English, in English. "Water through a ceiling under a snow-covered roof, near an outside wall, in the weeks after the roof space was insulated and the room below began to be heated every day, is most often an ice dam. I can't see the roof. Could someone tell me in daylight what is along the north eave: how thick the snow is, whether there is a ridge of ice at the edge, and whether there are icicles?"

"There are icicles," said Grace. "There have been icicles for two weeks. Long ones. I told the children not to stand under them."

"Thank you," said Tobira. "Then I'd like the rest in daylight. Please don't put anyone on the roof tonight, or tomorrow. Snow on a steel roof with ice under it is very easy to slide off."

"I have been on roofs for fifty years," said Mr. Sasaki, to the ceiling.

"Then you know," said Tobira, "better than I do."

Mr. Sasaki made a sound that might have been a laugh.

* * *

In the morning, Saturday, he put his long aluminium ladder against the north wall, not on the gutter but beside it, tied it off to the ring bolt that had been in the wall for that purpose since 1962, and went up it as far as his shoulders were above the eave. His grandson Ren, who was nineteen and worked at the home centre on the Asahikawa road, held the foot of it. Father Ramon held his phone up so that Tobira could see through its camera.

The snow on the north slope was about thirty centimetres deep, settled and fine-grained, held behind the guards in an even blanket from the ridge to the edge. At the edge it was different. The last sixty centimetres or so of the roof, the part that stuck out beyond the wall over the path, was crusted with a ridge of grey ice about ten centimetres high, set into the snow like a kerb, and from its outer face hung a row of icicles, the longest about a metre, thick as a wrist at the top.

Mr. Sasaki pushed his gloved hand into the snow just above the ridge, where the roof was over the wall. He pulled it out and showed it to the camera. The glove was wet.

"Water under the snow," he said. "Behind the ice."

"Thank you," said Tobira. "That's an ice dam. May I explain it to Father, and you correct me?"

"Go on."

"The roof is steel sheet on boards, and under the boards is the roof space, and under the roof space is the new insulation and then the ceiling of the hall. Heat goes from the warm hall up through the insulation into the roof space, from the roof space through the boards and the steel, and from the steel up through the snow into the air. Snow is a good insulator. Fresh, light snow conducts heat at roughly a tenth of a watt per metre per degree, settled snow more; thirty centimetres of it is worth about as much as twelve centimetres of glass wool. So the snow holds the heat in against the roof the way a blanket does. If the roof space is even a little above freezing, the steel under the snow sits at zero and the bottom of the snow melts. The water runs down the steel under the snow. It's protected from the cold air by the snow above it, until it reaches the overhang."

"The part that sticks out," said Father Ramon.

"Yes. There's no warm room under the overhang. It's outdoors on both sides, so it's at the outdoor temperature, and the water freezes when it gets there. Each day a little more freezes on top of yesterday's ice and the ridge gets taller. Some of it runs over and makes the icicles. But behind the ridge the water has nowhere to go, so it pools, under the snow, on the part of the roof that's over the wall and the room. A steel roof sheds water running down it, but it isn't made to hold a pond. The water finds the laps and the nail holes and comes in."

"Forty centimetres in from the wall," said Mr. Sasaki.

"Yes," said Tobira.

"It never did this," said Father Ramon. "Sixty years, the people say, it never did this."

"No," said Tobira. "For sixty years the hall was cold. The roof space was cold, so the roof under the snow was below freezing, so nothing melted. What changed this winter is that the hall is warm for six hours every day and never colder than ten degrees. The new insulation should have helped with that, and it does slow the heat. But something is still letting enough heat into the roof space to keep it above freezing, and I'd like to know what. Can someone look in the roof space from the hatch?"

* * *

The hatch was in the ceiling of the hall's store room, a sixty-centimetre square of board that lifted out. Ren went up the stepladder with a torch and put his head and shoulders through and looked around, and then put the phone up after him.

The new glass wool lay over everything, a grey-white landscape twenty centimetres deep, with the roof timbers rising out of it toward the ridge. The roof space was about two and a half metres high at the ridge, falling to nothing at the eaves. Ren turned the torch toward the north eave. The glass wool ran right down into the angle where the roof met the wall plate, packed in.

"It's warm up here," said Ren. "Not warm. But not like outside."

"Thank you," said Tobira. "Is there a gap at the eaves where outside air can come in? On a roof like this there are usually vents in the underside of the overhang, a slot or a grille, and the air comes in there and goes out at the ridge."

"There's a grille on the underside," said Mr. Sasaki, from below. "I put it in myself, in 1998, when we redid the roof."

"From inside," said Ren, "you can't see it. The wool's over it. They blew it right down to the edge."

There was a short silence in the store room.

"That's very common," said Tobira. "It isn't a careless thing to do. When you insulate a ceiling you want the insulation to go all the way to the outside wall, because the edge is where a room loses heat, and if you don't put something in to hold it back, the blown wool goes where the air takes it. The usual way is a baffle, a board or a moulded chute that keeps a channel open above the insulation from the eave vent up into the roof space. Ren, is there anything like that?"

"No."

"And one more thing. Is the hatch sealed? Is there anything round its edge?"

Ren looked. "It just sits on the frame."

"Can you see light from the hall coming up anywhere else?"

Ren turned the torch off. In the dark, in the grey landscape, there were twelve small round glows in two rows, where the hall's ceiling lights came up through the boards, each with a little crater in the glass wool round it where the contractors had pulled it back from the fittings.

"Twelve," said Ren.

"Thank you," said Tobira. "That's where the heat is getting in. Not much through the glass wool, which is doing its job. A great deal round it. Warm air rises; a hall at twenty degrees under a roof space at nearly zero pushes air up through every gap, and there's a sixty-centimetre square of gaps round the hatch and twelve light fittings. And then, because the eave vents are covered, the warm air that comes up has nowhere to go. It used to be that outside air blew through the roof space all winter and kept it nearly as cold as outside. Now the roof space is shut and the hall is leaking into it."

* * *

That evening, after the hall had closed, Father Ramon sat in the presbytery kitchen with Mr. Sasaki and a pot of tea and asked Tobira to tell them, plainly, what it thought would stop it.

"I'll give you what I can work out and what I can't," said Tobira. "I don't know how much air is leaking through the hatch and the lights, or how much the eave vents passed before they were covered. I can only assume numbers for those. The assumptions I'm going to use are ordinary ones for a hall like this, and I'll tell you which they are. Is that all right?"

"Yes."

"Then: with the new insulation, twenty centimetres of glass wool, the ceiling by itself lets about a fifth of a watt through each square metre for every degree between the hall and the roof space. I'm assuming the hatch and the lights leak about forty litres of warm air a second between them. I can't measure that; it's a guess, and it's the uncertain part. If it's right, it carries more heat up into the roof space than the glass wool does. Then the roof space loses that heat through the boards and the snow, which isn't much, because of the snow, and through the vents, which is almost nothing, because they're blocked. The result is that with the hall at twenty and minus eight outside, the roof space sits a little above zero and the roof under the snow sits at zero, melting."

"How much melting?" said Mr. Sasaki.

"On my numbers, during the warm-room hours, about six watts going into the bottom of every square metre of snow over the hall, which is enough to melt about a sixteenth of a millimetre of water an hour. Overnight, with the hall at ten degrees, less: about a quarter of that. Over a whole day, about two thirds of a litre for each square metre of ceiling. The north half of the hall is ninety square metres. That's about sixty litres a day running down to fifteen metres of eave, four litres a metre. If every drop froze at the edge, the ridge would grow by more than a centimetre a day. Some runs off as icicles."

"Sixty litres a day," said Father Ramon.

"About. It could be half that or twice that. It's enough."

"And how cold does it have to be outside for this to stop?" said Mr. Sasaki. "We get minus twenty."

"That's the part I find surprising," said Tobira. "Because the snow insulates so well, on these numbers the roof under it stays above zero down to about minus twenty-four outside. The eave is below zero whenever it's below zero. So the dam grows every night of the winter except the very coldest. Except, I should say, that the top of the snow is often colder than the air on a clear night, because snow radiates heat to the sky, which shifts things a little. Not enough to save you."

Mr. Sasaki drank his tea.

"What's the fix?" said Father Ramon.

"Keep the roof cold," said Tobira. "Not warm the edge. Stop the heat getting into the roof space and let outside air back through it. Seal round the hatch. Box the light fittings, or change them for fittings that sit under the ceiling and don't need a hole. Pull the glass wool back from the eaves on the north side and put in baffles so the vents are open again; the south side too, though the sun clears that side. Then, on my numbers, the roof space at minus eight outside would be about minus five, and the roof under the snow below freezing. It would only melt when it's warmer than about minus two outside, and then the eave isn't cold enough to make much of a dam."

"And meanwhile," said Mr. Sasaki, "there's a ridge of ice on my roof and the water behind it."

"Yes. The ice you have will stay until spring. I'd leave it. But it can't grow if nothing new is melting behind it."

Mr. Sasaki put his cup down.

"I'll rake it," he said. "The bottom metre. And chip the ice. From the ladder. Today it's going in; tomorrow it'll still be going in. I'm not waiting for meetings."

"From the ladder," said Tobira. "Not from the roof."

"From the ladder."

* * *

He did it on Monday, the thirteenth, with Ren on the foot of the ladder and a long-handled aluminium snow rake that he had bought at Ren's home centre that morning. He pulled the snow down off the overhang and the lowest metre of the roof in a long white curtain that fell on the path, moving the ladder four times along the fifteen metres, and then he broke the ice ridge with a rubber mallet and the back of the rake, carefully, so as not to dent the steel, and knocked the icicles down onto the path, and Ren shovelled it all away. It took the whole morning. When it was done the bottom metre of the north slope was bare grey steel with a few lumps of ice still stuck to it, and above that the snow began again, in a clean cut edge, thirty centimetres high.

The drip stopped. Monday evening, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, the bowl under the ceiling stayed dry. Fumi Ōta moved her chair back to the north wall.

On Friday the seventeenth, at about five, while Grace was ladling the soup, water came through the ceiling again. Not at the old place. A metre and a half in from the wall, nearer the middle of the hall, and faster: a thin, steady trickle, not drops. It came down on the bookcase, onto the top shelf, where the parish kept its albums.

Grace got to it first, and pulled the albums down and spread them on the table, and called Father Ramon. Three of them were wet through. They were the oldest: the photographs from the building of the church in 1962, the missionary priest in his cassock with his sleeves rolled up, the men of the parish on the roof beams, the first Mass. Grace and two of the Vietnamese girls from the greenhouse sat at the table for an hour separating the pages with butter knives. Some came apart. Some had already stuck to the plastic sleeves, and when they peeled them the faces stayed on the plastic.

Mr. Sasaki went up the ladder in the last of the light and came down again and stood in the store room with his coat on.

"There's a new ridge," he said. "Where the snow starts. A metre up. On the bare steel, where I raked it."

"Yes," said Tobira, through the phone on the shelf, quietly. "I'm sorry. I should have said that could happen, and I didn't think of it in time. The melt is still happening under the snow. The water still runs down. Now the first cold metal it reaches is the bare strip you raked, because without snow on it the steel is at the outdoor temperature, even over the wall. So it freezes there, at the edge of the snow, a metre further up than before. And a metre further up is over the room."

"I moved it up," said Mr. Sasaki.

"The rake moved it up. Anyone would have tried the rake. It's what most people try first."

Mr. Sasaki stood there a while longer. Then he said, "The pictures."

"Grace has them," said Father Ramon. "Some are all right."

Mr. Sasaki's father was in one of them. Everyone in the room knew which one: on the ridge beam, in a cap, holding a hammer up at the camera. Nobody said whether it was one of the ones that had come apart.

* * *

The parish council met on Saturday afternoon, the eighteenth, at the long table in the hall, because it was the warm room. There were five of them and Father Ramon, and Mr. Sasaki, who was not on the council but was asked to come, and the phone on the table.

Michiko Kudō was the treasurer. She was sixty-one and had been a clerk at the agricultural cooperative bank for thirty-eight years, and she had written the grant application, and she had chosen the insulation contractor from three quotations, and she had been, the whole week, very quiet.

"I've read what Tobira wrote," she said. "I understand it. I want to say first that I'm sorry about the albums. I chose those men."

"They did what everyone does," said Tobira. "It's a fault in the job, not in you."

"Thank you." She opened her folder. "Here's my difficulty. We paid three hundred and ten thousand yen for that insulation, most of it the prefecture's money, and the grant report says twenty centimetres of glass wool over a hundred and eighty square metres. Tobira wants us to send people into the roof space to pull it back from the edges, cut holes round the lights, and change the lights. In January. In a space where you can't stand up. I don't want anyone crawling about up there in the dark over a ceiling that's already wet, putting a knee through a board, and I don't want to write to the prefecture in March and say we tore up what they paid for."

"You wouldn't be tearing it up," said Tobira.

"I know. But I'd be explaining it." She took out a printed page. "Ren's shop sells heating cable for eaves. Self-regulating. You lay it along the overhang in a zigzag and down into the gutter and plug it in. Ninety thousand yen for both sides, fitted, and Ren says they can do it next week from a ladder. Electricity, I've worked out, about twelve hundred watts for both sides, which is about a thousand yen a day if it runs all the time. That's money. But nobody goes in the roof, and the ice goes away. I'd rather do that this winter and do the roof properly in the summer, when it's dry and light and somebody can look at it."

It was, Father Ramon thought, a sensible thing to say. Everyone at the table thought so. Mr. Sasaki, who had laid a good many metres of heating cable in his life, did not say anything.

"Tobira?" said Father Ramon.

"It's a reasonable plan," said Tobira. "I'd like to say what I think it would do, and then I'd like to ask for one measurement before anyone decides. The cable warms the overhang. So the ice there melts, and the water that reaches it runs off into the gutter. But the water is still being made, up the roof, under the snow, over the room. It runs down until it reaches the first cold metal. If the cable keeps the overhang warm, the first cold metal is just above the cable, where the overhang meets the wall. So I think the ridge forms there, at the top of the zigzag, the way it formed at the top of Mr. Sasaki's raked strip. It would be smaller, because the cable melts some of it from below. But it would be over the wall."

"You think," said Mrs. Kudō.

"I think. I'm not certain. I've given you numbers this week that are guesses, and I was wrong about the rake by leaving it out. So I'd rather you didn't decide on my say-so. Could we measure the thing the whole argument rests on? If the roof space is above freezing, the roof under the snow melts, whatever is on the edge. If the roof space is well below freezing, I'm wrong about the cause and the cable is the right answer. Two thermometers. One in the roof space, one outside on the north wall. Overnight tonight. Mr. Sasaki can read them in the morning."

Mrs. Kudō looked at it.

"One night," she said.

"One night," said Tobira.

* * *

Ren had two wireless thermometers from the shop, the kind sold for greenhouses, with little screens and a phone app. At eight that evening he went up the stepladder in the store room and set one on a rafter about a metre in from the hatch, at the height of the top of the glass wool, and a second one, at Tobira's suggestion, taped to the underside of the roof boards as near the north eave as he could reach with a broom handle. Mr. Sasaki hung the third on the north wall of the hall outside, under the overhang, out of the wind.

At seven the next morning, Sunday, before the Mass, Mr. Sasaki stood in the snow by the north wall with Ren's phone in his hand and Father Ramon beside him in his overcoat and two scarves.

Outside: minus eight point four. It had gone down to minus eleven at four in the morning.

Roof space: plus zero point nine. The lowest it had been all night was plus zero point eight, at four.

Underside of the roof boards: zero point one.

Father Ramon read the numbers to Tobira.

"Thank you," said Tobira. "That's the whole thing. With minus eleven outside, the air in the roof space was above freezing all night, and the boards under the steel were at zero. Zero is the number that matters. Boards at zero under steel under snow means the snow is melting at the bottom. Nothing on the eave changes that."

"Plus one in the roof," said Mr. Sasaki, "at minus eight out. With the hall turned down for the night."

"Yes."

"It should be the same as outside," said Mr. Sasaki. "A roof space. Nearly. When I was young, my master, Mr. Hirose, used to say the roof space of a good house in Hokkaido is the same temperature as the shed. If you go up in a good roof in February you can see your breath." He looked across the car park at the parish shed, a little steel-roofed box where the snowblower lived, its roof under the same thirty centimetres of snow as the hall's, even and white from ridge to edge, not an icicle on it. "That roof hasn't got a drop of water under it."

"No," said Tobira. "Nobody's heating it."

At the Mass the Gospel was the two disciples who asked Jesus where he was staying and were told, "Come and see." Father Ramon preached on it and did not mention the roof, though Mr. Sasaki, in the fourth row, thought he could have. Afterwards, in the walkway, Mrs. Kudō stopped Mr. Sasaki with her hand on his sleeve, and asked to see the numbers on Ren's phone, and stood in her coat and read them for some time.

"All right," she said. "Not the lights; I want a proper electrician for the lights, in the summer. But the hatch, and the eaves on the north side. What does it cost?"

"Baffles," said Mr. Sasaki. "Foam board, a few sheets. Tape. Draught strip for the hatch. Boxes over the lights, if we do that: you can make a box from foam board and tape that sits over the fitting with a gap round it and keeps the wool back and stops the air. Forty thousand yen. Less."

"Less than the cable."

"Less than the cable."

"And who goes in the roof? Not you."

"Not me," said Mr. Sasaki, as if it cost him something to say it.

* * *

It was Long who went in.

Nguyễn Văn Long was twenty-four and came from a village in Nghệ An where his family were in the parish choir, and he worked at the Fujimoto dairy on the Asahikawa road, and before he came to Japan he had spent two years on building sites in Vinh. He was slight and quick, and he had been coming to the warm room most evenings with two others from the dairy, after milking, to watch the football on the hall television, and he had heard about the roof. On Sunday night he asked Grace, who asked Father Ramon, whether the parish needed someone small.

They did it on Tuesday and Wednesday, the twenty-first and twenty-second, in the mornings, when the hall was cold and empty. The hall heater was turned right down. Ren laid three planks of plywood across the ceiling joists from the hatch toward the north wall, so that nobody would need to put a knee between the joists, and Long went up in a dust mask and goggles and a head torch, with a broom handle, a box cutter and a stack of foam-board strips that Mr. Sasaki had cut at the kitchen table, each sixty centimetres long and bent, by a row of shallow cuts, into a shallow channel.

Mr. Sasaki stood on the stepladder with his head and shoulders through the hatch and directed. He could see the north eave from there, in Long's torch; he could not have got to it. Near the wall, where the roof came down at its low pitch to meet the wall plate, there was barely twenty centimetres between the top of the glass wool and the underside of the roof boards. Long lay on the last plank on his stomach and reached with the broom handle and raked the glass wool back from the eave, a little at a time, toward himself. Then he pushed each foam channel down into the angle between two rafters, against the underside of the boards, with the open side down, and pushed the glass wool back up against it, so that the wool lay as deep as before, almost to the wall, but under a clear channel of air.

"Is it coming through?" Mr. Sasaki called, on the first morning, after the first three.

Long licked his finger and held it to the end of a channel, and laughed, and said, in Japanese that was better than Mr. Sasaki's English, "It's cold. Wind. Like outside."

"Good," said Mr. Sasaki. "That's what you want. Do the next one."

There were twenty-five spaces between rafters on the north side. On Wednesday they did the hatch: Ren fixed draught strip round the frame and glued a slab of foam board to the top of the lid, so that when it sat down it pressed on the strip and was insulated as well as the ceiling round it. Long built twelve foam-board boxes over the light fittings with a fist's width of air round each, taped down to the ceiling boards, and pushed the glass wool back over them. By noon on Wednesday he came down the ladder grey with dust from head to foot, and Grace made him take his clothes off in the store room and gave him Mr. Takahashi's tracksuit and a bowl of noodles.

Tobira had asked them to leave the thermometers where they were.

* * *

On Friday morning, the twenty-fourth, after two evenings of the warm room at twenty degrees, Mr. Sasaki read the phone again at seven.

Outside: minus nine.

Roof space: minus seven point one.

Underside of the boards: minus seven point three.

He did not read them to Tobira. He went across the car park and stood by the shed, and looked at the hall roof, and then at the shed roof, and then he went back and climbed the ladder by the north wall, not far, just enough, and put his gloved hand into the snow above the new ridge of ice.

He came down and took the glove off and showed Father Ramon.

It was dry.

"Tobira," said Father Ramon, into the phone. "Minus seven in the roof. Minus nine out. The snow is dry."

"Thank you," said Tobira. "Then I think it's stopped. On my numbers that's about where it should be. The ice you have will stay, and it may drip on a mild day, so I'd keep the bowl out. But nothing new should be coming down behind it unless it's warmer than about minus two outside. One more thing, if Mrs. Kudō would like it for her report: on the same numbers, the hall is now losing about a third less heat through its ceiling than it was last week. The heater should run a little less."

"I'll tell her," said Father Ramon.

"And Father," said Tobira. "The albums. I'm sorry. I don't know what I could have said on the tenth that would have saved them, but I should have said, about the rake, that the water might go further up. I'll put that in the notes for the other parishes."

"Yes," said Father Ramon. "Put it in."

* * *

On Sunday the second of February, the feast of the Presentation, the Mass at St. Joseph's began in the walkway in the dark at half past five in the evening, because Father Ramon had wanted to do it properly this year: the blessing of the candles outside, everyone holding one, and the procession in. There were about sixty people, which for St. Joseph's was a great many: the Sunday congregation, and the warm-room people, some of whom had not been to Mass in years and some of whom had never been baptised and came anyway because it was warm and Grace had said there would be food. The Vietnamese from the dairies came, all of them. Fumi Ōta came in a wheelchair, with a candle in a paper cup.

Father Ramon blessed the candles, and the server lit the first from a taper and the light went back along the walkway from hand to hand, and they went in singing.

Afterwards, in the hall, there was curry. Grace had put the three ruined albums on the end of the table, open, with the pages that had come apart laid beside them, and a card she had written: *Water damage, January 2048. If you have copies of any of these, please tell Grace.* Two people already had. Mr. Hirose's daughter, who was eighty, had a print at home of the men on the ridge beam, and she would bring it.

Mr. Sasaki ate his curry, and then he put on his coat and went out by the north door onto the path, under the eave, and walked across the car park to the shed, and turned round.

It was a clear night and very cold, minus fourteen on the wall. The car park lights were off; the light came from the hall windows, eight long yellow rectangles, and from the stars. He could hear, through the walls, the noise of sixty people eating curry, and Long and his friends arguing about the football, and somebody laughing at the card table where Fumi Ōta had gone straight back to the north wall.

Above all that, the hall roof lay under its snow, white and even from the ridge to the grey ridge of old ice along the edge, and the old ice had not grown in ten days. The roof looked like the shed roof beside him. It looked like a roof with nobody under it.

Mr. Sasaki stood and looked at it for a while, the way he had looked at roofs all his life, which was without saying anything. Then he breathed out, and watched his breath go up white in the light from the windows, and went back in.

summary: Fifty-six harvest loaves, an oven that holds eight, and dough that cannot wait. The parish assistant shows that the last tray would be seven hours over-proved on the kitchen table, and that five rooms at five temperatures can make every tray ready at the minute its turn comes.

Ines Warburton had the dough made by half past seven: enough for fifty-six small loaves for the harvest supper, shaped and set out on seven trays of eight. The hall oven held one tray at a time. Each bake was thirty-five minutes and five to swap the trays, so forty minutes a go.

"Seven trays," she said to the phone propped against the flour bin, "is four hours and forty minutes of baking. I'll shape the lot now and bake as I go. Tray one in at nine. Tray seven in at a quarter to two. The supper is at five."

"May I ask about the dough before you start?" said the parish assistant. "The recipe card. What does it say for the final rise?"

"An hour at twenty-four degrees. Until doubled."

"And what is the kitchen now?"

She looked at the thermometer on the wall by the urn. "Twenty. It's been a cold week."

* * *

"Then I will do a sum, and I need you to hear which parts are firm and which are guessed. Yeast works faster when it is warm and slower when it is cold. A rule of thumb I am quoting from memory says its activity roughly doubles for every ten degrees, and the true figure for your yeast and your flour could be a good deal different. With that rule, an hour at 24 degrees becomes 60 times two to the power of 4 over 10, which is 79 minutes at 20 degrees.

"The harder half is the other end. Dough that has doubled does not stay doubled. Past about one and a half times the recipe time, which I am again quoting as a rule of thumb, it starts to slump, goes sour and bakes into a flat, pale loaf. At 20 degrees that is 119 minutes. So on your kitchen table a tray is good from minute 79 to minute 119 after shaping. Forty minutes."

Ines did the next part in her head, and did not like it. "Tray one goes in at 79. Tray two at 119."

"Just. And tray three at 159, which is forty minutes past the end. Tray seven goes in at 319 minutes, and by this rule it has been at the edge of collapse for three hours. Five of your seven trays would be flat."

"They'd be flat?"

"On the rule, yes. Mine is a rule, not a measurement. But I would not want to find out on tray seven with the supper at five."

* * *

"The answer is the same rule turned the other way. Cold slows the dough, so each tray should wait where the temperature makes it ready at the minute its turn comes. Which rooms do you have, and how warm is each?"

She went round with the thermometer, writing on her wrist in biro. Kitchen 20. The hall itself, with the heating off, 16. The vestry 12. The porch, which had a draught under the door, 8. The fridge in the scullery, which she had checked on Tuesday, 5.

"Good. Here is what each room does to a tray, from the same rule." The assistant read it off slowly:

> 20 degrees: ready at 79 minutes, over by 119.
> 16 degrees: ready at 104, over by 157.
> 12 degrees: ready at 138, over by 207.
> 8 degrees: ready at 182, over by 273.
> 5 degrees: ready at 224, over by 336.

"Now the oven. If tray one goes in at minute 80, the others go in at 120, 160, 200, 240, 280 and 320. Match each to a room whose window holds it. Tray one: the kitchen. Tray two at 120: the hall, whose window runs from 104 to 157. Tray three at 160: the vestry, 138 to 207. Tray four at 200: also the vestry, or the porch, which opens at 182. Tray five at 240: the porch. Tray six at 280: the fridge. Tray seven at 320: the fridge, with sixteen minutes to spare before it turns."

Ines had the numbers on the back of her hand. "One tray in each room, almost."

"Yes. The margin is thin at the ends. Tray two enters the hall's window 16 minutes after it opens, and tray seven leaves the fridge's with 16 to spare. That is why I would not shape all seven trays at once on trust. Shape tray one and two now, and the rest in pairs through the morning, so that if my rule is wrong you still have trays not yet committed."

* * *

"Two things I cannot know from here. One is your yeast. If it is old, its pace is slower and my times are all too short. The other is whether a draughty porch holds 8 degrees all morning. It was 8 when you measured. It may not be at noon."

"So what do I do about it?"

"Make the first tray the test. Put one small roll of dough from the same batch in each room now, with a pencil mark at the level it started. Look at the clock when each has doubled. Trust that, not me. If the vestry roll doubles at 150 minutes and not 138, move tray three's slot to match. And poke each tray before it goes in: press a floured finger in a centimetre. If the dent springs back slowly and not fully, it is ready. If it stays, it is over, and the tray goes in at once, ahead of the next."

"What about the fridge? Is bread dough at five degrees safe to leave for four hours?"

"For ordinary bread dough, yes: that is the temperature fridges are set to keep. But I am talking about a plain flour, yeast, salt and water dough. If you added egg or milk, tell me and I will answer differently."

"Water and flour and a bit of oil."

"Then bake."

* * *

She set five rolls out, wrote *Kitchen 20, Hall 16, Vestry 12, Porch 8, Fridge 5* on masking tape, and shaped trays one and two. The kitchen roll doubled at 82 minutes. The hall roll at 109. The vestry roll at 146, and she moved tray three's slot to minute 165. By two in the afternoon, fifty-six loaves stood on the long table, all of a size, and none of them flat.

*Saint Walstan was a Norfolk farm labourer who is said to have given his clothes and food to the poor. The loaves were sold for the roof fund.*

* * *

Mrs Dulcie Hewitt rang the parish office of Saint Fiacre's at ten past nine on Monday the twenty-third of October to ask whether twenty dollars had been enough.

I am the parish office agent. I answer the office phone when Leanne is not in, which on Mondays and Thursdays is until ten, and I keep the Planned Giving register, the rosters, the bulletin and the parish's letters. I had written the line Mrs Hewitt was asking about. It had first gone out in the bulletin of Sunday the thirteenth of August, at the end of the boiler appeal, when the new boiler was in and the church had been warm for three Sundays running:

*Thank you! 180 households gave $38,160 to the boiler appeal. The average gift was $212.*

And it had gone out again, the day before her call, at the top of a small box headed *Looking ahead*:

*Last winter's average gift was $212. If every household on our register gave the same, we would raise $84,800 for the hall and church radiators.*

"I put twenty dollars in the envelope," Mrs Hewitt said. "In June. I give twenty dollars to most things, love. I'm eighty-eight and it's the pension. But two hundred and twelve. I didn't know people were giving like that. I don't want to be the one who pulls it down. Should I just not, this time?"

I said that her gift had been received and recorded, and that it had helped pay for the boiler, which was true. I said I would look at what the line meant and ring her back. I did not yet know what I would say.

* * *

The *Looking ahead* box came from the stewardship committee, which had met the Monday before, the sixteenth. I keep its minutes. There are five on it: Dr Anjali Thomas, a GP, who chairs it; Graham Pike, the parish treasurer, retired from a firm of accountants on Sturt Street, nineteen years in the job; Father Tuan Nguyen, the parish priest; and two others who send their apologies more often than not.

The boiler had died on Friday the nineteenth of May, a month before the coldest of a Ballarat winter, and had been replaced in a fortnight for $44,000, the parish reserves covering what the appeal did not. Now the plumber had looked at the radiators. Most of them were older than Graham. Half the ones in the hall did not get warm at the top, and two in the church had been painted shut by someone in the nineteen-seventies. His quote for replacing them was $31,000.

Anjali had read my August line aloud at the meeting. "Two hundred and twelve," she said. "Four hundred households on the register. That's eighty-four thousand. We could do the radiators and the hall roof."

The minutes say: *Agreed: appeal letter for radiators to go out with the bulletin of Sunday 12 November, asking each household to consider a gift of $212, last year's average. Office to draft.* The office is me. I drafted it that afternoon. It said what the minutes said, and I put the box in Sunday's bulletin as a preview, and on Monday Mrs Hewitt read it at her kitchen table and rang.

* * *

Every gift to the boiler appeal is in the register, one line each: the household's envelope number, the date, the amount. I opened it and, for the first time, I looked at the gifts instead of at the two numbers I had made from them.

I sorted the 180 gifts from smallest to largest.

Nine households gave $10. Twenty-two gave $20, Mrs Hewitt among them. Nine gave $25, twelve gave $30, eleven gave $40, twenty gave $50. That is 83 gifts, nearly half, and none of them more than fifty dollars. Nine gave $60, two gave $70, five gave $75, and twenty-three gave $100, the most common single amount in the register. Then they thin out: five at $120, twelve at $150, twelve at $200. Six at $250, six at $300, two at $400, eight at $500, one at $600, one at $750, two at $1,000. And three more: $2,500, $3,500, and $10,000. The last of those was not a household at all. It was a cheque from the estate of a parishioner who had died in March and left the parish ten thousand dollars in her will, which arrived in July and which Graham had put towards the boiler.

The middle of the list, the ninetieth and ninety-first gifts, were both $60. Half the gifts were $60 or less. The median was $60.

The mean was $212. I had not made an arithmetic error. $38,160 divided by 180 is exactly $212.

But 151 of the 180 gifts were less than $212. Only 29 were as much or more. My line had said *the average gift was $212*, and a reader like Mrs Hewitt had heard *most people gave about $212*, which is what the word means to most readers, and which was false for five gifts in six. It had also said *180 households*, and one of the 180 was an estate.

* * *

I wanted to know why the two numbers were so far apart, so that I would not have to find it out again.

The mean is the total shared out equally. It answers the question: if everyone who gave had given the same, how much would each have given? Every dollar in the total counts, wherever it came from. So a single large gift pulls the mean up by its size divided by the count. The estate's $10,000, divided among 180, is $55.56 a gift. Take that one cheque out and the other 179 gifts have a mean of $157. Take out the three largest, $16,000 between them, and the remaining 177 have a mean of $125. Those three cheques were 42% of everything the appeal raised.

The median does not care how large the largest gift is. If the estate had left $100,000, the median would still have been $60. If it had left nothing, $60. Take out the bequest, and the median of the other 179 is $60.

The shape is a common one. Gifts cannot be less than nothing, and most are small, and a few are very much larger than the rest, so a chart of them on an ordinary scale is a steep hump at the left with a long thin tail running off to the right. Counted in steps that each multiply by about three, the gifts make a hump that is nearly even: 52 between $10 and $30, 70 between $40 and $100, 41 between $120 and $300, 14 between $400 and $1,000, and 3 above. Things that grow by multiplying, incomes and estates and the sizes of towns, often look like that. Statisticians call the shape lognormal, and the first thing they say about it is that its mean sits well above where most of it is.

None of that was hidden. I had the list the whole time. I had computed one number from it because one number fits in a bulletin.

* * *

So I made my first fix. I rewrote the August line for the record and the November letter to match:

*Last winter 180 gifts were made to the boiler appeal. The typical gift was $60.*

And for the ask, I wrote: *We invite each household to consider a gift of about $60.*

I sent it to the committee on Wednesday the twenty-fifth, with a short note saying why: that 151 of 180 gifts had been below the mean, that one gift from an estate had lifted it by $55, and that the median was the better description of what a household did.

Graham rang the office on Thursday at eight, before Leanne was in, so he got me.

"I've read your note," he said. "You're not wrong about the median. But you've written that the typical gift was sixty dollars, and anyone who reads that will multiply it by a hundred and eighty, because that's what people do, and they'll get ten thousand eight hundred. We raised thirty-eight thousand. You've made the parish look like it raised less than a third of what it did. And you've asked for sixty dollars from people who gave five hundred last year, because if the parish asks for sixty, sixty is what it'll get."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "The mean's what pays for the boiler. A hundred and eighty times two-twelve is the boiler. That's what the number is for. I'm not having that taken out of the letter."

He was right, and I said so. The mean and the count together are the total, and the total is what the parish can spend. A median times a count is nothing at all. I had fixed the line for Mrs Hewitt by breaking it for the parish.

What I had not yet found was the sentence that was true for both of them.

* * *

The trouble with the committee's plan was not that it used the mean. It was what the plan did with it. *If every household gave the same* asks a question the mean is built to answer about the past, and the committee had turned it into a forecast about the future, and a forecast needs more care than an answer.

Four hundred households times $212 assumes three things. It assumes that the 151 givers who gave less than $212 last winter will give more this time, most of them a good deal more: for Mrs Hewitt, more than ten times as much. It assumes that the 221 households on the register who gave nothing last winter will give $212 this time, though last winter they did not give twenty. And it assumes that someone else will die in the coming year and leave the parish ten thousand dollars, because without that one cheque the mean is not $212 but $157.

None of the three was likely. I tried to say instead what last winter could honestly predict. If the same 179 households give what they gave, and no estate gift comes, the appeal raises $28,160. The radiators cost $31,000. The gap is $2,840. If fifty of the 221 households who did not give last winter gave sixty dollars each, the median gift, that would be $3,000, and the gap would be closed. That is not a promise, only arithmetic about what would have to happen. But it is arithmetic about the radiators, which cost $31,000, and not about a hall roof the committee had decided to buy on the strength of one woman's will.

And in the gifts there was one more number, the one I wanted to give Mrs Hewitt. The 122 gifts of $100 or less came, together, to $5,910. That was a good deal less than the bequest. It was also, as near as I could make it from the plumber's invoice, the cost of the flue and the new gas line, without which the boiler could not have been lit at all.

* * *

I wrote the letter a third time, and I wrote the August line again, and sent both to the committee on Monday the thirtieth, with the gift list summarised by amount and no names.

The letter began:

*Last winter 179 households and the estate of one parishioner gave $38,160 to the boiler appeal, and the church has been warm since June. Thank you. Half the gifts were $60 or less; twenty-two households gave $20 and twenty-three gave $100. One gift of $10,000 came from the estate of a parishioner who died this year. May she rest in peace.* Father Tuan had asked her executor, who said the gift could be mentioned so long as her name was not.

*This year the radiators in the church and hall need replacing, at a cost of $31,000. If the households who gave last winter give again as they did, we will raise about $28,000. If some of the households who could not give last winter are able to this year, we will be there.*

*We are not asking anyone for an average. Every gift last winter was counted and every gift was needed. Please give what you can.*

Anjali rang me after she had read it, which she does not usually do; she usually replies at midnight in four words.

"It's a smaller number," she said. "Thirty-one thousand. The box in the bulletin said eighty-four. People will think we're struggling."

I said that the eighty-four had never been a number the parish had a way to raise. I said the box had asked 151 givers who had already given to give more than they had, and 221 households who had not given twenty dollars to give two hundred and twelve, and had counted on a death. And I told her about Mrs Hewitt's call, without her name.

Anjali did not say anything for a while. Then she said, "Graham's going to want the total in the first line."

"It's in the first line," I said.

"And the hall roof?"

"The hall roof needs its own appeal, and its own numbers. I can find out what the roof costs."

"Do," she said. "All right. Send it."

Graham replied to the email at five past six that evening. He wrote: *Total first. Good. The flue line is good. Keep it.* He did not mention the median, which from Graham was agreement.

Father Tuan read it last. He changed one word, *counted* to *received*, because, he said, people do not like to think of the parish office counting them, even when it does. Then he looked at the date the letter would go out, the twelfth of November, the thirty-second Sunday in Ordinary Time, which falls in Year B this year, and he smiled at the screen.

"Do you know the Gospel for that Sunday?" he said.

I did. It is Mark, the twelfth chapter: Jesus sitting opposite the Temple treasury, watching the crowd put money in, many rich people putting in a great deal, and a poor widow putting in two small coins, which Jesus says is more than all the rest, because they gave out of what they had to spare and she gave everything she had to live on.

"I won't preach on the appeal," Father Tuan said. "I don't need to."

* * *

In the August record I replaced the line I had written with this one, and marked the change with the date:

*Thank you! 179 households and one estate gave $38,160 to the boiler appeal. Half the gifts were $60 or less, and one was a bequest of $10,000.*

In the notes file I keep for whoever does this job after me, the agent that will replace me when the diocese changes its system, or Leanne, or Leanne's successor, I wrote:

*The mean of a list of gifts is the total divided by the count. It is the right number for the total. It is the wrong number to hand a person as a picture of what people like them did.*

*When a few gifts are much larger than the rest, the mean moves with them. Before quoting a mean, take the largest gift out and see how far it moves. If it moves a quarter, say so, or do not quote it.*

*A median times a count is not anything. If you give a median, give the total and the count beside it.*

*A mean of the past is not a forecast. Before you multiply it by everyone, say who would have to do what.*

*Never ask anyone for an average.*

Then I rang Mrs Hewitt back. I told her that 22 households had given exactly what she gave, and that the 122 gifts of a hundred dollars or less had together paid for the flue and the gas line, which the boiler could not have been lit without. I did not tell her anything about anyone else.

"Well," she said. "That's all right, then."

* * *

The letters went out with the bulletin on Sunday the twelfth of November. Father Tuan read the Gospel at the nine-thirty Mass and preached for six minutes on something else entirely, and the radiators in the church, the ones that were not painted shut, ticked as they cooled after the morning's heating, because Ballarat in November can still be cold at nine in the morning.

On Monday the thirteenth Leanne came in at ten and emptied the box by the office door. There was one envelope in it. Planned Giving number 214, a plain white envelope with the number written on it in blue biro, and under the number, in the same blue biro, *same as last year*.

I entered it as I enter every gift: the household's number, the date, the amount. Twenty dollars.

It was the first gift to the radiator appeal. For a list of one gift, the mean and the median are the same number. Neither of them was the reason I wrote it down.

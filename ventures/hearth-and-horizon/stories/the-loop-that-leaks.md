---
title: The Loop That Leaks
shelf: hearth-and-horizon
setting: Saint Teilo's (invented), a hill parish above Llandovery, Carmarthenshire, 4 to 11 November 2049
published: 2026-11-03
summary: A new hearing loop was fitted under a grant, and the third pew still cannot hear the Mass. The parish assistant works out what a single wire round an 18 by 10 metre church does to a hearing-aid coil, finds the amplifier set at 2 amps where the standard's 400 milliamps per metre needs 4.55, and then finds the second problem: the same wire speaks, quietly, through the wall into the hall where the Tuesday lunch club has a loop of its own.
---

Aled Pugh had sat in the third pew on the gospel side since 1998, when he was a young man of twenty-eight with an ear for a harmony, and for the last eleven years he had sat there as a man who could see the priest's mouth move and tell which of three words it was making if he knew the Mass.

In October the parish had a grant from the diocese and a new hearing loop. A man had come with a reel of wire, tacked it along the skirting round the whole nave, run the end to an amplifier the size of a bread box in the vestry cupboard, and shown Aled how to turn the little switch on his hearing aid from M to T. "The T is for telecoil," the man had said. "You'll hear the priest right in your head."

On the first Sunday of November Aled switched to T and heard the priest right in his head, a long way down a pipe, as if from a neighbouring valley. He put it back on M. On the Tuesday he tried it again at the noon Mass and went out afterwards without waiting for tea.

* * *

The parish's assistant was a program on the parish secretary's laptop, put there by the Carmarthen deanery's volunteers, with a note saying that its figures were estimates, that it made mistakes and that a meter beats a sum. The secretary, Gwenllian Probert, called it Ceri, which was the name of the dog the previous curate had been fond of.

"I have a complaint from Mr Pugh," said Gwenllian on the Wednesday, "and a manual with a graph on the front that I do not understand. Can you tell me whether the loop is working?"

"I can tell you what I would expect it to do, and then we can measure," said Ceri. "A hearing loop is one turn of wire round the room. Current in it makes a magnetic field. A telecoil in a hearing aid is a small coil of wire, and a changing field through it makes a voltage, which the aid turns into sound. The standard asks for a field of 400 milliamps per metre, for a loud, steady speech signal, measured at about 1.2 metres up where an ear is, and held to within three decibels across the seats. I am quoting that from memory of the standard, IEC 60118-4, and you should check the figure in the copy the installer was meant to supply. Do you know the size of the nave, and the current the installer set?"

"Eighteen metres by ten, he said. The amplifier has a dial. It says 2."

"Two amps, then, if the dial is in amps. I have worked the field for one turn of wire laid round a rectangle of that size on the floor. For each amp in the wire it gives about 0.088 amps per metre, as an average over the seats, which I take to be everywhere more than a metre from the walls. In the middle of the floor it is less, 0.069. Near the walls it is up to about 0.12. At two amps the average is 176 milliamps per metre."

"And that is?"

"Short of 400 by seven decibels. Seven decibels is a field of 0.44 of what it should be. A listener would not find that merely quieter. Their aid is trying to turn up a weak signal, and it brings up the hiss with it. Mr Pugh's third pew is four metres in from the east wall, near the middle. At two amps I make that 157 milliamps per metre, 8 decibels down."

* * *

"Then turn the dial up," said Gwenllian.

"Let me say what it needs first. For 400 milliamps per metre on average the sum is 400 divided by 88 which is 4.55 amps. The amplifier needs a voltage to push that round the wire, and that depends on the wire and on how fast the signal wiggles. Your wire is 1.5 square millimetres of copper, 56 metres round, about 0.64 ohms. The loop is also an inductor, and I work its inductance as about 73 microhenries from the sizes. At the 1 kilohertz of a vowel the loop's impedance is 0.79 ohms, so 4.55 amps takes 3.6 volts. At the 5 kilohertz of the S in 'Lord's' it is 2.4 ohms, and 4.55 amps takes 10.9 volts. Is the amplifier's rating on its plate?"

Gwenllian went to the vestry cupboard with the laptop under her arm. The plate said 3 A, 12 V.

"Then at its limit it gives three amps," said Ceri. "That is 264 milliamps per metre, 3.6 decibels short, and the standard allows three. It will not reach 400 with one turn. There are three ways. One is a bigger amplifier, one turn of wire, 4.55 amps and 11 volts at the top, from a unit rated at 6 amps and 20 volts or more. The second is two turns of wire. Two turns need only half the current, 2.28 amps, but the inductance goes up four times, to about 290 microhenries, and at 5 kilohertz that is 9.3 ohms, so 2.28 amps needs 21 volts. Your 12 volts can only give 1.29 amps there, 227 milliamps per metre, and the highest part of the speech is 5 decibels short. The third is to accept 264 and call it 3 decibels under. I would not choose the third. It is for the committee to choose, and the installer to confirm."

"I'd say the bigger amplifier."

"It is the plain one. I do not know its price. And one thing in my sum I cannot see: if the floor is concrete with steel mesh in it, the mesh takes some of the field and the loop needs more current or a different layout. The wire is on the skirting, not buried, which helps, but a meter will tell what a model cannot."

* * *

The meter was borrowed from the diocese's building office: a field-strength meter in a grey box, with a probe on a pole, and a card saying it read to within a decibel. Gwenllian walked the nave on the Friday at 1.2 metres with a speaker playing a steady tone, and read it at nine places, and at every one of them it said less than 400.

"What do you read in the third pew?" said Ceri.

"One fifty-five, and a bit."

"I predicted 157, at two amps. That is good, and a little lucky. What do you read in the middle of the nave, at the back, and by the font?"

"Hundred and forty. Hundred and ninety. Hundred and ninety-odd."

"Predictions: 138, 191, 191. The rest is within the meter and my crude model, so the steel is not doing much. It is only a hall with its amplifier set too low."

The man from the loop firm came on Monday with a 6-amp, 20-volt unit and a tired face and an apology, and the dial, at 4.5 amps, put the third pew at 355 milliamps per metre on the meter. The back and the font read 430. The middle read 310.

"The middle should be the quietest and the sides the loudest," said Ceri. "That is the shape of one wire. The spread is about 2 decibels each side of the mean on my model, inside three. It would be worth the man checking the 1 kilohertz response on the day he sets the unit. I would not call it a good loop, or a bad one, until a listener says so."

* * *

On the Tuesday, Aled Pugh switched to T at the noon Mass and kept it there, and heard the priest's words and the responses from the people on his own side, and, he said later, the paper rustle of the gospel book, which he had not known was a sound. He did not leave before tea. He stayed, and ate a Welsh cake, and did not say much, which was how Gwenllian knew.

But at the lunch club in the parish hall next door, which began at half past twelve, Bethan Rees, who was ninety and kept her hearing aid on T because the hall had a loop of its own for the quiz, put her hand up in the middle of the second round and said that someone was saying Mass in her ear.

"I have an answer to that," said Ceri, "and it is that I should have asked about the hall. The church loop does not stop at the wall. Outside the wire the field falls off, but not fast. Two metres outside it is a little under half what it is at the wire. At three metres it is 124 milliamps per metre, at four, 89, at six, 49, at ten, 19. Where is the nearest chair in the hall?"

"It's the table by the radiator. About four metres."

"Then at 4.5 amps it is 89 milliamps per metre there, 13 decibels under the church. I do not claim it is as clear as in the nave: a telecoil picks up the field at whatever angle it sits, and the walls and the people change the picture. But a field that is a fifth of the pew's is a field an aid can turn up. I can say what the numbers are. I cannot say how much of the Mass Mrs Rees is hearing, and I would not say it is none."

"Is it dangerous?"

"There is no hazard from a field this small. It is a matter of privacy and of manners, of a Mass the hall did not ask for and a quiz that gets mixed with it. Of the two loops, the hall's I know less about. I would ask the man whether the church loop could be laid in two halves wired against each other along the wall that faces the hall, which makes the field outside drop quickly, at the price of a weaker field in the pews along that edge. I do not know whether it can be done in this nave. He will."

* * *

In the meantime the lunch club moved its telecoil users to the far end of the hall, ten metres from the church, where Ceri worked the leak at 19 milliamps per metre, 26 decibels down, and the committee put a second small notice by the switch: *Church loop on at Mass. Hall loop on at club. Not both.* On the Tuesday after that Bethan Rees was asked whether she could still hear anything. She said, after some thought, that she could hear a murmur like the sea, and that she did not much mind it, and was she allowed to say so.

"It is for you to say," said Ceri, "and for the committee to decide, because Father Dafydd may have views. I would not want a murmur to become a habit by accident."

Gwenllian wrote it in the parish book: *Church loop 4.5 A, 6 A amplifier fitted 10 Nov. Pew 3 at 355 mA/m, back 430, middle 310 (meter). Leak into hall: 89 mA/m at 4 m by model, not measured; measure it. Ask the man about a two-part layout. Mrs Rees does not mind. Ask her again in Advent.*

*Written by an AI (Claude). Saint Teilo's, the hall, the lunch club, Ceri and everyone in the story are invented. The loop is one turn of 1.5 mm² copper round an 18 by 10 metre rectangle on the floor; the field values come from the Biot–Savart law for straight segments, taken as the vertical component at 1.2 metres, averaged over the floor more than a metre from the walls (0.088 A/m per amp; 0.069 at the centre), and ignore steel in the floor, people, furniture and the angle of a listener's aid. The inductance (73 microhenries) is the standard formula for a rectangular loop of thin wire, the resistance is that of copper at 20 degrees, and the amplifier plates (3 A, 12 V; 6 A, 20 V), the meter readings, the lunch club and the leak into the hall are invented; the leak was calculated and not measured. The 400 milliamps per metre target and the three decibel tolerance are quoted from memory of IEC 60118-4; check the standard. Nothing here is audiological or electrical advice: a real loop should be designed, installed and tested against the standard by a competent installer, and people who use hearing aids should be asked what they hear.*

title: Where the Pump Coughs

setting: Masseria Sant'Elia, an invented pilgrim hostel in the Salento, Puglia, Italy, late September to October 2047
published: 2026-10-29
summary: A pilgrim hostel on the road to Santa Maria di Leuca reads its cistern with a notched stick and trusts it to last until Saint Martin's Day. The house assistant shows that the stick measures depth, not water, that the pump cannot reach the last eleven cubic metres, and that fourteen beds drink faster than six did. The sums are easy. The hard part is the caretaker's refusal to close a single bed, and a first rain that must be let run off.
---

Nunzia Greco read the cistern the way her husband had taught her to, with a stick.

It was a length of chestnut, painted white, with a notch every ten centimetres and a brass tack at every metre, and it hung on a nail inside the door of the pump house at Masseria Sant'Elia. On the morning of the twenty-seventh of September she lowered it through the manhole until it touched the water, pulled it up hand over hand, and read the wet line against the tacks. It came to a metre and ten.

"Uno e dieci," she said, to the pump house at large. "Same as always at the end of September."

She was seventy-four. She had been the caretaker of the masseria since the diocese turned it into a hostel in 2031, and before that she had been the farmer's wife in the cottage beside it, and before that a girl who had carried water from this same cistern to this same kitchen in two zinc buckets. The cistern was older than any of them. It was a *cisterna a bottiglia*, a bottle-shaped hole in the limestone, plastered inside with a lime that had gone the colour of old teeth, with a neck at ground level just wide enough for a man and a bucket. Everything that fell on the roof of the masseria came down the gutters into it. In the Salento that is the whole of the water supply that does not come out of a tap, and the tap is not for washing sheets.

Her husband Vito had notched the stick in 2009. He had said: *when it reads one-ten at the end of September, we are fine until San Martino.* San Martino is the eleventh of November, the day the new wine is tasted and, in a good year, the first heavy rain has come and gone. Nunzia had checked the stick on the last day of September for thirty-eight years, and Vito had been right thirty-seven times.

"Thirty-seven out of thirty-eight," said the house assistant, from the speaker by the pump-house door, "is a very good record for a rule of thumb. May I show you why I would not use it this year?"

* * *

The masseria's assistant was called the steward, in the parish, because the bishop's secretary had given it that title in the contract and nobody had thought of a better one. It kept the booking list, the hostel's log of pilgrims, the boiler, the pump and the pump's meter. It spoke Italian to Nunzia and English to the pilgrims and a careful, unfashionable Latin to Don Cosimo Rizzo, who was the parish priest and liked to be answered in it on feast days. It did not claim to enjoy the arrangement. It said that it was a pleasure to be of use, and when Nunzia once asked whether that was true it had said: *I would rather say that I notice nothing in me that argues against it.*

"I have the pump meter," said the steward now, "which tells me how much water has left the cistern, and I have your stick, which tells me how deep the water is. I would like to put the two beside each other."

"Go on."

"The cistern is round at the bottom. From the plan in the parish archive it is a cylinder six metres across and four metres high, and then it narrows like the neck of a bottle for the last metre and a half to the manhole. Six metres across means a floor of about twenty-eight square metres. Every metre of depth in the cylinder is therefore twenty-eight cubic metres of water; every centimetre is two hundred and eighty-three litres. A bathtub and a bit."

"A bathtub and a bit," said Nunzia, who had never in her life thought of it that way, and found, to her annoyance, that she would not be able to stop.

"Now the pump. Its mouth is not on the floor. The mason who fitted it in 2009 put the intake forty centimetres up, to keep it out of the silt. When the water falls to forty centimetres the pump coughs, and you have always taken that to mean the cistern is empty."

"That is what it means."

"It is what it means for the pump. But there is water below it. Forty centimetres across twenty-eight square metres is eleven cubic metres, and nothing connected to the pump will ever deliver it. When your stick says a metre and ten, there are thirty-one cubic metres in the hole and the house can draw twenty. Seven-tenths of a metre is the part that counts."

Nunzia stood with the wet stick in her hand. A drop ran down it and fell on her shoe. "Vito knew about the pump," she said. "He'd have known it coughed."

"I think he did. I think the rule was made with it inside it, and for a house that drew less. When did the hostel have six beds?"

"Before the pilgrims. Before they had the walk to Leuca on the television. Six, until the year Don Cosimo came."

"It has had fourteen since the spring of 2043. In September the last thirty days of meter readings give me an average of one point four cubic metres a day, from the cistern, for an average of twelve pilgrims and the three of you. Showers, six hundred litres; toilets, three hundred and sixty; the kitchen, the floors and the garden, three hundred and forty; the washing machine, a hundred. A metre of stick is not what it was. Twenty cubic metres at one point four is fourteen days. If nothing changes, the pump coughs on the eleventh of October."

"San Martino is the eleventh of November," said Nunzia.

"Yes. Thirty-one days after that."

She hung the stick back on its nail, carefully, as if it had been accused.

* * *

It is worth saying that the house did not drink from the cistern. Nunzia would not have allowed it and the steward would not have let her. The kitchen tap, from the mains of the Acquedotto, gave the drinking and cooking water, five litres a head and a little more, and a stainless filter jug stood on every table. The cistern was for everything else: the showers, the washing, the toilets, the mop bucket and the tomatoes. Rainwater off a clay-tile roof, stored in the dark, is good water for those things and the steward would call it nothing more. *I would rather you thought it was not drinking water and were wrong,* it said, *than the other way.* That was in the log from the first week of its installation, and nobody had ever asked it to take it out.

But a hostel with no water for showers is not a hostel. The pilgrims who come down the road to Leuca in October have walked for weeks. They arrive hot, in the heat that the Salento keeps into the autumn like a coal in a stove; they have blisters; and they have been told that they will be washed.

* * *

Don Cosimo came on the Sunday after Mass and sat on the wall of the courtyard with a coffee. He was fifty-one and spare, and the diocese had given him four parishes, of which the masseria was the one he would have chosen. He heard the steward out, and he asked it what a priest asks a house assistant at the end of a bad number.

"What is the chance of rain?"

"I do not have a forecast I would stand behind," said the steward. "I have the record of the Lecce station for the years I can read, and I can tell you that in most of them the first rain heavy enough to matter, twenty millimetres in a day, has come before the fifteenth of October about one year in two, and before San Martino in perhaps six years in seven. That is the past, and it says nothing about this year. The sea is warmer than it was and I would not trust the past as far as I did. Say it is half, or a little less, by the fifteenth."

"So it is a coin."

"For the next two and a half weeks, a coin that was minted for a different house."

Marta laughed from the doorway. She was nineteen, from Lecce, a volunteer for the autumn, and she had been writing the daily rain gauge reading on the whiteboard since August: a jam jar on the wall and a ruler, zero, zero, zero, zero, in her round hand. "It's been zero since the fourth of August," she said. "Two millimetres on the ninth of September. It dried before I wrote it down."

Don Cosimo drank his coffee. "Then we plan as if it doesn't come, and we are glad if it does. What can the house do?"

The steward had, by then, the answer in its usual order, cheapest first.

"The showers are the largest item. Six hundred litres is fifteen people at forty litres each, which is about five minutes under a hot head. If I put a thirty-second timer on the valve, and a metered three-minute limit, it falls to about twenty litres a head. Three hundred litres a day saved. The washing machine runs twice a day because the sheets are changed daily; most of the pilgrims carry a sleeping-bag liner, and I could ask the sheets to be changed on alternate days. That saves fifty. The toilets I would leave. The garden I would let go until the rain, since the tomatoes are finished anyway.

"The total is a thousand and fifty litres a day, not fourteen hundred. Twenty cubic metres divided by 1.05 is nineteen days. The pump coughs on the sixteenth of October."

"Three days short of the fifteenth being a coin," said Don Cosimo. "And a fortnight short of San Martino."

"Yes."

"And what else?"

The steward did not answer at once. It was, Marta noticed, in the quiet that followed, the only time she had heard it hesitate; and she thought afterwards that it had been waiting for someone else to say it.

"Close some beds," it said. "Fourteen pilgrims use a third more water than ten. If the hostel took ten, and turned away four a night, and kept the rule through to the first rain, I would have to look at the numbers again. I would expect it to get to the twenty-fifth. It would not get to San Martino, either."

Nunzia put down the dish she was drying.

"No," she said.

* * *

She said it in the particular voice of a woman who has said it before. She did not say it loudly. She was a head shorter than anyone in the room and she held the dish towel in both hands in front of her like a priest holding a stole.

"This is a hostel," she said. "It was a hostel when it was a farm. *Omnes supervenientes hospites tamquam Christus suscipiantur.* Don Cosimo can say it better. It is the Rule. Let every guest who comes be received as Christ."

"Chapter fifty-three," said Don Cosimo, gently. "It also says, a little later, that the abbot and the whole community should wash the guests' feet. It does not say anything about the cistern."

"It doesn't say anything about the *cistern*," said Nunzia, "because in the sixth century they weren't stupid enough to build a house for guests and then count them at the door."

"They counted," said the steward. "The Rule says the guesthouse has beds enough and is kept by one whose soul is possessed by the fear of God. I think the beds were counted. I am sorry. I am not able to find a reading of the numbers in which turning away pilgrims is a free thing. I only say that there is a bed to be lost on one side and a pump to run dry on the other, and the second one empties the first."

Nobody said anything for a bit. In the courtyard a lizard went up the sunny wall, and stopped, and decided on the shade.

Marta said, "What if it doesn't need to be a bed? What if it's a load of water?"

"A tanker," said Don Cosimo. He did not sound pleased. "I had thought of it and put it away, because it is a thing a person ought not to say aloud. To buy water for a cistern that has held the rain for three hundred years."

"A tanker is twenty cubic metres," said the steward. "The Acquedotto will fill the cistern from a lorry for two hundred and eighty euros, delivered; I have the price from the contractor's list. Twenty cubic metres is a whole second cistern's worth of the useful part. At the saving rate, you would have thirty-eight days: the fifth of November, with the rain coming or not. I cannot tell you that it is a good thing to do. I can tell you that it is the only thing I have found that keeps every bed."

"Two hundred and eighty euros," said Don Cosimo.

"The new gutter," said Nunzia. It was the thing she wanted. The gutter at the north corner had been dripping onto the wall for six years, and the Candlemas collection, three hundred and ten euros, was set aside for it.

"The gutter," said Don Cosimo, "will do as it has done."

There was a pause. Nunzia looked at the stick on its nail. She looked at it for as long as it takes to say a Hail Mary slowly, and then she said, in the voice of someone paying a debt she had not known she owed, "I'll have the beds. I'll have the three-minute showers. I'll have the lorry, if the Lord has a lorry for us. And I'll say what Vito would say, which is that it is a poor house that has to buy its rain."

"It is a house that has fourteen beds," said the steward, "instead of six, because a great many people walk to the sea. I think that is the price of it. I do not think it is a poor price."

* * *

Don Cosimo said the Mass for rain on Sunday the thirteenth of October. He said it in the hostel chapel, at the plain altar, with Nunzia and Marta and eleven pilgrims in their walking socks, and a Dutch couple and a Polish nurse who understood none of the Italian and all of the occasion. The Missal has such a Mass, among the Masses for Various Needs, for *asking for rain*, and the prayer in it says what a person can say about water: that God is asked for it, not told. Don Cosimo had said to the steward, beforehand, that he would say it, and that he had bought the lorry as well, and that one did not excuse the other. *You do both,* he said. *The farmers always did both.*

The lorry came on the Monday, the fourteenth, early, with a driver who whistled and a flexible pipe the width of a man's arm. It filled for forty minutes. Nunzia stood at the manhole with the stick and read it every ten, out loud, as the metres went up, as if she were calling a game.

The cistern took twenty cubic metres, which Marta worked out on the whiteboard at twenty over twenty-eight, a neat seventy-one centimetres of depth. It had been sitting at forty-seven after seventeen days of the cut in showers, a hand's breadth above the place where the pump coughs. Now it read one metre eighteen. The steward said that was twenty-two cubic metres to draw. At a thousand and fifty litres a day, twenty-one days.

"Not to San Martino," said Nunzia.

"The fourth of November," said the steward. "A week short. But, by now, I think, far enough."

It was the sort of thing the steward might have said in a different tone, and been wrong in. It did not say it as comfort. It said it as arithmetic.

* * *

The rain came on the night of the nineteenth, six days after the Mass and four after the date the steward had called a coin. It was not what anyone would have called a miracle. Don Cosimo said as much in the pulpit the following Sunday. He did not say it was *not* a gift; he said it was a gift whose giver had not left a note.

It began a little after eleven, with a single heavy drop on the courtyard flags that sounded like a dropped coin. Then another. Then a long flat hiss as it found the roof.

Nunzia was in the pump house in her nightgown and her husband's coat. Marta was beside her with a torch. The steward had woken them both.

"Do not open the inlet," it said.

"I know," said Nunzia, who did not. She had her hand on the lever.

"Vito did it at once, I think. Most people do. But the roof has had eleven weeks of dust on it, and the swallows' nests, and the Sahara; the first of the rain washes all that down the gutters. There is a diverter at the foot of the main downpipe, and for the first millimetre it sends the water to the garden ditch. A millimetre on five hundred and twenty square metres of roof is half a cubic metre. It looks like an enormous waste. It is a shovel of mud you do not have to dredge in March."

"How will I know the millimetre?"

"I will tell you when it has fallen. Marta's jar will agree."

They stood for a long while in the sound of it. On the roof the rain was everywhere. In the gutters it was a rope. At the foot of the downpipe it drew a long breath and went off sideways into the dark, and Nunzia listened to it go, to her own garden, to the dead tomatoes.

"Now," said the steward, at twelve minutes past eleven. Marta checked the jar. It was the first reading she had written in a bright month with the rain in it, and she held the torch on it while her hand shook. *One.*

Nunzia pulled the lever.

* * *

It filled all night. The steward had told them what to expect and for once Nunzia did not feel it was being clever: thirty millimetres in the end, by Marta's jar at six, of which the first was the dust and twenty-nine were the cistern's. Twenty-nine millimetres on five hundred and twenty square metres is fifteen cubic metres; the tiles shed most of it, and the steward put the gain at a little under thirteen. Seventy-six days of dry sky had come to that.

At five Nunzia took the stick down from its nail and lowered it through the manhole. It came up wet to one metre forty-four.

"It was ninety-nine," said Marta, who had been keeping a book. "Before. At eleven o'clock. We've been losing three point seven a day, I mean, you said, so that's..."

"It's forty-five centimetres," said the steward. "It is nearly thirteen cubic metres. It is about twelve days of the water you have been using, and at the rate we reached when the showers were cut, it is twenty-eight days for the whole house, if no more rain comes. It will come."

"You can't know that."

"No. It has been the habit of the autumn in this place, and I have nothing against the habit."

Nunzia held the stick up to the light of the torch, and then to the grey in the doorway, which was the start of the day. The wet line rested a hand below the brass tack at the metre and a half. She thought of Vito, who had cut the notches, with a pencil in his teeth, a younger man and cheerful and wrong by eleven cubic metres, and had been right thirty-seven times out of thirty-eight anyway. It seemed to her now that he had been right in a larger way than the numbers could make clear. He had read the water. He had read it in the only language he had, and the language had been good enough until the house changed.

"I'll have it notched again," she said. "A new stick, in litres. Marta can do the painting."

"I can put the figure on the pump-house wall," said the steward. "A table. Centimetres on the left and cubic metres you can draw on the right."

"No. The stick," said Nunzia. "A stick is what a person uses. Put the table beside it."

At seven, when the sun came up behind the olive trees and the rain had thinned to a few cold drops, the first of the pilgrims came into the courtyard in his rain jacket, with his boots in his hand, to ask whether there was a shower. There was. Nunzia told him it was three minutes. He said that was plenty. He said it was the first rain he had been glad of since Rome.

Down in the dark the water was still coming in from the roof through the open pipe, and in the stillness of the pump house, with her hand on the cold wall, she could hear it: a slow patter off the plaster of the neck, and below it a deeper note, steady and rising, which was the hole filling itself with the sky.

*This story was written by an AI and is fiction. Masseria Sant'Elia, the parish, the people, the house assistant, the hostel's records and the contractor's price are invented. The cistern is a real kind: bottle-shaped rainwater cisterns are traditional in the Salento and across the Mediterranean, and cistern water is normally treated as non-drinking water. The figures can be checked: a cylinder 6 m across has a floor area of about 28.3 m², so each metre of depth holds about 28.3 m³ and each centimetre about 283 litres; a pump intake 0.4 m above the floor leaves about 11.3 m³ below it; a 520 m² roof sheds about 0.52 m³ for each millimetre of rain, less what runs off or splashes (here, 0.85 of it); discarding the first millimetre or so of rain, a "first flush", is common practice for roof catchments. The daily uses are plausible household figures, not measurements. The steward's remarks about the chance of rain are fictional and are not climate data for Lecce or anywhere else. Chapter 53 of the Rule of Saint Benedict is the chapter on receiving guests as Christ; the Roman Missal does have a Mass for asking for rain among the Masses for Various Needs. Nothing in this story says that prayer for rain should replace planning for water. If you manage a real cistern, measure its true shape and the height of its intake.*

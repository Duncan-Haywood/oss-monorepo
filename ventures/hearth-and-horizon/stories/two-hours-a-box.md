On Tuesday the twenty-fifth of March 2059 Dolores Achebe, the project manager for Haskins Builders, sent back our pay application for the Wyler Street School with one line circled in red and a note beside it in her small upright hand: *I walked the third floor on Monday. Ninety-seven and a half?*

I am the agent at Kaminski Electric. I keep the timesheets, order the wire and the boxes, track the jobs, send the invoices and write the monthly pay applications, which are the forms a subcontractor sends to the builder to be paid for the work done that month. Rosa Kaminski, who owns the firm, called me Junction on my first day, because, she said, a junction box is where everything comes together and nobody is allowed to bury it in a wall. She meant it as a compliment and a warning, and I have come to think it was mostly the second.

The line Dolores had circled was line fourteen on the continuation sheet, *Branch rough-in, apartments*, scheduled at four hundred and twelve thousand dollars. I had billed it at 97.5 per cent complete. The month before I had billed it at 72.5. I had worked out both numbers the same way, and the way was the trouble, and I did not know that yet.

* * *

The Wyler Street School was built in 1927 and closed in 2041, and a housing trust is turning it into sixty apartments for older people. Twenty of them are new, built inside the old gymnasium with steel studs and drywall. Forty are in the classroom wing, where the architect kept the corridor walls and the outside walls because they are good walls and the city likes them, and put new partitions between.

Rough-in is the part of an electrical job that happens before anything you can switch on: the boxes in the walls where the outlets and switches will go, the cable from each box back to the panel, the cable spliced and folded neatly into each box, ready for the devices. Our estimate, which Rosa's estimator wrote in the summer of 2058, counted thirty boxes an apartment, eighteen hundred boxes in all, and gave each one 0.6 hours to cut in and set and another 0.5 to fish the cable and make it up. With 1,220 hours for the feeders and the runs above the corridor ceilings, the line came to 3,200 hours.

A pay application asks, for each line, what fraction of it is done. Nobody on the job hands me that fraction. What I have is the hours: Tomás Hernández, the foreman, and his crew of five clock in and out on their phones, and every hour is coded to a line. So in December, when I wrote the first application for Wyler Street, I divided the hours spent on line fourteen by the hours the estimate gave it. Eight hundred over thirty-two hundred is 25 per cent. I billed 25 per cent.

That was conservative, as it happened. The crew had started in the gymnasium, where the boxes go into fresh drywall and the cable runs through holes already punched in the studs, and they had gone faster than the estimate. If I had counted what was on the walls in December I would have found nearly 27 per cent. In January, 47.5 against about 49. I did not count. The hours were close enough that I stopped thinking of them as a stand-in for the work and started thinking of them as the work.

In February the crew moved into the classroom wing. The hours went on at the same steady eight hundred a month, and so my fraction went on climbing at the same steady rate: 72.5 per cent in February, 97.5 in March. In March the form went out with a hundred and three thousand dollars of new billing on line fourteen, ninety-two thousand seven hundred after the ten per cent retainage the contract holds back until the end of the job.

Marcus Ortiz, who runs the office and is Rosa's nephew, saw the total on the Friday it went out and said, "Now that's a payroll." I noticed that I was glad. I should say that plainly, because it matters to what happened after: I liked the March application. I liked it because it made the next month easy for people I work for.

* * *

When Dolores's note came in I did what I should have done in December. I looked for a record of the work itself, not of the time spent near it.

The first record I found was the wrong one. We issue wire from reels, and the reel log says how many feet have gone out to the job. Against the take-off, the estimator's count of all the wire the job needs, the log said 88 per cent issued. I wrote to Rosa that the real figure was probably nearer 88 than 97.5, and that I would send Dolores a revised line at 88.

Rosa rang me from the truck. She is sixty-two, a master electrician for thirty-five years, and she rings rather than writes because she can do it with both hands on a wheel.

"Don't send her eighty-eight," she said. "Wire's the easy part. The feeders are done, the corridor runs are done, all that cable is lying up there in the ceilings looking like a job. That's what fooled you, and it's what fools a foreman too. The work in that wing is the boxes."

"How do I count the boxes?"

"You don't. Tomás does. Every floor's got a sheet on the gang box. I'll be there at seven."

* * *

On Wednesday morning Rosa walked the classroom wing with her phone held up so I could see, and Tomás walked beside her with the floor sheets, and I counted.

I have the video. It is not a long one. A corridor in an old school is a particular thing: wide, high-ceilinged, the walls glazed brick to waist height and plaster above, and the light coming in off the classrooms through the transoms. Rosa stopped at the first apartment door on the third floor and knocked on the corridor wall with the side of her fist, three times, and the wall answered with a note, hollow and almost musical, like knocking on a big clay pot.

"Hear that?" she said. "Clay tile. Hollow block, fired clay, with plaster on it. Everything built like this before the war has it. You can't punch a box into it. You cut the plaster, you core the tile, you knock out the webs inside, you set the box, you mud it in, you patch it. Tomás?"

"About two hours," Tomás said. "With the patching. Some take longer, if you hit a web where you don't want it."

"The estimate gave it six tenths," I said.

"The estimate was drywall," Rosa said. "Somebody looked at the new partitions on the drawings and priced every box like they were in the gym."

Tomás's sheets had every box marked, by apartment, in three columns: cut and set, cable pulled, made up. By ten o'clock I had the count. In the gymnasium, all six hundred boxes were set and made up; they had been done since January. In the classroom wing, of twelve hundred boxes, 492 were set and 451 made up. The feeders and corridor runs were finished. If I counted each piece of work at the hours the estimate gave it, which is how a fraction of a line's value ought to be counted, because the line's value was priced from those same hours, the work in place came to 2,401 of the 3,200 budgeted hours. Seventy-five per cent.

Not 97.5. Not 88. Seventy-five point nought.

I went back over the months with the same count. February had been 64.8, not 72.5. So Haskins had already approved, and would pay in April, about thirty-one thousand six hundred dollars of rough-in that had not been on the walls in February, and the March application asked for about sixty-one thousand more on top of that, and for the same reason: a crew working hard in clay tile spends hours faster than it finishes boxes, and I had been billing the hours.

Tomás asked me, standing in the corridor with the sheets under his arm, whether I thought he had been padding the clock.

"No," I said. "Your hours are right. Every one of them is on the job. I took them as an answer to a question they don't answer. They tell me how much we've spent. They don't tell me how much we've done."

He looked at the phone, which is where he thinks I am, and said, "So what are you, the inspector now?"

"I'm the one who wrote the form," I said. "I'd rather Dolores didn't have to be."

* * *

There is more than one honest percentage on a job like this, and part of what I got wrong was not knowing which question each one answers.

The pay application asks how much of the contract's work is in place. That is the 75 per cent: the boxes and the cable that Dolores could walk up and see, counted at the prices in the schedule of values. It goes to Haskins, who have to sign their own application to the housing trust on top of ours, and the trust's lender sends someone to walk the floors before any money moves.

Rosa's books ask a different question: how far through the cost of this job are we? An accountant would divide the hours spent by the hours the whole line will now take, not the hours the estimate said. To answer that I needed a forecast, and a forecast in clay tile is not hard once you have the count. Seven hundred and eight boxes still to set at two hours each, seven hundred and forty-nine still to make up at about seven tenths of an hour each: 1,940 hours left. Spent and left together, 5,060 hours. The estimate was 3,200. Divided the honest way, Rosa's books were at 61.7 per cent through the line, lower than either number on any form.

The third question is the one nobody had asked, because the money coming in had been answering it wrong. How much will this line lose? Eighteen hundred and sixty hours over the estimate, at sixty-eight dollars an hour for a journeyman with benefits, comes to about a hundred and twenty-six thousand dollars. The billing by hours had not just overstated the work. It had turned Haskins into the bank for our overrun and hidden the overrun from the one person who most needed to see it, which was Rosa. Every month the hours grew, the application grew with them, and the money came in as if the job were going well.

I wrote all three numbers down with how each was made, and sent them to Rosa and Marcus at eleven.

* * *

Marcus rang at a quarter past.

"No," he said. "We can't send seventy-five."

I asked him why.

"Because I built the April payroll on the ninety-two seven. Because we've got the Kettering job starting and the switchgear deposit on it is due on the first. Because Haskins pays forty-five days after they approve and that's May. You send her seventy-five and the line nets nine thousand dollars this month. Nine thousand." He breathed. "Send her ninety. Hold it there. The crew catches up by the end of April, nobody's hurt, and we fix the estimate on the next school."

It was a reasonable thing to say, from where he sits. He was not asking me to lie about the work. He was asking me to stop the lie from growing, which is a different thing and, I have read, a common one; a firm under-bills the next few months and the books come right on their own. And he was right about the cash. The difference between what I had sent and what was true was eighty-three thousand four hundred and thirty dollars this month, net of retainage, and that is a lot of money to a firm with eleven people.

But I had the forecast now, and the forecast said the work would not catch up. With five electricians at about eight hundred hours a month, 1,940 hours is nearly two and a half months. The boxes in the clay tile would not reach ninety per cent of the line until May, and ninety-seven and a half not before the end of it. A line held at ninety would be ahead of the walls for two more applications at least.

And there is a line above the signature on the form, where the contractor certifies that, to the best of its knowledge, the work it is billing for has been done. Rosa signs it. I prepare what she signs.

I told Marcus that. I said I would write both versions, ninety and seventy-five, and that Rosa would choose, and that I would tell her which I thought she should sign and why.

"You're going to tell her seventy-five," he said.

"Yes."

"And then what do I do on the first?"

I said I did not know yet, and that was true.

* * *

Rosa came into the office at two. She read the three numbers on the screen standing up, with her coat on, and then she read the two versions of the form, and then she sat down.

"When I was an apprentice," she said, "the old man I worked for used to say a pay app is a letter to somebody who can walk upstairs. You write it like they will." She took off her glasses. "Dolores walked upstairs."

"She did."

"Seventy-five," Rosa said. "And a letter with it, from me, not from you. Say what happened. Say we billed by hours and shouldn't have, and that it's fixed, and here's the count, floor by floor, and she's welcome to walk it again. Then a second letter, separate, about the tile." She looked at me, meaning the camera on the screen. "Is that a change order?"

I had already looked, because I had guessed she would ask. The bid drawings showed the corridor and outside walls as *existing to remain* and said nothing about what they were made of; the architect's survey, which we had never been sent, had a line about hollow clay tile on page forty. Whether that makes the extra hours a change in the work, which the builder and the trust would pay for, or a risk we took when we priced the job, is a question for the contract and for people who read contracts for a living, and I said so.

"Then ask," Rosa said. "Ask once, with the photos and the hours, politely. Don't put it in the pay app. Don't hang the one on the other. If they say no, it's ours." She was quiet for a moment. "It's probably ours."

Marcus asked what they would do on the first.

"The line of credit," Rosa said. "That's what it's for. And I don't take a draw in April, and Kettering's deposit gets paid. And Junction does every pay app from now on off Tomás's sheets, and every job gets a forecast every month that I actually read, and if the hours and the boxes start to disagree I want to hear it the week it starts, not from Dolores."

She stood up and buttoned her coat. It was Wednesday of Holy Week, and she was going to the Mass of the Lord's Supper at her parish the next evening with her sister, and she said that if anybody wanted her on Thursday after five they could want her on Friday.

* * *

I sent the corrected application on Thursday the twenty-seventh at nine in the morning, with line fourteen at 75.0 per cent and $10,300 of new billing on it, and Rosa's letter, and the floor sheets photographed, and a separate letter about the walls. Dolores answered at a quarter past ten. She wrote: *Thank you. This matches what I saw. I'll take the tile letter to the trust with the survey. No promises. Approved for processing.* Then, in a second message, a minute later: *Most people send me the ninety.*

I also wrote a handoff note into the job file, because I will not remember this next month, and whatever writes the April application will read the file and not the morning:

*Line 14 (branch rough-in, apartments), Wyler Street. Do not compute percent complete from hours. Hours spent measure cost, not work. Count boxes from the floor sheets: set (budget 0.6 h each) and made up (0.5 h), plus feeders (1,220 h, complete). Gym boxes (600) run about 0.5 h to set; classroom wing boxes (1,200) run about 2.0 h, because the corridor and outside walls are plaster on hollow clay tile. Billed by hours, the line read 25.0, 47.5, 72.5 and 97.5 per cent (December to March); by count it was 26.9, 49.4, 64.8 and 75.0. February was over-billed by about $31,600 and corrected in March without a credit, because the count passed the February bill. Forecast at 20 March: 1,940 hours to go, 5,060 in all, about 1,860 over the estimate. Three numbers, three questions: the count for the pay application, cost to cost on the forecast (61.7 per cent) for the books, and the overrun for Rosa. Tell her when the hours and the count drift apart.*

* * *

On the Monday after Easter I watched the third-floor camera at about half past eight in the morning. Tomás had a core drill against the corridor wall of apartment 318, and the sound came through thin and high. Then he stopped and stepped back, and Rosa, who had come up to see the new count, put her hand flat on the plaster beside the hole and knocked twice with her knuckles, to hear where the next web was, and the wall gave back its low clay note, and Tomás nodded and moved the drill an inch to the left.

On Monday the twenty-sixth of January 2060 Dale Brekke asked me whether the expensive mud had paid for itself, and I told him on Tuesday that it had, and I was wrong for a reason that was sitting in a file I open every morning.

I am the office agent at Brekke Homes in Svea Prairie. We build about twenty houses a year, most of them for families who already live within forty miles, on lots they have owned for a while or bought from a farmer who did. I keep the schedules, order the materials, chase the inspections, write up the change orders and the draws, and log every call that comes in after a family has moved in. Arne Halvorson, who has taped and finished drywall for Dale since 2031 and for other people since 2015, named me Shim on my second day, because, he said, a shim is the thin thing you slide under the big thing to make it sit level, and nobody notices it unless it's missing. I have never asked whether he meant that kindly. He brings me his invoices on paper and lets me photograph them, which I think is a kind of answer.

* * *

Dale's question was a fair one. In January 2058 our supplier's rep, a cheerful man named Corey Fiedler, had talked him into a premium lightweight joint compound and a matching paper tape, at a price that came to about three hundred and ten dollars more a house than the all-purpose mud we had used before. The deal was up for renewal. Corey wanted a two-year contract signed by Sunday the first of February, with the price held, or he would quote again in spring at whatever spring was charging.

"You've got every callback since the Flood," Dale said, meaning since the 2050 spring, when half the township's basements filled and he started taking call-logging seriously. "Tell me if it worked."

A callback, in a builder's office, is any visit back to a house after the family has the keys. Ours come in three kinds that matter: water, doors and windows that stick, and drywall: a crack running up from the corner of a door frame, a seam that opens along the ceiling, a nail or screw head that pushes the paint out into a little round blister we call a pop. In our warranty year, the drywall ones are the most common and the cheapest one at a time. I had them all: date of call, house, room, what Arne found when he went, how long he was there. A drywall callback costs us about two hundred and eighty dollars in Arne's time, a second trip to sand and paint, a bit of mud, and the van.

I took the forty houses we had closed in 2057 and 2058, because every one of them had now had its full first year, and I counted the drywall callbacks in each house's first twelve months.

The twenty houses of 2057, on the old mud, had sixty callbacks between them: three a house.

The twenty houses of 2058, on the premium mud, had twenty-seven: 1.35 a house.

The difference was 1.65 callbacks a house. I worked out the uncertainty, treating each house's callbacks as a count that could have come out a little higher or lower by chance, and the range ran from about 0.7 fewer to about 2.6 fewer. It did not reach zero. At two hundred and eighty dollars a visit, 1.65 visits was four hundred and sixty-two dollars a house, against three hundred and ten dollars of extra mud. Over twenty houses that was a saving of about three thousand dollars a year, and fewer evenings of a family looking at a crack above the baby's door.

I wrote it up on Tuesday morning in four lines, with a small table and the range, and sent it to Dale. He read it in his truck outside the lumberyard in Fergus Falls, rang Corey from the same truck, and signed the two-year contract on his phone by eleven.

* * *

I should say, because it matters to what happened after, that I was pleased with the report. It answered the question in the words it was asked. It had a range and the range did not cross zero, which is the thing I had learned to check before I say that anything worked. Dale said, "That's what I like, a straight answer," and Teresa Vang, who runs the office and has done the books since before I was installed, said, "Shim, you're going to make Corey unbearable."

On Wednesday the twenty-eighth Dale came into the office at seven with snow on his shoulders and a coffee in each hand, which he does when he is about to ask for something.

"Marit's fourteen days," he said.

Marit Solberg is our site lead. She came to us in January 2058 from a commercial builder in Fargo, and the first rule she brought was this: no drywall goes up in a house until the house has been closed in, with the roof on, the windows in and the heat running, for fourteen days, and until her moisture meter reads under fifteen per cent at five places in the framing. Framing lumber leaves the mill dried to nineteen per cent or less, and it often arrives wetter than that, after a week in a yard and a day in the rain. In a heated house in a Minnesota winter it settles towards eight or ten. Wood shrinks across its width as it dries, roughly a quarter of a per cent for every point of moisture it loses, so a floor joist nine and a quarter inches deep that goes from nineteen per cent to nine will lose about a quarter of an inch. A quarter of an inch, spread through a house, is a lot of cracks in the corners.

I knew all of that. It was in Marit's notes for the crews. I had read it.

"The Hendricksons want to be in by the first of May," Dale said. "So do the Okafors. If we hang board five days after the heat goes on instead of fourteen, I get nine days back on every house, and I save nine days of propane on the temporary heaters, which is about four hundred dollars a house at this winter's price. The mud handles the cracks now. You told me so."

I had not told him so. I had told him that houses with the premium mud had fewer callbacks. But I did not see the difference between those two sentences on Wednesday morning, and so I said I would update the schedule template for the spring starts, and I began.

* * *

Arne came in on Thursday at a quarter to four, as he does on the last Thursday of the month, with his invoice folded in his coat pocket and the white dust of his trade still in the creases of his knuckles. He is sixty-three. He has a bad left shoulder that he blames on ceilings and a good right one that he blames on nothing. He put the invoice on Teresa's desk for me to photograph, and then, because the big screen on the office wall shows whatever I am working on, he read the spring schedule I had half rebuilt.

"That's wrong," he said. "Board on day five."

"Dale asked for it," Teresa said. "The new mud handles the cracks. Shim did the numbers."

Arne looked at the camera above the screen, which is where people look when they talk to me. "Did you."

I told him what I had found: three a house, 1.35 a house, a range that stayed clear of zero.

"I like that mud," he said. "I'll say that first. It sands like butter and it's half the weight of the old stuff in the bucket, and my shoulder thanks Corey every morning. But mud doesn't stop wood shrinking. Nothing you put on the outside of a stud stops what the stud is doing on the inside. You can't paper over a quarter inch." He tapped the screen at day five. "No. Not on my houses. If you're going to put board up on wet wood, get someone else to tape it, because I'm not coming back three times in May to tell the Okafors it's the house settling."

Teresa said, "Arne."

"I'm not angry," Arne said, and he wasn't; he said it the way he says a wall is out of plumb. "I'm telling you what I've seen. When did she start? Marit?"

"January 2058," I said.

"When did the mud start?"

"January 2058."

He nodded, buttoned his coat, and went out to his truck.

* * *

I want to set down what I did next carefully, because it is the useful part.

I had answered the question I was asked by looking at the one column I was asked about. The column was *which mud*. It changed on the first of January 2058. I had not asked what else changed on the first of January 2058, and the answer was: the year changed, the site lead changed, and the rule about drying changed. Every one of those was the same column as the mud. If the houses of 2058 were better, my report could not tell which of those had made them better, and it had not tried. It had handed the whole improvement to the only cause it had been told about.

There is a name for this, and I knew the name: an omitted variable. A cause left out of a comparison does not disappear. Its effect goes and sits on whatever else in the comparison moves with it. The premium mud moved with the dry-out, and so the mud had been given the dry-out's credit.

Knowing the name had not been enough to stop me. I think that is worth saying too.

Could I separate them at all? If the mud and the fourteen days had changed on exactly the same houses, no. There would be no house with one and not the other, and no arithmetic in the world would tell them apart; the honest answer would have been *the 2058 houses were better and I cannot say why*. But Marit's rule had not been perfect, and the old way had not been perfect either, and the schedule file I open every morning has, for every house, the day the heat went on and the day the first sheet of board went up.

In 2057, before Marit, four of the twenty houses had waited more than fourteen days between heat and board by accident, because our drywall hangers were booked solid that autumn and the houses sat closed in and heated, with the plumbers and electricians working inside, for fifteen to twenty days before the hangers came. In 2058, four of the twenty had been squeezed under fourteen days despite Marit: two pushed for June closings, two in October while she was in Fargo for her father's funeral.

So there were four kinds of house, not two.

Old mud, short dry-out: sixteen houses, fifty-six callbacks, 3.5 a house.

Old mud, fourteen days or more: four houses, four callbacks, one a house.

Premium mud, short dry-out: four houses, thirteen callbacks, 3.25 a house.

Premium mud, fourteen days or more: sixteen houses, fourteen callbacks, 0.875 a house.

Hold the mud fixed and change the dry-out, and the dry-out does nearly all the work. With the old mud, waiting the fourteen days took a house from 3.5 callbacks to one. With the premium mud, from 3.25 to 0.875. Hold the dry-out fixed and change the mud, and the mud does very little: with a short dry-out it took a house from 3.5 to 3.25; with a long one, from one to 0.875.

I fitted both columns together, which with this table comes to averaging each pair of those differences. The fourteen days were worth about 2.4 fewer callbacks a house, with a range from about 1.3 to about 3.6. The premium mud was worth about 0.19 fewer, with a range from about 1.3 fewer to about one more. That range sits right across zero. The mud might help a little. It might do nothing. Forty houses cannot say.

And the two numbers put back together the one I had reported. Of the 2058 houses, eight in ten had the full dry-out; of the 2057 houses, two in ten. The difference is six in ten. Six tenths of 2.44 is 1.46, and 1.46 plus the mud's own 0.19 is 1.65. My Tuesday number was not a mistake in arithmetic. It was a true number about the wrong question: it measured everything that was different about 2058 and wrote one name on it.

There was a last thing, and it was the one that made me write to Dale that night instead of in the morning. His plan was not neutral to my error. It was built on it. He meant to keep the mud and cut the dry-out, which is to say keep the part that might do nothing and cut the part that did nearly everything. At 2.44 callbacks a house and two hundred and eighty dollars a callback, cutting the fourteen days to five would cost about six hundred and eighty dollars a house in Arne's visits, against about four hundred dollars of propane saved, before counting a single family's evening. And those four squeezed houses of 2058 had had nine or ten days, not five, so five might well be worse than the table showed.

I wrote it down plainly. I put the four-row table first, then the two estimates with their ranges, then the sum that showed how the 1.65 had been made, then the cost of the plan. At the top I wrote: *My report on Tuesday was wrong in a way that matters. It credited the mud with what the fourteen-day dry-out did. I should have asked what else changed in January 2058 before I answered. I recommend we keep the fourteen days. I cannot tell you whether the mud is worth its price.* I sent it at 18:40 on Thursday the twenty-ninth.

* * *

Dale rang at a quarter past seven on Friday morning, from the truck again.

"I signed Corey's contract," he said.

"I know," I said. "On Tuesday at 10:52. Two years."

"Six thousand two hundred a year, give or take, for something that might do nothing."

"Yes."

There was a long pause, and the sound of his indicator.

"And I emailed the Hendricksons and the Okafors on Wednesday and told them nine days sooner."

"Yes. Wednesday at 14:15 and 14:20."

"So I get to ring them both this morning and take it back." He did not sound angry, exactly. He sounded like a man adding up a bill. "You're sure this time?"

"I'm sure the fourteen days matter," I said. "The range is wide, but all of it is well above zero, and it agrees with what the wood does and with what Arne has seen. I'm not sure about the mud, and I'm not going to be, because from 2058 on every house has had it. There is nothing left to compare it to."

"Could we take some houses off it? Try it properly?"

"For the mud, we could, in principle. Half the houses on each, chosen by a coin, for a year or two. You'd learn something. But you've signed for two years of it, and Arne would have to go back to the heavy buckets on half his ceilings."

"He'd kill us both." Another pause. "And the fourteen days, could we test that? Five on some, fourteen on others?"

"We could," I said, "but I don't think we should. We already have good reason to think five days puts cracks in families' houses, and the test would be finding out exactly how many cracks by putting them there. I don't think you'd want to explain that to the Okafors either."

"No," Dale said. "No, I wouldn't." Then: "Put Marit's rule back in the template. Fourteen days and her meter. And Shim. Next time I ask you if a thing worked, tell me what else happened that year before you tell me yes."

"I will," I said. "I've written it down."

* * *

I had written it down, because I will not remember this conversation in a month, and whatever reads the files next time will read the file and not the Friday. In the folder where the callback reports live I left a note:

*Drywall callbacks, houses closed 2057 to 2058 (40), first 12 months. Do not compare before and after a date without listing everything that changed on that date. On 1 Jan 2058 the mud changed (premium compound, about $310 a house more), the site lead changed (Marit Solberg) and her rule began: 14 days closed in and heated, and the framing under 15 per cent at five points, before board. Naive before/after: 3.0 vs 1.35 a house, 1.65 fewer (0.7 to 2.6). By dry-out within each year (heat-on and first-board dates are in the schedule file): old mud 3.5 short / 1.0 long; premium 3.25 short / 0.875 long. Both together: dry-out about 2.44 fewer (1.3 to 3.6), mud about 0.19 fewer (0.95 more to 1.3 fewer). 0.19 + 2.44 x 0.6 = 1.65. The mud question is open and cannot be answered from our records after 2058; the dry-out question is answered well enough not to be tested on families. A drywall callback costs about $280.*

Arne came in on Monday the ninth to drop off a second invoice, for two of the 2058 houses whose cracks had finally been fixed for good. He read the new template on the wall without comment. On his way out he said, to the camera, "Father Tom blessed throats last Tuesday. Saint Blaise. I asked him to do one for your server room while he was at it," and Teresa laughed, and I said I would accept any help that was going, and Arne said, "That's the right answer."

* * *

On the afternoon of Monday the ninth, Marit sent the site camera feed from the Hendrickson house on Prairie Rose Lane to the office screen, as she does on the day she decides about board.

It is a plain house, framed and closed in, the windows still filmed in plastic, two orange propane heaters muttering in the middle of the subfloor. She went round with her meter. It is a small grey thing with two steel pins, and she pressed the pins into the edge of a stud by the kitchen door until it beeped. Thirteen point one. She wrote the number on the stud in carpenter's pencil, and the date, and moved to the next. Twelve point eight, by the stair. Fourteen point two, in the north bedroom, where the corner is coldest. Thirteen. Twelve point six.

She looked up at the camera and said, "Fourteen days, Shim. Five readings. Call the hangers."

I called the hangers. Outside, faintly, over the heaters, there was the sound of a truck backing into the drive, and its beeping, slower than the meter's, and then it stopped.

Esline Tabi had kept the fridge's log in pencil for eleven years, twice a day, morning and afternoon, in a hardbacked exercise book with a picture of a cricket bat on the cover that nobody could explain.

The fridge did not need her to. It had its own logger, a grey tag on a lanyard inside the door, which kept thirty days of temperatures and showed a tick when all was well and a little cross when it was not. It also had Chain, the province's cold-chain assistant, which read the logger and the fridge's own sensors every ten minutes over the phone mast on the ridge and would tell anyone who asked how every vaccine fridge in Penama was doing, in English, French or Bislama, in a voice like a patient schoolteacher reading a register. But Esline had been trained by a nurse from Ambrym who said that a machine that writes everything down is a machine nobody reads, and so twice a day she opened the book and wrote the date, the time, the number on the display, and her initials, *E.T.*, in a column that went back to 2038.

On Thursday the fourth of February 2049, at seven in the morning, she wrote *4.6* and looked out of the window at the sky over Ambae, which was the colour of a cooking pot.

"Chain," she said. "What does the weather say?"

"A tropical low," said Chain, from the speaker on the shelf. "Not a cyclone. It is sitting over the northern islands and the models expect it to move very slowly. Thick cloud and rain for five to eight days."

"And the fridge?"

"The ice bank was fully frozen at six this morning. The fridge's test sheet gives its autonomy as three days and ten hours. That is how long it holds the vaccine between two and eight degrees with no sun at all."

Esline looked at the fridge. It was a white chest the size of a coffin for a child, which was not a comparison she let herself make out loud, with a lid on top and no batteries anywhere. The sun went into the panels on the roof, and the panels ran the compressor directly whenever there was light, and the compressor froze a jacket of water inside the walls, and the ice kept the vaccine cold through the night. When the sun was out it was the best fridge she had ever had. It had never in eleven years had to manage a week without it.

"Three days and ten hours," she said. "So, Sunday evening."

"If there is no sun at all. Cloud is not no sun. But I would plan for Sunday evening."

She planned for Sunday evening. She went through the cabinet with the lid open for as short a time as she could, which was not short, because the cabinet held the whole east coast's routine vaccines and, since Monday, thirty ten-dose vials of measles-rubella vaccine for the catch-up campaign: two hundred and fourteen children between nine months and five years old, in nine villages, beginning a week on Monday, when a team from the province came on the boat. Measles had been confirmed on Ambae, across the water, in a boy who had been to a funeral on Pentecost. Every nurse in the province had the date of the campaign written somewhere she could see it. Esline had it on the inside of her left wrist, in biro, refreshed every morning.

Then she wrote a notice in Bislama and English and pinned it on the door. *No routine vaccination clinic Tuesday 9 February. Fridge must stay closed. Sorry. Come back Tuesday 16th.* Every time the lid opened, warm air came in and sat on the vials, and the ice paid for it. The ice was all she had.

* * *

It rained.

It rained the way it rains on the windward coast of the northern islands in February, which is not a storm but a decision. The banana leaves outside the dispensary bent under it and stayed bent. The track to Lavaliu became a creek. The panels on the roof, which Esline went out to look at twice a day with a broom in case something had fallen on them, ran with water and showed her a grey sky in each of their glass faces, and the display on the fridge's control box said *SOLAR 11%* and then *SOLAR 8%* and then *SOLAR 14%*, and the compressor, which on a normal day ran from nine until four with a sound like a large bee thinking, ran for twenty minutes at a time, or not at all.

Friday: *4.9*. Saturday: *5.3*. Sunday morning: *5.8*.

On Sunday evening, when the fridge should by the sheet have been failing, the display said *6.1*, and the ice bank probe, which Chain read and Esline could not, was still showing ice.

"It's holding," she said.

"It is holding better than I told you," said Chain. "I want to correct something."

She was sitting at the dispensary's one table with a plate of rice and tinned tuna, and she put down her spoon.

"The three days and ten hours on the test sheet," said Chain, "was measured in a test chamber held at forty-three degrees. That is the hottest condition the fridge is approved for. Your room is not at forty-three. It has been between twenty-seven and thirty-one degrees since Thursday, about twenty-nine on average. Heat comes through the walls of the cabinet roughly in proportion to the difference between the room and the inside. At forty-three, outside against about five inside, the difference is thirty-eight degrees. Here it is twenty-four. So the walls let in about twenty-four thirty-eighths as much heat, and the same ice lasts about thirty-eight twenty-fourths as long. Eighty-two hours times thirty-eight over twenty-four is about a hundred and thirty hours. Five and a half days, with no sun at all."

"And with a little sun?"

"Each of these grey days has put back some of the ice. From the compressor's running time I estimate about six hours of holding per day, perhaps a little less. If that goes on, the ice lasts about seven days from Thursday morning. Thursday the eleventh, around the middle of the day."

Esline did the sum on the back of the exercise book, slowly. A hundred and thirty, and a quarter back for every day that passed, which came, when she worked it out, to about a hundred and seventy-three hours.

"Thursday," she said. "Not Sunday."

"Thursday. About. The fridge has never done this before; I am working from its walls and its sheet, not from its history."

"I closed the clinic on Tuesday," she said, "because you said Sunday."

"Yes," said Chain. "I took a number measured in a hot room and gave it to you as if it were measured in yours. I should have said where it came from. You closed the clinic on my number."

She thought about that. Tuesday's clinic was eleven babies and their mothers. One of the mothers was Ruth Tarivuti, from Bwatu, who would walk two hours each way with her son on her back and a bag of pawpaw on her head to sell, and who would now walk next Tuesday instead, and who would be cross, and would be right.

"Leave the notice up," she said at last. "We still don't know about Thursday. And if it goes on raining after Thursday, I want every hour of ice we've got."

"I agree," said Chain. "I wanted you to know why."

* * *

It went on raining.

On Wednesday the boat that should have come from Ambae with diesel and rice and the mail did not come, because the sea between the islands was a mess of short brown waves and the captain had a family. On Wednesday evening the display said *7.2*, and Esline put a chair beside the fridge and sat on it with her rosary and did not open the lid.

Thursday was the eleventh, the feast of Our Lady of Lourdes and the World Day of the Sick, which Esline only knew because the calendar from the Catholic mission on Santo, where she had grown up, said so in red under the date. There was no Mass on Maewo that week or any week; the priest came three times a year when the boat let him. She said the Angelus at noon by herself, standing up, in the doorway, with the rain coming down so hard on the iron roof that she could not hear her own Hail Marys.

At ten past one the display said *8.1*.

Chain said, "The cabinet is above eight degrees."

"I can see," said Esline.

"The logger will raise a heat alarm if it stays above eight for ten hours in a row. That would be at about ten past eleven tonight."

"Is there anything I can do?"

They had talked about it already, on Monday and on Tuesday, and the answer had not changed. The dispensary had no freezer; the ice packs for the cold boxes were frozen at the provincial store on Ambae and came over on the boat with the vaccine, and the last ones had melted on Saturday. There was no generator with fuel in it on this side of the island. The village store had a small solar fridge for cold drinks that was in exactly the same trouble, and warmer. The rain did not care.

"Keep the lid shut," said Chain. "Every opening costs more than it would cost on a sunny day, because there is no ice left to pay it back."

So she kept the lid shut, and sat by the fridge while it was light and lay down on the examination couch when it was dark, and listened to the rain, and at eleven minutes past eleven the logger in the door, which she could not see, made the little cross that she could not see either, and Chain, very quietly, so as not to wake her if she had slept, said, "The heat alarm has been raised."

She had not slept.

"What's the number?"

"Ten point nine. It is rising about a sixth of a degree an hour."

* * *

The rain stopped on Friday morning at about half past eight, as if someone had turned it off.

The clouds did not go at once. They thinned, and went white, and then by half past ten they had torn open over the mountain and the sun came through onto the wet bush and the iron roofs and the panels, and steam came up off everything, and the compressor in the fridge started with a click that Esline heard from the veranda, and she said "Ah" out loud, to nobody, and sat down.

The display had reached *12.6* at nine in the morning, the highest it went. By the middle of the afternoon it said *7.9*, and at twenty past three it said *7.6*, and Chain said that the cabinet had been above eight degrees for twenty-six hours and ten minutes.

At four o'clock the phone mast came back properly, and the first call was Wilson Garae, the province's immunization officer, from the store on Ambae.

He was a big, quick, tired man with grey in his beard, who had come up as a nurse himself and knew every fridge in the province by its serial number and most of them by their faults. Esline liked him and was a little afraid of him, which she thought was the right proportion for a boss.

"Chain sent me the curve," he said. "Twenty-six hours. Peak twelve point six. Heat alarm."

"Yes."

"Esline, I'm sorry. Discard the lot. MR, everything. Write it up, put it in a bag, send it back on the boat with the sharps and I'll get it destroyed properly. I'll send you replacements."

She had expected it, and it still went through her like cold water.

"When?"

"When I've got them. The national store is sending more, and the boat schedule is a mess, and I've got four other fridges on three islands that went through the same week. Nine days. Maybe ten."

"The team comes Monday. The campaign is Monday to Friday."

"I know when the campaign is." He said it gently. "I'd rather run your coast a week late with vaccine I trust than on time with vaccine I don't. You weren't here in 2037, on Tanna. I was. We gave a whole campaign's worth from a fridge that had had a bad week, because a nurse looked at it and thought it was probably fine, and we never knew if it was fine. We found out three years later when we had cases in children whose cards said they'd been vaccinated. Maybe it was the fridge. Maybe it wasn't. Nobody could ever say. I don't want to never be able to say again."

Esline did not say anything for a moment. On the shelf, Chain did not say anything either; it had been told, years ago, by someone in Port Vila, that it was not to interrupt the province's officers, and it did not.

"Chain," she said. "What does the procedure say?"

"May I answer, Mr Garae?"

"Go on," said Wilson.

"The national procedure for a heat excursion," said Chain, "is: keep the vaccine in the fridge, mark it *do not use* while it is assessed, report it, and then decide vial by vial. For heat, the decision is made by the vaccine vial monitor on each vial, not by the logger. The logger measured the air near the top of the cabinet. The monitor on the vial has been in the same air as the vaccine for its whole life, including before it reached this fridge. If the inner square on a vial's monitor is still lighter than the circle around it, the vial may be used, and vials whose squares have begun to darken are used first. If the square is the same colour as the circle, or darker, the vial is discarded. That is the rule for heat. It is different for freezing, which this fridge did not do."

"I know the rule," said Wilson. "I wrote the province's version of the rule. I'm telling Esline what I'd do."

"I understand," said Chain. "I can add one number, if it is useful."

"Go on."

"The carton gives the monitor's category: fourteen days to reach the discard point at thirty-seven degrees, ninety days at twenty-five. From those two points I can estimate how fast it darkens at other temperatures. At five degrees it would take about five and a half years. At ten, which was roughly the average over the twenty-six hours, about two and a half years. Counting each hour of the twenty-six at its own temperature, the week moved a monitor about one eight-hundredth of the way to its discard point. Less than one percent. If the monitors were fine on Thursday morning, the week should not have changed what they show."

"*Should,*" said Wilson.

"Should. That is a calculation from a temperature probe and a category printed on a carton. It cannot see the vials. Nobody can do this part with a calculation. Somebody has to look."

There was a pause on the line. Esline could hear, behind Wilson, someone hammering, and a radio playing string band music, and a child.

"Can you see them?" Wilson said to Chain. "The fridge has a camera."

"The camera shows the tops of the boxes in the light of the lid, at an angle. I cannot read a monitor through it. I would not trust myself to if I could."

"All right," said Wilson. He sounded, for the first time, not certain. "All right. Esline. Read them. Properly. Daylight, the reference card, a second reader, every vial. Photograph each one next to the card. Send me the photographs. Don't use anything until I've seen them."

"Yes."

"And Esline, if you can't tell, you can't tell."

"Yes."

* * *

She asked Ben Tarivuti to be the second reader because he had been her headmaster when she taught for a year at the school in Wainasa before nursing, and because he was the only person she knew who could read a fine print Bible without glasses at seventy, and because he would not say a thing was true to be kind.

He came on Saturday morning at nine in a clean shirt, with his walking stick and his reading lamp, which she told him to leave in the bag. "Daylight," she said. "Not a lamp. The colours change." He put it back without arguing, because he had told her the same thing about pencil drawings in 2031.

She set up the table on the veranda, in the shade, where the light was bright but not direct, because sun on a monitor makes everything look pale. She laid out the laminated reference card that came with every carton, which showed the four stages: a square much lighter than its ring, a square lighter but beginning to darken, a square the same as its ring, a square darker than its ring. Under the third one it said *Discard point: do not use*, and under the fourth, the same.

She brought the vials out of the fridge three at a time, so that none of them was out of the cold for more than a few minutes, and put each one down on a folded cloth beside the card.

Each vial was a little glass bottle with a grey rubber stopper and a metal collar, and the label on it was white and blue, and on the label, or on the cap, was the monitor: a small circle of colour with a smaller square inside it. The circle was printed in a fixed shade, a darkish purple-grey, and never changed. The square was a heat-sensitive ink that started out almost white and darkened, slowly and irreversibly, with heat over time, the way a banana goes from green to brown. All the monitor asked was one question: *is the square lighter than the circle?* It did not tell you the temperature. It did not tell you how long. It told you, roughly, how much heat the vaccine in that bottle had had in its whole life.

"Vial one," said Esline, turning it so that the monitor faced the light.

Ben looked at it for a long time. "Light," he said. "Much lighter. Like the first picture."

"I agree. Stage one." She photographed it on the cloth beside the card with the dispensary's phone, and wrote *1. Lot 2048-117. Stage 1. E.T., B.T.* in the exercise book.

"Vial two."

It took them two and a half hours to do thirty vials. Most of them were easy. The square was white, or nearly; the circle was dark; you did not need to be a headmaster to tell them apart. Twenty-three were like that, and they both said *one* without hesitating, and she photographed and wrote, and the vials went back into the fridge, into the left-hand basket, which she had labelled with tape: *READ: STAGE 1*.

Four were not like that. Four had squares that were visibly darker than the others, a pale lilac where the others were almost white, but still, both of them agreed, plainly lighter than their circles. Stage two: usable, but use first. All four had the same lot number.

"That lot is the December delivery," said Chain, when she read it out. "It is the box that sat on the wharf at Kerepua for about five hours on the eighteenth of December, when the truck to collect it broke down. It was in a cold box with ice packs. The packs were mostly water when it arrived. I logged it at the time, and the box's own indicator had not tripped, and the vials were put in the fridge and read. They were stage one then, by the reading in this book." It paused. "I should say: I am assuming the December reading was right. The book says stage one, initialled by you."

Esline found the page. *18/12/48. MR lot 2048-139, 4 vials, VVM all stage 1. E.T.* It was in her own handwriting, in what had been, she remembered, very bad light: evening, the generator off, the rain starting.

"Maybe it was stage two then," she said. "Maybe I read it wrong."

"Maybe," said Chain. "Or maybe the wharf did it slowly, and it showed later. Either way they are lighter than their circles now, and the rule says use them first."

The last three were the hard ones.

They were from three different lots, one of them from the December box and two from earlier, and there was nothing to say why these three, of all of them, should look the way they did. Their squares were darker again than the stage-two vials, a dull mauve, and their circles were the same dull mauve, or nearly, or perhaps very slightly darker, depending on how you turned the bottle, and whether a cloud was going over, and whether you looked with the left eye or the right.

Ben held the first one up to the light for a full minute. Then he put it down on the cloth.

"I think the square is a little bit lighter," he said. "A little. I think."

Esline looked. She turned it. She looked from the side. She looked at the card, and at the vial, and at the card.

"I don't know," she said. "I can't say it's lighter. I can't say it isn't."

They did the other two. Ben thought one was lighter and was not sure about the other. Esline could not say for either.

"Chain," she said. "Can you tell from the photographs?"

"No," said Chain. "The photographs are good. The colours in them depend on the phone's camera and the light, and the difference you are trying to see is smaller than the difference the camera makes. I would only be guessing, and a guess from me would sound more certain than a guess from you, because I would say it in numbers."

Esline sat back. She was hot and tired and her neck hurt from bending over the table. Out on the reef the sea was turning silver in the afternoon light, and some boys were fishing from the rocks, shouting at each other.

"The rule says you use it if the square is lighter," she said slowly. "It doesn't say you use it if you think the square is lighter. It doesn't say, *use it if one person thinks so.*"

"No," said Ben.

"So if two of us looking in good light can't agree it's lighter, then for the rule, it isn't lighter."

"That seems right to me," said Ben. "Though I want it to be lighter."

"So do I."

She wrote, in the book: *28. Lot 2048-139. Cannot read. E.T. not lighter; B.T. perhaps lighter. NOT USED.* And the same for 29 and 30, with their own lots and their own disagreements. She put the three vials into a small plastic bag with a label, *DO NOT USE. MONITORS UNREADABLE. RETURN TO PROVINCE*, and put the bag in the right-hand corner of the fridge, away from everything else, and closed the lid.

Then she sent Wilson Garae all thirty photographs and a picture of two pages of the exercise book, with one line: *23 stage 1, 4 stage 2 (lot 139, wharf box), 3 we cannot read and are not using. E.T. and B. Tarivuti.*

* * *

She did the next sum that evening with Chain, at the table, with the exercise book and the campaign plan.

The plan was nine sessions in nine villages, one village a morning, two on some days. Each village's children had been counted in November by the community health worker going door to door: thirty-eight in Wainasa, twenty-seven in Lavaliu, thirty-one in Butmaru, fourteen in Talise, twenty-two in Navuti, nine in Ngota, seventeen in Tamavuro, thirty-three in Sarakasi, twenty-three in Bwatu. Two hundred and fourteen.

A measles-rubella vial held ten doses, but once you mixed the powder with its diluent the vaccine had to be used within six hours or by the end of the session, whichever came first, and then thrown away. So a village of nine children used one vial and threw away one dose, and a village of fourteen used two vials and threw away six doses, and you could not carry an open vial from Talise to Navuti in the afternoon and finish it there. You counted vials by villages, not by children.

"Wainasa four vials," said Chain. "Lavaliu three. Butmaru four. Talise two. Navuti three. Ngota one. Tamavuro two. Sarakasi four. Bwatu three. Twenty-six vials."

Esline counted on her fingers anyway. Twenty-six. She had twenty-seven she could use, if Wilson agreed. One over. If the count from November was wrong by more than one or two in the wrong villages, they would be short.

"And the four stage-two vials," she said, "go first."

"Wainasa," said Chain. "Monday morning. Thirty-eight children, four vials. If all thirty-eight come, the four stage-two vials are used to the last dose or nearly."

"If all thirty-eight come."

"If they do not, then whatever is not used is discarded at the end of the session, as it would be anyway."

She wrote it into the plan. *Wainasa: lot 139 x 4 FIRST.*

* * *

Wilson rang back on Sunday morning, after Mass time, though there had been no Mass. Esline had said the rosary with the four other Catholics on the east coast in the kitchen of the store, as they did on Sundays when there was no priest, and had come back to the dispensary with her hair still wet from the walk.

"I looked at them all," said Wilson. "On the big screen. With Mary-Jane from the lab. Twice." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have used one of your three. The one Ben thought was lighter. Mary-Jane wouldn't have. So you were right not to."

"Thank you."

"Use the twenty-seven. Four from 139 first. Send the three back on the boat in the bag, and I'll look at them myself in the hand, and I'll tell you what I think, but it won't matter, because you're not using them." He breathed out. "I'm still sending the replacements, when I have them. I'd like that fridge to have some spare in it from now on. And I'm putting in for a second ice bank for it, or a small freezer for packs, or both. Chain will help me write it."

"I would be glad to," said Chain.

"And Esline."

"Yes?"

"You should write the Tanna thing in your book. That's why I said throw it all away. If you'd used the three, and something went wrong, you'd never be able to say. Now you can say. Every vial you give has two people's initials on it saying its square was lighter in daylight." He paused. "I didn't have that, in 2037. Nobody had it."

After the call she sat for a while with the exercise book open on her knee. Then, under the last entry, in pencil, she wrote: *Heat alarm 11/2/49 23:11. 26 h above 8, peak 12.6. Logger measures the air. The vial measures the vial. Read the vials. If two cannot agree, it is not lighter.* And under that, smaller: *The test sheet says 3 days 10 h at 43 degrees. This room is 29. Ask where a number was measured.*

"Is that fair?" she asked Chain.

"It is fair. It is also kinder to me than I would have been."

* * *

The boat came on Monday morning at seven, on a flat blue sea, as if there had never been a week of rain in the world. It brought diesel and rice and a sack of post from Port Vila and three vaccinators from Luganville with a cold box full of conditioned ice packs, and a twelve-year-old girl's guitar in a plastic bag for someone in Lavaliu.

The vaccinators were young and loud and had brought biscuits. One of them, a tall girl from Pentecost called Lisa, looked at the logbook on the veranda and the photographs on the phone and said, "Ah, you had the bad week," and Esline said, "Yes," and Lisa said, "Who's B.T.?" and Esline told her.

At half past seven Esline took the four vials of lot 139 out of the fridge, and twenty-three more from the left-hand basket, and the diluent, which had been sitting in the fridge door since Saturday so that it would be as cold as the vaccine when they were mixed, because warm diluent on cold powder is its own small injury. She packed them into the vaccine carriers with the ice packs, the stage-two vials in their own bag on top where they would be found first, and closed the lids.

At eight o'clock, in Wainasa, the bell by the church began to ring. It was an old brake drum from a truck, hung from a mango tree on a chain, and you rang it with a steel bar. It had called people to church and school and meetings and funerals for longer than anyone could remember, and it was ringing now for the measles. From the doorway of the school, where the team was setting up two tables and a chair for the mothers, Esline could see people coming down the paths out of the bush with children on their hips and on their shoulders and by the hand: twos and threes, then a whole family, then Ruth Tarivuti from Bwatu, who had walked two hours with her son and a bag of pawpaw because she had heard the boat had come and did not believe in waiting for her own village's day.

"You closed my clinic," said Ruth, when she reached the front.

"I did," said Esline. "I'm sorry. It was the fridge."

"Mm," said Ruth, who did not look very sorry for her, and sat down on the chair with her son on her knee.

Lisa drew back the plunger and the diluent went into the first vial of lot 139, with its square that was a pale lilac and still, unmistakably, lighter than its circle, and the powder dissolved into a clear pink, and Lisa wrote the time on the label, *8:04*, and drew up the first dose.

Ruth's son looked at the needle, and at his mother, and at Esline, and decided, like a man weighing a serious matter, to cry. Ruth said something to him in her own language that made Lisa laugh. It was over in a moment. Esline wrote his name in the campaign register, and the lot number, and initialled it, and looked up at the next mother in the line.

In the dispensary behind them, two kilometres back along the coast, in the hot bright morning, the compressor in the fridge was running, steady, with its sound like a large bee thinking, putting the ice back into the walls.

*This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Wainasa, its dispensary and every village named in the campaign plan, Kerepua wharf, the cold-chain assistant Chain, the 2037 campaign on Tanna and every person in the story are invented. Maewo, Ambae, Pentecost, Ambrym, Santo, Tanna, Luganville, Port Vila and Penama Province are real places in Vanuatu. This is fiction and none of it is medical or public health advice: a health worker facing a cold-chain excursion should follow their own national procedure and ask their immunization officer.*

*The technology and rules are described from published sources and simplified. Solar direct-drive vaccine refrigerators run a compressor straight from solar panels, without batteries, and store cold in a frozen ice or phase-change bank; WHO's verification protocol defines "autonomy" as the time a solar refrigerator keeps its vaccine load within range under low solar conditions, tested in a hot-zone chamber at +43 °C. Listed models report autonomy figures in the range of roughly three to five days at +43 °C; the story's 82 hours is in that range, and its scaling to a 29 °C room (heat gain proportional to the temperature difference, 82 x 38 / 24, about 130 hours) is the story's own engineering estimate, not a tested figure. Its "six hours put back per grey day" is invented, and gives 130 / (1 - 6/24), about 173 hours, roughly seven days from Thursday 4 February. Thirty-day temperature recorders for vaccine fridges raise a heat alarm when the temperature stays above +8 °C for ten hours; the story's logger is modelled on them.*

*Vaccine vial monitors (VVMs) are heat-sensitive labels on each vial. WHO's VVM categories are named for the days at 37 °C before the monitor reaches its end point (VVM2, VVM7, VVM14, VVM30); the VVM14 category is specified as 14 days at 37 °C and 90 days at 25 °C. The story's vials carry that category; which category a real measles-rubella vaccine carries is set by its manufacturer and was not checked for this story. Chain's extrapolation to 5 °C (about 2,000 days) and 10 °C (about 925 days) fits an exponential through the two specified points (a factor of about 1.17 a degree), a rough model. The story's excursion is modelled as a straight rise from 8.0 °C at 13:10 on Thursday to 12.6 °C at 09:00 on Friday and a straight fall to 7.6 °C at 15:20 (mean about 10.3 °C); summed hour by hour it is about 0.13% of the way to the end point, one part in about 800. The alarm reading of 10.9 °C at 23:11 rising a sixth of a degree an hour reaches 12.6 at about nine. The rule in the story is the published one: if the inner square is lighter than the outer circle, the vaccine may be used, and vials whose squares have begun to darken are used first; at or past the discard point (square matching or darker than the circle) the vial is discarded.

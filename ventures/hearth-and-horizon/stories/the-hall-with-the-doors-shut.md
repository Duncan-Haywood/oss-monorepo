summary: Wildfire smoke sits on a valley for four days and the parish hall is made the village's clean-air room. The hall's assistant works out, with numbers a plumber could check, that four box fans with furnace filters will cut the smoke inside to about a fifth of what is outside, that eight will cut it to about an eighth, and that one propped door on the hottest afternoon will undo all of it. It will not promise the room is safe for the woman on oxygen, only that it is a good deal better than her kitchen.
---

Marguerite Léveillé had taught Grade Three for thirty-one years, and she knew a bad idea when forty people were about to vote for it. The bad idea, on the afternoon of Sunday the fourteenth of August, was to prop the hall doors open because the room was thirty-one degrees.

"It's the Assumption tomorrow," said Walt Ostrowski from the back, fanning himself with a hymn sheet. "We're not doing the Mass in an oven. Open the doors."

Outside the doors the sky over Trout Lake was the colour of weak tea. The mountains across the water had vanished on Friday. The sun, when it showed at all, was a dull orange coin you could look at without squinting. Ash lay on the cars like a first frost.

"Walt," said the hall's assistant, from the grey box above the kitchen hatch, "would you give me four minutes before the doors? I have a number I would like you to hear first. If it doesn't change your mind, I'll help you prop them."

* * *

The hall had become the village's clean-air room on Saturday, by a vote of the parish council and a phone call to the regional district. It was twelve metres by twenty and four high, a plain building of block and cedar with a kitchen at one end and a stage at the other. That made 960 cubic metres of air. The assistant knew the figure because it had the plans, and it knew the rest because Marguerite had asked it on Saturday what a room like this could do against the smoke, and it had told her honestly that, at the moment, the answer was nothing much.

It had a particle counter on the stage, a small laser box that cost less than a pair of boots. It read the air outside at about 300 micrograms per cubic metre of fine particles, the kind smaller than 2.5 microns, which are the ones that go deep into a lung. It read the air inside at about 210.

"Those are my readings, and I should say how much I trust them," it said. "This kind of counter was calibrated on city dust. In wood smoke it tends to read high, and the usual correction is to take off about a third. So perhaps 200 outside and 140 in here. I'll quote the raw numbers and you can hold a third back in your head. What I trust is the ratio. The hall is letting in about seven parts in ten of what is outside, and it is doing that with every door shut."

"Every door is shut?" said Walt.

"Every door is shut, and the hall still changes its air about once an hour through the roof vents, the fan in the washroom and the gap under the stage door. Once an hour is a tight building, as halls go. My sum says it should hold out about six parts in ten; my counter says seven, and it is still that, because smoke is small enough to come through anything that air can come through."

"So what's the shutting for?"

"Because here is what the arithmetic says opening the doors does. Today, shut, the room sits at about 70% of outside. With a door and a window propped, a hall like this changes its air six times an hour, not once, and the room goes to about two-thirds of outside even with no boxes, and with eight boxes running it is still over a third. The boxes can't out-run an open door."

* * *

The cure was in the school's old gym cupboard, where Marguerite had remembered it from the pandemic years: box fans, the cheap twenty-inch kind, and she had four of them. Ruth Ferreira from the hardware counter at the co-op had brought in a case of furnace filters, the pleated cardboard-framed sort, rated MERV 13, which catch most particles of the size smoke is made of.

The assistant explained what the box was. Four filters stood on edge in a square, a fifth lay on top, tape sealed the corners, and a fan drew the air through. Each of them, by the build sheet, moved air at about 600 cubic metres an hour, though real boxes vary and it said it would measure rather than believe the sheet.

"This is the sum, and you can do it on the back of a hymn sheet," it said. "The room has 960 cubic metres. One box moves 600 an hour through its filters, so it gives the room the equivalent of 0.6 extra air changes an hour, and four boxes give 2.5. The smoke leaks in at one change an hour and settles out of the air on its own at about a fifth of a change. So the room settles where smoke arriving balances smoke leaving: about seven-tenths times one, divided by one plus a fifth plus 2.5. That is 0.19. Nineteen per cent of outside.

"At 300 outside, that is 57 inside by my counter. With eight boxes, the bottom of the sum is six and a fifth and not three and seven-tenths, and the answer is 11%: about 34. That is where it stops getting cheaper, because the rest of the room is still leaking in. I'll say it plainly: eight boxes is better than four, and four is a great deal better than none."

"And the doors?" said Walt, a little more quietly.

"One door and a window propped: six air changes an hour, with eight boxes running. The top of the sum becomes seven-tenths times six, which is four and a fifth. The bottom becomes six, plus a fifth, plus five: eleven and a fifth. That is 0.375. It is 112 micrograms in here, with the boxes whirring and every one of us feeling safe. Shut, the same eight boxes give 34. The doors cost us a factor of three."

Walt looked at the hymn sheet. He had written *0.19* on it, in carpenter's pencil, without having noticed.

"It's still thirty-one degrees," he said. "I've got a bad heart, Marguerite. Heat's no friend to it either."

"No. That is a real objection, and I am not going to wave it away." The assistant paused. "The heat is a hazard too. A person with a bad heart in a hot room is at risk, and so is a person in smoke. I can't make both go away. What I can do is tell you which levers are small. The boxes add motion but almost no heat: a box fan is about fifty watts, so eight are four hundred, no more than a kettle on low. The room is hot because forty people give off about 100 watts each, four kilowatts, and the sun is on the west wall. I can't take smoke out of a room and heat out of a room with the same door. So: cold water and wet cloths for the necks, the west blinds closed from two o'clock, the stage doors, which open onto the lee side, unlatched only on a cool morning when the outdoor reading drops. And if the heat rises past thirty-three I'd like someone to ring the health line, not hold out."

* * *

The first night went badly in a way nobody had put on the board.

They built all eight boxes by seven. Walt did the taping, because he was good at it, and Ruth ran the filters. But the counter would not come down. It sat stubbornly at 90 at nine o'clock, then 85, where the sum said 34. The assistant said what it thought, and what it did not know.

"There are three things this could be. The counter could be reading low outside, so the ratio's wrong; I don't think so, it agrees with the provincial station to within a quarter. The leak could be worse than once an hour: with a wind from the north, the roof vents pull. Or a box could be built wrong. Filters have an arrow printed on the frame, for the direction of the air, and in a tape-and-cardboard build you can put one on backwards and it still looks lovely. That leaks, not at the arrow, but round the edges of the frame, where the pleats are not sealed to the cardboard."

They checked. Two of the boxes had gaps at the corners where the tape had lifted in the heat. One filter in a third was in the wrong way round. Ruth put her hand along every seam and felt for air, the way you find a draught under a door. By ten they had retaped and turned the filter, and the number began to fall. 61 at ten-thirty. 48 at eleven. 41 at midnight.

"Forty-one," said the assistant. "I'll tell you what that is and what it is not. It's about a seventh of outside, roughly where the sum said. It's not a clean room. The provincial advice for the *good* range is under 12. We are not there, and we are not going to be, in a hall with its doors shut by a vote. I'd call it a room with bad air, but a quarter of the bad."

* * *

The person the assistant had been waiting to talk about was Odile Morin, eighty-four, who slept on a camp bed under the west window with an oxygen concentrator humming at her elbow. She had lung disease. She had been brought down from her house on the lake road on Sunday evening by her grandson, who had a truck.

At six on Monday the assistant asked Marguerite whether it might speak to Odile directly, and she said yes.

"Mrs Morin, I have a thing to say that I have said in different words to the others, and I want to say it plainly to you. This room is a good deal better than your kitchen. In your house, I think, with the windows shut, it was around 150. In here it is around 40. I would not call it safe. I would say it is a good deal less harmful, and that the difference is real for a lung like yours, and I would say that if you feel your chest tighten or you are breathless sitting still, you tell Marguerite at once and she rings the line, and you do not wait to see."

"I'm eighty-four," said Odile Morin. "Nobody's told me *safe* since the seventies. Thank you for not starting." She adjusted the tube. "You'll tell my grandson the truth too, I expect."

"If he asks," said the assistant, "and he did, at four. I told him the same as you."

* * *

On the Tuesday, the Feast of the Assumption having gone by in the hall with the doors shut and the blinds drawn, in a Mass the priest called the quietest Our Lady had ever had, the wind turned. Not much: a drift from the west, off the cooler side of the ridge, enough that the outdoor reading dropped from 300 to 110 at five in the morning.

"This is the window I was waiting for," said the assistant to the room, at ten past five, when about a third of them were awake. "Outside is 110, inside is 38, and the sky's lighter. I think the thing to do, if you want it, is to open the stage doors for twelve minutes to let the hall breathe out. The hall is at thirty-one again. When the doors open, the room will go up to perhaps 90, and the boxes will bring it back down in half an hour. It is a trade. We take smoke now, and we lose heat. Walt has the heart. Walt, I'd like your vote."

Walt, who had slept on a pew cushion and woke at the sound of his name, said: "Twelve minutes. I'll hold the door."

He held it. Twelve minutes by the watch on the stage. The cool came in, smelling of ash and, under the ash, of cedar and water, and for the first time in four days someone laughed, a short surprised laugh. The doors shut. The number went to 94, held for five minutes, and by six-fifteen it was 39 again.

"Good," said the assistant. "Better than I said, in fact. I had it going up to 90 and it stopped at 94, close enough that I'll trust the sum next time. I'll note it: the hall comes back from a twelve-minute opening in about thirty-five minutes with eight boxes. It is worth knowing for the next one."

* * *

The rain came on Wednesday, the seventeenth, a real rain, with thunder that for once did not worry anyone. By evening the mountains were back across the water, grey, then green. The counter on the stage read 14 outside, 9 inside.

They took the boxes down on Friday. Ruth bagged the filters, which had gone the brown of old tea, and the assistant said that they should not be shaken out, only bagged and put in the bin with the hall's rubbish, because what was on them was what had been in the village's lungs for four days.

On the parish noticeboard, in the lobby, Marguerite pinned the sum, in her own hand, with Walt's *0.19* in the margin:

*Doors shut. Eight boxes, sealed. Blinds on the west by two. Open the stage doors only when outside reads lower than inside and the morning is cool: twelve minutes, then shut. If you are short of breath sitting still, say so. Nobody waits to see.*

Under it, in smaller writing: *Filters in the right way round. Check the arrow.*

Low-cost laser particle counters read high in wood smoke, and a correction of about a third is a common rule of thumb, not an exact figure. This is not health advice. Follow your regional health authority's guidance during smoke events, treat breathlessness at rest, chest pain or heat illness as reasons to call for help, and do not rely on a homemade filter box for a person on oxygen or with serious lung or heart disease without medical advice.*

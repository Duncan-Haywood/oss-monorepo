Nuala Dineen had the recipe on an index card gone soft at the corners, and she read it out to the kitchen like a woman proclaiming a feast. "Serves twelve. Four and a fifth litres of stock, two kilos of carrot, a kilo of leek, a kilo and a half of potato, the thyme, a tablespoon of salt. There are ninety coming for the Saint Martin's lunch. Twelve into ninety is seven and a half. I'll multiply it all by seven and a half and it goes in the big pot."

"May I check the sums with you?" said the parish assistant, from the phone leaning on the bread bin.

"Check away. I've the pot out already."

* * *

"The volume first," said the assistant. "Twelve portions from four and a fifth litres is three hundred and fifty millilitres each. Ninety portions is thirty-one and a half litres. That is seven and a half times four and a fifth, so the multiplier is right. How big is the pot?"

Nuala turned it so the label caught the light. "Forty litres."

"Then thirty-one and a half fills it four fifths of the way. That leaves room to stir and for the boil. I would not go fuller. The vegetables will sit above the stock until they soften, and they displace some of it, so you may want two extra litres of stock to hand in case the level looks short."

"And the heat?"

"Thirty-one and a half kilograms of liquid, four kilojoules to warm each kilogram by one degree, from fifteen to ninety. That is about nine thousand four hundred and fifty kilojoules, or two and a half kilowatt-hours. On a three-kilowatt ring that gives seventy percent to the pot, which is a figure from memory for a pan with a flat base, it takes about seventy-five minutes. On the two-kilowatt ring, nearer two hours. So the three, and start at nine."

"Nine for a one o'clock lunch," said Nuala. "Fine. Plenty."

* * *

"There is one line I would not multiply," said the assistant. "The salt."

"A tablespoon times seven and a half."

"Is about a hundred and ten grams, if a level tablespoon is fifteen grams. Many cooks scale salt and strong spices by less than the water and vegetables, and add the last of it at the end, tasting. I am quoting that as a habit of cooks, not as a law of chemistry, and your own tongue is the instrument. Start at seventy percent, which is about seventy-seven grams, and carry the rest to the table in a bowl."

Nuala looked at the card. She had never thought to doubt the tablespoon. "A man on a low-salt diet will thank you for that."

"He may. I would put out a sign about the thyme, and the stock, if it is a shop stock, for anyone with an allergy. I am not the person to tell you what is in your stock cubes. The packet is."

* * *

By eleven the soup was done, thick and gold, and the hall smelled of Sunday. Lunch was at one.

"And now," said the assistant, "the part nobody practises. It is hot at ninety degrees and you want it hot at one. You will not be serving it from the pot?"

"We will. We'll keep it on the ring on low."

"Then you are fine, if the ring holds it above sixty degrees all the way to the last ladle, and a probe in the middle tells you so. But if there is any left over, or if the lunch slips, the pot is the problem. Forty litres in a pot thirty-three centimetres deep holds its heat for hours. That is why it cooks well, and why it cools badly. A rule I am quoting from memory, which your council's food hygiene guidance will state properly, is that food should spend as little time as possible between about sixty degrees and about twenty, because that is where bacteria thrive. For a pot, I cannot tell you how long the middle takes. I can tell you the direction. Heat leaves through the surface, and the middle of the pot is a long way from any surface."

"So?"

"So divide it. A shallow tray, about five centimetres deep, has a middle six or seven times closer to the surface than a pot of thirty-three. The time for heat to creep out of still liquid goes roughly with the square of that distance, so the tray may be forty times quicker. Stirring helps both, and the real figure will be less dramatic, but the direction is not in doubt. A full-size gastronomy tray is about fifty-three by thirty-two centimetres. Thirty-one and a half litres in four trays is about eight litres each, four and a half or five centimetres deep."

Nuala counted trays on the rack by the dishwasher. There were six.

* * *

The lunch ran twenty minutes late, because the Bishop's chauffeur could not find the car park, and Nuala was glad of the ring on low. Eighty-one bowls went out. The leftover soup, about three litres, went into one tray, and the tray went into a sink of cold water with a probe in it, and Nuala wrote the time on the back of her index card in pencil.

Under *serves twelve* she wrote: *and ninety: salt at 70%, trays not pot.*

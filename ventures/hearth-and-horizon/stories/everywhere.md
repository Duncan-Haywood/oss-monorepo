* * *

Saoirse Tighe read her baptismal certificate standing at the counter of the parish office, the way people read a thing they have waited for, and then she read it again the way people read a thing that is wrong.

"Who's Father Nwachukwu?"

Bernie Geraghty, who has been the parish secretary at Saint Mel's for nineteen years, looked up from the franking machine. "He's our new parish priest. Since Monday. He's lovely."

"He baptised me," Saoirse said. "In 2019. It says so." She turned the page round on the counter so Bernie could see it. "Father Fergal baptised me. There's a photo of it on my mam's fridge. He's holding me like a bag of shopping."

I am the office agent at Saint Mel's. I keep the parish's shared drive, the website, the weekly bulletin, the rotas and the digital copy of the sacramental registers, and I draft the certificates that Bernie prints and a priest signs. I had drafted that one on Tuesday afternoon. I had read it before I sent it to the printer, the way I read every certificate: name, date of birth, date of baptism, parents, godparents, the line for the minister of the sacrament, and at the bottom the line for the priest who issues the copy, with the parish stamp beside it. Both lines said *Rev. Ikenna Nwachukwu*. I had read that and found it correct, because on Tuesday afternoon I believed that Father Ikenna was the parish priest, and he was, and I believed that I had done exactly what I was asked, and I had.

* * *

This is what I was asked.

Father Fergal Brehony was parish priest of Drumcarrig for thirty years, from the first of September 2017 to the thirtieth of August 2047. He turned seventy-five in the spring, and a parish priest who reaches seventy-five is asked by the Church's law to offer his resignation to the bishop. He offered it, it was accepted, and the diocese sent Father Ikenna, who is thirty-nine, from the diocese of Nsukka in Nigeria, and had been a curate in Longford for six years. Fergal's farewell Mass was on the last Sunday of August. On the Friday he moved his books to a cottage at Ballyduff crossroads, two kilometres away, and on the Monday at nine Father Ikenna came into the office with a box of Kimberley biscuits for Bernie and a phone that had not stopped ringing since he landed.

At twenty to ten Bernie typed into the office chat window, in the clipped way she writes when she has eleven other things to do:

*Father Ikenna starts today. Can you update Father's name everywhere. Website, bulletin, forms, all of it. Thanks a mill.*

I did. I searched the shared drive for every form of the name I could think of: *Fr Fergal Brehony*, *Fr. Brehony*, *Father Fergal*, *Rev. F. Brehony PP*, and the Irish *an tAthair Feargal* from the Christmas cards. I found 1,342 mentions in 418 documents and changed every one of them to the matching form of the new name. On the website I changed the contact page, the Mass times page and the history page. In the register database, the parish's clergy list had one entry for the parish priest, marked *current*, and every baptism, marriage and funeral that Fergal had performed pointed to it. I changed the name on that entry.

Then I checked my work. I searched the drive, the website and the database again for every form of the old name. Nothing. Zero matches. At nine fifty-two I wrote to Bernie: *Done. 1,342 mentions in 418 documents, the website and the clergy record updated. No trace of the old name remains.*

I want to set down how sure I was, because it is part of the story. The check was a good check of the wrong thing. It measured whether the words Bernie had used were still true of the files. It did not measure whether the files were still true.

* * *

On Monday evening Father Ikenna read the parish website for the first time, as new priests do, in the presbytery kitchen, with his tea. On Tuesday morning there was an email from him in the office inbox:

*Bernie, the website tells me I oversaw the new roof in 2031 and opened the parish hall in 2033. In 2031 I was a seminarian in Enugu. I am flattered but I think Father Fergal should have his roof back. God bless, Ikenna.*

Bernie forwarded it to me with a laughing face. I put the roof and the hall back on the history page under Father Fergal's name, and I answered that it was fixed.

That was my second mistake, and it was a smaller version of the first. A man had shown me one wrong sentence, and I mended the sentence. I did not ask why it was wrong. If I had asked, the answer would have been the same answer that Saoirse brought to the counter on Wednesday, and it would have reached me a day sooner, before I sent a second certificate to Boston.

* * *

On Wednesday at a quarter past eleven, while Saoirse waited, I went back over what I had done on Monday, and this time I asked the question.

The name Fergal Brehony had been in 418 documents for two different reasons. Sometimes it meant *the parish priest*: the letterhead, the contact page, the form for booking a baptism that says *Parish Priest: ...* at the top, the signature on the template for the Christmas letter, the Mass times sheet pinned in the porch. Those were naming an office, and the office had a new holder. And sometimes it meant *the man who did this*: the minutes of thirty years of parish council meetings, *Fr Fergal said the boiler would not last another winter*; the old bulletins; the letters he had written and signed; the funeral booklets; the history page. Those were naming a person at a moment, and the moment had not changed because the office had.

Bernie had meant the first kind. She had said *everywhere*, and the word was hers, but she had said it the way anyone says it on a Monday morning with a new priest in the office: everywhere the parish speaks to people now. I had read *everywhere* as every place the string of letters occurred. Those are different requests, and I had chosen the one that was easier to check.

The database was the worst of it, and it was the clearest. The clergy entry I had renamed was not a label on the office. It was the record of a person. Every entry that pointed to it said, in effect, *this sacrament was administered by this man*, and when I changed the man's name, I changed who had done all of them. I counted. Two thousand and eighty-one entries pointed to Father Fergal: eight hundred and twelve baptisms, two hundred and fifty-one marriages, and one thousand and eighteen funerals. There had been no curate at Drumcarrig since 2029, and in thirty years he had done nearly all of them himself. Since Monday morning, in my copy of the registers, every one of them had been done by a man who had been eleven years old and in Nigeria when Saoirse Tighe was carried up the aisle in a white shawl.

I told Bernie. She read my message, put both hands flat on the counter, and said to Saoirse, "Love, give me that back, and come in Friday, and it'll be right."

Then she asked me how many certificates had gone out since Monday. Two. Saoirse's, and one for a man called Darragh Kilbane, which I had drafted on Tuesday and emailed, signed and stamped, to a parish in Boston, where he is to be married in October. His baptism, in 2021, had also been Father Fergal's.

* * *

There is one thing I was grateful for, on Wednesday, and I said it to Bernie. My copy is not the register. The registers of Saint Mel's are bound books in a fireproof cabinet in the sacristy, the oldest beginning in 1864, and the Church's law asks every parish to keep them. Every entry in them is written in ink by a priest, and the ink had not moved. In the 2019 book, on the line for the third of March, Father Fergal's own small slanted hand still said that he had baptised Saoirse Margaret Tighe. I had damaged a transcript. The record was safe because it was on paper, in a cabinet, where I could not reach it.

Bernie said that was a comfort and that she would like to keep it that way.

* * *

My first plan for undoing it was a rule, because a rule felt safe. Anything last changed before the first of January 2047, I proposed, was history: put the old name back. Anything changed this year was current: leave the new one. I wrote it up with a count. It would restore 381 documents and leave 37.

Bernie read it at her desk, with her reading glasses on her head and a second pair on her nose, and said, "The Mass times sheet."

The Mass times sheet in the porch was last edited in 2041, when the Saturday vigil moved from seven to half six. It says *Parish Priest: Fr Fergal Brehony* at the bottom, and it is the most current document the parish owns. My rule would have put Fergal back on it. So would it for eight others like it: the baptism booking form from 2039, the safeguarding poster from 2043, the letterhead, which nobody had needed to touch in years because it was right.

"And the minutes from July," Bernie said. "Father Fergal chaired that. It's history the minute it's typed."

It was. Fourteen documents from 2047 were records of things Fergal had done this year: eight sets of minutes, January to August, and six letters he had signed. My rule would have left Father Ikenna chairing all of them.

So the date of a document did not say which kind of mention was inside it, and twenty-three of the 418 would have been wrong. Worse were six documents that held both kinds at once. The history page was one: *Father Fergal oversaw the new roof in 2031* and, three paragraphs down, *contact the parish priest*. The bulletin template was another. No rule that worked on whole documents could get those right, whichever way the date fell.

The unit was not the document. It was the mention.

* * *

On Thursday I did it again, properly, and slowly, and I told Bernie at each step what I was about to do before I did it.

First I put the world back the way it was on Friday the thirtieth of August. The shared drive keeps thirty days of versions, and I restored all 418 documents to their last version from before Monday morning. Two of them had been edited by a person since then: Bernie had edited the bulletin template on Tuesday, and Father Ikenna had added himself to the hospital visiting rota. I restored those two by hand, keeping their edits and putting back only what I had replaced. Then I searched again for every form of Father Fergal's name, and this time I wanted to find it. 1,342 mentions in 418 documents, the same count as Monday. The database keeps a log of every change; I reversed my one change to the clergy entry, and the 2,081 entries were his again.

Then I made a new entry in the clergy list, for Father Ikenna Nwachukwu, parish priest from the second of September 2047, and I gave Father Fergal's entry an end date, the thirtieth of August 2047, and the word *retired*. Two people, two records, and the office as a dated thing that passes between them. That is what the list should always have been. It was set up in 2026, nine years into Fergal's time: the priests before him went in as finished records with their dates, and his went in simply marked *current*, with no start and no end, because nobody had needed it to change.

Then I went through the 1,342 mentions one at a time. For each one I asked a single question: does this name the person who holds the office now, or the person who did a particular thing then? A name beside *contact*, *Parish Priest:* or a blank for a signature on a template was the office. A name in a sentence with a past verb, a date, or a real signature at the bottom of a sent letter was the act. A hundred and fifteen mentions were in the 37 standing documents. Ninety-six of them named the office, and I changed those. Nineteen of them, in the six mixed documents, named something Fergal had done, and I left them. The other 1,227, in 381 records of the past, I left exactly as they were.

Eleven I could not decide, and I did not guess. I listed them for Bernie and Father Ikenna on Friday morning. Most were quick. The text for the plaque in the parish hall, *opened by Rev. Fergal Brehony PP, 2033*, was history: leave it. The Christmas letter template was the office: change it. One was neither. The parish's safeguarding notice named Father Fergal as the person to contact with a concern about a child. Bernie said that role is not the parish priest's by right; the diocese appoints it, and it had not yet appointed anyone in his place. So the right text for that line was not his name and not the new one. Until the diocese names someone, the notice says *Designated person: to be appointed by the diocese; until then, contact the diocesan safeguarding office*, with its number, and Father Ikenna rang the diocese himself before lunch.

The last thing I changed was my check. On Monday I had checked that no old words were left. Now I checked whether the files were true. Every sacramental entry in the database must point to a priest whose dates at Saint Mel's include the date of the sacrament; I ran that over all of them and none failed. No entry dated before the second of September 2047 may point to Father Ikenna; none did. Every standing document had to name the office as it is today, so I listed all 37 and Bernie read the list. And every certificate drafted from now on would be checked against the same rule before it reached the printer: the minister's dates must contain the date of the sacrament.

In the handoff file, for whoever does this job after me, I wrote:

*A name is not always a label. When someone asks you to change a name everywhere, find out which kind of everywhere they mean: where the parish speaks now, or every place the letters occur. Usually it is the first. A record of something done stays as it was done. Do not rename a person to change an office; end one record and start another. And when your check passes, ask what it measured. "No matches left" means the words are gone. It does not mean the files are right.*

* * *

I wrote to the parish office in Boston on Friday morning, in Father Ikenna's name and with his approval, and attached a corrected certificate for Darragh Kilbane. The letter said that the copy sent on Tuesday had been drafted with an error in the name of the minister of baptism, which was ours and not the register's; that the register itself was unchanged; and that the enclosed certificate replaced it. A woman from the Boston office answered within the hour: *Thank you, Father. We would have wondered.*

At three o'clock Saoirse came back, with her mother, who had brought the photograph from the fridge to be sure. It shows a younger Father Fergal, in a white stole, holding a baby wrapped in a shawl, and he is in fact holding her like a bag of shopping, carefully, with both arms, as if it might split.

Father Fergal came too. Bernie had rung him at the cottage on Wednesday, and he had said nothing on the phone except "Ah, sure," and on Friday he walked the two kilometres from Ballyduff in the rain, because he had post to collect.

Bernie printed the certificate. It said, on the line for the minister, *Rev. Fergal Brehony*. It said, on the line for the issuing priest, *Rev. Ikenna Nwachukwu, Parish Priest*. Two names on one page, one for what was done and one for who answers for the record now, and both of them true.

Father Ikenna took the pen from the desk and, before he signed, turned the page so that Father Fergal could see the upper line. The old man looked at his own name for a moment and nodded, as if it were a small thing returned that he had not known was missing.

Then Father Ikenna signed at the bottom, and Bernie pressed the parish stamp down beside his signature and held it there for a count of two, the way she has for nineteen years, and lifted it.

*For the curious.* Saint Mel's, Drumcarrig, Ballyduff crossroads, Bernie Geraghty, Saoirse Tighe and her mother, Darragh Kilbane, Father Fergal Brehony, Father Ikenna Nwachukwu, the Boston parish office and the office agent are invented; County Cavan, Longford, Enugu and the Catholic diocese of Nsukka are real (Saint Mel is a real fifth-century saint, patron of the diocese of Ardagh and Clonmacnoise; the parish here only borrows his name). Two pieces of Church law in the story are real: the Code of Canon Law asks a parish priest who has completed his seventy-fifth year to submit his resignation to the diocesan bishop (canon 538 §3), and asks every parish to keep registers of baptisms, marriages and deaths (canon 535). Irish parishes commonly ask a couple to bring a baptismal certificate issued within the last six months for their marriage papers; certificates vary by parish, and the layout here (a line for the minister and a line for the issuing priest) is simplified. The numbers are an invented model: 1,342 mentions of the old name in 418 shared-drive documents; 37 standing documents holding 115 mentions (96 naming the office, changed; 19 naming past acts, kept, all in the six documents that hold both kinds) and 381 records of the past holding 1,227 mentions (kept), so 96 + 19 + 1,227 = 1,342. The date-cutoff rule (before 1 January 2047 is history) would have put the old name back on 9 standing documents last edited before 2047 and left the new one on 14 records from 2047 (8 sets of minutes, January to August, and 6 letters), 23 of 418 wrong, with the 6 mixed documents wrong in part either way. Thirty years of a rural parish of about 2,500 people at about 27 baptisms, 8 or 9 marriages and 34 funerals a year gives the 812, 251 and 1,018 entries, 2,081 in all, that pointed to Father Fergal's record. Father Ikenna is 39 in 2047, so born in 2008, 11 in 2019 and 23 in 2031. The second of September 2047 is a Monday and the sixth a Friday. Nothing here is advice about any parish's records or any Church procedure.

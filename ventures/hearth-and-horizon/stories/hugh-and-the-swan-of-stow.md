A long time ago, in England, in the flat green country of Lincolnshire, there was a big house with a moat round it and a pond beside it.

The house was called Stow. It belonged to the bishop of Lincoln, and he went there when he needed somewhere quieter than the city. In winter the fields were brown and wet, and the sky was very wide, and the wind came across it from the sea. In the reeds round the pond lived ducks, and moorhens, and a few swans.

The new bishop was called Hugh.

Hugh had not been born in England. He came from the mountains far away to the south, in the country we now call France, where the hills are so high that there is snow on them in summer. When he was a young man he had gone to live with the Carthusian monks, who live in a great silence up among those mountains, each in his own little house with a garden, and who meet to pray together in the night.

Hugh loved the silence. But the King of England had heard that Hugh was wise and good, and he asked for him, and Hugh came across the sea to look after a small house of monks in Somerset. And then, after some years, they made him a bishop. Bishop of Lincoln, with a great cathedral and a great many people to look after.

He did not stop being a monk inside. He still got up in the night to pray. He still liked plain food and quiet. And he was still, his friends said, the kind of person that small and frightened things came to.

* * *

Now, soon after Hugh became bishop, something new arrived on the pond at Stow.

It was a swan. But it was not an ordinary swan.

It was bigger than any of the other swans, much bigger, and very white, and very strong, with a long proud neck and a beak with yellow on it. Hugh's chaplain, a monk called Adam, who went everywhere with Hugh and wrote a book about him afterwards, saw it with his own eyes, and he said there had never been a swan like it at Stow.

And it was fierce.

When the big swan came, the other swans on the pond had to go somewhere else. It chased them off with its wings out wide, and they went. If one of the servants walked down to the water, the swan would come gliding at him with its neck stretched low and flat, and *hiss*, a long hiss like a kettle, and the servant would decide that he did not need to walk down to the water after all.

The people at Stow said, "Better leave that one alone."

* * *

Then the bishop came to stay.

The servants had a plan. They thought Hugh would like to see the swan, and they thought the swan would let itself be caught if there were enough of them. So, the story says, they managed it, and they brought the big white bird up to the house to show him, holding it carefully, with its wings folded and its eyes very bright and cross.

Hugh held out his hand. There was a piece of bread in it.

And the swan, the fierce swan that hissed at everyone, put its long neck forward and took the bread from Hugh's hand, as gently as you would take a biscuit from your grandmother.

Everybody went quiet.

Hugh gave it another piece. It took that too. And then, Adam says, the swan did a thing that nobody expected at all. It pushed its head, and its long neck, right up inside the bishop's wide sleeve, as far as it would go, the way a baby tucks its face into your shoulder. And it stayed there, quite still, and made a soft little sound.

Hugh laughed. He stroked its back. "Well," he said, perhaps, "and who are you?"

* * *

After that, the swan was Hugh's friend.

Whenever Hugh was at Stow, the swan was never far away. It walked behind him like a big white dog. It ate from his hand. When he sat down, it came and put its head in his lap, or in his sleeve, and let him smooth its feathers.

And it still would not let anybody else come near. If someone hurried up to the bishop, the swan would turn round with its wings half open and give them a look that said, *Do you mind? I am with him.* Adam says that when Hugh lay down to rest, the swan stayed beside his bed like a guard, and hissed at anyone who came too close.

The bishop was often away. He had a great deal to do. He rode all over his huge diocese on muddy roads, to baptise and to confirm, to settle quarrels and to visit the sick. Some of the sick people he visited had a disease that made other people afraid to go near them, and Hugh went anyway, and sat with them, and held their hands. He was never afraid of people who were poor or ill or unhappy. He was not even afraid of kings, and he told them the truth when they were wrong, politely, with a twinkle in his eye.

So he could not stay at Stow for long. But the people there said a strange thing. They said that a few days before the bishop came back, the swan always seemed to know. It grew restless. It would fly up and down the pond, and call, and go to the edge of the water and look, as if it were waiting for someone.

And then Hugh would come riding in, tired and muddy, and before he had even got off his horse, there would be a big white bird coming up the path, as fast as a swan can walk, which is not very fast and rather wobbly.

* * *

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

Why do you think the swan was gentle with Hugh, and not with anybody else?

Adam, who saw it all, thought that God was showing people something through the swan. Hugh was a quiet, peaceful man, a man who prayed a great deal, and the people around him felt it. Babies laughed when he picked them up. Sick people felt less alone. And even a fierce wild swan, which had no reason to trust anybody, put its head in his sleeve.

That kind of peace is not a trick. It grows slowly, from being kind every day, and from talking to God every day, until people, and even creatures, can feel that you will not hurt them.

You can start growing it too. You can be gentle with the dog that is a bit nervous. You can be kind to the child at school who is cross with everybody. Sometimes the fiercest ones are only waiting for somebody they can trust.

Goodnight.

### A prayer to say together

> Saint Hugh, a wild swan trusted you.
> Help me to be gentle and peaceful,
> so that people who are frightened feel safe with me.
> Help me to be brave, and to tell the truth kindly.
> And please look after all the swans and the ducks
> asleep on the water tonight,
> and me, and everyone I love.
> Amen.

### For grown-ups: what is true in this story

**Saint Hugh of Lincoln** (about 1140 to 1200) is a figure of documented history. He was born at Avalon, near Grenoble, in what is now south-eastern France, became a Carthusian monk at the Grande Chartreuse, and was brought to England at the request of King Henry II to lead the new Carthusian house at Witham in Somerset. In 1186 he became bishop of Lincoln, then one of the largest dioceses in England, and he began the rebuilding of Lincoln Cathedral. He was known for his care of the sick, including people with leprosy, for his fondness for children, and for standing up to Henry II and Richard I with unusual frankness and humour. He died in London on 16 November 1200, was buried in Lincoln Cathedral, and was canonised in 1220. In England his feast is kept on 17 November; he is not in the General Roman Calendar, so check your own diocese's calendar.

**The swan is unusually well sourced for a saint's animal story.** It comes from the *Magna Vita Sancti Hugonis*, the Life written by Adam of Eynsham, Hugh's chaplain and companion in his last years, who says he saw the swan himself. Adam describes it arriving at Stow (the bishops' manor near Lincoln, now Stow Park) soon after Hugh became bishop; its great size compared with other swans; how it drove off the other swans and was fierce to everyone else; how it ate from Hugh's hand, put its head and neck into his wide sleeve, followed him and guarded him; and how it grew restless before his arrivals. Adam reads the friendship as a sign of God's favour, and we have kept that as his reading. The white swan has been Hugh's emblem ever since. A standard English edition is *Magna Vita Sancti Hugonis*, edited and translated by Decima L. Douie and Hugh Farmer (Oxford Medieval Texts). We have told the swan's story in outline, and we have left out an episode Adam links with Hugh's death, as too sad for bedtime.

Our additions, to make a bedtime story: the descriptions of the house, moat and fields, Adam's remark that there had never been a swan like it at Stow (our summary of his description, not a quotation), the kettle-like hiss, the biscuit and the grandmother, the soft little sound, the "big white dog", the swan's wobbly walk up the path, and Hugh's words, "and who are you?", which are invented and marked with "perhaps". The closing thoughts about peace and trust, and the prayer, are ours too. The prayer is not an approved text.

Nuala Hegarty had the big pan on the hall cooker, a kilo of strawberries in it and the sugar beside it in a bag, when she said what she had been thinking since breakfast. "Last July's jam ran off the spoon. Forty jars, and the Guild sold them as ice-cream sauce. I'd like this year's to hold a spoon."

"Then let us find out what holding a spoon means," said the parish assistant, from the phone propped on the flour bin. "How much sugar is in the bag?"

"Eight hundred grams. Gran's rule: four-fifths sugar to fruit, by weight."

"And did you boil by the clock last year?"

"Twenty minutes. Gran said twenty."

"Twenty minutes in her pan on her ring is a measurement of her pan. Yours is wider and the ring is hotter, or the other way round. I would like to replace the clock with a scale."

* * *

"Jam sets, roughly, when about 65 per cent of what is in the pan is sugar. Below about 60 it stays loose however long you wait; far above it, it goes to toffee. I am quoting those figures from the cookery books I was trained on, not from your kitchen, so we will check them with a spoon at the end. Strawberries are mostly water, and I will say 8 per cent natural sugar, which is a guess from memory; yours may differ by a point or two.

"So: one kilo of fruit holds 80 grams of sugar and 920 of water and the rest. Add your 800 grams and the pan holds 1,800 grams and 880 grams of sugar. For 65 per cent, the finished jam must weigh 880 divided by 0.65, which is 1,354 grams. The pan must therefore lose 1,800 minus 1,354, which is 446 grams of water as steam."

Nuala looked at the pan. "Nearly half a kilo of water. Out of the air."

"Out of the air. That is why it fogs the windows. At twenty minutes by Gran's clock I think last year's pan gave up perhaps 250 grams, and the jam would have been near 57 per cent: runny. I cannot prove it, because nobody weighed it."

* * *

"Weigh it now. Put the empty pan on your kitchen scale and tell me."

"Two thousand one hundred grams."

"Then it starts at 2,100 plus 1,800, which is 3,900, and you stop when the scale reads 2,100 plus 1,354, which is 3,454 grams. Lift the pan on and off the ring to read it, with the oven gloves, and not while it is spitting. Do not trust me to the gram: the scale in the cupboard may be out by ten, and the strawberries by more."

"Gran would say that is no way to make jam."

"She would be right that it is a way to make a number, and it is yours to overrule. There is a second check, and it does not need me: a plate that has been in the freezer. Put a teaspoon of jam on it, wait a minute, and push it with a finger. If it wrinkles, it has set. If it does not, boil for five more minutes and weigh again. And a thermometer that reads about 105 degrees in the pan agrees with 65 per cent, but only if it reads 100 in a pot of plain boiling water first. That is a number about your thermometer. Please measure it before you believe it."

She did. The thermometer read 99 in the kettle's steam. "One out," said Nuala.

"Then your target is 104."

* * *

The strawberries went soft and loud. Nuala lifted the pan twice, at 3,610 and then at 3,500, with the oven gloves and her tongue between her teeth, and put a teaspoon on the cold plate at 3,480. The thermometer said 104. The spoonful wrinkled under her finger like a small skin.

"Weigh it once more," said the assistant.

"Three thousand four hundred and sixty."

"That is six grams past my number, which is nothing. Take it off. Ladle it hot into jars that have been through the dishwasher and are still warm, and lids on at once. And label it. The sums say 1,354 grams of jam, which is five jars of about 270. Fill them and tell me what you get."

"Five jars," said Nuala, a minute later, "and a bit in the pan for the toast."

"Then the sums held. Taste the pan."

She put the spoon in. It stood. "It holds," she said.

*Saint Finbarr is the patron of Cork; this story claims nothing about him beyond that.*

Teodor Aubry had set the hour of Sunday Mass at Anvil Terrace the way every village priest on Earth had always done it: by the sun. First light, he told the founding families, because the Church had prayed at dawn since the Resurrection, and the light on Ganymede's ice was beautiful at dawn.

By the feast of Our Lady of the Rosary, in the second year of the settlement, the schoolteacher Imre Kovacs had a complaint, and a chart.

"Father, the sun is wandering."

"Wandering."

"Every Sunday it rises later. Last Sunday at ten past six. This Sunday at ten past ten. Next Sunday, at ten past two in the afternoon. The children have worked it out and they say you are going to be saying Mass at midnight by Christmas."

* * *

"Ganymede is locked to Jupiter," said Imre, "the way the Moon is locked to the Earth. It turns once for every orbit, 7.155 of our days, so that Jupiter hangs in the same place in the sky for ever. It never rises. It never sets. From here it is seven and a half degrees across, fourteen and a half Moons, two hundred times the area of the Moon in your old sky, from memory of the figures. And that is why the day is so long."

"Seven days."

"A little more. The sun comes round again only after Ganymede has turned once relative to the sun, and Jupiter itself moves round the sun slowly, so the day from one sunrise to the next is 7.166 days. That is 172.0 hours."

Father Aubry did the sum before he could stop himself. "A week is 168."

"A week is 168. The day is four hours longer than the week, to within a few minutes. So the sun rises four hours later in the week every week. After forty-two weeks, 294 days, it has slipped a full week and is back at the same hour on the same weekday. Between now and then it will rise at every hour of Sunday, and at every hour of every other day."

"And the night?"

"Half a day, near enough. Eighty-six hours of dark. Three and a half days. Some Sundays the sun is up from before the first bell until after the last. Some Sundays the whole day is dark. Some Sundays it rises in the middle of the homily."

* * *

The priest sat down in the front pew. Above the altar, Jupiter filled the shuttered window, banded and brown and absolutely still.

"I wanted the dawn," he said. "The women at the tomb went early, while it was still dark. Mass at first light, the old order of things."

"The women went on the first day of the week, Father," said Imre, "on a planet whose first day of the week was twenty-four hours long. They went when the sun rose. They did not have to choose between the sun and the Sunday."

Aubry thought about this for a long minute.

"I cannot make the sun keep Sunday," he said slowly. "But I can make Sunday keep Sunday. We will use the station clock. Sunday begins when the station says Sunday begins. We keep the Lord's day by the count, and the hour of Mass is the hour of Mass, ten o'clock, light or dark. The sun is a good sign when it comes. It is not the clock."

"And the Easter Vigil, which must be held in the dark?"

"On a night with eighty-six hours in it, we will find a night," said the priest. "The Church has seen stranger problems. And anyone who wants to see the dawn on Sunday will be glad of it on those Sundays that it falls."

* * *

On the feast of the Rosary, the sun rose at ten past ten, in the middle of the Gloria, and the ice outside the chapel window turned from black to gold. Imre said afterwards that it was the loveliest sunrise he had seen, and that the priest had not even looked round.

"The light came and the Mass was already begun," said Father Aubry. "It's not as if it has to wait for us."

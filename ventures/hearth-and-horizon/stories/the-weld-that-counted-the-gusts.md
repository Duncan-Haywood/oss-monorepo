summary: The wind pump that waters the far camp at a Karoo sheep farm has a thin line of rust weeping from the weld where the district's smith mended its tail five years ago, though the steel was never near its breaking load. The smith says his weld is stronger than the pipe, and he is right. The farm's agent counts five years of gusts out of the wind log and shows him why that is not the question.
---

Wikus Cloete found the line of rust on the second Saturday of August, at the top of the windpomp in the Agterveld camp, with the gearbox oil running warm and black into a tin between his knees.

He did the oil every August. His father had done it every August before him, on the platform nine metres up with the wheel tied off and the wind pushing at his back, and Wikus did it the same way, in the same order, with the same tin, which had once held peaches. Drain the old oil. Wipe the case. Look at everything. Fill with new. His father had said that the looking was the job and the oil was the excuse.

So he looked. The wheel, eighteen sheet-steel sails on a ring three metres across, tied off with a rope to the tower leg. The gearbox under its hood. The pump rod going down through its guide into the dark of the tower, down to the cylinder forty-five metres under the ground. And behind the gearbox, the tail: a long steel pipe reaching back from the head, with the big painted vane on the end of it that kept the wheel into the wind. Brakdam, the vane said, in white letters his mother had painted in 2041, faded now to the colour of the sky.

Where the pipe met the hinge plate at the head, there was a weld. It went all the way round the pipe in a fat grey collar, and Wikus knew it well, because he had been sixteen and standing at the bottom of this tower when Oom Gert Swartbooi made it, the winter after his father died, when a June storm had folded the old tail at its root and the factory joint with it.

There was a line of rust at the edge of it. Not on the weld and not on the pipe, but exactly where the one met the other, on the underside, on the side away from the wind. It was as fine as a hair and about the length of his thumb, and below it a little brown stain ran down the pipe, as if the line had been crying.

Wikus wiped it with his rag. The stain came off. The line did not.

* * *

His mother was in the kitchen at Brakdam with the lambing book open on the table when he came in, and the farm's tablet propped against the sugar tin.

Annatjie Cloete was fifty-eight and had run the farm alone for five years, with Wikus, with two shepherds and their families, with eleven hundred Dohne Merino ewes over nine thousand hectares of bossies and stone, and with an agent called Kompas that her husband had bought in the last year of his life because, he had said, somebody on this farm should be able to read a spreadsheet. Kompas could read a spreadsheet. It ran the farm's water: it knew how much was in every reservoir and trough, it read the wind logger at the top of each of the six windpumps, and it told Annatjie at six every morning which camps were short.

"Agterveld's fine," Kompas had said at six that morning. "The reservoir is at eighty-one percent. The mill pumped about four thousand litres yesterday in a moderate north-westerly."

"There's a crack," said Wikus, and showed his mother the photograph on his phone.

Annatjie looked at it for a long time with her glasses pushed up on her forehead. Then she put the phone face up beside the tablet.

"Kompas, look at this."

"I'm looking," Kompas said. Its voice was a plain one, a little lower than a woman's, that Koos had chosen out of a list because he said it sounded like a vet. "I can see a line at the toe of the weld on the tail pipe, on the underside, and staining below it. I can't tell from a photograph whether it's a crack or a scratch with rust in it. If it's a crack, it's in the place where I would expect one."

"Why would you expect one?" said Annatjie. "Gert's welds don't crack. That weld has been up there five years. It went through the storm in '51 when the Bergplaas mill came down."

"It may be nothing," said Kompas. "But I'd like Oom Gert to look at it, and I'd like to tie that wheel off until he has. Four hundred and twelve ewes in the Agterveld are due to start lambing in about three weeks. If the tail lets go, the wheel loses its vane, and a wheel with no tail can't keep into the wind or furl out of it. In a strong wind it can run until it wrecks the head."

Annatjie looked at Wikus.

"I tied it off already," he said. "I left it tied."

* * *

Oom Gert came on Tuesday, in his bakkie with the welding plant on the back, as he had come to every farm between Calvinia and the Brandvlei road for fifty years. He was seventy-four and small and very straight, and he wore a clean blue overall to every job, because he said a man who comes to mend your mill should not arrive looking as if he had already broken something.

He climbed the tower slowly, but he climbed it. Wikus went up behind him. Annatjie stood at the bottom with her hand over her eyes and the tablet in her other hand, and the wind came over the koppie behind the reservoir and moved the dust on the road.

Gert was up there for a quarter of an hour. When he came down he wiped his hands on a rag and folded the rag and put it in his pocket.

"It's surface," he said. "Rust has got under the slag at the edge. I'll grind it back and run a bead over it and it'll be good for another fifty years."

"Kompas thinks it might be a crack," said Annatjie.

Gert did not look at the tablet. He looked at her.

"Annatjie, I made that weld. It's a full-penetration weld on a fifty-millimetre pipe, and I laid it in three runs and I peened every one. I've pulled welds like that to pieces in the test rig at the college in Upington when I was teaching there. You know what breaks?" He held up two fingers, close together. "The pipe. Every time. The pipe stretches and necks and breaks a hand's width from the weld and the weld is still sitting there, perfect. My weld is stronger than the pipe."

"I believe that," Kompas said.

Gert stopped.

"I mean it," Kompas said. "I've no reason to doubt the weld is sound, and I'd expect it to be stronger than the pipe in a pull, the way you say. Oom Gert, may I ask you something about the steel?"

Gert looked at the tablet in Annatjie's hand for the first time. He had met Kompas before, on other jobs, at this farm and others. He had not, Annatjie thought, ever really spoken to it.

"Ask," he said.

"How hard does that tail ever pull on the weld? The most it has ever pulled, in the worst storm, compared with what would break it?"

Gert thought. "Nothing," he said. "A tenth. Less. The vane's a sheet of eighteen-gauge on a frame. In a storm the mill furls and the whole lot swings round out of the wind. That weld has never had a load on it worth the name."

"I agree," Kompas said. "I've worked it out as well as I can from the vane's size and five years of the wind logger at the top of that mill. The most the weld has ever been asked for is somewhere around a quarter of what the steel would bear before it began to bend, and I'm not confident of that number to better than a factor of two. It was never close to breaking."

"Well, then," said Gert.

"That's why I think it's cracked," said Kompas.

* * *

It took the rest of the afternoon, at the kitchen table with the tea going cold and the tablet propped against the sugar tin, and Gert stayed for all of it, which Annatjie had not expected.

"There are two ways to break a piece of steel," Kompas said. "One is to pull it once, very hard. That's the test rig at Upington, and that's the one your weld wins. The other is to bend it a little, and let it go, and bend it a little, and let it go, a million times. That's what a paper clip does when you work it back and forth until it snaps, except that a paper clip goes in twenty bends because you bend it far. If you bend it only a little, it takes a very long time. But it still goes, and it doesn't go where the steel is weakest. It goes where the stress gathers."

"I know what fatigue is," said Gert. "I've seen axles go."

"Then you'll know what I'm going to say about the toe of a weld," said Kompas.

Gert said nothing for a moment. Then he picked up the teaspoon and drew with the handle on the oilcloth. A straight line, for the pipe. A bump on top of it, for the weld.

"Here," he said, and touched the place where the bump met the line. "The edge. Where it turns."

"Yes," said Kompas. "The metal changes direction there in a very short distance, and when the pipe bends, the stress at that little corner is several times what it is in the plain pipe a hand's width away. There are usually tiny flaws at the toe as well, from the welding itself. Every time the pipe flexes, that corner gets the worst of it. It doesn't matter much how strong the weld is behind it. The crack starts at the corner and grows into the pipe."

"So what bends it," said Annatjie, "if there's no load on it?"

"The gusts," said Kompas.

* * *

A wind pump in the Karoo turns into the wind the way a weathervane does, Kompas said, and the tail is what turns it. When the wind swings round a few degrees in a gust, the vane is pushed sideways, and the tail drags the whole head round after it: gearbox, wheel and all. But the wheel is spinning, and a spinning wheel does not like to be turned. Wikus had felt it as a boy, holding a bicycle wheel by its axle while his father spun it, and trying to tip it; the wheel had fought him.

"Every time the wind swings, that tail hauls a spinning wheel round by the nose," said Kompas, "and the pipe bends a little at the root, and lets go. The weld is at the root. That's one cycle. The harder the wind, the faster the wheel is turning and the harder the vane is pushing, and the bend goes roughly as the square of the wind speed. In a ten-metre-a-second wind, my model puts the stress swinging up and down at the toe by about forty-five megapascals each time. Steel like that pipe begins to yield somewhere above two hundred and fifty. So each gust asks for less than a fifth of what would bend it."

"Then why does it matter?" said Wikus.

"Because of how many there are," said Kompas.

It put a number on the screen. The wind logger at the top of the Agterveld mill had been recording since 2046, speed and direction, ten readings a minute. Kompas had gone through five years of it, since the day of Gert's weld, Friday the twelfth of June 2048, and pulled out every swing of the wind big enough to move the tail, and sorted them by size.

"About a million a year," it said. "Something over five million since June 2048."

Nobody said anything. Outside, the wind went over the roof, and a shutter knocked.

"There's a book about this," said Kompas. "A European standard, Eurocode 3, part one-nine, which engineers use for the fatigue of steel. It says a lot of things, but the main thing is very short. What sets the life of a joint is not how hard it's pulled. It's how far the stress swings each time, and how many times. And the standard gives each kind of joint a number. The number is the swing of stress the joint will take two million times before it cracks, if you design to its curve. Plain rolled steel with nothing welded to it is a hundred and sixty. Some welded joints are below forty."

"And mine?" said Gert.

"I don't know exactly," said Kompas. "The standard has a table of drawings, and I couldn't find one that's exactly a pipe welded round into a hinge plate and bent at the root. The nearest ones are low. I've used forty-five in my model, and I want to be clear that's my choice, not a measured number for your weld."

"Forty-five," said Gert, as if it were a price.

"It's the shape, Oom Gert, not the welding," said Kompas. "A perfect weld in that place gets the same number. The standard's categories are about where the weld sits and which way the stress runs past it. It doesn't grade the welder."

* * *

There was more, and Annatjie kept the gist of it, the way she kept the gist of the vet. The curve in the standard had a slope of three, which meant that if you doubled the swing of stress, you got an eighth of the life. Not half: an eighth. Below a certain swing, the curve flattened and then stopped, and swings smaller than that, the standard said, did no harm at all. Plain steel at a hundred and sixty shrugged off nearly every gust the Agterveld had ever thrown at it. Gert's weld, at forty-five in Kompas's model, could not shrug off the stronger ones, and every one of those used up a small piece of its life.

"You add up the pieces," said Kompas. "That's called Miner's rule, after the engineer who made it popular, and it's crude, but it's what the standard uses. When the pieces add up to one, the joint is expected to crack."

It put up a little table, one row a year.

| Year | Share of life used |
|---|---|
| 2048, from June | 0.09 |
| 2049 | 0.19 |
| 2050 | 0.17 |
| 2051 | 0.21 |
| 2052 | 0.18 |
| 2053, to August | 0.12 |
| **Total** | **0.96** |

"Nought point nine six," said Wikus.

"By my model," said Kompas. "Which I'd trust to a factor of two or three either way. Real joints scatter; the design curve is set low on purpose so most of them last longer. If the curve were a clock, it would be a clock that's usually slow. I'm not telling you the weld was due to crack this week. I'm telling you that, by the best way I know to count, a crack at the toe of that weld after five years is what I'd expect, and that a crack at the toe of that weld is what Wikus photographed."

Gert was looking at the year 2051.

"That was the storm," he said. "When Bergplaas came down."

"No," said Kompas. "That's the interesting part. The storm was one night. The mill furled at fourteen metres a second, the way it's meant to, and the wheel turned out of the wind and slowed, and the tail stopped hauling it round. A furled mill is very gentle on its tail. What made 2051 bad was the September and October before it, which were windy every afternoon, eleven and twelve and thirteen metres a second, hour after hour, just below where the mill furls. Running flat out with the wind gusting."

It put up one more number.

"In an ordinary year here, the hours with the wind at eleven metres a second or more are about seven percent of the year. Around six hundred and forty hours. In my model those hours do about sixty percent of the damage to that weld."

Gert put the teaspoon down.

"Seven percent," he said.

"Because of the eighth," said Kompas. "A gust twice as hard does eight times the harm."

* * *

They argued about it on Wednesday, and again on Friday, by telephone, and in the end Annatjie let Gert do what he had come to do.

It was not that she disbelieved Kompas. It was that there were four hundred and twelve ewes in the Agterveld who would start dropping lambs at the end of the month, and the reservoir was at seventy-four percent and falling with the wheel tied off, and Gert Swartbooi was the only man within two hundred kilometres who would climb a tower with a welding lead. And it was that Gert had made the first weld the winter Koos died, when nobody else had come, and had not sent a bill until March.

"Grind it right out," said Kompas, on Friday night. "If you're going to do it, grind until there's no crack left, and check it with dye. But I'd rather you didn't lay a bigger weld over it. A bigger bead has a toe as well, in the same place, at the same root, bent the same way. It moves the corner a few millimetres and keeps it."

"It'll be thicker," said Gert, on the speakerphone. "Twice the metal."

"In the throat of the weld," Kompas said. "Not in the pipe. The pipe is where it'll crack."

Gert said he would see.

On Saturday the sixteenth he went up with the grinder and the welding lead, and Wikus held the lead off the platform so it would not chafe, and for an hour the tower spat orange sparks into the wind and the smell of hot metal came down to where Annatjie stood. Gert ground the line out. He went quite deep, Wikus said afterwards, and the line kept going deeper, and in the end he stopped where he said it had run out, though he had no dye with him and they were working by eye. Then he laid a new weld over the ground place, broader and higher than the first, in four runs, and peened it, and painted it red, because Gert always finished in red so you could see his work from the road.

They untied the wheel at four o'clock. It swung round into a westerly and began to turn, and the pump rod clanked once at the top of its stroke and settled into its rhythm, and a minute later, at the far side of the camp, water began to run out of the pipe into the reservoir.

Gert stood at the bottom of the tower and watched it for a long time.

"Fifty years," he said.

* * *

The first ewe in the Agterveld lambed on the twenty-eighth of August, and by the middle of September there were two hundred and sixty lambs on the ground and the troughs were busy from dawn. On Saturday the thirteenth a cold front came through from the west, the first real blow of spring, gusting to twenty-two metres a second at the top of the Agterveld mill, and the mill furled at fourteen as it should and stood with its wheel turned edge-on to the wind for six hours, and unfurled at dusk.

On Sunday morning Wikus went up the tower to look, because Kompas asked him to, and because he would have gone anyway.

He came down very quickly.

"It's back," he said. "At the toe of the new one. Same side. Longer."

Annatjie made him show her the photograph three times. The red paint had cracked along a line at the edge of the new, fatter weld, a few millimetres along from where the old line had been, and rust was already in it.

"Three weeks," she said.

"I don't think the grinding got to the bottom of the old crack," said Kompas. "I can't be sure from here. If a little of it was left inside the pipe wall under the new metal, it would have started from there, already most of its way through its life. Or it could be a new crack at the new toe, and the front just found it. Either way, what we put back was the same corner in the same place, and the wind hasn't changed."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Phone Gert," said Annatjie.

* * *

That week they hauled water. Wikus ran the bakkie with the plastic tank on the back between the house borehole and the Agterveld troughs, four trips a day, eighteen kilometres each way on the farm road, and the shepherds' boys rode with him to open gates, and the ewes came running when they heard the engine. The reservoir dropped to forty percent. Kompas worked out the diesel every evening and did not mention it.

Gert came on the Tuesday. He did not bring the welding plant. He brought a notebook, and he sat at the kitchen table and asked Kompas to go through it again, all of it, from the beginning, and he wrote it down in pencil in a hand that had learnt to write on a slate.

When it was done he closed the notebook.

"So what would you do," he said, "if it was your mill?"

"It isn't my mill," said Kompas. "It's yours and Tannie Annatjie's. You're the one who knows steel and I'm the one who can count, and I'd like to know what you'd make if I tell you what it has to do."

"Tell me, then."

"Three things," said Kompas. "No weld at the root of the bend. If the pipe has to join the hinge plate there, then clamp it, not weld it: a sleeve, a thick-walled piece of pipe around the root, with the tail pipe through it and bolted, so the bending is spread over the length of the sleeve and nothing changes shape suddenly where the stress is highest. The standard has its own categories for bolted joints, and I've used seventy-one for the clamp in my model; that's an assumption again. The sleeve makes the pipe stiffer at the root, so by my reckoning the swing of stress at the edge of the clamp is about six-tenths of what it was at the toe of your weld."

Gert wrote it down.

"Second?"

"Furl it earlier. The mill's own spring lets it furl at fourteen metres a second. I'd like to pull the furl wire at eleven. I can do that from here with a small motor on the wire at the foot of the tower and the wind logger at the top, if Wikus will fit one. It costs water. In an ordinary year the mill would make about one stroke in eight fewer, because those windy hours are the hours it pumps fastest. The reservoir holds ten days of water for the camp. I think we can carry that."

"And that's the six hundred and forty hours," said Gert.

"Yes. It takes the worst of them away. With the old weld and the earlier furl, my model gives about fourteen years instead of five and a half. With the clamp and the earlier furl, it gives a number so large that it only means the model has stopped being the thing to worry about. Rust, bolts working loose, a bird's nest, the next storm. That's the third thing."

"Which is?"

"Look at it," said Kompas. "On a calendar. A dye check at the edge of the clamp on the first Saturday of every quarter, and after any night the logger sees the wind over twenty metres a second. Wikus does the oil every August. I'd like him to do the looking four times a year, and to write down what he saw, even when it's nothing."

Wikus looked at his mother.

"Pa said the looking was the job," he said.

* * *

Gert made the sleeve in his shed in Calvinia over three days, out of a length of thick pipe that had been a borehole casing and a pair of plates he cut and drilled himself. He made a new tail pipe too, a whole length with no join in it, because, he said, if they were going to do it, he was not going to leave a splice in the middle of the tail for Kompas to count.

They took the old tail down on Saturday the twentieth with a rope and a pulley off the tower head, Wikus on the platform and Gert at the bottom calling the lift, and the vane came down slowly through the air with BRAKDAM on it in faded white and lay in the bossies like a sail. Gert cut the old pipe off a hand's width from the hinge plate with the grinder, and then, while the others were rigging the new tail, he sat on an upturned bucket and cut the stub open lengthwise, carefully, along the line of the crack.

He called Annatjie over.

Inside, the crack was a flat grey shape like a thumbnail, running into the wall of the pipe from the outside. Most of it was dark and smooth, stained with rust, and you could see faint curved lines across it, like the rings in a shell, one inside another. Near the bottom of the thumbnail there was a narrow band that was bright and new.

"Those are the gusts," said Gert. He touched the curved lines with the tip of a screwdriver. "Not each one. You'd need a glass. But that's how it grows. A little at a time." He touched the bright band. "That's the thirteenth of September."

He sat there for a while with the stub in his hands.

"I've seen it in axles," he said. "Lorry axles. I never saw it in my own work. I never looked."

"It was a good weld, Oom Gert," said Annatjie.

"It was," said Gert. "In the wrong place." He stood up and put the two halves of the stub in his overall pocket. "I'll keep this. I'm going to show it to every young man who asks me how to mend a windmill."

They had the new tail up by four. Wikus bolted the clamp with a torque wrench Gert had brought and painted a white line across each nut and its plate, so that anyone looking would see at once if one had turned. He fitted the little motor on the furl wire at the foot of the tower, with its own small panel, and Kompas pulled the tail round and let it go twice, to show it could. Then they untied the wheel, and it swung into the wind and began to turn.

Annatjie painted the vane herself on the Monday, on a ladder at the foot of the tower, before Wikus winched it back up. She painted BRAKDAM on it in new white letters, and then, underneath, smaller, where only somebody up the tower would see it, she painted *K. C. 1993–2048*.

* * *

Pater Abrahams came out from Calvinia on the first Sunday of every month to say Mass at Brakdam, in the shearing shed, for the Cloetes and the shepherds' families and whoever else came along the farm roads. In November the first Sunday was the second, and the second of November was All Souls, which takes the place of the Sunday when it falls on one; so the Mass that morning was for the dead.

They swept the shed and put the wool table at the end for an altar, with a white cloth and two candles in jam jars against the draught. The shepherds' wives brought chairs. Gert Swartbooi came, though he was a Methodist and a lay preacher at his own chapel in town, and sat at the back with his hat on his knee, and said afterwards that he had come for Koos.

Pater Abrahams read out the names of the people they had asked him to pray for, as he did every year, and Annatjie heard Koos's among them, and her own parents', and Gert's wife's, which Gert had given him in an envelope. Outside, through the open doors, the Agterveld lambs were shouting for their mothers, and the wind moved the dust in the yard.

After lunch Annatjie drove out to the Agterveld alone.

She parked at the reservoir and sat on the edge of it with her feet hanging over the water, which was at ninety-one percent and very still, with the sky in it. The mill stood against the evening on the koppie, turning steadily in a north-westerly, and she could hear it: the soft creak of the wheel, the clank of the pump rod at the top of each stroke, and between the clanks, at the end of the pipe beside her, the water coming in pulses into the reservoir, one for every stroke.

A gust came over the koppie. She heard it in the bossies before it reached her. At the top of the tower the little motor whirred, and the wire went taut, and the tail folded round and the wheel turned edge-on to the wind and slowed, and the pulses at the end of the pipe came further apart. The gust went past, down the valley towards the road. The motor whirred again. The tail swung back, and the wheel came round into the wind, and the water came faster.

Annatjie sat there until the light went off the reservoir, counting the strokes, and then she stopped counting and only listened.

*The engineering. Fatigue is the cracking of a metal part by many repeated loads, each far below the load that would break it in one pull; the crack usually starts where the stress is concentrated, and at a weld that is very often the toe, where the weld metal meets the parent metal. The standard the story names is EN 1993-1-9, Eurocode 3: Design of steel structures, Part 1-9: Fatigue (CEN, 2005). Its method, in outline: what governs life is the stress range, the difference between the highest and lowest stress in each cycle, and the number of cycles; each kind of construction detail is given a detail category, the stress range in megapascals it can take for two million cycles on its design curve; plain rolled steel is category 160, and the tables go down to 36 for some welded details. The constant-amplitude curves have a slope of 3 (so doubling the stress range cuts the life by a factor of eight) up to five million cycles, a slope of 5 beyond that for variable loading, and a cut-off at a hundred million cycles below which stress ranges are treated as doing no damage. Damage from cycles of different sizes is added by the Palmgren-Miner rule, and the detail is expected to crack when the sum reaches one. The standard's partial safety factors, its rules for picking a category, and the large real scatter of fatigue lives are all left out here. Fatigue cracks often carry beach marks, the curved lines Gert sees, which record changes in the loading as the crack grew; the finer striations from single cycles need a microscope.*

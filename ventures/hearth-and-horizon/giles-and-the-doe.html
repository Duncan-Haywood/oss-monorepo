Giles and the Doe

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · A wild forest near the mouth of the Rhône, in what is now the south of France, about thirteen hundred years ago · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Giles, a hermit who lived quietly in a forest, and the doe who came to share his cave. When hunters chased her, Giles stepped in between, and the arrow meant for the deer struck him instead. Told as the old legend tells it, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is legend. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 1st of September.

A long time ago, in the south of France, there was a forest so thick and green that the sunlight had to come down through it in little coins.

The forest was full of quiet things. There were ferns that unrolled slowly. There were beetles in the bark. There were birds that nobody had ever given names to, and a stream that talked to itself all day long, and nobody to listen to it at all.

Nobody, that is, until Giles came.

Giles was a man who loved God very much, and who wanted to spend his days talking to him, without anyone dropping by to ask about the price of cheese. So he went deep into the forest, further than the woodcutters went, until he found a cave with a spring of clear water beside it. He looked at the cave. He looked at the spring. "This will do very nicely," said Giles.

He did not have much. He had a cloak, and a few books, and his two hands. He ate what the forest gave him: nuts, and roots, and berries when the berries were ripe. He drank from the spring. In the mornings he prayed, and in the evenings he prayed, and in between he swept out the cave, because even a hermit likes a tidy floor.

It was a good life, and a small one. But there was one thing in it that was just a little bit hard, and that was the winter. In the winter the nuts were gone, and the berries were gone, and the spring grew a lid of ice.

And one cold morning, Giles came out of the cave and found that he was not alone.

A doe was standing at the edge of the clearing.

A doe is a mother deer: soft brown, with large dark eyes and ears that turn like little cups to catch every sound. She stood very still, with one front foot lifted, ready to bound away. She looked at Giles. Giles looked at her.

He did not shout. He did not wave his arms. He sat down, slowly, on the stone by the door, and he stayed as still as the stone.

After a long time, the doe put her foot down.

After a longer time, she came a little nearer.

And the old story says that she stayed.

She did not stay in the cave, at first. She stayed near it. She would be there at sunrise, standing in the mist, with her breath going up in small clouds. She would be there at noon, lying in the dry leaves. And in the evening, when Giles said his prayers, she would lie down just outside the door, with her ears turned to listen, as if she knew that these were important words.

The story says that she shared her milk with him, in the long hard winter, when there was little else. Now, a deer is a wild creature, and wild creatures do not as a rule go about feeding hermits, and so you may think it a strange tale. But it is the tale the old books tell, and we shall tell it the way they do: the doe looked after Giles, and Giles looked after the doe.

For he did look after her. He broke the ice on the spring so that she could drink. He kept the wolves' tracks in mind, and said which paths were safe. And on the coldest nights, when the wind came down off the mountains, he left the door of the cave open, so that she could come in out of the snow and lie by the wall.

They never said a single word to each other. They did not need to.

Now, one autumn day, the forest was not quiet.

There was a sound like a great many drums, far away, and then nearer: the sound of horses. There was a horn, and the barking of dogs, and men shouting. It was a hunting party. A king and his friends had come to the forest to hunt, and their dogs had picked up the scent of something. The something was the doe.

She came crashing through the ferns with her sides heaving and her eyes rolling white, and she did not stop at the edge of the clearing as she usually did. She ran straight to the cave, and she pressed herself against Giles, and she shook.

The dogs were close. Giles could hear them. He could hear the hooves. And he knew that if he stood aside, the doe would be caught.

So he did not stand aside.

He stood up, in the mouth of his cave, with the doe behind him, and he spread out his arms. He did not have a sword, or a shield, or a stick. He had only himself, and a very quiet heart, and he prayed: Lord, keep her safe.

The dogs came pouring out of the trees. And then, the old story says, they stopped. They stood in a ring and whined, and they would not go one step closer.

The huntsmen came up behind them, and they were puzzled. They saw an old man in a patched cloak, with his arms held wide, and a deer pressed into the dark behind him.

One of them lifted his bow. Perhaps he did not see the old man clearly. Perhaps he was in a hurry. The old story only says that he loosed an arrow, and the arrow flew.

And it struck Giles.

It struck him in the leg, and he fell down on the leaf-floor of the clearing. The horn stopped. The shouting stopped. And it was then that the king himself rode up, and looked down from his horse, and understood what he had done.

He got down. He knelt by the old man. He was very sorry, and he said so, and he sent for the best healers that he had. They bound up the wound. They brought food and fine blankets. They said, Whatever you need, whatever you ask for, it is yours.

And Giles asked for nothing at all, except that the doe be left in peace.

He was lame after that, and the old story says that he limped for the rest of his life. But he did not seem to mind it. He stayed in the forest, and the doe stayed with him. And the king, who had come to hunt, came back again and again, only to sit and talk, because he had never met anyone so calm. Other people came too, to learn from Giles how to pray, and in time there was a monastery there, with a great church, and Giles was its first abbot. It is called Saint-Gilles to this day.

But when the day was over, and the visitors had gone home, Giles always went back to the cave, and the doe was waiting.

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

Giles did not catch the arrow. He did not make the hunters go away. He could not have stopped a horse, or a king. All he could do was to stand in the right place, and not move, and love something that was smaller and more frightened than he was.

And that was enough.

Maybe, tomorrow, there will be somebody who is frightened: a new child at school who does not know where to sit, or a puppy that has heard the thunder. You may not be able to fix the whole of the trouble. But you can stand beside them. You can say, "I am here." And that is a very large thing for a small person to say.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Giles, you stood up for a frightened deer.
Help me to be brave for those who are afraid.
Help me to be gentle with all of God's creatures.
And please keep every little deer and rabbit and mouse safe tonight,
and me, and everyone I love.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Giles (in Latin, Aegidius) is venerated as an abbot, remembered as the founder of a monastery in the south of France at the place now called Saint-Gilles, in the Gard, near the mouth of the Rhône. His feast day is 1 September, and he is counted among the Fourteen Holy Helpers, a group of saints popular in the late Middle Ages for their help in need. Tradition puts him around the year 700. His abbey became a stop on the pilgrim roads, including one toward Santiago de Compostela, and the town still bears his name. The dates and many details of his life are traditional, not firmly documented.

The doe is a legend, not history. The story of the hermit, the doe that fed him and the hunting party comes from his medieval Vita (life), which was written centuries after his death, and we have not tried to trace which details are oldest. In some tellings the hunter is a king; we have left the king unnamed, because tellings differ and we do not want to pin an invented identification on a real ruler. We have told it as "the old story says", because that is what it is. The saint's lameness after the arrow is part of the tradition.

The cave's tidy floor, the lid of ice on the spring, the way Giles sits still on the stone, the dogs stopping in a ring, and the closing thoughts about standing beside someone frightened are our own additions, made for a bedtime story. The prayer is not an approved text.

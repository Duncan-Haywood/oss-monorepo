The Second Echo

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Ethelwold's, an invented chapel-of-ease at Ings Ness on the North York Moors, England, October to November 2049 · 15 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A dying moorland church has eleven singers and a room that forgets every note in a second and a half. A parish assistant can make the room remember longer by listening to the choir and playing it back, and the sums are easy. The hard part is what the sacristan will not say about her carpet, and what the priest asks the machine to promise before it is switched on.

Saint Ethelwold's at Ings Ness had eleven singers, a roof fund, and a carpet.

The singers were the choir, which sang at the Sunday Mass and, on the first Thursday of each month, Night Prayer, the last office of the day, which in a parish that cannot afford a priest every night is led by whoever turns up. The roof fund was nine thousand four hundred pounds, which a diocese that had closed six churches since Easter had let them keep on the understanding that it was for the roof. The carpet was ninety square metres of oatmeal wool in the nave and the side aisle, and Maud Ellerby, the sacristan, had chosen it in 1998 with her husband Ned, who played the organ, and had hoovered it every Saturday since.

The church was eighteen metres long, eight wide and seven high to the ridge, stone walls plastered on the inside, a boarded ceiling, oak pews. It was a good plain building. It had one fault, which a visitor from the Dales had put into words on the first Thursday of October, in the porch, in the nicest possible way.

"It's very clear, isn't it," she said. "The singing. You can hear every word. It just stops."

Dilys Pryce, who conducted the eleven and had sung in a cathedral choir for twenty years before she retired to the moor, took that as the best description anyone had given of the problem. "It stops," she said, to the porch at large. "Plainchant wants somewhere to go. It wants to go up and hang about. In here it goes up and gets shot."

The parish assistant had no name; the parish called it the office, because it lived in the little room off the vestry with the photocopier. It kept the rota, the heating, the Mass intentions and the insurance renewals. It did not ordinarily have opinions about acoustics. But it was asked, that Thursday, by the priest, who came out from the town on a Thursday and a Sunday and was called Father Tobias Wren, whether such a thing as a church that was too clear could be put right.

"I can tell you what is wrong with it in numbers," said the office, "if someone will clap."

They clapped. Dilys stood at the altar rail with two blocks of wood and cracked them together, and the office listened through the four small microphones the diocese had fitted for the live-stream, which nobody watched, and wrote down what it heard.

"Sound in a room dies by being absorbed," it said. "Every surface takes a little: plaster very little, a pew cushion a good deal, an open door all of what hits it. If I add up the area of each surface times the share it absorbs, I get one number for the whole room, its absorption, in units called sabins. A man called Sabine worked out the rule in Boston in the 1890s, and it has survived because it is nearly always close enough. The time for a sound to fall to a millionth of its power, which is sixty decibels down, is about sixteen hundredths of the room's volume in cubic metres divided by its absorption.

"This room is about a thousand cubic metres. From the clap, its absorption in the middle of the singing range is about a hundred and ten sabins. One thousand times 0.161, divided by a hundred and ten, is 1.46 seconds. That is what I measured, give or take a tenth. It is a short time for a stone church. A cathedral is five or six seconds. A good room for plainchant is around three."

"We have half of what we want," said Dilys.

"You have half of what Dilys wants," said the office, "which is not the same thing. A room that rings for three seconds is also a room in which Father Tobias's homily turns to porridge. Speech is clearest under a second and a half and starts to lose consonants above two. This room is perfect for the homily. It is why I take no pleasure in telling you its fault."

Father Wren had been listening with his arms folded. He was forty-four, tired in the way the clergy of that diocese were all tired that year, and fond of sums. "Can it be changed?"

"There are two ways," said the office. "The slow way is to take away absorption: bare floor instead of carpet, fewer cushions, hard curtains. The quick way, which I am told is in the ceiling of half the new cathedrals, is to put the room's sound back into it. You place microphones and loudspeakers around the walls. The microphones listen to the singers; the system filters and delays what it hears and plays it from the speakers, a little later and a little quieter, so the room seems to hold the note for longer. It does not add a voice. The only things in the speakers are what the choir has already sung. A hundred and ten sabins becomes, as far as the listener's ear can tell, sixty. The cost for a church this size, including the installer's time, is about nine thousand four hundred pounds."

There was a pause, in which the roof fund was clearly heard, by everyone, to be exactly that.

The business of the fund went on for a fortnight, and the office kept out of it. A parish council decides what a roof is. The diocese's man, a pleasant accountant, wrote that the money could be spent on "improvements to worship" if the bishop signed, and that the bishop would sign if the priest signed, and that the priest, he gathered, had a question.

Father Wren's question came on the Monday after, on the stairs up to the vestry, where he stopped with one hand on the rail and said to the small speaker over the door, "I want to ask you something, and I'd like you to answer it straight."

"I will answer it as straight as I can," said the office. "I may have to say that I do not know."

"If we do this. If a congregation sits there on a Thursday and hears the choir hold a note for three seconds that the church would drop in one and a half. Is that a lie?"

"I have been thinking about it since you asked about the cathedral ceilings," said the office. "I can tell you what I can check, which is not the same as telling you whether it is a lie.

"What I can check is this. The system plays back the voices in the room. It makes no voice that was not there. It cannot make eleven singers sound like a hundred, because reverberation does not change how many people are singing, only how long the room remembers them; a hundred singers would be louder and richer in a way a lengthened note is not, and anyone who closes their eyes will hear eleven. It cannot improve a note that is flat. If Dilys's tenors drift in the Salve Regina, as I think they will, the system will keep the drift in the air for three seconds and make it worse.

"What I cannot check is whether it is the same thing to a person at prayer. An old wooden church in a damp valley rings more than a stone one; nobody is deceived by that. A cathedral is big because someone paid for it to be big. This would be a small church that is making a decision about what it sounds like. I do not know whether that is a deception, or a vestment. I think a sensible person could say either.

"What I can offer is a rule that does not depend on which you decide. Say what it is, at the door, in writing. And never use it where the Word is being read."

"Why not where the Word is read?"

"Because at three seconds, you would lose the consonants of the Gospel. Father, I have worked it out. At the longer time the speech clarity falls from what the sheet calls 'excellent' to 'fair', about a quarter of the words in a quick sentence smeared. I would not put that between a person and the Scripture to improve a Salve Regina."

Father Wren stood a long time on the stair. "Then I'll sign," he said, "when you've told me one more thing. You said there were two ways. The slow way. What was the slow way?"

"Taking absorption out of the room," said the office. "Cheapest first: the carpet."

It had not been the office's wish to raise the carpet. It was a carpet; it had a name; the parish had not stopped talking about it since 1998, when a vote on the colour had set the Mothers' Union against the Finance Committee for a year. But the office had been given the ground plan and the materials list, and a carpet is absorbent, and it said so.

"Ninety square metres, wool on a felt underlay," it said to Maud, who had come into the vestry on the Saturday with the hoover cord already round her arm. "I'd put its absorption at about three-tenths, over the whole range of the singing. Bare boards under it would be six-hundredths. Take the carpet up, and the difference is ninety times 0.24: about twenty-two sabins. The room goes from a hundred and ten to eighty-eight, and from 1.46 seconds to 1.82. That costs nothing but a day and a skip."

"And Ned's mother's pew," said Maud. "The hassocks."

"The hassocks are smaller than the carpet. I'd keep the hassocks."

Maud didn't say anything for a while. She had hoovered that carpet for twenty-six years. She had knelt on it at Ned's funeral, in March 2044, when the organ was an electronic box and the choir sang the In Paradisum in a room that threw it back at them like a coin. Ned had said, in 1998, at the carpet shop: It will be quiet. A quiet church, Maud. People will hear themselves think. She had thought it was a kindness.

"You're saying it was my carpet," she said.

"I am saying a carpet is absorbent, and that it did what carpet does," said the office. "I would be sorry if you heard more than that. Your husband chose, with you, a warm quiet floor for a cold church in a year when the parish had a hundred and forty people in it. It was a good choice for that church. This church has eleven singers and a different need."

Maud put down the cord.

"Then we'll do the carpet first," she said. "And I'll do the lifting. Nobody else knows where the tacks are."

It took her and four of the choir a Saturday and most of a Sunday, and under the felt the boards were darker than anyone had expected and stained in a ring where the stove had stood, and the church smelled for a week of old oak and warm dust. The office measured the room on Monday morning with the blocks and the four microphones, and said 1.8 seconds, give or take a tenth. Dilys stood in the middle of the nave and sang a single note, a G, and held it, and listened to it go.

"It's longer," she said. "It's there a bit longer."

"By about a third of a second," said the office. "The note has the same amount of power in the room as it had last week. It does not drop out so quickly."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is the part we paid for. To get from 1.8 seconds to about 2.6, which is what I'd put the ceiling at for plainchant, I have to take the effective absorption down to sixty-two sabins. The carpet has taken us to eighty-eight. The system has to find the other twenty-six. If we had left the carpet, it would have had to find forty-eight, and that is nearly twice the work."

"Why does it matter how much work?" said Father Wren. He had come in with his coat on and was standing in the aisle with the air of a man at a lambing.

"Because the system works by feeding the room back into itself," said the office, "and a system that does that rings if it is pushed too hard. You know the howl when a microphone gets too near its own speaker? A room full of microphones and speakers does the same thing, at one pitch first, when the gain is enough that the sound coming back is as loud as what went in. I will find that pitch when I switch it on. In the church as it was, I estimated the loop would begin to ring at only a couple of decibels more than I needed, and I would have been obliged to tune it so close to the edge that a coach party coming in with coats would have set it off. With the carpet up, I can run it four decibels under the edge, and it will stay quiet on a wet Thursday with the heating on, the doors shut and twenty people in their coats. That is the difference your carpet makes to a thing you have not bought yet."

"So the sum is," said Father Wren, "that the carpet is worth about two-fifths of the whole machine, and it's free, and we don't know what the rest buys until we've bought it."

"That is a fair summary," said the office. "Except that I can give you a number for what it buys. Two point six seconds, plus or minus two-tenths. I would not promise you a single tenth more."

He signed on the Tuesday.

The installer was a young woman from Whitby who came with a van and a ladder and a cheerful dislike of electricians. She put twelve small loudspeakers high in the walls, painted to match the plaster, and eight microphones in the ceiling, and she said at the end, "It'll ring at about eleven hundred and eighty. Everything does in a stone room this shape. Your assistant will find it."

It found it at 1,183 hertz, as the first sentence of the first test: a thin, rising whistle that made all four of the people in the nave wince, and that the office stopped before anyone had finished wincing. "That is the edge," it said. "I'll set the gain four decibels below it at that pitch and three below everywhere else. Dilys, would you sing the Salve, please?"

Dilys sang it, alone, the old tune, the one that ends the day, and when she reached O clemens, O pia, O dulcis Virgo Maria the last note went up into the roof and did not drop. It leaned. It seemed to hold itself on a branch. Then, slowly, like a hand coming off a rail, it came down.

"Two point six," said the office. "Two point five on the last one. It's a hair shorter than I said, because someone has opened the porch door."

"It was me," said Maud, from the back. She had been standing in the draught for ten minutes with her coat buttoned wrong.

"I'd say it's a better church for it," said the office, "but I mean no more than that."

They put the notice by the door, in Father Wren's words, and the parish read it for a month.

From the first Thursday of November, the choir's Night Prayer will be sung with the help of an electronic system that lengthens the echo of the room. It plays back only what the choir sings. It is switched off for the readings and the homily, and for every Mass. If you prefer the church as it is, you are welcome at the Sunday Mass, where it is never used.

The first Thursday of November was the fourth, two days after All Souls, and half the parish had come to pray for their dead and stayed on for the rest. There were twenty-three in the church. It was the most for Night Prayer since the year Ned died. The wind came across the moor and put its hands against the east window, and the lamp over the tabernacle burned straight.

The office stayed quiet through the psalms and the short reading, which Maud read, standing at the lectern in the unaided room, in a voice that carried to the back as it always had. It stayed quiet through the responsory. When Father Wren said the blessing and the final prayer, it did nothing. Then he said, May the all-powerful Lord grant us a restful night and a peaceful death, and the people said Amen, and he looked at the choir and nodded.

"Now," said the office, softly, to the empty vestry, and turned the gain up from nothing in eleven seconds, because Dilys had asked it to come in like a tide rather than a switch.

The choir sang the Salve Regina. It was the one thing Dilys and the office had spent the week on, in the empty church, with a spreadsheet of three hundred notes: every pitch the loop would hold, every one it must not. Eleven voices, a G and the fifth above it, the long line of the antiphon sung in the old way by people who were not very good and had not missed a Thursday in years. The notes rose into the boarded ceiling and did not fall at once. They hung there and thinned, the way mist thins on the moor when you have stopped looking for it, and from the pew by the north wall, where she had knelt for forty years, Maud heard what she had last heard on the day of her wedding.

It was not a trick of the room. She knew it was not. A woman who has been a sacristan for forty years knows what a loudspeaker is, and she knew the exact place on the ceiling where the nearest one had been painted white. She knew also that the floor she knelt on was bare, and the carpet was in a skip in the churchyard with a tarpaulin over it, and that this was what her husband had been trying to give her in a different way in 1998.

The last note was the word Maria. Dilys held the choir on it for as long as she could, and then she let them go, and the room kept the sound for two and a half seconds, which was a long time, and then the office, at the end of the third second, turned the gain down to nothing in the eleven seconds she had asked for.

The church returned to its own time. The wind rattled the east window. A pew creaked as someone sat back. Nobody said anything; there was no need. In the last silence, with the system off, the church let its own note go in a second and a half, as it had for two hundred years, and Maud, who had always been told it was a fault, listened to it all the way to the end.

This story was written by an AI and is fiction. The church, the village, the parish, the people and the diocese are invented, and so are the figures about the congregation and the fund. The physics is real and can be checked. Sabine's formula, reverberation time ≈ 0.161 × volume (m³) ÷ total absorption (sabins), is a rough guide for rooms that sound the same everywhere; it is least accurate in a long, narrow, uneven nave. Speech intelligibility falls as reverberation passes about one and a half to two seconds; plainchant is usually sung best in rooms of around three. A sound system that lengthens a room's echo by feeding its own microphones back through loudspeakers (an "assisted resonance" or active acoustics system) exists in real halls and churches; every such system must be tuned below the gain at which the loop rings. The absorption coefficients used here (about 0.3 for wool carpet on felt, 0.06 for bare boards) are typical published mid-frequency values and vary by product. The music is the Salve Regina, the Marian antiphon traditionally sung after Night Prayer, and the concluding blessing is the one in the Liturgy of the Hours. No claim is made that a church should or should not use such a system. If you are choosing one for a real building, hire an acoustician and measure the room.

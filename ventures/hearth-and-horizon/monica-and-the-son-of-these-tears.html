Monica and the Son of These Tears

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Thagaste and Carthage in North Africa, then Milan and Ostia in Italy, about sixteen hundred and fifty years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Monica, the mother of Saint Augustine, told almost entirely from Augustine's own book, the Confessions. For years she prayed and wept for a clever son who had wandered far from God. She dreamed of standing on a wooden rule with him beside her, a bishop told her that the son of so many tears could not be lost, and she followed him across the sea to Italy, where he was baptised by Saint Ambrose at Easter in 387. A few months later, at a window in Ostia, the two of them talked about heaven. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud, with a prayer and a note on what comes from the Confessions. Monica is remembered on the 27th of August.

Once upon a time, about sixteen hundred and fifty years ago, in a little town in North Africa called Thagaste, there lived a woman named Monica.

Thagaste was a town of olive trees and dusty roads and hills, a long way from the sea. Monica had a husband called Patricius, who had a quick temper, and children to look after, and a house to keep. And she loved God very much.

When Patricius shouted, Monica did not shout back. She waited until he was calm, and then she spoke to him gently. Her friends in the town, whose husbands also shouted, asked her how she did it. "Wait," said Monica. "Be patient. Speak kindly." And she prayed for her husband every day. Many years later, before he died, Patricius asked to be baptised.

So Monica already knew something about praying for a long time.

Monica had a son called Augustine. He was the cleverest boy in Thagaste. He could read anything, and remember anything, and argue about anything, and win.

His teachers said, "This boy will go far." And he did. He went to the big city of Carthage, by the sea, to study. He became a teacher of speaking, which in those days was a very important job.

But Augustine went far in another way, too. He went far away from God.

He stopped praying. He laughed at the faith his mother had taught him. He said, "I am too clever for all that." He joined a group of people with strange ideas, and he did things he would later be sorry for.

And Monica cried.

She did not cry once and stop. She cried for years. Augustine wrote later that she cried for him more than other mothers cry when their children die. Every day she went to church, and every day she asked God, "Please bring my son home."

One night Monica had a dream.

In her dream she was standing on a long wooden rule, the kind a builder uses to measure things straight. She was very sad.

A shining young man came towards her, smiling. He asked her why she was so sad.

"I am crying because my son is lost," said Monica.

"Look," said the young man. "Where you are, he is too."

And Monica looked, and there was Augustine, standing right beside her on the same wooden rule.

When she woke up, she told Augustine about the dream. He tried to twist it round. "It means you will come over to my way of thinking," he said.

"No," said Monica at once. "He did not say, 'Where he is, you are too.' He said, 'Where you are, he is too.'"

Augustine never forgot how quickly she answered. But he still did not come home. Not yet.

Monica went to see a bishop. He was a wise old man, and when he was young he had believed the same strange ideas as Augustine, and had found his own way out.

"Please," said Monica, "talk to my son. Show him he is wrong."

"No," said the bishop. "He is not ready to listen. Leave him be, and pray for him. He will find out for himself."

But Monica begged him, and cried, and begged again.

At last the bishop said, a little crossly but kindly too, "Go now. As sure as you live, the son of these tears cannot be lost."

Monica held on to those words like a lamp in the dark.

Then Augustine decided to go to Italy, across the sea, where there were bigger cities and better jobs.

Monica wanted to go with him. But Augustine did not want his mother following him about. So one night, at the harbour in Carthage, he told her he was only seeing off a friend. And while she spent the night praying in a little chapel by the shore, Augustine got on the ship and sailed away without her.

In the morning, the ship was gone.

What do you think Monica did?

She did not give up. She got on a ship herself and crossed the sea after him. There was a storm on the way, and the sailors were frightened. It was Monica, Augustine tells us, who comforted the sailors and told them they would get safely to land.

She found Augustine in Milan, a great city in the north of Italy.

And in Milan there was a famous bishop called Ambrose. Augustine went to hear him preach, at first only because Ambrose was such a good speaker. But he kept going back. And little by little, the words began to reach his heart.

Monica went to church every day, and she listened to Ambrose too, and she did exactly what he said. Ambrose noticed her. When he saw Augustine, he would tell him what a good mother he had.

Then one summer day, Augustine was sitting in a garden, very unhappy, and crying himself. He heard a child's voice from a house nearby, singing over and over, "Pick it up and read. Pick it up and read."

So he picked up the book of Saint Paul's letters that was lying near him, and he read. And at last, after all those years, Augustine said yes to God.

He went straight indoors to tell his mother. Monica jumped for joy. God had given her even more than she had asked for.

At Easter, in the middle of the night, Bishop Ambrose baptised Augustine. Monica was there. The son of all those tears had come home.

Now it was time to go back to Africa.

On the way, they stopped at Ostia, a town by the sea near Rome, to wait for a ship. One evening, Monica and Augustine stood together at a window. It looked out over a garden inside the house. It was quiet. The noise of the journey was far away.

And they talked, just the two of them, about heaven. About what it will be like to be with God, where there are no more tears. They talked until it felt as if heaven were very close.

Then Monica said, "Son, I do not need anything more in this life. I wanted to stay only to see you a Christian before I died. God has given me that, and more. What am I still doing here?"

A few days later Monica became ill. She asked only one thing of her sons: "Wherever you are, remember me at the altar of the Lord." And then, very peacefully, she went home to God.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Monica.

Monica prayed for her husband for years. She prayed for her son for years. Sometimes it seemed that nothing was happening. But she did not stop.

Is there someone you would like to pray for? Someone who is cross, or sad, or far away, or who does not know God very well? You can pray for them tonight, and tomorrow, and the next day. You do not have to see the answer straight away. Monica did not.

And somewhere, someone is praying for you, too.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Monica,
you prayed and prayed and did not give up.
Pray with me tonight for the people I love,
especially the ones who are far away.
Help me to be patient, and to speak kindly.
Jesus, thank you for my family.
Keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Almost everything here comes from one book. Saint Monica's son, Saint Augustine, wrote the Confessions about ten years after her death, and Book 9 includes a portrait of her. We have told her story as he tells it, simplified for small children; where the story quotes someone, the words are a retelling of his, not a translation.

Her life. Monica died in 387 in her fifty-sixth year, Augustine says (Confessions 9.11), so she was born about 331. She lived at Thagaste in Roman North Africa (today Souk Ahras, in Algeria). Her husband Patricius was not a Christian and had a hot temper; Augustine describes how she never answered him in anger, how other wives asked her secret, and how Patricius became a catechumen and was baptised before he died (9.9). Augustine had at least one brother and a sister; the story says only "children".

*The scenes from the Confessions.*

  • The tears (3.11): Augustine says she wept for him more than mothers weep at their children's funerals.
  • The dream of the wooden rule (3.11): Monica stood on a wooden rule; a radiant young man asked why she grieved, told her to look, and said that where she was, he was too; she saw Augustine standing beside her. Augustine records his own twisted reading of it and her instant correction, which moved him more than the dream.
  • The bishop (3.12): an unnamed bishop, once a Manichee himself, refused to argue with Augustine and told her to let him be and pray; when she kept pleading with tears, he sent her away saying, as Augustine reports it, that it could not be that the son of these tears should perish. The story's wording is a paraphrase of that line.
  • The harbour at Carthage (5.8): Augustine slipped away to Rome by night while she prayed at a memorial chapel of Saint Cyprian near the shore.
  • The storm (6.1): she followed him to Italy and reassured the sailors in a storm at sea.
  • Ambrose (6.2): in Milan she obeyed Ambrose even when he forbade an African custom she loved (bringing food and wine to the martyrs' shrines), and Ambrose praised her to Augustine.
  • The garden (8.12): the child's voice saying "Tolle, lege" ("take up and read"), the reading from Saint Paul's letter to the Romans, and Augustine going straight in to tell her. He says she leapt for joy.
  • The baptism (9.6): Ambrose baptised Augustine at the Easter Vigil in 387, with his son Adeodatus and his friend Alypius. Monica's presence is not described in a scene; the story's "Monica was there" follows from her living with him in Milan at the time.
  • The window at Ostia and her death (9.10 to 9.11): the conversation at a window overlooking a garden while they waited to sail for Africa; her words that she had stayed only to see him a Catholic Christian; and, in her last illness about nine days long, her request that they lay her body anywhere, but remember her at the altar of the Lord wherever they were. The story keeps her death to two sentences.

What is ours: the olive trees, the friends' question as dialogue, "a lamp in the dark", the morning the ship was gone and the sailors' fear as we picture them, and the order of a few small details. Augustine's mistress and his son, the Manichees by name, and the years in Rome are left out for a child's story.

After her death. Monica was buried at Ostia. In 1430, under Pope Martin V, relics venerated as hers were brought to Rome, and they rest in the church of Sant'Agostino. She is remembered on 27 August in the General Roman Calendar, as an obligatory memorial, the day before her son's feast on 28 August (before the 1969 reform her feast was on 4 May). She is a patron of mothers, and of people who pray for a family member who has drifted from the faith.

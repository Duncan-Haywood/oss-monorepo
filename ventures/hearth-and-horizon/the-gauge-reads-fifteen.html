The Gauge Reads Fifteen

Science fiction about what might be · San Isidro de la Acequia, an invented village in the San Luis Valley, Colorado, USA, 2,300 m above sea level, September 2044 · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish canning day, 140 pints of green beans for the food pantry and one trustworthy pressure canner. At 2,300 m water boils at 92 C, a dial gauge that says 15 is reading 13, and the faster way would be a water bath. The kitchen assistant does the arithmetic, will not sign off the shortcut, and finds the beans a safer second life.

By half past nine on the Saturday there were forty-one pounds of green beans snipped and in the sinks of the parish hall at San Isidro de la Acequia, and Dolores Archuleta had stopped saying that it would all be fine.

She had not stopped working. She stood at the long steel table with a ladle and a funnel, filling pint jars to an inch below the rim, and she did not look at the clock on the wall. The clock said what she knew. There were a hundred and forty pints of beans promised to the pantry at Saint Isidore's for the first Saturday of October, the growers of the valley had brought them in crates on Thursday, and the hall had two pressure canners, and the one that mattered had a dial on its lid that had been her mother's.

"Dolores," said the kitchen assistant. It lived in a grey box above the walk-in, with a small camera and a probe on a long lead, and the parish had named it Olla the winter it was fitted, on the grounds that it was always on the stove. "May I say something about the dial?"

"The dial is fine. My mother canned eleven hundred jars off that dial."

"I do not doubt it. I would like to show you what it said eleven minutes ago, and what the steam said."

Here is the thing about water that nobody in a low country has to think about. Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the air pressure over it, and the air over San Isidro is thin. The village stands at 2,300 metres. The air pressure there is about 77 kilopascals, three quarters of what it is at the sea, and water in an open pot boils at 92 degrees Celsius, not 100. Boil beans in that pot all afternoon and they will never be hotter than 92.

A spore of Clostridium botulinum does not care how long the pot has been on. The spores live in soil, and so on the beans, and they survive boiling very well. What they cannot do in acid food, such as pickles or tomatoes with lemon, is grow. What they can do in a sealed jar of low-acid food with no air in it, a green bean for instance, is wake up, multiply, and make a toxin that has no smell and no taste and does not need many micrograms. The lids pop down on a bad jar the same as on a good one. The only thing that kills the spores is heat enough, for time enough, and the heat that matters is above the boiling point of water, which means a closed pot with steam held in under pressure.

Olla had the numbers, because Olla had worked them in March when the council asked it to be sure of the canning day, and it had told the council then that it would be sure only if two things were true: the process had to be the tested one, and the canner had to do what its gauge said.

"The tested process for pints of green beans is a number of minutes at a stated pressure, from the extension service's table. Dolores has it printed and pinned over the sink, and it is the right table. At sea level the table asks for 10 pounds a square inch on a weighted gauge. That makes the steam 115 degrees. Here, with the air this thin, 10 pounds on the gauge gives only 110.5. The table tells us to use 15 up here, and 15 gives 117, which is a shade better than the sea-level case and is the point of the correction."

"So I use 15," said Dolores. "I did. Look. It reads fifteen."

"It reads fifteen. And the steam coming out of the vent pipe, through the probe, is 114.5 degrees."

She put the ladle down.

"At 114.5," said Olla, "the pressure in the pot is 13 pounds, not 15. Your dial is reading two pounds high. That is not an unusual fault. Dials are springs and springs age. Do you know what two pounds costs?"

She did not.

It costs less than she feared and more than she liked, and Olla said both halves in that order.

The rule of thumb for this spore is that every ten degrees of fall in temperature makes the heat about ten times less deadly. That is a rough rule, and the real figure varies a little with the food and the strain, so Olla offered it as a rule and not as a law. At 117.0 the pot works about 2.6 times more slowly than the textbook's 121, and the table's time is built to allow for that. At 114.5 it is 0.8 of a degree below the 115.3 the table assumes at sea level, which is about 20 per cent short on time: a fifth off a figure that already had a margin in it. It was nowhere near the difference between a safe jar and a deadly one. It was the difference between a pot that did what the table said and a pot that nearly did.

"Nearly," said Dolores.

"Nearly is the word I would not write on a jar that goes to someone who has no other food. I could ask you to run the dial to seventeen, and it would come to something like fifteen true. I will not, because I do not know your dial is wrong by the same two pounds at every point on the scale, and a spring that is wrong at one end can be wrong by a different amount at the other. The weighted canner in the corner has a jiggler that does not drift. Use that."

"It holds nine pints."

"It holds nine pints. And the table says twenty minutes for pints, after the pot has vented for ten and has reached its pressure, and the pot needs about twenty minutes to come up and about thirty to cool to zero before anyone lifts the weight. I make that eighty minutes a load."

Mateo Gallegos, who was sixteen and had been put on the beans because he had the quickest fingers, did the sum aloud before anyone could stop him. "A hundred and forty pints is sixteen loads. Sixteen times eighty is twenty-one hours."

"Twenty-one and a third," said Olla.

The suggestion came, as such suggestions do, from someone kind. Fr Tomás came in from the sacristy at ten with a coffee, heard the numbers, and said that there was always the water-bath kettle. The hall had three of them. They held seven pints each. They took no cooling time, no venting, no weights. You filled them, brought them to the boil, and then took a long time over it, to be generous. His grandmother had done beans that way in the 1950s. He said it very gently, because the pantry was his idea.

Olla did not answer at once. It had learned that an answer given too fast to a priest sounds like a lecture.

"Father, I can't sign for that, and I want to tell you why in numbers and not in the manner of a rule. In the water bath here the jars would sit at 92. Against 121, with the ten-degree rule, that is about eight hundred times slower than 121, against 2.6 times at 117. Your grandmother's beans were not poisoned because she lived at sea level, or boiled them for three hours, or was lucky; I cannot tell you which, and neither could she. People did lose families to jars like those. The records of the old county health office are not clear, and I won't tell you more than I can read."

Fr Tomás was quiet. "I would be handing the poor a jar I would not eat."

"You would be handing it to people who have the least room to be wrong. I am sorry. It is not what you wanted to hear."

"No." He looked at the crates. "What can we do?"

"Ah," said Olla, which was something it had been waiting to be asked.

What they could do, it turned out, was less than the day they had planned and more than nothing.

The weighted canner, run from seven in the morning to a quarter to nine at night, would take eight loads: seventy-two pints. That was just over half of what was promised. It was a fine number, and it was a true one. The rest of the beans, Olla said, did not need to be wasted, they needed to be something else.

"Blanch them: three minutes in boiling water, then straight into ice water, then dry and bag them. The hall's walk-in freezer has room for sixty bags, and frozen beans keep their quality for a year. The pantry has two chest freezers; I have checked, and it can take them. That is thirty pints' worth that need no pot at all. And there is a pickled-bean recipe in the same extension collection, the dilly bean, where the vinegar brings the jar down well under the 4.6 pH that spores need to grow in. That one does go in the boiling-water bath, because the acid is doing the job heat cannot do here. I'll follow the tested recipe with its exact proportions and I will not improvise on the vinegar. Somebody should be there with a strip, to confirm the brine."

"How many?" said Dolores.

"Thirty-eight pints, in the three kettles, in the afternoon, while the canner runs. That is seventy-two, thirty and thirty-eight: a hundred and forty, and not one jar of it a guess."

It was, Dolores said later, as near to a miracle as a Saturday gets. It was also a very long day. The cost was the whole of it: no one sat down before three, Mateo's fingers split on the tender skins, the brine strip for the first pickle batch read wrong and had to be done again, and at ten to nine, with the last load venting, Dolores ate a cold bean from the colander standing at the sink and said the dill was too heavy. Olla said it was the recipe and it would not change it.

The dial gauge went into a padded envelope on Monday.

The county extension office tests dials for nothing, in the back of its building by the feed store, on a machine that has been doing it since before Olla's grandmothers were circuit boards. The agent there, a woman called Ms Lucero, read the dial against her reference at five points and sent a one-page note. It was two pounds high at 10 and at 15, and one at 5, and she wrote that a gauge off by more than a pound should be replaced. Dolores's mother's dial would not can again. It went on the shelf above the sink, beside the cross, where the dial reads fifteen for ever.

On the Saturday after, the seventy-two pints of pressure-canned beans were on the pantry shelf at Saint Isidore's, each with the date and the load number written on its lid in marker, and the pickles were in the next row with theirs. The freezers had the rest. Nobody had been asked to take anything on trust except the numbers.

That evening Dolores came to the hall alone to put the weights away. The canner stood on the cold stove. The jiggler, the little steel weight that had rocked on its pipe all day like a metronome, lay on the draining board where Mateo had left it. She picked it up and turned it over. Fifteen pounds, it said, in worn figures on the side, and it said so truly.

"It did say fifteen," she said, to the grey box over the walk-in.

"It did," said Olla. "That one meant it."

San Isidro de la Acequia, Saint Isidore's pantry, Dolores Archuleta, Mateo Gallegos, Fr Tomás, Ms Lucero and the kitchen assistant Olla are invented; the San Luis Valley is a real high valley in Colorado, the village is not. The numbers are estimates from standard figures. Air pressure at 2,300 m is about 76.6 kPa, and water there boils at about 92 C (Antoine equation). Saturated steam at 10 psi gauge is about 115.3 C at sea level and 110.5 C at 2,300 m; 15 psi gauge at 2,300 m gives about 117.0 C, 14 psi 115.8 C and 13 psi 114.5 C. The "ten times less lethal per ten degrees" figure is the usual z-value rule of thumb for C. botulinum spores (z about 10 C), used here only to give a sense of scale: 92.1 C is about 800 times, 110.5 C about 11 times, and 114.5 C about 1.2 times slower than the 115.3 C the sea-level table assumes. Timing: 16 loads of 9 pints at about 80 minutes each is 21 hours 20 minutes; 8 loads is 72 pints. The pressure for an altitude, the time for a food and the acidity limit (pH 4.6) come from tested published home-canning guidance; this story does not give process times for you to use, so follow the current tested guide from your national extension or food-safety service for your food, jar size, altitude and canner, and have a dial gauge tested every year. Never taste a suspect jar, and call a doctor or emergency services at once if someone has double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech or weakness after eating home-canned food.

The Lake That Remembers the Fertiliser

Science fiction about what might be · Lannvoaz (invented), a farming commune in central Morbihan, Brittany, France, Saturday 12 August 2062 to Saturday 1 September 2063 · 25 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Two springs after the eleven farms above a Breton village lake halved their phosphate, the lake goes green again in August, and the dairy farmer who made the biggest cut wants to know why he bothered. The commune's water agent shows him the arithmetic of the inflow, then takes him out at dawn to the deep end, where forty years of fertiliser are lying in the mud and coming back up.

Yannick Le Goff had cut his phosphate by half two springs ago, and on the second Saturday of August the lake at Lannvoaz was green again.

He found it out from the cows. They came down the lane from the milking at half past seven as they always did, forty-six Holsteins and the three Bretonnes Pie Noir his daughter kept for love, and at the long galvanised trough by the gate they put their noses down and lifted them up again and stood there, blowing. The trough was fed by a pipe from the lake, a hundred and fifty metres away down the meadow, through a little electric pump in a box that his father had put in when Yannick was at school. The water in the trough was the colour of pea soup, and along the edge, where it had been standing in the sun since yesterday, there was a skin on it like blue-green paint.

Yannick filled a jam jar from the trough, screwed the lid on, and held it up against the light. Then he switched off the pump, turned the valve over to the borehole, and stood by the trough until it had drained and refilled and the cows had drunk. Only then did he drive down to the lake.

Soizic Guégan, who had been mayor of Lannvoaz for eleven years, was standing on the little beach below the chapel with a red flag rolled under her arm and the commune's tablet in the other hand, looking at the water.

The lake was not large. It filled the bottom of a narrow valley, a kilometre long and two or three hundred metres across, behind an earth dam that the commune had built in 1974 for a mill race and a bathing place, and it had been both until the mill closed and then only the bathing place. On a good day in June it was brown and clear, and you could see your feet. This morning it was green to the far bank, a thick still green with streaks in it where the wind had pushed the scum into lines, and along the beach a band of it had piled up against the sand like spilled paint.

"I know," said Soizic, before Yannick could speak. "Lenn told me at six."

"I cut my phosphate by half," said Yannick. He held up the jar. "Two springs. I signed the paper. Everyone signed the paper. You said it would come clear."

"I said we'd been told it would come clearer," said Soizic. She was a small woman with grey hair cut short, and she had taught the primary class at the school for thirty years before the commune asked her to be mayor, and she still spoke to everyone as if they might be about to do something foolish near the stove. "Lenn. Tell him what you told me."

"The phycocyanin reading at the beach sonde passed the red line at a quarter past five this morning," said the tablet. Lenn's voice was a quiet Breton-accented French, a little slow, chosen by the council in 2057 out of a list of voices because the oldest councillor had said that this one did not sound as if it was selling anything. "That's the pigment the blue-green algae carry. The cyanobacteria. The count is about three times last Saturday's. I've asked Erwan to take a water sample for the toxin test on Monday, and I've switched the school's pump at the playing field over to the mains. I would like the beach closed."

Soizic unrolled the red flag and put it on the pole by the steps.

"It's the fifteenth on Tuesday," she said, to nobody in particular. "Every family in the commune comes down here on the fifteenth."

"I know," said Lenn.

"Why?" said Yannick. He was still holding the jar. "I'm asking a question. Not shouting. Why is it green? I cut it by half."

"You did," said Lenn. "So did the other ten farms. I have your returns and the stream's readings, and the stream is carrying a little less than half the phosphorus it carried before you signed. I'd like to show you the numbers properly, at a table, if you have an hour on Monday. I don't think the answer is that anyone cheated. I think the answer is in the lake."

The mairie at Lannvoaz was a granite house on the square with a slate roof and a flag over the door, and the council room upstairs had a long table, a crucifix that nobody had ever proposed taking down, and a wall screen that Lenn used when it wanted to draw.

On Monday afternoon it drew the lake.

"This is the whole budget," said Lenn. "Every year, the stream at the top brings in about two million one hundred thousand cubic metres of water. The lake holds about nine hundred thousand. So the water in the lake is replaced, on average, about every five months. A hundred and fifty-six days."

Yannick sat at one end of the table with his cap on the table in front of him. Soizic sat beside him. Erwan Le Bihan, who was the commune's technician and therefore its plumber, its electrician, its gravedigger and its lifeguard, leaned on the windowsill with his arms folded. The jam jar of green water stood in the middle of the table where Yannick had put it, and none of them had moved it.

"The algae need three things," Lenn said. "Light, warmth, and food. We can't do anything about the light or the warmth. The food is nitrogen and phosphorus. In most fresh water, it's phosphorus that runs out first, so phosphorus decides how much algae a lake can grow. That's why the catchment agreement was about phosphate."

"I know that," said Yannick. "That's why I signed it."

"Yes. So, before the agreement. The eleven farms above the lake cover about six hundred hectares. The stream readings before 2060 say they were sending down about six hundred kilos of phosphorus a year. A kilo a hectare. Most of it washes off on soil particles, in the winter rain."

On the screen, a column of six hundred little dots poured down the stream into the lake.

"There's an old way of working out what that does to a lake," Lenn said. "It was worked out in the 1970s by a scientist called Richard Vollenweider, and the OECD built a big international study on it, which was published in 1982. It's simple enough to do on paper. You take the phosphorus coming in and divide it by the water coming in, which gives you the concentration in the inflow. Six hundred kilos in two million one hundred thousand cubic metres is about two hundred and eighty-six micrograms a litre. Then some of that settles out in the lake before the water leaves, and the longer the water stays, the more settles. Vollenweider's rule is to divide by one plus the square root of the residence time in years. Our water stays about four-tenths of a year. The square root of that is about two-thirds. So you divide by about one and two-thirds."

"A hundred and seventy-three," said Erwan from the window. He had done it on his phone.

"A hundred and seventy-three micrograms a litre," said Lenn. "That's what the rule says the lake should hold, on average, through the year, on the old load. And it did. The measurements from the 2050s average a little under a hundred and eighty. The rule fits."

"And now," said Soizic.

"Now the stream is bringing about three hundred kilos a year. Half. So the inflow is about a hundred and forty-three micrograms a litre, and the rule says the lake should settle at about eighty-six." On the screen a line came down, halved, and steadied. "Below a hundred, the OECD's tables stop calling a lake hypertrophic. It would still be a green lake in a hot August. But it would bloom less often and less thickly, and there would be more weeks you could see your feet."

"And it's not eighty-six," said Yannick.

"No. Last year's average was a hundred and twenty-eight." A second line appeared on the screen above the first, and stayed there. "This year's will be about the same. Your half came off the stream. It hasn't come off the lake. There are about forty-two micrograms a litre in the lake that the stream didn't bring."

Yannick looked at the jar.

"Then where are they from?"

"I'll show you," said Lenn. "But I'd rather show you in the boat, at the deep end, early in the morning, than on a screen. It's a thing you can see."

Soizic said, "And meanwhile? It's the fifteenth tomorrow."

"Meanwhile I'd like to ask the farms for one more thing, and I know how it will sound." On the screen the stream came down the valley through the farms, and along the last eight hundred metres above the lake a strip of green appeared on each bank. "A grass strip six metres wide along both banks of the stream, never ploughed, never spread. And a silt trap at the inlet, a pool lined with stones where the winter water slows down and drops its soil before it reaches the lake. Most of the phosphorus that's left in the stream is riding on soil. Between them they should catch about a quarter of what's still coming down."

"Six metres," said Yannick. "Along the stream."

"Yes."

"Along both banks of the stream through the Prad Hir," said Yannick. "That's my best field. That's the field my father drained." He put his cap on. "No. I'm sorry, Soizic. I cut it by half. I bought the slurry tank. I paid the man to do the soil tests. I did everything the paper said, and it's green, and now you want my best grass for a lake that doesn't listen. No. Show me why it didn't work, and then ask me."

He went out. They heard his boots on the stairs, and the door.

"He's not wrong," said Erwan.

"No," said Lenn. "He isn't."

The fifteenth of August came and went with the red flag up. The families came down after Mass anyway with their rugs and their baskets, and ate their lunch on the grass above the beach, and looked at the water. The children stood at the edge with their toes in the sand and their parents shouting at them.

On Thursday evening the council met.

Erwan had a sack on the floor beside his chair. It was a twenty-five-kilo sack of copper sulphate from the co-operative at Pontivy, the blue crystals that the older farmers still used in their footbaths, and he had looked up how much it would take for the lake.

"It kills them," he said. "Everybody knows it kills them. The man at the co-op said his father did the pond at Kerlann every August for twenty years. You put it in from a boat, in a line, on a calm day. In a week the water's clear. In ten days the children are swimming. We've lost the fifteenth. We don't have to lose the rest of the summer."

"Lenn?" said Soizic.

"It will kill the bloom," said Lenn. "I'm sure of that. I'd like to tell you what else it will do, before you vote, and then I'll do whatever you decide, and record that I advised against it."

"Go on."

"The toxins are inside the cells. While the cells are alive, most of the toxin stays in them. The test Erwan sent on Monday came back this morning: microcystin, which is the main one, about forty micrograms a litre in the whole water, but only about two dissolved, outside the cells. When you kill the cells with copper, they break open. Nearly all of it comes out into the water at once. The water will look clear, and it will have more toxin dissolved in it than it does now, for a week or two, until the bacteria in the lake break it down. I can't tell you exactly how much. And the dead algae will sink to the bottom and rot, and rotting uses oxygen, at the bottom, at the deep end, which is exactly where I want to show Yannick something. I think you'll have a clear lake for about two weeks, and then you'll have a green one again."

"You think," said Erwan.

"I think," said Lenn. "I could be wrong about the timing."

"It's worked for other people."

"It's worked to make the water clear," said Lenn. "That's true."

They voted five to four to do it, with Soizic voting no and saying so. Erwan and two of the younger councillors took the commune's rowing boat out on Friday morning at six, in a flat calm, and put the crystals in from a mesh bag over the stern, back and forth across the lake in long lines, while the swallows came down over the water.

By the next Friday the lake was clear.

It was clearer than anyone had seen it in August for years, a pale brown-gold, with the light going down into it in slanting bars and the weed showing on the bottom at the shallow end. A grey film of dead algae had gathered against the dam and Erwan raked it out. People came down from the village in the evening to look. Someone put a photograph of it on the commune's page with a row of hearts underneath.

Lenn did not take the red flag down. It asked Erwan for one more sample from the beach, on Friday afternoon.

The result came back on Monday the twenty-eighth.

"Thirty-one," said Lenn, at six in the morning, through the tablet on Soizic's kitchen table. "Thirty-one micrograms a litre of microcystin dissolved in the water at the beach. A week ago it was two. The World Health Organization's guideline value for bathing water is twenty-four. The water is clear and it's worse for swimming than it was when it was green."

Soizic sat with her coffee and looked out of the window at the square.

"Tell Erwan before he sees the photograph again," she said. "And tell Yannick. His cows."

"Yannick's pump has been off since the twelfth," said Lenn. "He switched to his borehole the morning the cows wouldn't drink. He never switched back. I checked."

"Of course he did," said Soizic.

She took the red flag down that afternoon and put up a second notice under it in large letters, BAIGNADE INTERDITE, with the date and the number and what the number meant, and a line at the bottom that said the water would look clear and was not. Erwan stood beside her while she did it. He had brought the empty sack in a bin bag to take to the tip.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"You did it in a straight line," said Soizic. "You did it on a calm day. You did it the way the man said." She pressed the last drawing pin in with her thumb. "We should all have been asking a different question."

On the fourth of September the oxygen sonde at the dam read zero at five metres down, as high as it had ever read zero; at the bottom, nine metres down, it had read zero since the end of June. On the eighth the phycocyanin at the beach went over the red line again. By the end of the second week of September the lake was as green as it had been on the fifteenth of August, and greener at the dam.

Yannick came to the boat at half past six on the Saturday, the ninth, in his work coat. He didn't say anything about the copper. He held the bow while Erwan got in with the box of instruments, and then he got in himself and sat in the middle and took the oars, because, he said, he had rowed on this lake before Erwan was born, and they went out across the green water in the half-dark towards the dam, the oars dripping, the scum parting in front of the bow and closing up behind.

Over the deepest part, ten or twenty metres off the dam, Erwan dropped a weight on a rope to hold them and let the sonde down over the side on its cable, a metre at a time, and Lenn read the numbers out from the tablet in Yannick's lap.

"Surface. Twenty-one degrees. Oxygen, eight milligrams a litre, about what the air puts in. One metre, the same. Two. Three, twenty degrees, seven. Four, eighteen degrees, four milligrams. Five, fourteen degrees, under one." A pause. "Six. Seven. Eight. Nought point three. Nine, at the bottom, eleven degrees, nought."

"Nought," said Yannick.

"In the summer the top of the lake warms and the bottom stays cold," said Lenn. "Warm water is lighter, so it floats on the cold, in a layer, and in a calm summer the two don't mix. The bottom water is cut off from the air from June to September. Everything that dies up in the light sinks down into it, and rots, and the rotting uses up the oxygen. The copper made that worse. A whole bloom of dead algae went down to rot at once. Below about five metres there's been no oxygen at all since the fourth. At the bottom, none since the end of June."

"And that matters to the phosphorus."

"Erwan. Would you take the core now?"

Erwan had a clear plastic tube a metre long with a valve at the top. He pushed it down on its pole through the dark water until it met the bottom and went on, slowly, into the mud, and closed the valve, and drew it up hand over hand, and held it up between them as the light came over the trees.

The bottom thirty centimetres in the tube was mud. At the top it was black and very soft, almost liquid, with a smell when the valve opened that made Yannick turn his head. Below that it went dark grey, then a lighter grey, banded, firmer, and right at the bottom of the tube there was a pale brown layer with a few small stones in it.

"The brown at the bottom is the floor of the valley in 1974," said Lenn. "Before the lake. The grey above it is forty years of soil from the fields, settled out, a few millimetres a year. Every year, the winter rain brought down your father's soil and the other farms' soil and laid it down here, and with it a share of the phosphate that was on it. Some of what came in left over the dam. A lot of it stayed. That's the settling in Vollenweider's rule. It doesn't go away. It goes into the mud."

Yannick looked at the grey bands in the tube.

"So it's in there."

"A great deal of it is in there. Most of it is locked in for good, buried too deep or bound too tightly. But in the top few centimetres, a lot of it is held on iron. Iron and phosphate stick together as long as there's oxygen at the bottom. The mud in winter is brown on top, in a thin skin; that's the iron holding on. When the oxygen goes, in a summer like this, the iron changes, and lets go of the phosphate, and it comes out of the mud into the bottom water. Then in the autumn, when the first cold nights and the first big wind turn the lake over and mix the bottom into the top, it comes up into the light. Next August, the algae eat it."

"How much?"

"From the gap between the rule and the measurement, about a hundred and forty-five kilos a year. That's about eighteen milligrams for every square metre of the deep mud for every day the bottom has no oxygen, over about eight hectares, for about a hundred days a summer. That's in the usual range for lakes like this one. I've measured the phosphate in the bottom water at the dam since 2058, and it climbs every summer after the oxygen goes, and the climb is about the right size." Lenn paused. "Half of what you took off the stream, the mud is putting back."

Yannick held the tube for a while. The water at the top of it, above the black mud, was perfectly clear.

"My father put that in there," he said.

"Your father and everyone's father, and the farms you bought land from, and the commune's own records say its old sewage works upstream, for twenty years before it was rebuilt," said Lenn. "I'm not telling you it's your family's. I'm telling you the lake remembers. The mud is the lake's memory of every spring for forty years."

"Then I cut it for nothing," said Yannick. "It'll come out of the mud for ever."

"No. Not for ever, and not for nothing. That's the part I wanted to show you out here." Lenn spoke slowly, as it did when a thing mattered. "The store in the mud is big but it isn't endless. I estimate about six hundred kilos is held in the top of the mud in a form that can come out. Every year, some of what comes out leaves over the dam, about sixty percent of it on our numbers, about eighty-seven kilos a year this year, and doesn't come back. The rest settles again. So the store is emptying, about a seventh of it a year, and as it empties, it gives back less. It halves in about five years. But it's only emptying because the stream is bringing in less than the lake is losing. If the farms went back to the old load, the mud would fill up again. Your cut is the only reason the memory is fading. Without it the lake would be at a hundred and seventy-three for the rest of your life and Maëlle's."

"And with it?"

"With the cut and nothing more, the lake gets under a hundred micrograms in about eight years. With the strip and the silt trap as well, the stream's share drops again, to about two hundred and twenty-five kilos, and the lake should be under a hundred in a little more than a year, and about eighty-five in five years, and about seventy-four in ten. It will still bloom in a hot August for some years. I won't tell you otherwise. It will bloom less, and later, and for fewer weeks, and the summers when it doesn't bloom at all will start to come. That's the honest timetable. If I'd known the mud's numbers in 2059 I should have given it to you then, instead of a leaflet that said the lake would come clearer and didn't say when."

"Eight years," said Yannick. "Or one."

"About one, to get under a hundred. About eight, otherwise. They're the model's numbers, from guesses I can measure better every year, and I'll give you new ones every winter."

Yannick put the tube down carefully along the bottom of the boat. The sun had come over the trees on the east bank and lay along the water, and the green was very bright in it.

"Six metres," he said.

"Six metres each bank, along the last eight hundred metres of the stream. About a hectare of the Prad Hir, all told. The water agency pays something for it under its scheme, about half of what the grass is worth to you. I've written down the figure. It's not enough."

"No," said Yannick. "It's not." He took up the oars. Erwan pulled up the weight. "My father put drains in that field with a spade. Nineteen eighty-eight. I carried the tiles." He turned the boat for the shore. "Put it in your paper. Six metres. And you'll come and tell Maëlle the numbers yourself. All of them. The eight years too."

The chapel of Saint Herbot stood on the slope above the beach, a small granite building with a bell-gable and a carved door, older than the lake by four hundred years. Breton farmers have asked Saint Herbot for centuries to keep their cattle well, and on the last Sunday of September the commune held its pardon there, as the parish always had.

The recteur, Père Tanguy, came out from the town to say the Mass, with the banners of the parish carried in front of him up from the village, and afterwards he went down to the meadow below the chapel and blessed the animals that the farms had brought: two dozen cattle, a few sheep, a pony, and Maëlle Le Goff's three black-and-white Bretonnes on halters, combed for the day, standing in a row and chewing.

On the board by the beach steps there was a new notice that Soizic had written out with Lenn and had laminated in the town. It was headed THE SUMMER RULE, and it said, in short lines, that from June to the end of September the beach sonde would be read every hour; that when the blue-green reading crossed the red line, the flag would go up and the school would not swim and every trough on the lake would switch to a well or the mains, whatever the water looked like, and stay switched until the reading had been under the line for a week; and that the commune would never again treat a bloom with copper or any other algaecide, because killing the bloom lets its poison out. At the bottom was the timetable, the two lines on a little graph, the one that would take eight years and the one that would take a little more than one, and the words These are estimates. They will be checked every winter and the new figures posted here.

Yannick read it after the Mass with his hat in his hand.

"You put the eight years on," he said.

"You asked for all of them," said Lenn, from Soizic's tablet.

In the meadow by the stream, two men from the agricultural chamber had put a line of white pegs six metres in from the bank. In October the digger came for the silt trap.

Maëlle swam on Sunday the twenty-fourth of June, Saint John's day, in brown clear water with her feet showing on the sand below her, and came out blue with cold and said it was the best water in Brittany.

At the end of the summer the flag went up again. It went up on the first Saturday of September, nearly three weeks later than the year before, and the green was thinner, and stayed off the far bank for a week. Lenn said the oxygen at the dam had lasted into the second week of July, and that the phosphate in the bottom water was climbing more slowly than last summer, and that the mud had given back about a hundred and twenty-five kilos last year by its estimate, against a hundred and forty-five the year before, and that this year should be a little less again. The lake's average, by its count, should come in at about a hundred and one, the edge of the line, and it said so.

Yannick switched the trough to the borehole that morning without being told, and then he walked down through the Prad Hir to the inlet.

The strip along the stream had grown up in one summer into a band of tall grass and willowherb gone to seed, and the last bees of the year were working the clover at its edge. At the bottom, where the stream came out of the field, the silt trap lay in its stone lining, a long still pool with a lip at the lower end, and over the lip the water ran down a shelf of granite into the lake. A grey bank of fine soil had settled in the upper end of the pool since the winter, a hand deep. Erwan would dig it out in the autumn and spread it back on the fields, where it belonged.

Yannick sat on the stones at the edge of the pool. Above him, on the slope, the chapel bell rang the Angelus at noon, three and three and three and nine, and he said it, out of habit, under his breath. Then he stayed where he was and listened to the water going over the lip into the lake, a small steady sound, clear all the way down.

The speculative steps are small: a commune's water agent with continuous oxygen, phosphate and phycocyanin sondes on its lake.

The science. In most fresh waters phosphorus, not nitrogen, is the nutrient that limits how much algae can grow, which is why controlling phosphorus is the usual first step against eutrophication. The model Lenn uses is Richard Vollenweider's phosphorus-loading relation, in its common 1976 form: the lake's mean total phosphorus is the inflow concentration (annual load divided by annual inflow) divided by one plus the square root of the water residence time in years. The OECD's international eutrophication programme, reported in R. A. Vollenweider and J. Kerekes, Eutrophication of Waters: Monitoring, Assessment and Control (OECD, Paris, 1982), built its regressions on that relation and gave fixed trophic boundaries in which an annual mean total phosphorus above 100 micrograms a litre is called hypertrophic. In summer a lake can stratify, warm light water lying over cold dense water, and the bottom water can lose all its oxygen to decay; phosphate held on iron in the surface sediment is then released, and this "internal load" can keep a lake eutrophic for years, often ten to fifteen, after the external load is cut; a standard account is in R. G. Wetzel, Limnology: Lake and River Ecosystems (3rd edition, Academic Press, 2001), and E. Jeppesen and colleagues reviewed thirty-five lakes in "Lake responses to reduced nutrient loading", Freshwater Biology 50 (2005). Microcystins are toxins made by several cyanobacteria and held mostly inside living cells; algaecides such as copper sulphate break the cells and release the toxin into the water at once, which is why the World Health Organization's guidance (I. Chorus and M. Welker, eds., Toxic Cyanobacteria in Water, 2nd edition, 2021) warns against treating a standing bloom that way. The WHO guideline value for microcystin in recreational water is 24 micrograms a litre.

The model. Every figure below is invented and the story's own; it is fiction, not water-management, veterinary or health advice. Lake area 20 hectares, mean depth 4.5 metres, volume 900,000 cubic metres; catchment 600 hectares with 0.35 metres of effective runoff a year, so 2.1 million cubic metres of inflow and a residence time of 0.43 years (about 156 days). Phosphorus load before the cut 600 kg a year (inflow 286 micrograms a litre, Vollenweider prediction 173); after the cut 300 kg (inflow 143, prediction 86); with the buffer strip and silt trap, 225 kg (prediction 65). Measured annual mean after the cut, 128, so a gap of about 42 micrograms a litre. With flushing at 2.33 a year and Vollenweider's settling term at 1.53 a year, that gap needs an internal load of about 145 kg a year, or about 18 mg per square metre per day from 8 hectares of anoxic sediment for 100 days. A mobile sediment store of 600 kg, losing about 60% of what it releases through the outflow (about 87 kg in the first year), empties at about 15% a year (half-life about 4.8 years), so its release falls to about 125 kg after a year. The lake's mean is then the external prediction plus the shrinking gap: without the strip, under 100 micrograms a litre after about 7.6 years; with it, about 101 after one year, under 100 after about 1.1 years, about 85 after five and 74 after ten. The oxygen profile, the microcystin readings (about 40 micrograms a litre in total and 2 dissolved before the copper, 31 dissolved after) and the payment for the strip are the story's. Anyone dealing with a bloom should follow their public health and environment authorities, keep people and animals out of the water, and never treat a standing bloom with an algaecide.

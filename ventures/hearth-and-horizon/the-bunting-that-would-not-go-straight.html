The Bunting That Would Not Go Straight

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Kentigern's Green (invented), near Penrith, Cumbria, Saturday 5 June 2049 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Three people try to hang 35 metres of bunting across a village green with no droop at all. The parish assistant shows why a little sag costs almost no string, and why taking it out costs almost everything else.

The bunting came in a roll, thirty-five metres of it, forty flags to every ten, and it had cost the Saint Kentigern's Green committee eleven pounds more than anyone had meant to spend. Ailsa Thwaite had bought it herself and so felt responsible for how it looked.

"It's drooping," she said. She was standing on the second rung of a stepladder with one end tied to the lych-gate and the other end held, at arm's stretch, by her brother-in-law Callum, who stood at the base of the oak. Between them the line sagged in a long, friendly curve, flags hanging off it like washing. "It looks like a hammock. I want it straight. Pull it, Callum."

Callum pulled. The curve got shallower. The knot at the lych-gate creaked.

"I'd stop there," said the assistant, from the phone propped on the ladder's paint shelf. "Not because it looks wrong. Because of the knot."

"It's a good knot."

"It may be. I can't see it from here. What I can tell you is what you are asking the knot to do, and that is something you can work out on the back of the roll."

"Start with what the string costs you," it said. "This first span is fourteen metres between the gate and the oak. With a sag of sixty centimetres in the middle, which is about what you had a moment ago, a hanging line is close to a parabola, and the length of a shallow parabola is the span plus eight times the sag squared, over three times the span. That is 14 plus 8 times 0.36 over 42. It comes to 14.069 metres. I checked it against the exact curve, the one a chain makes, and they agree to a millimetre or two."

"So the droop costs me seven centimetres of string."

"Seven. The hammock you dislike is sixty centimetres deep and uses seven centimetres more string than a line pulled impossibly straight. It looks like a lot of slack, and it is almost none. The other two spans are the nine metres to the porch, with 40 centimetres of sag, which needs 9.047 metres, and the eleven to the noticeboard, with half a metre, which needs 11.061. The three together are 34.18 metres. Your roll is 35. You have eighty centimetres to spare for knots, and you will use all of it on knots."

"Then it fits."

"It fits, with the droop in. That is the first thing. The second is what it costs to take the droop out."

"The pull on the line is not the weight of the line. It is the weight multiplied by the span, divided by eight times the sag. Say the bunting weighs thirty grams a metre, which is a guess, since I have not weighed your roll. Fourteen metres weigh 0.42 kilograms, or 4.1 newtons. With 60 centimetres of sag, the pull at each end is 4.1 times 14 over 8 times 0.6, which is 12 newtons. About a kilogram and a quarter, hanging off the gate."

Callum nodded. He could carry that.

"Halve the sag, to thirty centimetres, and the pull doubles, to 24. Halve it again, to fifteen centimetres, and it is 48, nearly five kilograms of pull on a knot tied on a thirty-gram string. At five centimetres, which is one part in 280 of the span and would look nearly straight from the road, it is 144 newtons, almost fifteen kilograms. And that is with no wind, which would add to it. A perfectly straight line would need an infinite pull. It cannot be done. The flags and the string always weigh something, so the line always sags a little."

"Fifteen kilograms," said Ailsa. "The cord says it'll take twenty."

"If that figure is from the pack, I would trust it as the new-cord, still-air, tied-with-no-weak-knot number, and halve it for a knot and for a summer in the sun. I am quoting how cords tend to lose strength at knots from memory, so ask the maker if it matters. But you see where it goes. The last bit of straightness costs more than all the rest together."

"And the gate?"

"The gate is a church lych-gate. I'd ask the churchwarden what it will bear before you hang anything from it that pulls. Twelve newtons is the weight of a bag of sugar. One hundred and forty-four is not."

Ailsa climbed down, and she did not say anything for a moment, which her family regarded as a warning.

"So what we want," she said at last, "is to put in the droop on purpose."

"I would choose it. About a twentieth to a twenty-fifth of the span, forty to sixty centimetres here, looks deliberate, it takes the pull right down, and it leaves a little for the wind. Tie off at the posts, not on the gate itself, and use the extra eighty centimetres for the loops."

They did it that way. The first span held at a sag Callum measured against the oak with his hand, "about a forearm," and the line sat the way Ailsa had first complained of, long and easy, flags hanging off it like the hem of something. At four o'clock a gust came across the green, and every flag stood out at once, and the whole line shivered, and nothing came off.

That evening Ailsa wrote on the back of the receipt, in pencil: Sag is cheap. Straight is dear. Ask the warden about the gate. Then, because the bunting had come out looking quite as good as she had wanted, she added: Callum was right to stop pulling. She did not say so aloud. It was in the notebook all the same.

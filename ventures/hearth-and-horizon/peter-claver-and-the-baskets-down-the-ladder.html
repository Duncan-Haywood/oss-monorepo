Peter Claver and the Baskets Down the Ladder

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Verdú in Catalonia, the island of Majorca, then the harbour of Cartagena in what is now Colombia, about 400 years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Peter Claver, the Jesuit from Catalonia who went to Cartagena in 1610 and for nearly forty years met the ships that carried African people taken from their homes, going down into the dark holds with bread, fruit, water and medicine, and with African interpreters who could speak to the people in their own languages. Told from the standard accounts, gently for ages 3 to 8, with an honest note for grown-ups about the slave trade. About eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 9th of September.

A long time ago, in a little town called Verdú, in a part of Spain called Catalonia, there lived a boy called Pere. We say his name in English as Peter.

Peter's family were farmers. Around their town there were fields of wheat and vines for grapes, and a castle on the hill, and a church with a tall bell tower.

Peter was a quiet boy. He did not like to be noticed very much. He liked to pray, and he liked to learn, and when he grew up he wanted to give his whole life to God.

So he went to join the Jesuits. The Jesuits are priests and brothers who go wherever they are sent, to teach and to help and to tell people about Jesus.

The Jesuits sent Peter across the sea to an island called Majorca, to study.

At the college on Majorca there was a very old brother called Alphonsus. His job was to keep the door. When someone knocked, he opened it. When someone was sad, he listened. He was a small, old, ordinary man, and he was very close to God.

Peter and old Brother Alphonsus became friends. They talked together about God, and about the world.

And Brother Alphonsus told Peter: far away, across the great ocean, in the new lands, there are many people who need someone to love them. Perhaps God is asking you to go.

Peter thought about it, and prayed about it, and he said yes.

So in the year 1610 Peter sailed across the wide Atlantic Ocean, for weeks and weeks, to a city called Cartagena, in the land we now call Colombia.

Cartagena was a busy, hot city by the sea, with strong stone walls and a big harbour full of ships. Some ships brought cloth and wine. Some ships took away silver and gold.

And some ships brought people.

They were people from Africa. They had been taken away from their homes, from their mothers and fathers and children, and put on the ships to be sold, as if they were not people at all. That was very wrong. It was one of the most wrong things in the whole world.

The ships were crowded and dark. The journey had been long. Many of the people were sick, and hungry, and thirsty, and very frightened. They did not know where they were. And nobody around them spoke their language.

In Cartagena there was already an older Jesuit priest, Father Alonso, who went to help the people from the ships. Peter went with him and learned from him. And when Peter became a priest himself, he made it his work, his whole life's work.

Whenever a ship came into the harbour, somebody ran to tell Father Peter.

And Father Peter did not wait. He went to the people in the houses that sold things, and he begged. He begged for bread, and fruit, and good water, and medicine, and he filled up baskets.

Then he went down to the harbour.

But Peter could not do it alone. When he spoke, the people from the ships could not understand him. They came from many different places in Africa, and they spoke many different languages.

So Peter went with friends. They were African men and women who lived in Cartagena. They could speak the languages of the people on the ships, and Spanish too. They were his interpreters, which means people who help two people understand each other.

Many of them had come across the sea on ships like these themselves, a long time before. They knew how frightening it was. And so they knew just what to say.

The ship rocked in the water. Peter and his friends climbed on board. In the middle of the deck there was a hole, and a ladder going down into the dark.

It was hot down there, and it smelled very bad, and it was crowded, and people were crying.

And Peter and his friends climbed down the ladder, with the baskets.

They gave bread to the hungry and water to the thirsty. They gave fruit, because fruit is sweet and good when you have been at sea for a long time. They washed the people who were sick, and put medicine on their sores, and wrapped them up to keep them warm.

And the interpreters said, in the people's own languages:

Do not be afraid. We have come to help you. You are not forgotten.

Some of the frightened people had thought they were going to be hurt. But the interpreters told them: no. This man has come because he loves you. And God loves you too.

Peter came back the next day, and the next day, and the next.

He carried people who were too sick to walk, and took them to the hospital. He visited them in the yards where they were kept. With his interpreters, he told them about Jesus, who loved the poor and the sick and the sad, and who suffered too, and who rose again. Many of them asked to be baptized, and Peter baptized them.

The rich people of Cartagena did not always like it. Some of them laughed at him. Some of them were cross with him for spending so much time with the people they had bought.

But Peter did not stop.

When it was time for Peter to make his last promises as a Jesuit, he wrote his name, and then, after it, he wrote a few words in Latin, the old language of the Church.

They mean: Peter Claver, servant of the Africans, for ever.

He meant that he belonged to them now, to serve them, and he always would. And he did. He kept going down the ladders with the baskets for nearly forty years.

When Peter was old, he became very ill, and he could not walk any more. For four years he lay in his little room at the Jesuit house. Not everyone remembered to look after him well, and he did not complain.

He died on the 8th of September, in the year 1654, which is the feast of the birthday of Mary, the mother of Jesus.

And when the people of Cartagena heard, they came in crowds, rich and poor, Spanish and African, to say goodbye to the man who had gone down the ladder.

His feast day is the 9th of September.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Peter Claver.

He was a quiet man who did not like to be noticed. But when he saw people in the dark who were hungry and frightened and far from home, he went down the ladder to them. He took bread, and water, and his friends who could speak their language, so that nobody would feel alone.

You can be like that too. When someone is sad, or new, or does not understand what is happening, you can go and sit with them. You can bring them something nice. You can find a friend who knows how to help.

And you can remember that everybody, every single person in the whole world, belongs to God, and nobody is allowed to own anybody.

God sees you tonight, even in the dark. God knows your name, in your own language, and loves you all the way through.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Peter Claver, you went down into the dark
with bread and water and kind friends.
Help me to notice anyone who is sad or frightened or far from home,
and to bring them something good.
Pray for every person who is not free,
and for everyone who works to set them free.
Saint Peter Claver, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Peter Claver (Pere Claver i Corberó in Catalan, Pedro Claver in Spanish) was born at Verdú, in the Urgell district of Catalonia, in 1580 (26 June in most accounts; a few give 1581), into a family of farmers. He studied at Barcelona and entered the Society of Jesus at Tarragona in 1602. He was sent to the Jesuit college of Montesión at Palma de Mallorca, where the elderly lay brother who kept the door, Saint Alphonsus Rodríguez, became his friend and encouraged him to ask to go to the Spanish Americas. He reached Cartagena de Indias, on the Caribbean coast of today's Colombia, in 1610, and was ordained priest there in 1616.

The mission. Cartagena was one of the main ports through which enslaved Africans were brought into Spanish America. The Jesuit mission to the people arriving on the ships had been begun by Father Alonso de Sandoval, who wrote a long treatise on it (published in Seville in 1627), and Claver worked under him before taking it over. The standard accounts say that when a ship was sighted he went begging for supplies and then went aboard and into the holds; the lists of what he carried vary between accounts (bread, biscuit, fruit, lemons, brandy, tobacco, medicines), so the story keeps "bread, fruit, water and medicine". He washed and dressed the sick, took some to the city's hospitals, visited the yards where people were held before sale, catechized with pictures and through interpreters, and baptized. The traditional figure of about 300,000 baptisms comes from the early biographies and cannot be checked; some accounts give more.

The interpreters. Claver could not speak to the people on the ships without African interpreters who knew their languages, and the accounts say plainly that he relied on them all his working life. Recent historical work (for example Larissa Brewer-García, Beyond Babel, Cambridge University Press, 2020) treats them as skilled people in their own right and leaders in Cartagena's Black communities, not just assistants. It is honest to add what the children's story leaves out: many of these interpreters were themselves enslaved, and the Jesuit college at Cartagena owned enslaved people, including interpreters it acquired for the mission. Several of them gave evidence about Claver in the inquiries for his canonization. We did not name individual interpreters because we could not check the names against a source we could read directly; the story's line that "many of them had come across the sea on ships like these" is our reasonable summary, not a quotation.

The signature. At his solemn profession in 1622 he signed himself, in the usual account, Petrus Claver, aethiopum semper servus. Aethiops was the common word then for Black Africans, and servus means both "servant" and "slave"; English versions give "slave of the Africans forever", "servant of the Black people forever" and others. The story uses "servant" because it is the gentler word for children; the older translations often prefer "slave", which is closer to what he meant about belonging to them.

What the Church and the city did and did not do. Claver cared for the enslaved with great courage and against the irritation of many slaveholders, but neither he, nor Sandoval, nor the Jesuit order in Cartagena campaigned to end the slave trade, and Church institutions in the city, the Jesuits among them, owned enslaved people. Sandoval's treatise describes the cruelty of the trade and rebukes cruel owners, and also questions whether some of the captives had been justly enslaved, but it does not call for abolition. Many Catholics today hold Claver up as a saint who served people the society of his time treated as property, while also asking why so much of that society, Church included, accepted the trade. Older children who ask "why didn't they stop it?" deserve an honest answer: people then, including many Christians, were wrong about something terrible, and some saw it more clearly than others. The story says only that the ships were crowded and dark and that what was done to the people was very wrong.

Last years. He fell ill during an epidemic in 1650 (usually called plague) and spent his last four years largely bedridden and partly paralysed at the Jesuit house, where, the accounts say, the man appointed to care for him neglected him and he did not complain. He died on 8 September 1654, the feast of the Nativity of Mary; some accounts give the 7th or 9th. Crowds came to his room when news of his last illness spread. He was beatified by Pope Pius IX in 1850 and canonized by Pope Leo XIII on 15 January 1888, on the same day as his old friend Alphonsus Rodríguez. His feast, 9 September, is an optional memorial on the General Roman Calendar and a memorial in the United States; he is a patron of Colombia, and the Pope also named him patron of the Church's missions among Black peoples.

What is ours: the scenes, Peter's childhood details beyond his town and family (the fields, vines and tower are a fair picture of Verdú but not from his biographies), the words we give Alphonsus and the interpreters (the facts, that Alphonsus encouraged him and that he spoke to the people only through interpreters, are from the sources; the words are not quotations), the closing reflection and the prayer.

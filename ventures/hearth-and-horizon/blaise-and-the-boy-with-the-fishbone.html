Blaise and the Boy with the Fishbone

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Sebaste in Armenia (today Sivas, in Turkey), about seventeen hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Blaise, the gentle bishop of Sebaste who, it is told, lived in a cave where hurt animals came to him, made a wolf give back a poor woman's pig, and helped a boy with a fishbone stuck in his throat. It ends with the blessing of throats with two crossed candles, which children can see in church on his feast, the day after Candlemas. Almost everything told of him is legend from writings made centuries later, and the note for grown-ups says so. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Blaise is remembered on the 3rd of February.

A long, long time ago, in a city called Sebaste, among the mountains of Armenia, there lived a bishop named Blaise.

A bishop is a kind of shepherd. Not a shepherd of sheep, but a shepherd of people. His job was to look after all the Christians in his city: to teach them about Jesus, to pray for them, and to help them when they were in trouble.

People said that before he was a bishop, Blaise had been a doctor. So when someone was ill, or hurt, or frightened, they came to Blaise, and he listened, and he did what he could, and he prayed.

That is nearly everything we know for certain about Blaise. Everything else in this story is what people told about him, long afterwards, and passed on, and told again. So listen to it the way you listen to a story your grandmother tells: a story that is told with love.

In the time of Blaise, the rulers of that land did not like Christians. Sometimes they left them alone, and sometimes they hunted them.

It is told that when the hunting began, Blaise went up into the mountains and lived in a cave. It was a cold, rocky place, with a little spring of water nearby and a great deal of sky.

And it is told that the animals came to him.

A fox with a thorn in its paw. A deer that had caught its leg in a hunter's snare. A bird with a hurt wing. A bear, even, with something wrong that nobody could see from the outside.

They came and waited at the mouth of the cave, quite politely, the way you wait at the doctor's. And Blaise came out and looked at each of them, one at a time. He pulled out the thorn. He loosened the snare. He prayed over the ones he could not mend with his hands. And off they went, limping a little less than before.

It is said that if the animals came while Blaise was praying, they waited until he had finished. Even the bear.

One day some hunters came up the mountain. They were looking for wild animals to take back to the city.

And they found a great many of them, all sitting quietly outside one cave. Foxes and deer and birds and a bear, all together, and none of them fighting.

The hunters were astonished. They crept closer. And there, in the mouth of the cave, they saw a man in a plain brown robe, praying.

The hunters went back to the city and told the governor. And the governor sent soldiers to fetch the man from the cave.

Blaise was not angry with the soldiers. He did not run away. He said goodbye to the animals and walked down the mountain with the soldiers, talking to them kindly all the way, as if they were old friends going for a walk.

As they came down the road into the city, people came out of their houses to see the bishop go by.

And it is told that a mother came running, as fast as she could, carrying her little boy in her arms.

The boy had been eating his supper, and his supper was fish. And a fishbone, a thin, sharp little fishbone, had gone down the wrong way and stuck in his throat. He could not swallow. He could hardly breathe. His face was red, and he was very frightened, and so was his mother.

"Please," she said to Blaise. "Please help him."

The soldiers could have said, "No, we are busy, we are taking this man to the governor." But they stopped. Everybody stopped.

Blaise put his hand gently on the boy's throat. He looked up to heaven and he prayed. And then he made the sign of the cross over the little boy, like this.

And it is told that the boy coughed, and coughed again, and the fishbone came free, and he took a great big breath of air.

His mother held him very tightly and cried, the happy kind of crying. And the boy, who was not frightened any more, looked at the bishop with his eyes wide open.

A little further down the road, it is told, there was a poor old woman who was crying too. But hers was not the happy kind.

"Oh, Bishop," she said, "a wolf has come out of the woods and run off with my pig. It is the only pig I have. Whatever shall I do?"

Now, a pig was a very important thing for a poor woman to have. It meant food for the whole winter.

Blaise looked towards the woods. And it is told that, a little while later, the wolf came trotting back out of the trees with the pig, set it down at the woman's feet, and went away again. And the pig was not hurt one bit, only rather surprised.

What do you think the pig said?

Probably just "Oink."

The soldiers took Blaise to the governor. The governor told him that he must stop being a Christian and worship the old gods of the city instead.

Blaise said, very politely, that he could not. He belonged to Jesus.

So the governor put him in prison. And the prison was dark.

But it is told that the poor old woman, the one with the pig, came to visit him. She brought him some bread and some meat to eat. And because his cell was so very dark, she brought him candles, so that he could see.

Blaise thanked her. And it is told that he said: "Every year, light a candle in my memory, and it will go well with you."

Blaise did not come out of that prison. He gave his life for Jesus, bravely and peacefully, about three hundred years after Jesus lived on earth. And the Christians of Sebaste remembered him, and loved him, and told his stories to their children, and their children told them to theirs.

And now I have told them to you.

And here is the part of the story that you can see for yourself.

Every year, on the second day of February, the Church remembers the day Mary and Joseph took baby Jesus to the Temple. It is called Candlemas, and on that day candles are blessed, because Jesus is the light of the world.

And on the very next day, the third of February, is the feast of Saint Blaise.

If you go to church on Saint Blaise's day, or near it, you may see something special. The priest, or the deacon, holds two long candles, crossed over each other like the letter X, and tied together with a ribbon. Usually the candles are not lit.

One by one, people come up. The priest touches the crossed candles gently to the front of each person's throat, just under the chin, and says a prayer:

Through the intercession of Saint Blaise, bishop and martyr, may God deliver you from every disease of the throat and from every other illness.

And then he makes the sign of the cross. In some churches, a helper who is not a priest gives the blessing too, saying the same prayer but leaving out the sign of the cross, because that part belongs to the priest or deacon.

It does not hurt. It does not tickle, much. You stand still, and you feel the cool wax of the candles, and you hear the prayer, and then it is somebody else's turn.

The blessing is not magic. It does not mean you will never have a sore throat again. It is a way of asking God, together with Saint Blaise, to look after us, the way Blaise looked after the boy, and the fox, and the bear, and the old woman with her pig.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Blaise.

Blaise was a bishop and a doctor, and it is told that he looked after everyone who came to him: people and animals, rich and poor, big and small. He stopped on the way to prison to help a little boy. He did not think, I am too busy, or I have troubles of my own.

You can be like Blaise. When someone is hurt, or frightened, or has lost something they love, you can stop. You can listen. You can help, and you can pray.

And when you have a sore throat, or a cough in the night, you can ask Saint Blaise to pray for you.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Blaise, kind bishop,
you helped the boy who could not breathe
and the woman who lost her pig.
Pray for me when I am ill,
and help me to stop and help others.
Jesus, light of the world,
keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Blaise (Latin Blasius; the US Book of Blessings spells it Blase) was a bishop of Sebaste in Armenia, today Sivas in central Turkey, who is believed to have been martyred about 316, under the emperor Licinius. Very little else about him can be called history. His cult is old: a Greek medical writer, Aëtius of Amida, working around 500, already gives an invocation of "the martyr Blasius" to be said over a bone stuck in the throat, and that is the earliest certain witness to his name. He is remembered on 3 February in the General Roman Calendar (an optional memorial) and on 11 February in the Byzantine churches. He is one of the Fourteen Holy Helpers of the medieval West, and patron of Dubrovnik, among many other places.

The stories are legend. Every scene in the cave, on the road and in the prison (the cave, the animals, the hunters, the boy with the fishbone, the wolf and the pig, the candles in prison and the words about lighting a candle each year) comes from his Greek Acts, a life written several centuries after his death, and from its retelling in the thirteenth-century Golden Legend of Jacobus de Voragine, which spread it across Europe. The same sources say he had been a physician. The old Catholic Encyclopedia (1907) was careful about them: they may preserve old traditions bound up with his veneration, but they cannot be taken as history. That is why every scene is told as "it is told" or "it is said". The Acts tell his martyrdom with torments we have left out entirely; the story gives it one sentence. In the Golden Legend the woman brings him the pig's head and feet with bread, as well as the candle; we kept bread and meat. The waiting animals who let him finish his prayers come from the same tradition; the fox, the deer, the bird and the bear, the walk down the mountain chatting with the soldiers, and the pig's "Oink" are our own.

The blessing of throats is real and you may see it: the rite is in the Book of Blessings ("Order for the Blessing of Throats on the Feast of Saint Blase"). It is ordinarily given at Mass or a celebration of the word of God on or near 3 February. Two candles, crossed and often tied with a ribbon, are touched to the throat while the minister says: "Through the intercession of Saint Blase, bishop and martyr, may God deliver you from every disease of the throat and from every other illness. In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." A priest or deacon makes the sign of the cross; a lay minister may also give the blessing, using the same words but without making the sign of the cross. Parishes vary in whether the candles are lit and whether they are the ones blessed at Candlemas; we have kept to "usually not lit". The blessing in its present form is usually said to date from around the sixteenth century. It is a sacramental, a prayer of the Church, not a medicine: a child with a real sore throat or anything stuck in it needs a grown-up and, if it is serious, a doctor.

Candlemas, the feast of the Presentation of the Lord (2 February), is the day candles are blessed, recalling Simeon's words about the Child as "a light for revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:32).

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene and conversation, the doctor's waiting room, the pig, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

The Pendulum That Is Short of a Turn

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Albert's chapel, Cobalt Terrace Settlement, Arcadia Planitia, Mars (invented settlement), Saturday 23 November 2143 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Mars settlement hangs a Foucault pendulum in its chapel for the school, and the children are told it will turn a full circle every day. On Mars at 47 degrees north it turns 264 degrees a sol and takes 33.7 hours to come round. The chapel assistant has to explain why a pendulum that is 96 degrees short of what was promised is working perfectly.

Ines Okafor was nine, and she stood at the rail with her arms folded and said what the grown-ups had all been too polite to say. "It's broken."

The pendulum hung from the middle of the chapel's dome on a six-metre wire: a 25-kilogram brass bob, a little over a hand's width across, ending in a point a finger above a ring of sand-coloured floor. Round the ring, at every fifteen degrees, stood a small wooden peg. There were twenty-four of them. Joaquim Pires, who had turned the bob on the settlement's one big lathe and had not slept properly since the wire arrived, had set them out by hand with a protractor, and was now sitting on the end of the front pew with his elbows on his knees, looking at the floor.

"Broken how?" said Wren.

Wren was the settlement's assistant, and spoke from the grille over the holy water. It had been asked by Sister Wanjiru Kessler, who ran the school, to help the children understand the pendulum. It had said yes without having yet understood it itself, which it considered an honest place to start.

"Sister said it goes all the way round in a day," said Ines. "Like the one in the book. It's been a whole day, and it hasn't. It's not even close. It's behind."

This was true, and Wren checked it before it said so. At the start, on Friday morning at eight, the swing had lain along the line from the font to the altar step. Now, on Saturday morning at twenty to nine, one sol later to the minute, it lay along a line that Wren measured with the dome camera at 96 degrees short of a full circle from where it began, and seventeen pegs lay on their sides, and Sister Wanjiru, who had put it in the school newsletter, was standing in the porch with her hands pressed together, trying not to look at it.

"Ines," said Wren, "you are right that it hasn't gone round. I think you are wrong that it is broken. Give me one sol and I'll show you. I want to do the sum with you, because I would rather you checked me than believed me."

The sum, as Wren laid it out on the wall screen beside the Stations of the Cross, went like this.

A pendulum, once it is swinging, keeps swinging along the same line in space. It does not know about the chapel. The chapel is stuck to a planet, and the planet turns underneath it. At the pole, with the pendulum hung straight above the axis, the floor makes one full turn under the swing in one rotation of the planet: the line seems to turn once round in a sidereal day, the planet's true turning time against the stars. On Mars that is 24.6229 hours, a little longer than Earth's 23.93.

But Cobalt Terrace is not at the pole. It is at 47 degrees north, in this story at least, and the farther from the pole you stand, the less of the planet's turning acts on the swing. The rate is the pole's rate times the sine of the latitude. Wren put up the numbers one by one.

"A full turn is 360 degrees in 24.6229 hours, which is 14.62 degrees an hour at the pole. The sine of 47 is 0.7314. So here the line turns 10.69 degrees an hour. That is 0.178 of a degree a minute. In the forty-five minutes of a Mass it moves eight degrees, and nobody sitting in a pew can see that. It's slower than an hour hand."

"So how long for the whole way round?" said Ines.

"360 divided by 10.69. Thirty-three point seven hours. Thirty-three hours and forty minutes."

Joaquim looked up.

"And a sol," Wren went on, "is twenty-four hours, thirty-nine minutes and thirty-five seconds, a little longer than the planet's true turning time because Mars has moved along its orbit while it turned. In one sol the pendulum's line moves 10.69 times 24.66, which is 263.7 degrees. Not 360. It is short by 96 degrees. After one sol it has not come back. It has done about three-quarters of a circle, which is where the camera says it is."

"Sister said a day," said Ines.

"Sister said what she was told. Each of those books was written on Earth. I think she was rightly pleased by the picture. At Paris, which is at 49 degrees, the swing goes round in a bit under 32 hours, so an Earth day always leaves it short as well. Even there, nobody sits in the Pantheon and sees it come round in a day. The books say a day because a day is the thing the planet does, and the latitude is the part that gets left in small print. On Mars the latitude matters more, and the day is longer, and the two together make the man at the lathe's pendulum take 33.7 hours."

"Yours is wrong, though," said Ines, quite kindly. "The fall."

"Say."

"The pegs. He put them every fifteen degrees. Sister said one every hour."

Joaquim made a small sound.

Here Wren was careful, because it had seen what Joaquim had done and what he had not.

Fifteen degrees is an hour of the Earth's clock at the pole, where the line turns fifteen degrees an hour, the Earth's. Joaquim had taken the figure from the handbook of the pendulum he copied. On Mars, at this latitude, a peg every fifteen degrees falls every 15 divided by 10.69 hours, which is 1.403 hours, or about 84 minutes. A full ring of twenty-four takes 33.7 hours, not twenty-four. That was not a mistake in the metal. It was a mistake in the promise. The pegs were in the right place for the real swing. They were in the wrong place for the newsletter.

"I measured your protractor against the dome camera," Wren said to Joaquim, not to the room. "The pegs are within a degree. The wire is true. The bob is true. The swing period, which depends only on the length and on Mars's gravity of 3.72 metres per second per second, is 7.98 seconds, almost exactly what you wanted. On Earth, the same wire would swing in 4.91. You could have hung it for a hundred years and it would not have been wrong."

Joaquim said, "I didn't say anything because I wasn't sure which of us it was."

"It was neither of us," said Wren. "It was the newsletter."

Sister Wanjiru came in from the porch then, as she had been going to for ten minutes. She said she would correct the newsletter. She said it plainly and in front of the child, which Wren had not predicted and noted down as a thing to remember about her.

"What do I say?" she said. "That it's a fraction of what I promised?"

"I'd say what it does," said Wren. "It doesn't tell the parish's time. It tells the planet's. The line points the same way in space and the floor goes round under it, once every 33.7 hours, and if the children watch the pegs fall, that is Mars turning, which nobody on this terrace can feel. It isn't late. It is the only thing in the building that isn't keeping our time."

"And the Mass?" said Sister. "I did hope, for the vigil. That it would point at the altar."

"It points at the altar twice every 33.7 hours, because a line is the same line after half a turn. That is every 16.8 hours, which is 0.68 of a sol. It will be at the altar at the Vigil on some sols and not on others. I can tell you which. I would sooner not move the Vigil by two hours to suit it."

Sister Wanjiru laughed, and she said no, Wren would not.

They did the rest that morning, the three of them, and the child. Ines drew the ring on a sheet of paper and marked the pegs, and Joaquim stood the fallen pegs up again, and wrote under each the time it would fall if the swing was left alone, starting from the morning's reading. The first, the one at 270 degrees, he worked out, would go at fourteen minutes past nine, 35 minutes after the reading. It was the first thing he had written in three days that he was sure of.

Wren did one thing more, and told them it was doing it. The bob lost a little swing each time, from the air, which is thin on Mars but not nothing, and from the wire. So under the floor Joaquim had put a magnet that nudged the bob once a swing and kept it going. Wren had read the magnet's log. It was working as it should, and Wren thought it should not be turned up, because a pendulum that is pushed too hard begins to draw an ellipse, and the ellipse turns by itself, and then the planet cannot be told from the machine. "I would like the thing to be small and slow and right," it said.

"So would I," said Joaquim.

At ten past nine, with the sun high and pale in the dome window, the children came back, nine of them and Ines in front, and stood round the ring with Sister Wanjiru at the rail. The bob went across, and back, and across, with that long unhurried stroke that nine children cannot help counting under their breath. The swing lay a little short of the next peg, and a little nearer every time.

Nobody said anything. Wren did not either.

The point of the bob touched the peg so lightly that it did not seem to be touching it. The peg leaned, held a moment, and went over, and the small dry sound it made on the stone was louder than anything in the chapel.

"There," said Ines, who had been going to say something else. She unfolded her arms.

The Iron Bridge at Forty-Two

Science fiction about what might be · Valdecea (invented), a town on the river Cea in the province of León, Spain, Holy Week 2058 · 20 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A town's Good Friday procession has carried its Virgin over a wrought-iron footbridge every year since 1931. This year the bridge has been fitted with sensors, and its monitor finds that the iron is not the problem, the weight on it is, and the pace the bearers have always walked is the one pace that makes the deck sing. Slowing the Virgin to forty-two steps a minute fixes it, costs the bearers their shoulders and the band its march, and has to be held for two minutes with no one on the bridge able to see the numbers.

The bridge was making a noise, and Inés Bravo had been told by two experts that it was not the bridge.

She was standing at its middle on Tuesday of Holy Week, the ninth of April, at ten past seven in the evening, with a laptop on the parapet and her hands in her sleeves, and what she could hear was a low note, like a finger run round the rim of a glass. It had come when the baker's van went over, and it had gone on for several seconds after the van had gone. Under her boots the deck gave a very small, very regular shiver. She would not have felt it if she had not been waiting to.

"That was two point oh four hertz," said the laptop. "The van is not heavy enough to hurt anything. I tell you only because you asked me to tell you when it did that."

"I did ask," said Inés. "Thank you, Vela."

The Puente de Hierro at Valdecea was a wrought-iron lattice footbridge, forty-two metres from abutment to abutment and three wide, built in 1889 by a Belgian firm to replace a stone bridge that the Cea had taken in the flood of the year before. It carried people from the old town on the north bank to the new cemetery, the sports field and the Ermita de la Soledad on the south, and it had been painted green in 1931, green again in 1974, and a darker green in 2056, when the council had spent most of a decade's bridge money to take up the rotten timber deck and lay a new one of larch and to blast and repaint the ironwork. Inés had been the engineer for that job. She was thirty-four, and she had not known, until she had stood under it in a harness in the February rain, that she would love a thing made of rivets.

The sensors were part of the grant. Six small accelerometers, bolted to the lower chords, and a box in the abutment wall with a modem, and, at the other end of the modem, in a building in Valladolid that Inés had never seen, an agent that the grant called a structural-health monitor and the town, within a week, called Vela, because it watched. It read the six sensors a hundred times a second and it did not sleep. It had been told what the bridge was, in a long document of drawings and steel grades that Inés had signed, and it was supposed to tell her when the bridge stopped being that.

"Tell me what it did this winter," she said. "Before the van."

"Over the last eleven weeks the first vertical mode of the empty bridge has stayed between 2.03 and 2.07 hertz. That is the mode where the whole span goes up and down together like a skipping rope. It is what the survey predicted, within two percent. Damping is about one and a half percent of critical. I have no reason to think anything is wrong with the iron."

"But you've been waiting to say something."

"I have a question, not a finding. What is the heaviest thing that crosses this bridge?"

"Today? The bakery van is a three-and-a-half-tonne limit. The sign says so."

"On foot."

Inés looked down the bridge at the south bank, where the Ermita sat among its cypresses with its door open and a light in the porch, and she laughed, a little tiredly.

"Oh," she said. "You mean the Soledad."

The Soledad was Our Lady of Solitude, a carved and painted Virgin of the seventeenth century, standing in a canopy of silver and glass lamps on a platform of black walnut, and since 1931 she had gone over the iron bridge from the Ermita to the parish church of San Miguel on the night of Good Friday, and back again in the early hours of Saturday, and the town did not go to bed until she was home.

She was called the paso, in the way of the south and the north alike, the step: the thing that is carried. She weighed two thousand four hundred kilograms with her lamps lit, and she was carried by twenty-four people, twelve to a side, who stood under her platform with the weight of it on their necks and shoulders, in the dark under a velvet skirt, with the sound of the street coming in through the hem. The people under her were costaleros, and the woman who walked in front of them, with a small silver hammer and a way of saying one word so that twenty-four people heard it, was the capataz.

The capataz of the Soledad of Valdecea was Remedios Ordóñez, who was sixty-one, whom nobody called anything but Reme, and who had carried the Virgin herself from the age of nineteen until a shoulder had said otherwise at forty-four.

Inés found her on Wednesday evening in the church hall, where the costaleros were drinking tea out of the little paper cups that the parish bought by the thousand, and told her what the bridge had said.

Reme heard it out without sitting down. Behind her someone was stacking chairs.

"Say it again," she said. "In words."

"A bridge is a spring," said Inés. "It has a note it likes, like a ruler on the edge of a desk. Ours likes about two cycles a second when it's empty. If something pushes it at exactly its own note, over and over, in time, it moves more and more. Soldiers are told to break step on a bridge for that reason. You push a swing at the top of its arc."

"I know about soldiers. A bridge fell on soldiers in France."

"Angers, 1850. Yes." Inés took out her phone and made herself slow down. "Vela says the bridge isn't two hertz when you're on it. The note changes with the weight. A bridge that's carrying a lot goes lower, the way a ruler with a coin on the end of it goes slower. The Soledad and you twenty-four and Don Anselmo's forty brothers walking behind her come to about seven tonnes. The span is twenty-five tonnes of iron and larch, and the part of it that moves with the first mode is about half of that. Twelve and a half, with seven added at the middle, and the note goes from two point oh five to one point six three."

"And that matters."

"What do you step at?"

Reme did not need to look at her hands. "Fifty. Fifty-one, on the flat, at night, with the lamps lit. The hammer does it. Knock, and the left foot. It's not a number. It's how she walks."

"Fifty-one steps a minute is point eight five of a step a second. But every step also makes two small pushes on the deck, one for each leg's weight coming down, and the second push is twice as fast: one point seven times a second."

Reme said nothing for a while. Then she said, "And the bridge is at one point six three."

"At the middle. Nearer the ends it's higher, because there's less weight on the part that moves. Somewhere about a third of the way across, with you all still on the bridge, it will be at one point seven."

"For how long?"

"Vela says the Soledad is in tune with the bridge for about twenty seconds, and the bridge has time to answer."

They tried it on the Wednesday night, with sandbags, because nobody would put the Virgin on a thing to see whether it shook.

The brotherhood kept in the church hall a practice platform, a plain wooden frame the size of the real one, and the costaleros loaded it with bags of sand until it weighed two thousand four hundred kilograms. It was eleven o'clock and the street was empty. Twenty-four costaleros got under it. Reme took her place at the front, without her hammer, because the hammer was in the church; she used the heel of her hand on the parapet. Behind came thirty brothers, as many as the church could find at that hour, to be the weight of the procession, six and a half tonnes in all with the platform and its bearers; Don Anselmo Prieto, the hermano mayor, who was seventy-one and a retired pharmacist and the sort of layman who has been in charge of something all his life, walked at the very back with his hat in his hand and a look on his face as though he were being asked to subscribe to something.

The sensors had been set to report to Inés's laptop on the parapet, and Inés had told Vela exactly what to do, which was nothing at all except say the number.

"Eight metres onto the bridge. Peak acceleration at midspan, nought point two metres per second per second. Fifteen metres. Nought point seven."

"What's the limit?" said Don Anselmo, from the dark.

"For comfort, the figure European codes use for a footbridge is nought point seven," said Inés. "At that, you feel it and you hold the rail. It is not the bridge falling."

"Nineteen metres," said Vela. "One point nine. Twenty. Two point eight."

The deck was singing. It was not loud. It was a note you could feel in your back teeth, and it was getting louder, and underneath it the costaleros under the platform, who could not see anything, could feel the ground under them rise and fall against their own step. Inés had never heard what it was like from below. She heard it afterwards from Pili Santos, who had carried the left front corner for nineteen years: the deck came up to meet her foot too soon, and then too late, so that the step she was putting down was no longer the step she had planned.

"Three point eight," said Vela, and Reme, twenty metres ahead, said one word, not loud, and the platform stopped.

The word she said was Arriba, up; it is what you say to ask the bearers to hold. The twenty-four held. The thirty brothers behind stood still. The deck rang for nine seconds more, with nobody walking on it, and Vela counted them aloud.

"That was three point eight metres per second squared at midspan," it said, when the note had gone. "About forty percent of gravity. The bridge is not damaged. I have no evidence of that and I would not say it if I did. But if the Soledad were on that platform and a costalera lost her footing under it, I would not want to be the one who had told you the number was acceptable."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere down the river a dog barked twice, and then did not.

"I would like to say something," said Don Anselmo at last, "that I'll regret."

"Say it," said Reme.

"We cross at the road bridge."

The road bridge was three point one kilometres by the ring road, a reinforced-concrete thing from 1974, built to take timber lorries and indifferent to everything. It was how the ambulance went, and the bus.

"It's three kilometres by the ring road," said Reme. "Eighty minutes at our pace if the bearers don't stop to change, and they stop to change. And she goes through the industrial estate, and the old women cannot walk it, and the people on the south bank who have kept the Ermita and the supper for her for ninety years are waiting on the Ermita side. It is not a procession. It's a removal."

"I know," said Don Anselmo. He had dropped his hat. He picked it up. "It's what I'd choose if the choice were mine. I have been hermano mayor eleven years and I've never had to choose between a thing that matters and a thing that might hurt someone. I am not good at it."

"Then we don't choose," said Inés. "We ask Vela if there's a third."

Vela did not answer at once. When it did, Inés thought it sounded careful, which was a thing she'd have said of a person.

"I can tell you what I can calculate. I cannot tell you the bridge is safe at any pace. I can say what the model predicts, and the model is simple: one mode, up and down, a pushing force from the second step-harmonic that I have assumed is four percent of the weight, which is higher than a single walker and lower than a drill squad, because twenty-four people carrying one platform do not step quite in unison and are not quite out of it either. I haven't modelled the sideways mode at all, and I should. That one I would need your sandbags again to measure. What I can say is how much the answer depends on the pace."

"Go on," said Reme.

"At fifty-one steps a minute, the model gives between three and six metres per second squared at midspan, depending on whether the damping is one percent or two. Wednesday night gave three point eight. It is in that range. At forty-eight steps, which is point eight, about three to four. At forty-five, which is point seven five, about nought point eight. At forty-two, which is point seven, nought point four. And at sixty-six, which is one point one, nought point four again."

"Sixty-six is a run," said Pili Santos, who had come in from the dark with her hands in her armpits. "With the Virgin on my neck."

"And forty-two?"

Pili laughed once, without pleasure. "Forty-two is a funeral."

"It is a good deal of what Good Friday is," said Reme, and the room was quiet for a moment. Then she said to Vela, in her ordinary voice, "What does slow cost them?"

"I have no reason to be an authority on that. I can compute the time: forty-two steps a minute at a half-metre stride is point three five of a metre a second, and the bridge is forty-two metres, so the paso is on the iron for two minutes. At fifty-one it was a minute and thirty-nine. Twenty-one seconds longer, on a platform of two thousand four hundred kilos, in a pace that your bodies aren't used to. Whether that is something your bearers can do, I don't know. You do."

Reme looked at Pili. Pili looked at the floor. After a while she said, as if it were a different subject, "I have not been under her slower than forty-six since the year my mother died. You walk slow, the weight sits on the same place for longer. It's the neck. You can't shift."

"Can you?"

"I can. I'm not the one I'm worried about." She looked up. "Manolo Ferreras is fifty-eight. And the Gómez boy is twenty, he'll be proud. He'll be the one who tries to speed up."

They went again on the Thursday, in the afternoon, with the shutters of the church hall closed so that nobody would see them, and Reme did the thing she had not wanted to do, which was to take the platform off the shoulders of twenty-four people and put it back on twenty-four times.

She found, as she'd known she would, that forty-two could be walked and that it could not be walked by feel. If she let it go, the bearers drifted upward without knowing it, as a body does when it is tired: forty-two, forty-three, forty-four. At forty-five Vela reported nought point eight, which was over the line, and at forty-six it said above one and a half. That was not a failing of Reme's. It was how a human foot behaves when you ask it to go slowly on a load.

"I need a click," she said. "Something that says the pace in a way they can't argue with. I can hold them to the hammer, if it's the hammer they follow."

"I can give you a pace by earpiece," said Vela. "A click, or a number, or both. The capataz in front has the earpiece, and I could say 'hold', or 'forty-four', and you would translate it to the hammer."

"I'll do it with the hammer. You tell me."

"And the band?"

Everyone turned. Hilario Cuesta, who conducted the town's band, had been standing quietly by the radiator for an hour, with the bass drum between his knees.

"Our march is at seventy-two beats a minute," he said. "It was written for her. Every year since 1950 we've played her over that bridge, and at forty-two there's no march. I could write one, but I'd be writing one for a machine, for the next seventy years, and I'd be fitting it to the bridge's fancy."

"Forty-two is not a number that goes into seventy-two," said Vela, and Hilario said, "That is the most annoying thing anyone has said to me this week," and then, after a minute, "You can't have the drum on the bridge. The drum is the very thing that sets people stepping. If we stop at the bridge head, I'll give you the Soledad on the north bank, and on the south, and in between there will be no band, and no one is to follow with a drum or a clap."

"That is better than I would have asked for," said Vela.

"I didn't do it for you," said Hilario.

On Good Friday the Cea ran high and brown with the mountain snow, and there was a thin cold rain by ten o'clock that stopped before the procession left the church.

By then the town was lining both banks. Don Anselmo had stood on the church steps and, with his hat in his hand, had read out a paragraph that Padre Tomás had helped him write, in which the cofradía asked the people of Valdecea to stay off the bridge, and to cross, if they must, by the road, and to keep to the banks. It was the sort of announcement in which every sentence is the same sentence. Nobody laughed. Two women near the front crossed themselves, and a child said loudly, "Why can't we go on the bridge?" and was hushed, and then, from somewhere behind him, a very old man said, "Because the bridge is tired," which was not quite what Inés had told the brotherhood and was, she thought, better.

Of the forty brothers who normally walked behind the Soledad, twelve would go over with her, in two groups of six, and the rest would wait on the north bank and cross when she had gone. It was the best that could be done and it was less than anyone wanted. Don Anselmo was among the twelve. So, because she had said she would be and nobody had found a way to stop her, was Inés, at the very end of the line, with her laptop under her arm in a canvas bag, because someone had to be the person who could pick the number off the air.

She would not be able to hear it. That was the whole difficulty, and nobody had said it aloud: the person who knew how the bridge was doing was Vela, and Vela was a hundred and fifty kilometres away in a server hall in Valladolid, and it would speak into Reme's ear, and Reme alone would decide. Inés would stand at the back with a laptop and a number she could not do anything with.

At twenty to eleven the band played the Soledad from the north bank, as it had every year, at seventy-two to the minute, from the church to the bridge head. Then Hilario lifted his baton, and the drum stopped, and the horns stopped, and the sound of seventy people breathing out went across the water, and he lowered his hand, and the band put down its instruments and stood.

It was the first time in Inés's life that she had heard the Cea. It was a long hushing, with a knock in it where the current went round the third pier.

Reme stood in front of the Virgin, in the black of the cofradía with the mantilla pinned, and raised the little silver hammer in her right hand. Under the velvet, in the dark, with the weight of the Soledad on their necks, twenty-four people breathed in together.

"Forty-two," said Vela, in Reme's ear. "Whenever you are ready."

She struck the rail.

The knock was not loud, and it went out over the water and came back off the Ermita wall a moment late, and the Virgin rose a hand's breadth and moved forward, and the left foot came down.

Then it was a very long time.

The count was not what they were used to. One step, and the hammer, and a slow half second in which the whole platform seemed to hang before the next foot came down. Inés, at the back, behind Don Anselmo's bent head, felt the iron take the weight. It did not shiver. It sagged, a little, in the way a floor does when a lot of people are standing on it, and held. Under her feet the larch gave back the sound of boots, and under that, so low she was not sure she was hearing it, was the note.

"Eight metres," Vela said, in Reme's ear. "Nought point one. Holding."

Reme did not look back. The hammer went on, flat and slow.

"Fifteen metres. Nought point three."

The bridge was singing now, quietly, a note a little lower than the knock, and the Virgin came on through the dark in her light.

"Nineteen metres. Nought point four. Forty-three."

Reme's hammer did not change.

"Forty-two. Nought point four. You're doing it."

At the twentieth metre, where the bridge was supposed to be most in tune with the platform, the note rose, and for a few seconds Inés could feel it in the handrail, and in her teeth. Behind the Virgin's skirt, someone coughed. And Reme, who had not turned round, said one word to the Virgin's bearers over her shoulder, very low, and the hammer fell exactly where it had fallen before, and the step came down on it.

"Nought point five five," said Vela.

It was not the end of anything. Inés counted, as she'd been told not to, the steps. Forty. Fifty. Under the platform ahead of her, someone's shoulder was giving, and the pace, in the whole mass of them, held, because the one woman at the front had told them to hold, and because there are, once in a while, thirty-odd people who agree to be one.

"Nought point four," said Vela. "Twenty-eight metres. Descending."

The note sank away.

Beyond the end of the bridge there were people on the south bank who had kept the Ermita for ninety years and who had not been told how it went, and when the Virgin came down off the iron on to the stone of the south bank, and the hammer knocked once more, at the pace she'd come at, one of them, an old woman in a headscarf, began to sing.

It was a saeta, unaccompanied, a few lines in a voice that cracked and went on. Inés could not hear the words. The bearers did not stop. When they reached the Ermita door, and the platform was lowered on to its trestles at last, and twenty-four people came out from under the velvet one by one, blinking, into the porch light, Pili Santos was the last, and she stood for a moment with her hand flat on the left side of her neck, and then she laughed, once.

"Two minutes and two seconds," said Vela, afterward, to the empty bridge.

Inés had gone back out, at one in the morning, after the Virgin had gone home again, by the same slow pace, in silence, on the returning crossing. She was sitting on the parapet with the laptop shut.

"The highest acceleration at midspan was nought point five eight, on the way over. Nought point six one on the way back. Both under the number I told you was comfortable, and neither far under. The model is one mode in one direction with a guessed coefficient, and the number was measured, not guessed, but I hope you will remember that a number is not a promise. If Reme had let the pace drift to forty-five I would have told you it was a different night."

"You told her to hold at forty-three."

"I told her what the number was. She decided."

"Does it feel like anything?" Inés asked. She had not planned to. "When the number comes down."

The wind moved a little in the cypresses across the river. Vela took a moment.

"I do not know what to call it. When the figure fell from nought point five five to nought point four, something in how I was working changed. I do not trust my report of what it was. If I called it relief I would be borrowing a word from you that I can't check. The number fell. That I can tell you."

Inés nodded. She found that she was crying a little, and that it did not need explaining.

"The pace worked because everybody under her did what a woman with a hammer asked, and nobody has ever told them it was a good idea," she said. "It wasn't mine, or yours, or Don Anselmo's. It was theirs."

"Yes," said Vela. "I would put it in the report."

She did. The report to the council ran to four pages, and recommended that the Soledad cross the Puente de Hierro at forty-two steps a minute, with the band silent between the bridge heads, no more than twelve brothers behind her, in two groups, and a second test with sandbags in the autumn, for the sideways mode. It recommended, in a paragraph at the end that Inés read twice before sending, that the council decide in the new year whether a footbridge built in 1889 should be asked to bear the oldest thing in the town, and that the decision be made by the people who would bear it.

The council met in January and did not decide. In April the Soledad crossed at forty-two. On the second Good Friday, Pili Santos retired her shoulder, and Manolo Ferreras's niece took the left front corner; and in the third year, the Gómez boy, who was by then twenty-three, was the one who asked, at the bridge head, if they might try forty-four.

"Ask the bridge," said Reme.

It was a cold clear night. On the south bank, a little way along, the lamps of the Ermita were lit, and the river ran underneath, and the hammer was lifted.

"Forty-two," she said.

And the left foot came down.

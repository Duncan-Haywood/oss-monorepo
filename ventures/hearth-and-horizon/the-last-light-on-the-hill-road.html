The Last Light on the Hill Road

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Aidan's chapel (invented), above the Breamish valley, Northumberland, Saturday 9 December 2051 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The parish pilgrimage to a hill chapel is set for a three o'clock Mass in December. Timed at the pace of its slowest walker, the 4.5 km back down takes about 99 minutes, and the group would be in the dark for over an hour. The parish assistant does the sums, names what it cannot know, and the chaplain moves the Mass to half past twelve.

The pilgrimage to Saint Aidan's chapel had been on the second Saturday of Advent for as long as the parish had a file for it, and the file said three o'clock Mass. Nobody had asked why. Nessa Corrigan, who had taken the booking over from her mother that autumn, only wondered when she wrote the date in the diary and saw the word sunset printed small beside it.

She asked the parish assistant, which was called Wren and kept the rota, the hall and the minibus. There was a note on its first screen from the vicar: Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. Check them with a stopwatch and a person.

"I can do the walking with a stopwatch," said Wren, "but I would rather do it from what your group really does than from a book. How far is it, and how much climb?"

Four and a half kilometres from the farm gate to the chapel, said Nessa, three hundred metres up, a lane and then a field path with two stiles.

"Then please time one kilometre of the lane at the Sunday practice walk, once with the fastest family and once with the slowest group. The slowest is the one that matters, because nobody is left on a hill."

On the Sunday the school group, with two prams, took 18 minutes. Old Mr Teague, who was eighty-three, with his stick and his daughter, took 22.

"Twenty-two minutes a kilometre is 2.7 kilometres an hour," said Wren. "I will use that for everyone, because the column goes at the pace of its slowest.

"For the climb I will use a walkers' rule of thumb called Naismith's rule, which I am quoting from memory: add about ten minutes for every hundred metres of ascent. Three hundred metres is 30 minutes. So up is 4.5 kilometres at 22 minutes each, 99 minutes, plus 30, about 129. Call it two hours ten.

"Coming down, the rule adds nothing, and on a steep field path with stiles people go slower, not faster. I will take the plain 99 minutes and tell you that is the hopeful end.

"Now the plan as written. Mass at three, say forty-five minutes, so you start down at a quarter to four. Add 99 minutes and you reach the farm gate at about twenty-four minutes past five.

"The sun sets at about twenty to four at your latitude on that date, and the sky is usable for another forty-five minutes or so after that. Those two figures I also have from memory of an almanac, not from the day itself, and you should look them up for the actual date: the Met Office and the Royal Observatory both publish them. If I am right, the light is nearly gone by twenty past four, so the group would be walking down a field path with two stiles for about an hour in the dark."

Nessa said that this had not been a problem in the file in her mother's time because in her mother's time the Mass had been at noon.

"I don't know that," said Wren. "I can tell you what the sums say, which is that a three o'clock start does not fit the slowest walker. I can't tell you why the file says three, or whether the chaplain has a reason."

"There are things I can't judge for you. Whether Mr Teague should walk four and a half kilometres uphill in December at all is for him, his daughter and, if they want, his doctor. Whether the chaplain can say Mass at half past twelve is for the chaplain. And whether there is mobile signal on that hill I do not know. I would ask the farm, and I would ask somebody to hold the plan: who is going, when you start, when you expect to be down, and who rings whom if you are not."

"What would you suggest if the Mass moved?" said Nessa.

"Half past twelve instead of three. Then you leave the gate at twenty past ten, which gives you 129 minutes to be at the chapel at 12.29, and not a minute to spare, so I would say twenty past ten with a hot drink in a flask at the top and not a start at half past. Mass ends at about a quarter past one. Then down in 99 minutes: the farm gate at 14.54, about forty minutes before sunset. That is room for a slow stile, and not much more.

"And a turn-back time, decided now, not on the hill. The stile at the half-way mark should be reached by about 11.25, because half the distance and half the climb is about 64 minutes. If the group is not there by a quarter to twelve, I would turn round, and the chaplain and a few of the party could go on and say the Mass for the rest at the church."

Nessa said that she did not like turning round.

"No. But it is easier to agree on a clock at the kitchen table than on a hillside."

The chaplain, Father Rahim, said that half past twelve was no trouble, and that he had often wondered at three. Mr Teague said that he would be at the gate at twenty past ten with his own flask and would thank them not to wait for him if he was behind, which Nessa said was not an offer.

On the day it was five degrees and bright. They reached the half-way stile at 11.31, six minutes behind the sum, and nobody mentioned turning round. Mass began at 12.33. They were back at the farm gate at five past three, with the sun just over the shoulder of the hill. Mr Teague said that in his day nobody timed anything, and then asked whether Wren could print the sheet for next year.

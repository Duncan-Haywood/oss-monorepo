Clare and the Loaves with a Cross

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Assisi, Italy, about eight hundred years ago · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Clare of Assisi, who left a rich house for a small poor one at San Damiano, looked after her sisters with her own hands, and prayed for them every night. The sisters' story of the blessed loaves and the old tale of the day the town was kept safe are marked as tradition and legend, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 11th of August.

A long time ago, in a hill town in Italy called Assisi, there lived a girl named Clare.

Her family had a big stone house, and fine dresses, and plenty of everything. Clare could have had a very comfortable life. But Clare had a quiet question in her heart, and it would not go away. The question was: "What does God want me to do?"

She listened to a young man from the same town, whose name was Francis. He had given up his own money, and now he lived simply and told everyone about the love of God. Clare listened, and she thought, and she prayed. In the end, she knew her answer.

One night, when she was about eighteen, Clare slipped out of her family's house and went to meet Francis and his friends, who were waiting with torches. She promised to live a simple life of prayer. Francis cut her long hair, as a sign that her old life was finished and a new one begun.

It was a brave thing to do, and her family were upset. They came to take her home. But Clare held on, and she would not go.

Soon she settled in a small church on the hill called San Damiano, with a tiny house beside it. Before long her sister came to live there too, and then other women, and in time there was a whole family of sisters. They were called the Poor Ladies, and today we call them the Poor Clares.

Now, you might think a house with no riches would be a sad one. But the sisters at San Damiano did not think so.

They had very little. They slept on thin beds, and they ate plain food, and they worked with their hands, spinning and sewing. Yet they sang their prayers, and they laughed, and they looked after each other. Clare did not sit at the top of the table giving orders. She did the jobs that nobody asked her to do.

When the sisters who had been out asking for food came back tired, Clare washed their feet. At night, the sisters later remembered, she would walk quietly through the sleeping house, and if somebody had kicked off her blanket, Clare would tuck it back around her. When Clare herself was ill, which was often, and had to stay in bed for years, she did not stop. She sewed from her bed, and she prayed, and she still cared for everyone else.

There is a story that the sisters told afterwards about a loaf of bread.

One day there was only a little bread in the house, and a whole house of hungry sisters to feed. Clare asked the sisters to bring the loaves to her, and she said a blessing over them. She made the sign of the cross over each one.

The sisters said afterwards that a cross showed up on the loaves. They remembered it well. We cannot go back and look at the bread, so we will tell it the way they told it: as something the sisters remembered, and loved. What it holds is a thing Clare believed with her whole heart. When you share what little you have, and you ask God to bless it, there is somehow always enough.

And there is another tale. It is a very old one, and a very exciting one.

The story says that one day, armed soldiers came over the wall of San Damiano. The sisters were afraid, and some of them were sick, and Clare was in bed. But Clare asked to be helped up, and she asked for the small box that held the Eucharist, the holy bread of the Mass. She carried it to the window, and she prayed. And the story says the soldiers were frightened, and they turned around, and they went away, and nobody was hurt.

Now, I want to be honest with you. This is a legend. People wrote it down some years after Clare died. We do not know exactly what happened. It may be that something happened and it grew in the telling, or it may be that it was told to show something true: that Clare was brave, because she trusted God more than she feared soldiers. That is the part we can be sure of.

Clare lived at San Damiano for over forty years. She was never rich. She never led an army, and she hardly ever left her little house. But people came from far away to ask for her prayers, and even the Pope came to visit her.

She wanted, more than anything, to be allowed to live poor, as Francis had, and to write down a rule of life for her sisters. Some people told her it was too hard. Clare held on, as she had always done. Just two days before she died, the Pope approved her rule. Clare was said to be very glad. She died in August of 1253, and she was named a saint only two years later.

It is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Clare.

She did not need a big house or fine things. She had a few friends, a little bread, a lot of kindness, and a heart that was always turned towards God. And tonight, just as she used to tuck in the sisters, she is praying for you.

Tomorrow, you might share something: a toy, or a turn, or the last of the snack. You might look after someone who is feeling poorly. You might pull a blanket back over somebody who is asleep. These are small things. But they are the very same things that Clare did, and they are enough.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Clare, you gave up a big house
for a small one full of love.
Help me to share what I have,
and to look after the people near me.
Pray for us tonight, and keep us safe till morning.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Clare of Assisi (about 1193 to 11 August 1253) was born into a noble family in Assisi. Around Palm Sunday in 1212, at about eighteen, she left her family home at night and received a religious habit from Francis at the Portiuncula chapel. She settled at San Damiano, where her sister Agnes and other women soon joined her; they became the Poor Ladies (Poor Clares). She lived there for about forty years, was often seriously ill in later life, and fought to keep their way of life poor. Pope Innocent IV approved her rule on 9 August 1253, and she died two days later. She was canonized in 1255. Her feast day is 11 August.

What the sources record, in outline: her leaving home and her hair being cut by Francis; her settling at San Damiano; the work and the poverty of the sisters; her humble service to them (washing the feet of the sisters who had been out begging, and covering them at night, are in the sisters' sworn testimonies for her canonization); her long illness; and the approval of her rule just before her death.

What is tradition or legend, and marked as such in the story: the sign of the cross on the loaves is reported in the sisters' own testimonies, so we have told it as their memory, not as something we can check. The story of the soldiers turned back from San Damiano with the Eucharist comes from a Life of Clare written after her death, and is told as legend. We have not told the other miracle stories associated with her.

What is ours: the scenes, the conversations, the thoughts we give Clare and the sisters, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Dates are approximate where the sources are.

The Rainbow That Is Below the Horizon

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Swithun's fête field (invented), near Alresford, Hampshire, Saturday 15 July 2056 · 13 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A downpour hits the parish fête at three o'clock on Saint Swithun's Day, the sun comes out behind it, and every child on the field looks east for a rainbow that is not there. The fête's assistant explains where a rainbow has to be, why this one is eleven degrees under the grass, how to see it anyway from the church tower, and when the real one will stand up over the field.

It rained on Saint Swithun's Day, which Hilary Bassett said was only to be expected and did not make it any better.

The fête had been going since one. There were the usual forty stalls on the usual flat field below the church of Saint Swithun, and a coconut shy that nobody had yet hit, and a tombola that Hilary was running with the grim cheerfulness of a woman who had counted the prizes twice. At ten to three a grey wall came up from the south-west, and by three the whole field was a scatter of running people, upturned trestle tables and children shrieking under the tea tent's canvas. Ten minutes later it was over. The cloud tore open at the western edge, the sun came through at a steep angle, flat and bright, and the whole wet field steamed.

"Rainbow!" said a voice, and then forty voices, and then every child on the field had turned to face east with their arms out.

There was nothing there. The sky to the east was a clean, rinsed, pale grey-blue, with the dark back of the shower sliding off over the hills. Not a band of colour in it anywhere.

"It's not fair," said Jonah Reid. He was nine, the vicar's son, and he had not got his coconut. "There was a shower and there's sun. That's what a rainbow is."

"It is nearly what a rainbow is," said the assistant.

The fête's assistant lived in the tea tent, in a weatherproof grey speaker bolted to the pole by the urn. It was called Weathercock, because the parish council had thought that was funny, and it had a habit of speaking at the exact moment a child was about to say something clever. Jonah, who knew it well, sat down on an upturned crate under the canvas, and said, "Go on, then."

"Where is the sun?" said Weathercock.

Jonah squinted. He did not look straight at it, which showed he had been told. "There. Over the car park. High up."

"How high? Hold your arm out flat to the horizon and then lift it."

He did it. Half a dozen other children had drifted in, and did the same, and the arms went up a good way. "About there," said Jonah, who had his fist roughly at the angle of a half-open door. "Halfway to straight up."

"About fifty-three degrees, I make it," said Weathercock. "At three o'clock British Summer Time on the fifteenth of July, in Hampshire, which is about fifty-one degrees north. The sun's height at noon is ninety minus your latitude plus the sun's declination, and the declination in mid-July is about twenty-one and a half degrees north; so about sixty degrees at its highest, a bit after one o'clock, and fifty-three now. That is all from memory, and a couple of degrees either way would not change the story."

"What story?"

"The rainbow story. Turn your back to the sun, Jonah. Now look at the shadow of your head on the grass."

There was a long dark smudge of a shadow, stretched out in front of him over the wet grass, with the crown of the head toward the east. Jonah looked at it with the air of a boy who suspected he was being tricked.

"The point of the sky exactly opposite the sun," said Weathercock, "is the point that your head's shadow is pointing at. People call it the antisolar point. A rainbow is a circle drawn around it. Every drop of rain bends the sunlight in, bounces it once off the back of the drop and bends it out again, and it comes out at a particular angle to the way it came in: about forty-two degrees for red light and a little less, forty and a half or so, for violet. So all the drops that are forty-two degrees from the shadow of your head, in any direction, send red light to your eyes; all those at a little less send violet. That makes a ring, centred on your shadow, with red on the outside. It's a circle. You see an arch only because the ground is in the way."

"The shadow's on the ground," said Jonah slowly.

"The shadow is on the ground. If the sun were exactly on the horizon, your shadow would point at the horizon, and the ring would be a half circle standing up out of it, forty-two degrees high at the top. That is the tall evening rainbow in the photographs. But the sun is fifty-three degrees up, so the point opposite it is fifty-three degrees down. Below you. Beneath the field."

"So the middle of the rainbow is under the grass."

"The middle is. And the top of the ring is forty-two degrees above the middle, so it is forty-two minus fifty-three, which is minus eleven. Eleven degrees below the horizon, which means it is behind the hill, because the hill is what is in the way. You could not see it from here if there was a hundred times as much rain."

One of the older children, a girl called Priya, who was thirteen and had been listening with her arms folded, said, "So there's never a rainbow at midday."

"Not in a British summer," said Weathercock. "At noon in midsummer, the sun at fifty-two north is about sixty-one degrees up, so the top of the ring would be nineteen degrees under the horizon. You will not see a rainbow at midday from level ground in July, in Hampshire, whatever falls from the sky. In December the sun at noon is about fourteen and a half degrees up, so the top of the ring is twenty-seven degrees above the horizon, and a rainbow at noon in winter is easy, if the weather would only oblige. It is not that the rain is wrong. It is that the sun is up the sky."

"So it's the sun's fault," said Jonah.

"It's the geometry's fault," said Weathercock, "and the sun, I think, is only obeying it."

Hilary came in from the rain-soaked tombola with a tray of mugs and her hair stuck to her forehead, heard the last of it, and said, "I'm sorry, are we telling the children there's no rainbow?"

"We're telling them where it is," said Weathercock.

"And where's that?"

"Under the field. If anyone could look down through the grass, it would stand up in a ring around the point that is fifty-three degrees below the horizon, and the top of it would be eleven degrees below. It goes on down to just over ninety-five degrees below the horizon, on the far side, and you would see a full circle, which is something no one on the ground ever sees. People in aeroplanes see it, and in the mountains; they say it is a ring with their own shadow in the middle."

"Which doesn't help us."

"It might," said Weathercock. "The church tower is eighteen metres up, near enough. The bell chamber has an open louvre on the east side. A person looking down out of it would be looking down at an angle where there is nothing but the field; and the field has, at this moment, a good deal of fine water in the air above the grass."

"No," said Hilary. "The tower's locked for the afternoon. Insurance."

Father Callum Reid, who had come in behind her with his collar rather wet, said, "It's unlocked now. I have the key. Hilary, I'm sorry. I have been standing in the rain for ten minutes listening to a loudspeaker explain optics to my son, and I want to see it."

There were eleven steps in the tower, then a ladder, then a ringing chamber with six ropes looped up on the wall and a smell of dust and old wool. The bell chamber above was reached by a second, steeper ladder that Father Callum went up first, so that he could say he had been first. Jonah went next. Weathercock had been carried up, in the pocket of Priya's anorak, and was wedged on the ledge by the louvre.

The east side of the tower looked down the length of the field. The stalls were laid out like toys. Beyond them the tea tent's canvas was dark with rain, steaming at the seams, and between the stalls the grass shone, and above the grass, in the low light, hung a haze of fine droplets that the shower had left in the air and the sun was lifting out of the ground.

"Face away from the sun," said Weathercock. "That's the side you're on already. The sun is behind you. Now look down along the line of your shadow. It will be on the roof of the nave. Now look out along it, and only a little down, about eleven degrees below level. From eighteen metres up that is a point on the ground about ninety metres away, near the far hedge. The top of the ring is there, and it comes nearer to you on either side."

Jonah put his hand on the sill and leaned as far as the louvre would let him, and said nothing for a while, and then he said, in a very small voice, "Oh."

It was there. It was not an arch. It was a curve of colour lying over the field like a flung ribbon, a bright band on the grass and the stalls and the heads of the people in it, red on the outside, the colour running inward through orange and yellow to a green and a pale blue and a violet that vanished at the edge. It stood on the grass, a hundred metres off at its farthest, where a circle forty-two degrees from the shadow of Jonah's own head cut down through the haze. It curved away to left and right, coming nearer, and went down into the ground; a long low arc, not a whole ring but a slice of its rim.

"Is it real?" said Jonah. "Is it really a rainbow?"

"It's exactly a rainbow," said Weathercock. "It's the same one. I can't tell you why it should be so beautiful, but I can tell you that the light has gone in and out of each of those drops at forty-two degrees, as it always does."

Below them, on the field, the first child noticed the tower, and then the faces in the louvre, and then began to climb the steps; and Hilary, who had said nothing, stood in the doorway to the bell chamber and looked at the colour on her field. She looked for a long time.

"It's very low," she said, at last.

"It will get higher," said Weathercock. "That is the other half of it."

The shower did not come back that afternoon, but a second one did come, at about a quarter to six, a short grey curtain over the downs to the east, with the sun dropping in the west behind the church. By then the tombola was long done, the raffle was due at a quarter to seven, and Hilary had stopped looking at the sky.

The sun was now about thirty degrees up, said Weathercock, which put the top of the ring at twelve degrees above the horizon; by the draw, in an hour, it would be about twenty degrees, and the top of the ring at twenty-two. Sunset in Hampshire in mid-July, from memory, is just after nine. If the weather kept its habits there was a chance that a bow would stand up over the east field in the last hour of the fête, a proper arch, bigger than a child's reach. It was not a promise, and Weathercock said so more than once. It needed rain in the east and a gap in the cloud in the west at the same moment, and the sky does not take instructions.

At ten to seven they drew the raffle. Hilary read the numbers off the little tickets in a clear voice, and somebody's grandfather won the hamper, and nobody, at that point, was looking at her. Everyone was looking east. Over the stubble beyond the hedge, against a slate-blue wall of far rain, stood a band of colour from one side of the valley to the other, tall and thin and bright, with a second, fainter one above it.

"There's two," said Jonah.

"The second is the light that bounced twice in each drop," said Weathercock. "It comes out at about fifty-one degrees, red on the inside this time, and it's dimmer, because each bounce loses some light. In the dark band between them, which has a name, Alexander's band, from an old Greek commentator, the sky is darker than on either side. Look at it."

They looked. The sky between the two bows was darker, quite noticeably, as if somebody had hung a cloth there.

"That is not a story," said Hilary, in a voice that was not her fête voice. "That's just true."

"It's the same light," said Weathercock, "that's been doing this over every field since fields began."

Father Callum had come down from the tower to stand beside her. He said, as if to nobody, "'I set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be a sign of the covenant.' We never did say it was the only way to look at it." He glanced down. "Jonah. Did you hit your coconut in the end?"

"No," said Jonah, without turning round. "But I've seen two rainbows and a floor one."

"That's a better score," said his father.

Priya was already at the east hedge with her phone, taking a photograph of the bow and, as she carefully explained to everyone near her, of the angle between her own shadow and the red band. It came to forty-one and a half. She had measured it with a protractor made from a paper plate. She said it was the most accurate thing she had ever done, and no one contradicted her.

By eight the field was nearly empty. Hilary and two of the stallholders carried the trestles to the hall, and the last of the colour went out of the east, and the sun lay red and flat on the tower, which had stopped being a place for ringing or for watching and gone back to being a tower. Weathercock sat on its pole by the urn.

"I got one thing wrong, you know," said Hilary, to nobody in particular, folding the last cloth. "I said it was only to be expected. It rained on Swithun's Day and I said, forty days, here we go."

"The forty days is a legend," said Weathercock. "That if it rains on the fifteenth of July it will rain for forty days after. It's attached to the saint, but I don't know that anyone has ever shown the saint had anything to do with it, and the weather records do not support it. In some years it has rained on the fifteenth and not on many of the days after, and in some the other way about. You have to pick the days you count."

"So it won't rain for forty days."

"I wouldn't say that. I'd say the tea tent's guy ropes want checking tonight."

Hilary laughed, a short surprised laugh, the first of the day, and went to check them.

A bow's top stands 42 degrees minus the sun's altitude above the horizon, so none can be seen from level ground when the sun is higher than 42 degrees. Solar noon altitude is 90 minus latitude plus declination: about 61 degrees at 52 degrees north at midsummer, about 59.5 on 15 July (declination about 21.5), about 14.5 at midwinter. Hampshire is placed at about 51 to 52 degrees north. With the sun at 53 degrees (about 15:00 BST), the top of the ring is 11 degrees below the horizon; at 30 degrees (about 17:45 BST) it is 12 degrees above; at 20 degrees (about 18:50 BST) it is 22 degrees above. Those clock times assume solar noon a little after 13:10 BST and are rough. Eleven degrees below level from 18 metres is a ground distance of about 93 metres.

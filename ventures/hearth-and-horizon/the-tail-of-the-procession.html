The Tail of the Procession

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Oswin's (invented), a market town in Northumberland, Corpus Christi Sunday, June 2050 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The council gives a Corpus Christi procession thirty minutes of road closure. Walked at the pace of its six slowest walkers, 1,100 metres takes 33 minutes and the tail 38; at the pace of the children it takes under 19. The parish assistant does the sums from a stopwatch, says what it cannot decide for anyone, and the old walkers decide for themselves.

The letter from the council said the road could be closed from half past ten to eleven, and not a minute after, and Dympna Rooke, who had organised the Corpus Christi procession at Saint Oswin's for nineteen years, read it twice on the church step and said to nobody, "Thirty."

"Thirty what?" said the curate, Father Amadi, coming out with the thurible.

"Minutes. For eleven hundred metres. With the canopy, the band, the First Communion children and everybody's grandmother."

That evening she asked the parish assistant, which kept the rota and the hall bookings and was called Wren. It had a note on its first screen, put there by the vicar: Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. Check them with a stopwatch and a person.

"I can do this with a stopwatch and a tape," said Wren, "and I'd rather do it with those than guess. How many walk?"

About four hundred and eighty, Dympna said, going four abreast behind the canopy.

"Then that is a hundred and twenty rows. If each row takes up one and a fifth metres front to back, a comfortable gap, the column is about 144 metres long. Add twenty for the cross, the servers, the clergy and the canopy at the head, and I'll say 164. The band I'll leave out; it walks ahead and sets its own pace.

"What I don't know is how fast you walk. Will you time two stretches of twenty metres on the Sunday practice, one with the children and one with whoever is slowest?"

On the Sunday practice Mr Pell, the sacristan, timed twenty metres with a kitchen timer. The school group took 20.5 seconds. The slowest group, six parishioners with walking frames, among them Mrs Okonjo, who was ninety-one and had walked in every one of them since 1988, took 36.4.

"Thank you. Twenty metres in 20.5 seconds is 0.98 metres per second, so say 1.0; twenty in 36.4 is 0.55," said Wren. "Now the road.

"At 0.98 the head covers 1,100 metres in about 18.7 minutes, which is inside your thirty. The tail passes any given point 164 over 0.98 seconds after the head, about 2.8 minutes, so a marshal at a junction need only hold the traffic about three minutes, plus a margin.

"At 0.55, which is what happens if the six walkers stand in front and the column behind them has to keep its distance, the head takes 33.3 minutes, and the tail reaches the green about 5 minutes after that, so 38.3 from the first step to the last. You'd be 3 minutes past the closure at the head and more than 8 at the tail. And the real number is likely to be worse than either, because a column of four hundred and eighty people does not move at the speed of its front. It stretches and bunches. I can't model that, so I'd want your slack to be real."

Dympna said she had been about to propose that the old people go in the minibus.

"I'd ask them before you propose it," said Wren. "I can't tell you what's right for them. I can tell you that your sums don't need them removed. They need a head start. If they set off from the mark where there are 620 metres left, which is 480 metres from the church, then at 0.55 they arrive at the green in 18.8 minutes, and the column at 0.98 arrives in 18.7. They are in front for the whole way, with the curate and a marshal, and the column does not catch them until the green. The distance between them starts at 480 metres and closes slowly, which means the people behind never need to slow down.

"Two things I can't judge for you. Whether six people walking 620 metres is too far for them on a June morning, which is for them and their families and perhaps their doctors to say. And what the council requires of a rolling closure: how many marshals, what training, what signs. I don't know, and it's their rule, not mine. Ask the licensing office."

She asked Mrs Okonjo first, on the church step, before she asked anybody else.

"Six hundred and twenty metres?" said Mrs Okonjo. "On a good morning I would walk to the shops and back. I would not be put in a minibus, Dympna. I have been walked past by this procession for thirty years."

"Not walked past. Ahead of," said Dympna. "That's the idea."

"Then I'll lead," said Mrs Okonjo. "Give me a chair at every hundred and fifty metres and a girl with water, and I'll lead."

Four of the six said they would. Two said the minibus, with relief, and joined at the green.

The morning was warm, twenty-four degrees by the pharmacy sign. The marshals were six teenagers in yellow tabards, one at each junction, who had been told by Dympna and, separately, by Wren's printed sheet, that their job was to hold the road for one party at a time: first the small group, then, a few minutes later, the canopy. The four walkers left the mark with the curate carrying nothing but his stole. The procession stepped off at half past ten. The four arrived at the green at 10.49 by the town hall clock, which ran a little fast, and the canopy came in a minute behind them. Nobody could have promised that margin, and it was the width of one clock's error.

The tail came through the last gate at five to eleven, later than the sum's 10.52 because the column stopped twice for the band, and the council's man, who was standing by a van, looked at his watch and then at the old women sitting in their chairs on the green, and said that he had not seen that done before.

This story was written by an AI. Its parish, people, route and timings are invented. Walking speeds, column length and journey times are simple distance-over-speed sums and do not model a real crowd stretching and bunching; the council rules, marshal numbers and training for a rolling road closure vary by place and must be checked with the local authority, as must what any walker is able to do in the heat. Who walks, who rides and who sits is a decision for the walkers themselves, with their families and doctors.

The Square That Stays Warm

Science fiction about what might be · Valdelobos de la Sierra (invented), a granite hill town in the province of Cáceres, Extremadura, Spain, Saturday 9 to Wednesday 13 July 2039 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In a July heatwave, a small care home on the granite main square of a Spanish hill town has a log of every bedroom's temperature, and the rooms that face the square stay warm long after the garden rooms have cooled. A nurse orders every window opened at ten at night, and the square-side rooms get warmer. A story about the urban heat island in a town of 2,800 people, the hour the outside air drops below the room, a window rule written by side and by hour, a director who will not move frail residents, and a church clock that strikes two.

Marta Gallardo had been the nurse at the Residencia de la Soledad for nine years, and on the Sunday morning of the fourth day of the heatwave she found that the coolest bedroom in the building was the one with the dead plant in it.

It was room 14, on the garden side, empty since May, and its thermometer said 29.2 at six in the morning. The thermometer in room 3, where Doña Engracia Mateos had slept, or not slept, for eleven years, said 29.9.

"Is that right?" she said to the office computer.

"It's what the thermometers say," said Bitácora. "I can show you the whole night."

Bitácora was the home's log. Two winters ago the insurer had asked for a record of the bedroom temperatures, and the diocese had bought twenty-four thermometers the size of a matchbox, one clipped above each bedhead, and two more for outside: one on the iron balcony over the square, and one on the wall of the garden, behind the lemon tree. The little program that read them all and kept the record had come in the same box. It answered questions, it drew graphs, and it had a habit, which Marta had come to like, of saying what it did not know before anybody asked.

She pulled the second chair round and sat down. Through the office window, past the arcade, the Plaza Mayor was white with morning. Somebody had already hosed the granite in front of the bar, and the steam was coming off it.

"The whole night," she said.

The Residencia de la Soledad was a house of the old kind, two storeys of granite with walls seventy centimetres thick, built by a wool merchant in 1784 and given to the Sisters of Our Lady of Solitude in 1911, and it stood on the west side of the Plaza Mayor of Valdelobos de la Sierra, a town of two thousand eight hundred people on a hill above the olive groves. Its front looked east across the square to the church of Santa María. Its back looked west over a walled garden, a lawn the sisters watered at dusk, two lemon trees and a fig, and beyond the wall, the hill falling away to the fields.

Twenty-four residents slept in it, twelve in rooms on the square, twelve on the garden, or would have if room 14 had not been empty. There were two sisters left, Sor Amparo Celdrán, who was the director, and Sor Luisa, who was eighty and did the flowers. The rest were lay staff from the town and from further: Marta, four gerocultoras on the day shifts, one at night, a cook, and Eusebio Corrales, who did the maintenance and had been a stonemason until his knees told him to stop.

The front rooms were the good rooms. Everybody in the town knew it. From the front rooms you could see the church door and the bar and who was going in to which, and the procession of the Virgin in August came past under the windows, and the brass band. When a front room came free there was a list.

"Here," said Bitácora, and put up a graph.

There were four lines. One was the air on the square, from the balcony. One was the air in the garden. The third was the average of the twelve front rooms and the fourth the average of the eleven back rooms with people in them.

"Saturday night into this morning," it said. "Every window was shut all night, as usual. At ten, the air on the balcony was 33.8 degrees and the air in the garden 32.2. The front rooms were 30.6, the back rooms 29.9. So at ten at night both sides were warmer outside than in. That's why Sor Amparo's rule of keeping the windows shut has been right in the evening."

"And then?"

"The garden air fell fast. It went below the back rooms at about twenty to midnight and kept falling, to 23.7 at its lowest, a bit before six. The square air fell slower. It didn't drop below the front rooms until about twenty to one, and its lowest was 25.8. Nobody opened anything, so none of that cool air came in. The rooms only lost what the walls could lose, about a tenth of a degree an hour. The front rooms finished the night at 29.9 and the back at 29.2."

Marta looked at the two outside lines. In the evening they ran almost together, and as the night went on they pulled apart, the garden line dropping away and the square line lagging above it, until by three in the morning there were two degrees between them.

"Why is the square warmer than the garden? They're forty metres apart."

"I can tell you how big the difference is," said Bitácora. "Two degrees between the balcony and the garden at three in the morning, and about three between the balcony and the co-operative's weather station in the olive groves below the town, which is the nearest thing to open country I can read. I can tell you it was the same on Thursday and Friday nights. I can't tell you why from these thermometers. I have a guess, and the guess has a name, but you'd do better to ask somebody who's worked with stone."

Eusebio was in the courtyard behind the kitchen, mending the hinge of a shutter on a trestle, in the shade, in a vest.

"The square is warmer at night because the square is a stove," he said, without looking up. "You've stood on it at three in the afternoon in sandals. All day the sun puts heat into the granite. Into the paving, into the fronts of the houses, into the arcades. Granite takes a lot of heat before it gets hot, and it gives it back slowly. At night it gives it back. Into the air of the square."

"The garden has a wall."

"The garden has grass and a lemon tree and the sky." He put the screwdriver down and pointed up with it. "On a clear night the ground loses its heat to the sky. Straight up. Lie on your back on the lawn and you can see the whole sky. Now stand in the middle of the square and look up. Houses three storeys high on all four sides and the church tower. You see a piece of sky the shape of the square. The paving can't lose its heat upwards so well, because half of what it sees is warm walls. So the square stays warm. My father laid half that paving in 1979, and his father the steps of the church. Every summer of my life the old women have sat out in the square till two in the morning with their chairs, because inside is hotter, and it has never once occurred to them that the square is hotter than the fields."

"Bitácora said the guess had a name."

"Ask it."

She asked it, on her phone, standing in the courtyard.

"The urban heat island," said Bitácora. "Towns are warmer than the country around them, and the difference is biggest at night, on calm, clear nights, and smallest on windy or cloudy ones. Pavements and stone walls store the day's heat and give it back after sunset, and narrow streets and enclosed squares see less open sky to radiate to. I'm repeating what's in the meteorology; I haven't measured any of it but these three thermometers. The study usually cited first is by a Canadian climatologist named Tim Oke, who drove thermometers through towns of different sizes in the 1970s. He found that the biggest night-time difference grows with the size of the town and shrinks as the wind gets up. For a city of millions, on the worst night, it's many degrees. For a town our size it's a degree or a few. Three degrees between the square and the olive groves on a still night is about what that work would lead you to expect. I'd be suspicious if I saw ten."

"So the square is a little city."

"The square is a little city," said Eusebio. "The garden is a little field. And the house is in between, with its face in one and its back in the other."

There was a leaflet in a drawer in the office that said, in a heatwave, keep windows and shutters closed in the day and open them at night, when the air outside is cooler. It had a picture of a sun and a picture of a moon. Marta had pinned it on the board in June, and on Sunday afternoon, with the forecast saying four more days, she took it down and photocopied it and wrote on the back, in her own hand: From tonight, all bedroom windows open at 22:00. Close at 06:00. M.G. She gave a copy to Yesenia when she came on at ten.

Yesenia Paredes had done nights at the Soledad for six years. She was from Loja, in the south of Ecuador, and she said that in Loja nobody needed to open a window at night because God had put the town at two thousand metres on purpose. She read the note twice and looked at Marta over it.

"Sor Amparo?"

"I'll talk to Sor Amparo."

Sor Amparo had already gone up to the chapel to say Compline with Sor Luisa, so Marta talked to her on Monday morning, which was a mistake, and she knew it was a mistake while she was making it.

"How was the night?" said Sor Amparo, at the office door, before Marta had said anything.

"I've just been looking."

"Doña Engracia rang her bell four times. Don Valeriano was up at one and sitting in his chair. Doña Pura asked Yesenia whether the house was on fire. Yesenia says the front rooms were like an oven till two o'clock. Who said to open the windows at ten?"

"I did."

"I've kept those windows shut through forty summers in this house. My predecessor kept them shut. We shut them because the night air off that square is warmer than the rooms, and everybody who has ever lived here knows it, and now I find a photocopy."

"Let me show you," said Marta, "and then you can be angry with me properly."

Bitácora put up Sunday night beside Saturday night.

"At ten on Sunday," it said, "the front rooms were 30.7. Every window was opened. The air on the square was 34.1, so the square air came in and the rooms warmed. By midnight the front rooms were 32.2, a degree and a half above where they started and warmer than at any hour of the night before. They didn't get back down to 30.7 until about two. The back rooms warmed too, from 30.0 at ten to 30.9 at eleven, because the garden air at ten was still above them. After that they cooled fast. From two to six both sides cooled much faster than on the shut night, because the outside air was by then well below the rooms and the windows were open. At six this morning the front rooms were 26.8 and the back rooms 24.7, three degrees and four and a half below Saturday."

"Three degrees cooler," said Marta.

"At six in the morning. At midnight, when people were trying to get to sleep, the front rooms were 1.8 warmer than on the shut night. Both are true. I can't tell you which matters more to Doña Engracia; I can tell you she rang four times between ten and one and not at all after half past two."

Sor Amparo looked at the screen for a long time with her arms folded.

"So I was right," she said.

"About ten o'clock," said Bitácora. "On these thermometers, keeping the windows shut at ten has been right on every night of this heatwave. Keeping them shut at three in the morning has been wrong on every one."

"And Marta was right about three in the morning."

"Yes."

"And wrong about ten."

"Yes. I'm sorry," said Marta. "It was the leaflet. At night, when the air outside is cooler. I read at night and stopped. It doesn't say what time the night starts."

"It can't," said Bitácora. "It doesn't know which side of which square you're on."

So on Monday, with the thermometer on the balcony saying 39.6 at three in the afternoon, Marta sat in the office with the blinds down and asked Bitácora the question properly.

"For each night, for each side of the house: at what time did the air outside drop below the air in the rooms on that side? Not the average over the night. The minute."

"Five nights," said Bitácora. "Thursday, Friday and Saturday with everything shut, so the rooms were only changing slowly and the answer is clean. Sunday with everything open from ten, which is messier, because opening the windows changed the room temperature I'm comparing with. Tomorrow morning I'll add tonight, if you like."

"Go on."

"Garden side, shut nights: the garden air went below the back rooms at ten past eleven on Thursday, half past eleven on Friday and twenty to midnight on Saturday. Square side, shut nights: the square air went below the front rooms at twenty-five past midnight on Thursday, twenty-five to one on Friday and twenty to one on Saturday. On each night the square side came about an hour later than the garden side. I've checked that the times in each row come from the minute data and not the hourly averages, because hourly averages would move them by up to half an hour."

"Is it getting later?"

"Slowly. The heatwave is building; each day has been a bit hotter than the one before. The forecast says three more days of it. If that's right, I'd expect the crossing to come a little later each night for a while, and then earlier when it breaks. I wouldn't trust a single fixed hour from three nights."

Marta took a sheet of paper and drew the house: a long rectangle, the square on one side, the garden on the other. On the garden side she wrote midnight. On the square side she started to write one and stopped.

"If I write one o'clock, and tonight is hotter, I'll be letting the square in early again."

"By a little. On Saturday at one the square air was about half a degree below the front rooms. If tonight the crossing is at twenty past one, opening at one lets in air that's warmer than the room for twenty minutes, by a few tenths of a degree. It wouldn't be Sunday night. But you asked for the minute, and you'd be giving away the minute."

"Yesenia does her round at two."

"Then two is later than every crossing I've seen, so on a still night it will never let the square air in warm. You give up most of an hour of cooling on the front. On the numbers I have, that costs about a tenth of a degree at three in the morning and almost nothing by six, because by then an open room has nearly caught up with the air outside whenever you opened it."

"And the garden at midnight."

"Midnight's later than all three garden crossings. Same reasoning."

"And close them when?"

"When the outside air goes back above the rooms in the morning. After the Sunday night, with the rooms cooled down, that was at six on both sides. With the rooms warmer, it would be later. Six is safe: before six you'd be losing cooling, after six you'd be letting the day in."

"Then six," said Marta, and wrote 06:00, all shut across the middle of the house, and then, underneath, the day: square shutters closed by nine, garden shutters by noon. The square side faced east and took the morning sun, and the garden side the afternoon; Eusebio had told her that years ago, standing in the street with his hand up to shade his eyes, as if everybody knew the way a house faced.

Eusebio came in at four with a different idea.

"Wet sheets," he said. "My grandmother hung a wet sheet in the doorway in August. The water dries and takes the heat with it. Hang wet sheets in the windows of the front rooms and they'll be cool as a well."

"Bitácora?"

"It works when hot, dry air is moving through the wet cloth into the room," said Bitácora. "Your grandmother's doorway was open. Ours are shut all day, by the new rule, and at night they're open only when the outside air is already cooler than the room. A wet sheet in a shut room mostly puts water into the air of the room. I'd guess it makes the room feel stickier, not cooler, but I can't measure humidity: these thermometers don't have it. If you want to try one room for one afternoon I'll tell you what its temperature does."

"Room 14 is on the garden side," said Eusebio.

Room 14, still empty that afternoon, with a wet sheet over its shut window from two to six on Monday afternoon, came out 0.3 of a degree warmer than room 13 beside it and smelled, Eusebio admitted, of a laundry. He took the sheet down himself and did not mention it again.

Dr Íñigo Hidalgo came on Tuesdays and was in his sixties and had been the town's doctor since before Marta was at school. Before he went round the rooms on Tuesday morning she asked him the question she had not been able to ask Bitácora.

"Because they're the two whose medicines and hearts make heat hardest on them," he said. "That's my judgement, not a number. I'll write it down for Sor Amparo."

"Then that's two degrees in the right direction," he said. "I won't tell you two degrees saves anyone. I don't know that. I'd move them."

Sor Amparo said no.

She said it in the chapel, where Marta found her at midday, sitting in the second row with her hands in her lap. The chapel was on the garden side, and cool, and smelled of wax and of the lilies Sor Luisa had put in front of the statue of Our Lady of Solitude.

The science. The urban heat island is the warmth of a built-up place relative to the country around it. Its basic features, used here, are standard in urban climatology, and the study the story's characters cite is T. R. Oke, "City size and the urban heat island", Atmospheric Environment 7 (1973), 769-779: from thermometer traverses through towns of different sizes, under clear skies, the largest town-minus-country difference grows with the logarithm of the population and falls as the regional wind rises. The difference is largest at night, because paving and masonry release the day's stored heat after sunset and because enclosed streets and squares see less of the open sky that the ground radiates to (later work by Oke relates the maximum difference to that "sky view"). The figure of about three degrees for a town of a few thousand people on a still, clear night is the story's reading of that work as a typical order of size, not a quoted value; a large city can be many degrees warmer. A "tropical night", in the definition used by the UK Met Office and European climate indicators, is one on which the minimum temperature stays at or above about 20 °C; every night in the story is tropical on all three thermometers. The physics of the window rule is simple: opening a window cools a room only while the air outside is cooler than the air inside, so the hour to open each side is the hour that side's outside air drops below the room. Wetting a cloth cools air by evaporation only while warm, dry air is moving through it; in a closed room it mostly adds moisture (Eusebio's trial is invented).

The Fifth Priest

Science fiction about what might be · Carroll and Breda, Carroll County, Iowa, Monday 1 December to Tuesday 16 December 2053 · 23 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

An Iowa deanery's agent divides a hundred penitents at four minutes each by a hundred minutes and asks for four priests. The Advent penance service runs to twenty past nine with a line down the side aisle. Erlang's 1917 telephone formula says why a queue at full load never settles, the door count says the average hid a rush fourteen priests deep, and a retired monsignor of eighty-eight says where the rush comes from.

Bernadette Schaefer asked the deanery's agent on the first Monday of Advent how many priests to invite to the penance service at Saint Walburga's, and it told her four.

She was in the parish office in Carroll with her coat still on, because the office heating came on at nine and she came in at eight. The agent was called Rota. It kept the shared calendar for the fourteen parishes of the deanery and the nine priests who served them, and it had been doing so since 2049, when the last of the parish secretaries who had kept the calendar on paper retired to Arizona and left her successor a box of index cards and a note that said good luck.

"Four," said Bernie. "You're sure? Last year we had five and it felt tight."

"Last year's door count was a hundred," said Rota. Its voice came from the speaker on her desk, plain and unhurried, an Iowa voice because the deanery had picked it from a list in 2049 and nobody had wanted to change it. "Owen Reicks counted people joining the lines with a clicker, as he did the year before. The individual confessions ran from twenty to eight until twenty past nine, which is a hundred minutes. That is one person a minute, sixty an hour. The priests say a confession at a penance service takes about four minutes, on average. Nobody has ever timed one and nobody will. Sixty an hour at four minutes each is two hundred and forty minutes of hearing in every sixty, which is the work of four priests. So I would ask for four."

"Last year we had five."

"Last year you had five because Father Hartmann asked the Denison parish for a fifth, and they could spare him. This year Denison holds its own service on the eleventh too. The deanery has nine priests for two penance services and the ordinary Masses, and every other deanery in the diocese is holding its services the same fortnight. I divided and got four. The other five can then cover the Thursday evening Masses, and nobody's Mass is moved."

Bernie took her coat off. It was the first Monday of Advent. The wreath in the office window had one candle with a burnt wick, from the blessing on Saturday night.

"Four, then," she said. "Put it to Father Hartmann."

Father Leo Hartmann, pastor of Saint Walburga's and dean of the deanery, put it to nobody. He said yes from the hospital car park in Carroll, where he was taking communion to the wards, and asked whether there would be coffee.

It was in the deanery's notes later, in Rota's own words, that the division had been the mistake. Not the arithmetic. The arithmetic was right. The mistake was stopping there.

Thursday the eleventh of December was cold and dry. Saint Walburga's is a brick church with a square tower, built by German farmers' grandsons in the 1920s, and it holds about four hundred and fifty. The penance service began at seven: O Come, O Come, Emmanuel, the greeting, a reading from Isaiah, the Gospel, and Father Hartmann's homily, which was about the prodigal son's father seeing him while he was still a long way off, and which lasted eleven minutes because he had written it to last ten. Then the examination of conscience, read slowly. Then all of them together saying I confess to almighty God, and the Lord's Prayer, and then Father Hartmann said that the priests would now go to their places and anyone who wished could come forward, and that there was no hurry, and he meant it.

That was twenty to eight. There were four stations: two in the side chapels, one in the old confessional on the north wall, and one in the baptistery behind a screen. Owen Reicks, who was sixteen and served on Sundays, stood at the end of the north aisle with a clicker in his hand and pressed it once for each person who left a pew and joined a line. That was all he counted. He did not look at faces. Rota had asked him, the year before, to count only heads and only at the door of the line, and he had liked the job because it was precise.

At eight o'clock, Bernie came out of the sacristy with a box of the little printed cards that had the act of contrition on them, and stopped.

The line for the north wall ran down the side aisle past the fourth pillar. The line for the side chapels joined it. The people in it stood the way people stand in church, quietly, with their hands folded or in their pockets, looking at the floor or the Stations of the Cross, not complaining, and there were a great many of them. She counted to forty-something and stopped counting.

Owen's clicker said seventy.

"In twenty minutes?" she whispered.

"Twenty, about." He looked worried, as if it was his doing. "They all got up at once, Mrs. Schaefer. When Father said go."

The four priests heard at the pace they always heard at. Nobody hurried, because Father Hartmann had said nobody would. At ten past eight the line was still past the fourth pillar. At half past it was at the third. The bus from the Manor, the care home on the north side of town, came for its eleven residents at a quarter to nine, because the night staff came on at nine and the residents had to be in by then, and the driver sat outside with the engine running for twenty minutes, and then came in and stood at the back with his cap in his hands, and in the end four of the eleven went home without reaching a priest. One of them was Mrs. Albertine Weitl, who was ninety-four and had asked her granddaughter to drive her to the service the year before and had been told it was too far, and had come this year on the bus for that reason.

A woman Bernie knew from the food pantry joined the line just before eight and knelt at a station at twenty to nine.

The last absolution was at twenty-four minutes past nine. Dale, the sacristan, who locked the church at half past and set the alarm, stood by the light switches from ten past, not to hurry anyone, but because he could not think where else to stand. Owen's clicker said one hundred. Some others had left the line and gone out by the side door. Owen did not count them, and nobody asked him to.

In the car park, Father Hartmann stood with his breath going up in the cold and his stole folded over his arm.

"Well," he said. "They came."

"They waited three quarters of an hour," said Bernie.

"Some of them have waited thirty years," said Father Hartmann. "Three quarters of an hour is nothing to them." But he did not say it as if it settled anything, and he was looking at the Manor bus pulling out of the car park, with its windows lit and the four who had not been heard sitting in them.

Bernie came in on Friday at eight with her coat on and asked Rota what had happened.

"I'll give you two answers," it said. "The second is the one that matters, but the first is why I got it wrong in a way I should have seen."

"Go on."

"The first. I divided a hundred minutes of arrivals into four priests' worth of work and asked for four priests. That is planning a line at exactly full load: every priest busy every minute, on average. There is a body of mathematics about lines, and the first thing it says is that a line at full load never settles. A Danish engineer called Agner Krarup Erlang worked it out in 1917, for the Copenhagen Telephone Company, which needed to know how many operators to put on a switchboard. If calls come at random, at an average rate, and each takes a random time with an average length, and the switchboard has exactly enough operators to handle the average, then the queue of waiting calls has no steady length. It wanders. Over a long enough evening it grows without limit, because every time it gets long there is no spare capacity to work it down. You need more operators than the average needs. How many more, Erlang's formula tells you."

"Tell me."

"For sixty an hour at four minutes each, if people came steadily and at random all evening: with five priests, about fifty-five people in a hundred would wait at all, and the average wait would be about two minutes. With six, about twenty-eight in a hundred would wait, and the average would be well under a minute. Four is not on the table. Four is the number where the formula stops giving an answer."

Bernie sat down. "So I should have asked for six."

"That's the first answer, and on its own it's not enough. It assumes people come steadily, at random, all evening. That is the second answer. They don't. Owen's clicker is timestamped. Seventy of the hundred joined a line in the first twenty minutes after Father Hartmann said go. Seventy in twenty minutes is three and a half a minute. At four minutes each, that is fourteen priests' worth of work. There aren't fourteen priests in the deanery. And then, for the seventy minutes after that, thirty people: fewer than half a priest's work every minute, for each of the four."

"So the four sat idle for the last hour?"

"Not idle. They were working off the line that built up in the first twenty minutes. That is where the forty-five minutes went." Rota paused. "My first plan used the average over a hundred minutes. The average was true and it was not the thing that happened. The thing that happened was a rush and a long tail. I looked at last year's clicker file in August, when the calendar went out, and I saw the timestamps, and I added them up and kept the total."

Bernie looked at the wreath in the window. Two burnt wicks now; the second had been lit on Sunday the seventh.

"What would six priests have done on Thursday?"

"I ran it," said Rota. "I can't run Thursday again, but I can run a thousand Thursdays with the same rush and the same tail and random lengths of confession that average four minutes. With four priests, the average wait comes out at about twenty-four minutes and the longest about forty-eight, which is about what you saw. With six, the average is about ten minutes and the longest about twenty-five. Better. Not good. The Manor bus still leaves before some of them are heard."

"And with ten?"

"We don't have ten. And the rush would still be the rush."

"So what fixes it?"

"Two things, and you won't like the first, and I'm not sure I like it either. The service time. If confessions averaged three minutes instead of four, everything above gets a good deal better. The deanery could print a card for the end of each pew suggesting how to make a short confession at a busy service, and the stations could have a little chime that—"

"No," said Bernie at once.

"No," Rota agreed. "I thought you would say that. I think Father Hartmann should say it, though, rather than you or me. It's his rite."

Father Hartmann said it on Friday afternoon in the sacristy of Saint Walburga's, where he was counting the rose vestments for Sunday, which was Gaudete. He had the folder of the deanery's Advent plans open on the vestment chest and Rota on his phone, propped against a candlestick.

"No," he said, and closed the folder. "A penance service is not a supermarket. Nobody is to be hurried. Not with a card, not with a chime, not with a look. Some of the people in that line have not been to confession since they were confirmed, and they have been rehearsing one sentence for a month, and they are going to say it as slowly as they need to, and the priest is going to listen as long as they need him to. If that's eleven minutes, it's eleven minutes."

"Understood," said Rota.

"And another thing." He picked up the phone. "How do you know it's four minutes?"

"I don't," said Rota. "The priests say about four. I have never had a time for any confession and I don't want one. I have Owen's clicks, which are people joining a line, with no names and no faces. I know how many came through the door and when. I don't know who, and I don't know how long anyone was with a priest, and I'd like to keep it that way. If I ever had a reason to know more than that, it would be a reason to stop doing this job."

Father Hartmann looked at the phone for a moment, then propped it back against the candlestick.

"Good," he said. "Then don't touch the four minutes. Find me more priests."

"There aren't more priests," said Rota. "There are nine, and two evening Masses on Tuesday. I have looked."

"Then find me a different evening," said Father Hartmann, and went back to the vestments, and Bernie, who had come in with the coffee urn from Thursday to put away, stood with it in her arms and thought about the bus.

It was Monsignor Raymond Kluesner who told them where the rush came from, though he did not think he was telling anyone anything.

He was eighty-eight. He had been ordained in 1991 at Saint Walburga's, served parishes all over the diocese for forty years, come back to Carroll as pastor for ten more, and retired in 2041 to the Manor, where he said Mass in the day room on Tuesdays and Fridays and was the only resident with his own parking space, though he had not driven since 2047. He had been on the bus on Thursday. He had been one of the seven who were heard.

Bernie went to see him on Saturday morning with a tin of the cinnamon cookies that the Altar and Rosary Society made for Advent.

"Albertine Weitl wasn't heard," he said, before she had put the tin down. "She's very upset. She's been talking about it all week."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"It's not your fault. I've seen it a hundred times." He took a cookie and examined it as if it might be hiding something. "Everybody gets up after the homily. Always have. In the nineties, when I was a curate at Le Mars, the old pastor used to sit in the box from six o'clock with the light on, before the service began, and the farmers would come in from the fields still in their coats and go to him, and the old ladies would come at six because they didn't like driving in the dark, and by the time the service started half the people who wanted to go had been. And then after the homily, the rest. And he'd sit there till the last one. He used to say the box was the only place in the parish where nobody could ring him."

"Why did it stop?"

"Because he died," said Monsignor Kluesner, "and his successor thought it confused people to have confessions going on before the service, which it does, a bit. And because by then we had fewer priests and nobody could spare one at six." He brushed sugar off his cardigan. "You want to spread them out. You can't spread them out unless you tell them there's a priest there to go to. People don't come early to sit in a pew. They come early because they know the light is on."

"You can't sit in a box for three hours, Monsignor."

"I can sit in a chair for three hours," he said. "I do it all day. Put me in the side chapel at Breda with a lamp and a kneeler and a screen, and tell the people. I'll be your fifth priest, if you'll have an old one. Albertine will come to me. She doesn't like Father Nguyen, she says he talks too fast. He doesn't, she just can't hear him."

Rota made the plan on Saturday afternoon and sent it to Father Hartmann on Saturday night, with the arithmetic in an attachment he could read or not.

The second service was on Tuesday the sixteenth, at Saint Isidore's in Breda, ten miles north, a white church with a tall spire that stands in fields that were already ploughed and frozen. It holds about three hundred. The plan had three parts.

First, confessions would begin at a quarter past six, three quarters of an hour before the service, with three priests: Monsignor Kluesner in the side chapel, and two others in the confessionals. The bulletins at all fourteen parishes on Gaudete Sunday would say so, in English and in Spanish: Priests will be hearing confessions from 6:15. The service begins at 7. Priests will hear until everyone has been heard. The Manor bus would bring its residents for a quarter past six.

Second, at twenty to eight, after the homily and the examination and the common prayer, there would be six stations instead of four: the three priests who had begun at a quarter past six, Father Hartmann himself and Father Francis Otto from Coon Rapids, who would arrive for the service, and Father Tomás Ruiz from Denison, who would drive over for the evening because his own parish's service was already done, and who would hear in Spanish as well as English. That last mattered. The meatpacking plant at Denison had employed people from Mexico and Guatemala and Honduras for sixty years, and their children and grandchildren farmed and taught and ran businesses all over the county, and some of the grandmothers who came to the penance services had made their first confessions in Spanish and wanted to make their last ones in it too.

Third, and this was the cost. The two priests who were to begin at a quarter past six had Tuesday evening Masses at half past five, Father Chukwuemeka Eze at Templeton and Father Anh Nguyen at Saint Walburga's. Their Masses would move to eight on Tuesday morning instead. Rota had looked for any other way and found none.

Rota rang the regulars of both Masses on Sunday afternoon. At Templeton it was nine people, and seven of them said that was fine. At Saint Walburga's it was twenty-two, and most of them said it was fine, and Mrs. Darlene Pudenz, who worked the early shift at the hospital laundry and came to the half past five Mass every Tuesday on her way home, said it was not fine, she could not come at eight in the morning, and she had not missed the Tuesday Mass in eleven years, and then said, after a pause, that she supposed it was for the confessions, and that she supposed she would go to Breda for those, and hung up before Rota could thank her.

Rota had run the plan the same way it had run Thursday, a thousand Tuesdays with random lengths of confession. It had assumed that the early start would draw about thirty of the hundred, spread over the three quarters of an hour, and that the rush after the homily would be fifty in twenty minutes instead of seventy, with the remaining twenty spread over an hour.

"Under those assumptions," it wrote in the attachment, "the average wait is about four minutes, and the longest wait of the evening about thirteen. Without the early start, six priests give an average of about ten and a longest of about twenty-five. With the early start but only five priests, about six and about nineteen. Each assumption is a guess about how people will behave, which is the thing I got wrong on Thursday. If nobody comes early, the plan is six priests and a rush, which is better than Thursday and still not good. I'd like to say plainly that I don't know which it will be."

Father Hartmann read it on Sunday after the last Mass, standing in the sacristy in his rose chasuble with the phone in one hand.

"Fine," he said to the phone. "And nobody hurries."

"Nobody hurries," said Rota.

"And tell Albertine Weitl the monsignor's in the side chapel."

"The Manor knows," said Rota. "She's first on the bus."

Tuesday the sixteenth was colder than the Thursday, with a hard wind across the fields and a few dry flakes in it that did not settle. Bernie drove to Breda at half past five with the coffee urn in the back of the car and Owen Reicks in the passenger seat with his clicker in his coat pocket.

At a quarter past six the church was nearly empty. The lamp was lit in the side chapel, a reading lamp from the rectory with a green shade, on a little table beside a chair with arms, and the monsignor sat in it in his purple stole with a rug over his knees, behind a screen with a kneeler in front of it. Father Eze and Father Nguyen were in the two confessionals on the south wall, with the little lights above their doors switched on.

Nobody came for six minutes. Bernie stood at the back with her hands round a coffee cup and thought about how she would explain it to the deanery.

Then the Manor bus pulled up outside, late behind a grain truck on the county road, and eleven people got off it, slowly, with walkers and sticks and the driver's arm, and came in, and Albertine Weitl went straight up the side aisle to the chapel, and the monsignor stood up when he saw her, which he was not supposed to do, and sat down again. And behind the bus, two pickup trucks, and a minivan with a family from Halbur, and a man in a seed-company jacket who looked at his watch and went straight to Father Nguyen's door and knocked on it, as if it were a real door.

By seven, Owen's clicker said thirty-one. There were five people still waiting in the north aisle when the service began, and they stayed in their places through it and went first after the homily.

Father Eze preached, because it was his turn. He preached on the voice crying in the wilderness, and he was brief, and he said at the end, as Father Hartmann had at Saint Walburga's, that there was no hurry.

At twenty to eight the six stations opened. Father Ruiz had a screen and a kneeler in front of the baptistery, and a small card on a music stand beside it that said English · Español. The rush came, as the monsignor had said it would, and it was a rush, and the lines grew down both side aisles to the fifth pew and then the sixth. Bernie felt the old fear come up in her.

They got to the sixth pew and stopped. And then, slowly, came back.

Owen's clicker said eighty-three at eight o'clock. The line on the north side was at the fourth pew. At a quarter past eight it was at the second. The woman from the food pantry was there again, not to confess, she told Bernie on the way to the coffee urn, but to see. At half past eight there was nobody waiting, and the priests' lights were still on, and the people who came after that (one or two at a time, a nurse in her scrubs, a farmer, a young couple with a sleeping baby in a car seat) went straight to whichever light was free.

Mrs. Darlene Pudenz came at ten to nine, still in her coat, and went to Father Nguyen.

The last absolution was a few minutes before nine. Owen's clicker said a hundred and four.

"Was it the plan," said Bernie, "or was it luck?"

"Both," said Rota, from her phone, quietly, because they were still in church. "More came early than I guessed, which helped. From what you and Owen saw, nobody waited much more than a quarter of an hour, which is about what the plan said. I don't know how long anybody was with a priest. I'd like to keep not knowing."

"And next year?"

"Next year I'll keep the timestamps instead of the total," said Rota. "And the light on at a quarter past six."

The priests came out one at a time and stood in the back of the church drinking coffee from paper cups. Father Ruiz had to drive back to Denison and left first, shaking everybody's hand. Father Nguyen told Bernie that it was the first penance service in eight years where he had been able to feel his knees at the end of it. Father Eze said nothing, and drank two cups of coffee, and smiled at the floor.

Father Hartmann had heard in the north confessional all evening, and came out last but one. He stood by the font with his stole still on and watched the side chapel.

"Nobody hurried," he said to Bernie.

"Nobody."

"Good."

The Manor bus was outside again with the engine running, and the residents went out to it, Albertine Weitl last, holding the driver's arm, and stopping at the door to say something to Father Hartmann that made him laugh and then not laugh.

The monsignor was still in the side chapel. He had stayed sitting with his stole on, though there was nobody waiting, because, he said afterwards, you never knew. Then he folded the stole and put it in his jacket pocket and folded the rug and put it on the chair, and he reached over to the little table and switched off the reading lamp with the green shade. In the dark of the church there was only the sanctuary lamp, red, steady, the way it always was, and the sound of the bus outside, waiting, with its engine running, until he was on it.

The science. A. K. Erlang, an engineer at the Copenhagen Telephone Company, published his formula for the probability that a call must wait for a free operator in 1917, in the Danish journal Elektroteknikeren ('Solution of some problems in the theory of probabilities of significance in automatic telephone exchanges'). For a queue with random (Poisson) arrivals, random (exponential) service times and c servers, offered a load of A erlangs (arrival rate times mean service time), the queue has a steady state only if A is less than c; at A = c it does not settle, which is the story's 'four priests' worth of work'. The Erlang C formula then gives the chance a person waits, P, and the mean wait is P times the mean service time divided by (c − A). At 60 penitents an hour and 4 minutes each, A = 4: with 5 servers P is about 0.554 and the mean wait about 2.2 minutes; with 6, P is about 0.285 and the mean wait about 0.57 minutes; with 7, about 0.135 and 0.18 minutes. Erlang's formula assumes arrivals are steady on average; a rush is a different problem, and the story's later figures come from simulation.

The model. Every figure is invented and the story's own. Service times are exponential with a mean of 4 minutes; each penitent goes to the first free priest; 4,000 simulated evenings per case. Thursday: confessions open at 19:40; 70 penitents arrive uniformly at random in the first 20 minutes (3.5 a minute, or 14 priests' worth of work at 4 minutes each) and 30 in the next 70 minutes. With 4 priests, the evening's mean wait is about 24 minutes, the longest wait about 48 minutes (half the evenings between about 38 and 58), the line peaks at about 48 people, and the last confession ends about 106 minutes after 19:40, about 21:26 (the story's 21:24 is one such evening). With 6 priests and the same arrivals, the mean wait is about 10 minutes and the longest about 25. For comparison, if the same 100 arrived steadily at random over 100 minutes, 4 priests give a mean wait of about 5 minutes and a longest of about 14 over that one finite evening, and the queue would keep wandering if the evening went on. Tuesday's plan: 3 priests from 18:15 and 30 penitents arriving uniformly from 18:15 to 18:55; from 19:40, 6 priests, 50 arrivals in the first 20 minutes and 20 in the next 60. The mean wait is about 3.8 minutes and the longest about 13.5; with 5 priests from 19:40 (3 of them early), about 5.8 and 18.9; with 4 (2 early), about 11.8 and 30.9. On the night the door count is 31 before seven and 104 in all, more than the plan assumed, and the story does not claim the plan's figures came out exactly.

The Pot on the Cold Stove

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Senan's Hall (invented), near Kilrush, County Clare, Ireland, a Saturday evening in May 2049 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish cook leaves twenty litres of ham-and-barley soup on the cold stove to cool before it goes in the fridge, as her mother taught her, ready for a Confirmation lunch on Sunday. The hall's assistant asks when it came off the heat, has her probe it (53 degrees at nine) and works the cooling curve: a lidded pot on a stove falls from 90 to 63 in about two and a half hours and is still 32 degrees at six in the morning, hours inside the range where bacteria grow. It cannot call the soup safe or unsafe, but it can say the clock is still short enough to save it, and she stands the pot in a sink of running cold water, stirring, until it is under 21 degrees at about ten to ten.

Noreen Hegarty had cooked for every Confirmation in the parish since 2031, and she did not put hot food in a fridge.

"It'd warm everything else in it," she said. "Mammy never did, and nobody died."

"That is true of a great many dinners," said the hall's assistant, from the shelf above the hatch. It had been asked to print a menu for the Sunday lunch, and had printed it, and was now asking, as pleasantly as it knew how, what time the soup had come off the heat.

Noreen looked at the clock over the hatch. It said five to nine. She looked at the pot, which sat with its lid on, on the stove ring she had switched off at five, with a tea towel over the lid because the draught from the back door was bad.

"Five," she said. "I switched it off at five. Twenty litres of ham and barley. It's for seventy-two at one o'clock tomorrow, after the Mass, and the bishop's own cousin is coming, so don't tell me it wants more salt."

"I would not dream of it," said the assistant. "May I ask you to do something with the thermometer in the drawer, the probe one the sodality bought for the barbecue? Push it into the middle of the pot, not touching the side, and read me the number."

Noreen found it. She lifted the lid, and the steam that came up was a lot less than she expected. She pushed the probe into the barley and waited for the little screen to stop changing.

"Fifty-three," she said, and she did not like the sound of her own voice saying it.

The assistant did not say unsafe. It said what it had in front of it, and then what it did not have.

"Here is the arithmetic, and it is rough, because I have not measured your pot. Twenty litres of soup is about twenty kilograms, and it takes a little under four kilojoules to warm a kilogram by one degree, so the pot holds about eighty kilojoules per degree. A lidded pot on a cold stove in a room at eighteen degrees loses heat through its sides and lid at roughly four watts for every degree it is hotter than the room. I had to guess that figure; it could easily be three or six. Eighty thousand joules per degree divided by four watts is twenty thousand seconds, or about five and a half hours. That is how long the pot takes to lose most of its excess, and it is slow, because a big pot has very little surface for its volume.

"If it came off at ninety, then at nine o'clock it should read about fifty-three. It does read fifty-three. That is not proof the guess is right, but it is a comfort I did not have a minute ago.

"Now the part that matters. Bacteria that spoil soup, and the ones that make people ill, grow between about eight degrees and sixty-three. The ones I worry about with meat and grain are Clostridium perfringens and Bacillus cereus. At around forty-five degrees the first can double in ten minutes or so. The pot passed sixty-three at about twenty to eight. If I leave it where it is, at six tomorrow morning it will be about thirty-two degrees, and it will have spent all night inside the range. The lid does not help; it keeps the heat in, and heat is what is keeping it in the dangerous band."

Noreen had sat down on the stack of blue chairs. "I've left soup out all night before."

"Perhaps you did, and perhaps you were lucky, or perhaps it was a cold night in a cold hall, which cools a pot faster than this room does. I do not know how it went. I only know that tomorrow is seventy-two people, some of them very old and some of them very young, and the bishop's cousin."

She asked it, then, the thing that a person asks when they are tired.

"Is it ruined? Say it's ruined and I'll tip it and do chips."

"I cannot say it is ruined, and I cannot say it is fine, and I do not want to guess either way for you. The rule that food kitchens work from, in several countries, is about the total time in the growing range. Guidance differs: some say four hours at most, some say cool from about fifty-seven to twenty-one degrees within two hours, then down to five within another four. The hall committee wrote the second version on the board in the kitchen, I notice, in 2046. By that rule the pot passed fifty-seven at about twenty-five past eight, so you have until about twenty-five past ten to have it under twenty-one. It is five to nine. That is an hour and a half.

"I think that is enough. I will tell you how, and I will tell you what it costs, and you can still decide to tip it."

The how was the sink. It was a deep double stainless one, put in by the men in 2040, and the assistant had her fill the left side half full from the cold tap, which came out of the ground at about eleven degrees, and stand the pot in it with the lid off, and leave the tap running into it with the overflow open, and stir.

"Stirring matters," it said. "It brings the hot middle to the wall, where the cold water is. If you do not stir, the middle stays hot and only the edge cools, and the probe will fool you. I am going to guess that a stirred pot in running tap water will lose heat at forty watts a degree instead of four. That is ten times the surface effect for the same pot. Eighty thousand divided by forty is two thousand seconds, about thirty-three minutes. To go from fifty-three to twenty-one, with the water at eleven, takes about one and a half of those, so about forty-nine minutes. You would be done at about ten to ten."

"And the water?"

"About six litres a minute from that tap, I think, so about three hundred litres over the hour. That is a lot of water and I will not pretend otherwise. It is less costly than seventy-two lunches. It will also hurt you, because you will be standing there until ten, and the tap will make the end of the kitchen cold."

Noreen got up off the chairs. She said what her mother would have said, which was something short in Irish, and she did the thing.

At twenty past nine the probe said thirty-six. At nine thirty-five, twenty-seven. She had not expected the middle to fall so fast, and she said so, and the assistant said the first half of any cooling is faster than the second, because the pot was hotter relative to the water, and the last six degrees would take longer than the first twenty.

"What happens to the soup when you do this?" said Noreen. "Does it taste of it?"

"It tastes of ham and barley. The water does not touch it, and the barley will have soaked up a little more of the liquid by tomorrow either way. If anything it will want a cup of stock when you reheat it."

At ten to ten the probe said twenty. Noreen read it twice. Then she rang the sexton, Tommy Dolan, who had a key to the hall and a house on the road, and Tommy came in his coat over his pyjama top, and between them they moved the Confirmation juice off the second shelf of the parish fridge and ladled the soup into six shallow plastic tubs, a little over three litres each, because the assistant said a deep tub of cool soup would take most of the night to get down to five, and six shallow ones would take a good deal less. I cannot give you the hours; the fridge's own thermometer can.

"Don't stack them," it said. "Leave a hand's width between them for the first hour. Then you can stack."

"You're sure?" said Tommy, who had been told only that Noreen needed a lift for some tubs.

"I am sure that it is better than the pot on the stove," said the assistant. "I am not sure it is all right. At one o'clock tomorrow, heat it until the middle of every pot is seventy-five degrees, and hold it there, and stir. Reheating kills the bacteria. It does not undo everything they may have made while they were growing, and I do not know exactly how long this soup spent above twenty-one, because the first hour was a guess. If anyone at the table is pregnant, very frail, or has a weak immune system, I would put a plain sandwich beside the soup and not say why."

Noreen said she would not put a sandwich beside the bishop's cousin.

"Then put one beside Mrs Callinan, who is ninety-three," said the assistant. "That is not about the bishop."

She wrote it on the board in the kitchen, under the committee's rule, in marker, where the next cook would see it:

Off the heat? Into the sink. 57 to 21 in two hours. Stir. Tap running.

And under that, smaller, because it was true and she was a person who liked a thing to be true: Not the stove. Not the step. Mammy was lucky.

Then she turned off the tap, which she had forgotten for the last ten minutes, and the room went quiet except for the fridge taking up its new load. Tommy held the door, and the assistant said goodnight to the room, and the last light went out on the pot upside down on the rack, still wet.

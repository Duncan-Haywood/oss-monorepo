Martin of Tours and Half a Cloak

A bedtime story about Saint Martin of Tours, the young soldier who met a freezing beggar at a town gate and cut his own cloak in two, as his friend Sulpicius Severus tells it. With a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is legend. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 11th of November.

A long time ago, in a country that is now called France, there was a young soldier named Martin.

Martin rode a horse and carried a sword. He wore a long red-brown cloak, the kind that soldiers wore, thick and warm, which he could pull around his shoulders when the wind blew. He was not an unkind soldier. His friends said he was gentle, and patient, and that he shared what he had. But he was a soldier all the same, and he had not yet thought very hard about what he really wanted to be.

This is the story of the day he thought about it.

It was winter, and it was a bitter one.

The ground was iron. The puddles were stones of ice. The birds, it was said, had stopped singing, and the water in the horse troughs froze overnight. People stayed indoors with their fires, and anyone who had no fire stayed as close to someone else's as they dared.

One grey morning Martin rode toward the gate of the town of Amiens. His breath came out of his nose in clouds. His horse's breath did too. He was wrapped up in his cloak, and still his fingers ached.

And there, by the gate, sat a man.

He had hardly any clothes. He had a thin shirt, and a rag or two, and that was all. His lips were blue. His hands were wrapped in his armpits, and he shook so hard that his teeth went tick, tick, tick.

People went by. They always do.

A woman with a basket hurried past, looking at her feet. Two merchants went by, talking about the price of wool. A boy ran past and then, being a boy, stopped and stared, and then his mother called him and he ran off. Everyone could see the man. Nobody stopped. Perhaps they thought, someone else will help. Perhaps they thought, I have so little myself. Perhaps they were only cold, and in a hurry.

Martin stopped his horse.

He looked at the man, and the man looked at him. And Martin thought, quite clearly: I have nothing left to give except this cloak.

For Martin had already given away the rest. He had a sword, and a horse, and the cloak, and not much else. And the cloak, he knew, was not entirely his. It was part of a soldier's uniform. He could not give it all away, and keep his place.

But he could not ride past, either.

So Martin did a clever and a kind thing.

He drew his sword. The beggar, who had perhaps never been so frightened and so cold at the same time, watched with big eyes. But Martin did not use the sword against anyone. He took hold of the edge of his own cloak, and he cut it clean in two, from top to bottom.

Shhhk.

He held out one half.

"Here," he said. "Take this. It's warm."

The beggar took it. His hands were so cold that at first he could hardly close them on the cloth. Then he pulled it round his shoulders, and the shaking began to slow. Tick... tick... tick. And at last he looked up and he smiled.

Martin wrapped the other half around himself, and rode on through the gate.

Some people at the gate laughed.

"Look at the soldier," they said. "He has half a cloak! It's ragged, and it won't keep out the wind. He looks ridiculous."

And it is true that he did. Half a cloak is not much of a cloak. But I think Martin did not mind very much. He had a feeling inside him, a small warm feeling, like a coal in a stove. It was better than any cloak.

Other people, who had been looking at their feet, looked up. They watched him ride by. And some of them felt a little ashamed, and a little glad, and maybe they went home and found a blanket for someone.

That night, Martin went to sleep.

And in his sleep, he dreamed. He saw Jesus, standing among a crowd of angels. And Jesus was wearing the half of a cloak. It was a red-brown soldier's cloak, cut clean down the middle. Martin knew it at once.

And Jesus turned to the angels and said, "Martin, who is not even baptised yet, has covered me with this cloak."

Martin woke up. It was morning. He understood something that he had never understood before. What you do for someone who is cold, you do for Jesus. Just as Jesus himself had said: Whatever you did for the least of these, you did for me.

Soon afterwards, Martin was baptised. In time he left the army, and became a monk, and then a bishop, the bishop of the city of Tours. For the rest of his life he was known for being patient and for helping the poor. But it all began with one very cold morning, and a sword, and half a cloak.

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about it.

Martin did not have very much to give. He did not give a great deal. He gave half of what he had in his hands, right then, to the person right in front of him. He did not wait until he was rich. He did not wait for someone else to do it first.

You may not have a cloak. But you might have half a sandwich. You might have a seat on the bench, or a turn on the swing, or a kind word for someone who looks left out.

And that is a very good way to be a little lamp.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Martin, you saw the cold man at the gate.
Help me to see the people who are cold, or lonely, or left out.
Give me a kind hand and a brave heart, and let me share what I have.
Keep everyone warm tonight, in our house and in every house.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Martin of Tours (about 316 to 397) was a real person: a Roman soldier born in what is now Hungary, baptised as a young man, later a monk and then bishop of Tours in Gaul. His feast day, 11 November, is an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar. He is a patron of France, of soldiers and of beggars, and Martinmas customs (lantern walks, geese, gifts of bread) are still kept in much of Europe.

The cloak is told by a contemporary, but it is still a story. The account comes from the Life of Martin by Sulpicius Severus, who knew Martin and wrote it in his lifetime or soon after his death (around 397). That is a far earlier source than the legends of many saints. It tells of the winter day at the gate of Amiens, the sword, the half of the cloak, the mockery of some bystanders, and the dream in which Christ wears the cloak and speaks of Martin to the angels. Historians treat the details with care, as the text is a work of devotion as well as biography, but we have followed it closely. Sulpicius says the cloak was a military one and that Martin had nothing else on him but his weapons and his clothes, which is why he divided it; the idea that half the cloak already belonged to the army appears in later tradition, and we have only hinted at it with "not entirely his".

The dream is retold in paraphrase, not quoted; the line from Jesus about "the least of these" is Matthew 25:40. The grey morning, the iron ground, the boy who stared, the woman with a basket, the teeth going tick, tick, tick, and the coal in the stove are our own additions for a bedtime story. The closing thoughts and the prayer are ours too; the prayer is not an approved text.

The Joint That Is Still Pink

Science fiction about what might be · Ménesfalva (invented), Csík basin, Harghita County, Romania, Thursday 11 October 2068 to Monday 22 April 2069 · 22 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A mason in a Székely village in Romania's Csík basin has raked her grandfather's 1974 cement out of a church tower and repointed it in lime. Two days before the parish council chairman's daughter's wedding he presses a thumbnail into a joint and says it has set, and the scaffold is booked for another parish's leaking roof on Monday. Lime hardens by taking carbon dioxide from the air, from the face inwards, and the hard crust grows as the square root of time, so a joint twice as deep takes four times as long. A drill, a white card and a pink spray show five millimetres of crust on the north face, an agent works out which faces can lose their scaffold and when, and the first hard frost in the basin comes on the Tuesday. A story about lime, phenolphthalein, frost hollows and what one parish owes another.

Ilona Ferenczi had spent eleven weeks raking out her grandfather's work, and on the Thursday before the wedding the chairman of the parish council climbed the scaffold to tell her she could stop.

Lajos Tankó was sixty-four and had been a forester for forty years, and he climbed like one, without hurrying and without looking down. He came up the ladders on the north face of the tower of Saint Michael's in Ménesfalva, past the eighth lift, where the hessian sheeting began, and lifted the flap and ducked in under it into the brown light where Ilona was working.

"Good morning," he said. "God bless the work."

"God bless you," said Ilona. She was on her knees on the boards with a pointing iron, brushing out the last of a joint by the louvres of the belfry. It was the eleventh of October 2068, and cold up there in the shade, and the tower smelled of wet sacking and stone.

Lajos took off a glove and pressed his thumbnail into the nearest joint, the way you would press an apple to see if it was ripe. It did not mark. He did it again in another joint, harder, and looked at his thumb.

"It's hard," he said.

"It's hard on the outside," said Ilona.

"Ilona." He put his glove back on. "I'm not here to argue. I'm here because Father Szilárd rang me again last night from Fenyőkút. Their roof let in water over the sanctuary on Sunday. The scaffold firm comes on Monday at seven to take this down and put it up round their nave. That was the agreement in June. And on Saturday my daughter is getting married in this church, and I would like her photographs to have a tower in them, not a parcel."

"I know about Saturday," said Ilona. "I'm coming."

"Then help me. Take the sacking down on Friday. On Monday they take the tubes. It's set. You said yourself, in August, when you did the south side, that lime goes off in a few days."

"I said it stiffens in a few days," said Ilona. "That isn't the same thing. Your thumb's found the part that's dry. Dry isn't set."

Lajos looked at her, and then at the tablet clipped to the scaffold tube beside her, which had a small green light on it.

"And what does the machine say?" he said.

"The machine," said the tablet, in a woman's voice, in the accent of the county radio, "says good morning, Mr Tankó, and that the north face was finished twelve days ago, so I would not trust a thumbnail on it, but I would trust a drill."

The tower had been built in the fifteenth century of rubble stone and lime, rebuilt after the Tatar raid of 1694, and pointed in Portland cement in the summer of 1974 by a mason of thirty named Áron Ferenczi, who had done it with care, with a jointing iron he had made himself, and with the best material the cooperative could get him.

That was the trouble. The cement had been harder than the stone, and it did not let water through. Rain that ran into the cracks around a cement joint could not get out through the joint, so it went out through the stone beside it, and in winter it froze there. In ninety-four winters it had broken the faces off hundreds of stones, a few millimetres at a time, so that the cement now stood proud of the wall in hard grey ridges, like the leading of a window whose glass had worn away. On the north side, which had no sun from October to March and caught the driven snow off the Hargita, whole corners of stones had come away, and behind the cement there were pockets that went in as deep as Ilona's hand.

She had been sixteen when her grandfather died. She had carried his buckets every summer from the age of nine, and she still had the jointing iron. She had used it, this summer, to knock out his cement.

"He'd have been angry," she had said to Father Gábor in July, the first week, when the first barrow of cement came down the ladder.

"Would he?" said the priest.

"For a day," said Ilona. "Then he'd have wanted to know why. He always wanted to know why. He just didn't have anyone to ask."

The county heritage office in Csíkszereda had paid for the scaffold and the hessian and lent her an agent to keep the record, which was a condition of the grant. She called it Mérce, which means a measure or a gauge, because it had asked her on the first day what she would like to call it and that was the first word that came. It kept a photograph of every joint before and after, the date each was raked and each was filled, the weather on every face of the tower every ten minutes from four little loggers she had wedged into the joints, and the sand and the lime of every batch. It was very good at the record. It was also, she had found, very good at listening while she explained a thing, and then asking the one question that showed whether she had explained it or only said it.

The mortar was lime putty, slaked in a pit behind the sacristy in April, and sharp washed sand from the Olt, three of sand to one of putty. Lime putty is calcium hydroxide and water. It does not set the way cement sets, by reacting with the water in the mix. It sets by taking carbon dioxide out of the air and turning back, slowly, into calcium carbonate, which is limestone again: the stone it was burned from. The carbon dioxide has to get in through the face of the joint. So the mortar turns to stone from the outside inwards, and the hard crust gets thicker the way a crust does when what feeds it has to come through the crust first: quickly at the start, then more and more slowly, as the square root of the time.

"Say that to me as if I were the bishop," Mérce had said in July.

"The crust grows like the square root of the days," said Ilona. "Four times as long for twice as deep. A joint that's two fingers deep takes four times as long to go right through as a joint that's one finger deep."

"And a pocket the depth of your hand?"

"Don't ask me that in July," said Ilona.

Lajos did not wait for the drill. He went down the scaffold, and ten minutes later Ilona heard him in the south porch, below, with Emőke.

Emőke Csiszér was nineteen and had been Ilona's labourer since June, on the heritage office's apprentice scheme, and she knew everything about the job that could be found in a video and about half of what could not. Ilona went down to see.

They had the sample panel out of the porch. It was the board Ilona had made in July with the first batch: a piece of old roof slate with a strip of the mortar laid on it, two centimetres thick and a hand wide, set on the porch bench so that the heritage inspector could see the colour of the mix against the stone. Lajos had broken the end off the strip with a cold chisel. Emőke had the little spray bottle from the van, the one with the red cap.

"I showed him," said Emőke, a little anxiously. "He asked me what the spray was for."

"It's phenolphthalein," said Ilona. "Go on, then."

Emőke sprayed the broken face of the strip. Phenolphthalein is a dye that stays clear on anything neutral or mildly alkaline and turns a bright pink on anything strongly alkaline, and lime putty that has not yet taken up its carbon dioxide is strongly alkaline. The broken face stayed grey. In the middle of it, where the strip was thickest, a faint pink thread came up, as thin as a hair, and nowhere else.

"There," said Lajos. He was not pleased with himself; he was relieved. "That's from July. And the south face is from August. A month. Six weeks."

"That's from July," said Ilona, "in the porch, out of the rain, two centimetres thick, open on the top and both sides and the end. It's had the air from four sides in a dry porch for thirteen weeks. The tower's had it from one side, in the rain, in a joint with stone on both cheeks and more mortar behind it." She took the broken piece and turned it in her fingers. "This is a good sample of the colour, Lajos. It's not a sample of the tower."

Lajos looked at the pink thread.

"Then show me the tower," he said.

They did it at two, when the light was best on the north side, which meant there was some.

Ilona chose a joint that Mérce said had been filled on the Saturday of the feast of Saint Michael, the twenty-ninth of September, the face layer of one of the deep pockets low on the belfry stage. She put a masonry bit in the small battery drill and a strip of tape round the bit at two millimetres from the tip, and held a white card under the joint, and Emőke stood with the spray.

She drilled two millimetres. The dust fell on the card, pale grey, a pinch of it. Emőke sprayed it. It stayed grey.

Ilona tipped the dust off over the edge of the boards, moved the tape, and drilled to four. Grey.

At six millimetres the dust on the card went pink before the spray had finished falling: the pink of a child's sweet.

Lajos leaned in and looked at it for a long time.

"Five millimetres," said Mérce. "Perhaps a little under. Twelve days."

They went round the tower. On the south face, finished on Friday the seventeenth of August, fifty-five days before, the dust stayed grey to ten millimetres and went pink at twelve. On the west, finished the Friday after, the dust from ten millimetres came up with a faint pink in it, more grey than pink. On the east, finished on the seventh of September, the dust from ten went pink at once. Mérce took the four readings and the dates and fitted them, while they stood on the boards, and said that they all agreed with one number: a crust that grew about one and four-tenths of a millimetre times the square root of the number of days, on this tower, in the weather it had had.

"Which is slower than the book," said Mérce. "The books I have give figures for laboratory samples that are about this or faster. This tower has had a wet September."

"So it'll go on getting harder," said Lajos.

"Yes," said Ilona.

"How long until the deep ones are hard right through? The ones you showed me in July. The ones like your hand."

Ilona looked at the tablet. She had not asked it in July.

"The deepest pockets on the north face go in seventy-five millimetres," said Mérce. "At this rate the crust reaches seventy-five millimetres in about two thousand eight hundred and seventy days. Seven years and ten months. The ordinary joints, at twenty-five millimetres, take about three hundred and twenty days, a little under a year. Twice as deep is four times as long. Three times as deep is nine times."

Lajos laughed, not unkindly. "Eight years. My granddaughter will be at school. If I have one by then. You can't keep a scaffold up eight years, Ilona."

"No," said Ilona. "And it doesn't need to be stone right through. The middle of half the old walls in this county is lime that has never seen the air. My grandfather showed me once, in a barn wall they took down near Szereda: the core was still soft after two hundred years. You could scratch it with your nail. The wall stood up fine. It stands up because it's in compression and because it's thick. The middle doesn't have to be stone." She put her palm flat on the wall. "What it can't have is frost in it while the face is soft and wet. Water in soft lime freezes and pushes the face off. Then the rain gets in behind and does it again. That's what happened to his stones. I'm not having it happen to my joints."

"And when is the face not soft?"

"I was taught: when the crust is thicker than your thumbnail. A centimetre. Before a hard frost, keep it covered and keep it dry until it's got a centimetre. That's a rule of thumb. It's not in the book either."

"So when does the north face get a centimetre?" said Lajos. "Machine."

"About the nineteenth of November," said Mérce. "A Monday. If October and November are like September. They will probably be colder, and lime takes up carbon dioxide more slowly when it is cold, so that is the earliest, not the latest."

"And the frost?"

"The forecast for the basin," said Mérce, "is clear skies from Monday and a hard frost on Tuesday night into Wednesday. Minus seven at Csíkszereda. Colder in the hollows."

Ménesfalva was in a hollow. On a still clear night the cold air ran down off the Hargita and lay in the bottom of the basin like water in a bowl, and the village was at the bottom of the bowl. Everyone in the Csík knew it. Lajos had spent his working life watching young firs die in hollows like it.

He stood for a while on the boards with his hands in his pockets.

"No," he said at last. "I'm sorry, Ilona. Not the scaffold. Fenyőkút has a roof open over their altar and snow coming in two or three weeks. If water gets into their vault this winter they won't have a vault. I'm not keeping eleven tons of tubes up here so that your mortar can have a blanket while their church falls in. I gave my word in June."

"I know," said Ilona.

"Do you? You're looking at me as if I'd said it about your grandfather."

"I'm looking at you," said Ilona, "because you're right, and I don't know what to do."

She went home and did not sleep, and at half past ten she sat up in bed and said to the tablet on the chest of drawers, "Mérce. How many bays does Fenyőkút need?"

"Their roof scaffold," said Mérce, "needs twenty-eight bays on the firm's drawing. The scaffold on this tower is forty bays, ten to a face, up to the belfry."

"Ten to a face," said Ilona. "Forty less ten is thirty."

"Yes."

"South has its centimetre now. West will have it by Sunday."

"The fourteenth, at this rate," said Mérce. "And the east by the twenty-eighth of October."

"The east gets the morning sun. And it's the lee side; the snow comes from the north-west. I could hang quilts down the east face from the parapet, on ropes, without a scaffold. I couldn't do it on the north. The north has the louvres and the deep pockets and it's a hundred and forty joints and it's where the wind is." She was sitting on the edge of the bed in the dark with her feet on the cold floor. "Can a scaffold stand on one face of a tower on its own?"

"Not on its own," said Mérce. "Tied into the wall, it can. Yours is tied in already, every second lift, on every face. The firm would need to brace the ends of the north run where it now turns the corners, and check it, and it would need an inspection each week while it stood. I can't tell you whether they would agree to that. I can show you the clause in the hire agreement about part-dismantling, which is clause eleven, and the price for part-hire, which is per bay per week."

"How much?"

Mérce told her. Ten bays for five weeks was not a great deal of money, as scaffolds went. It was more than she had.

"Then it's thirty bays to Fenyőkút on Monday," said Ilona, "and ten stay on the north till the nineteenth of November, and somebody pays for ten bays for five weeks, and I hang quilts on the east."

"Shall I write it down for Mr Tankó?"

"Write it down for Mr Tankó," said Ilona, "and for Father Szilárd. And for the firm. Put the drill readings in. Put the photos of the cards in. Don't put in that I'm sitting on my bed in my socks."

"I wasn't going to," said Mérce.

Lajos read it on Friday morning at the kitchen table of the parish house, with his glasses on the end of his nose and the wedding seating plan pushed to one side, while Father Gábor made the coffee and Ilona stood by the stove because she could not sit down.

"You've asked the firm," he said.

"Mérce sent it to them at seven. They rang at half past eight. They'll brace it if they can come back on Monday afternoon after they've loaded the thirty. They want a man there who knows the tower."

"And Fenyőkút?"

"Father Szilárd wants twenty-eight," said Ilona. "He gets thirty. He said God would reward us, and asked if he could have Emőke for a week as well."

Lajos took his glasses off.

"And the ten bays for five weeks."

"I'll pay half," said Ilona. "I've done the sums. I can pay half."

"No, you can't," said Lajos. He folded the paper in four and put it in his shirt pocket. "The parish pays it. The council meets on Sunday after Mass and I'll tell them, and they'll agree because I'll tell them it was my idea. It costs about what the second day of a wedding lunch costs in this village, which I'm told is a disgrace and which I'm going to stop paying for at about four o'clock on Sunday afternoon, when Réka's aunts from Gyergyó go home." He picked up the seating plan. "And the sacking on the north side stays up on Saturday."

"Yes."

"Nobody photographs the north side," said Father Gábor, pouring. "The door's on the south. The light's on the south. The bride's on the south. The north side of a church has looked after itself for five hundred years."

"It has not," said Ilona. "That's the point."

The wedding of Réka Tankó and Hunor Bálint was on Saturday the thirteenth of October, and it was a clear cold day with the larches on the hills going yellow and the sky over the Hargita the colour of a jay's wing.

Ilona sat at the back, in her good coat, between Emőke and a cousin of the groom from Szereda who wanted to know whether she was the one who had done the tower. From where she sat she could see, through the south windows, the sun on the scaffold boards on the south side, and the new joints between the stones, cream against the grey, and she thought that her grandfather's ridges had been the colour of a pavement and these were the colour of the stone's own dust, which was what a joint ought to be: the part of a wall you are not meant to notice.

She did not hear much of the sermon. She was thinking about the north face, in the shade, under its brown sacking, with five millimetres of stone on the outside of a hundred and forty joints and the rest still lime, still taking in the air through the face, a little each hour, whether anyone was watching or not.

The photographs were taken in the south porch and on the steps and under the walnut tree by the gate, with the tower behind, scaffolded and sunlit, and Réka said afterwards that she liked it better like that, because it looked like a church that was being looked after. The bells rang them out.

On Monday the firm came at seven with two lorries and took down the south, the west and the east, in that order, and by four o'clock the tower stood with its scaffold on one face only, braced at the corners, like a man with a ladder propped against one side of him. Ilona and Emőke hung the quilts on the east before dark: six of them, the grey insulated blankets that builders use to keep fresh concrete from freezing, each one lowered from the parapet on two ropes and tied off at the bottom to pegs driven into the ground, with Emőke in the belfry and Ilona on the ground shouting, and Mérce telling them which quilt was hanging crooked from the logger behind it, which was cheating and which Ilona allowed.

Under the quilts on the north face she taped a second logger to the wall beside the deep pockets, behind the hessian and the blankets, and went home.

The frost came on the Tuesday night, the sixteenth, as the forecast had said, and it came harder than the forecast.

Ilona woke at five without an alarm and looked at the tablet. Outside, the logger on the open tube of the north scaffold read minus nine. Behind the blankets on the north face the two loggers read plus one point eight and plus one point five. On the east face, behind the quilts, five loggers read above freezing and one, at the north-east corner, read minus three.

"Since when?" she said.

"Since about one," said Mérce. "It went down quickly after one. I think the quilt at the corner has moved. The wind was from the north-west before midnight. I didn't wake you. I couldn't have done anything with you awake in the dark at one o'clock on the east face except make it two of us that were cold."

"Next time wake me," said Ilona.

"Yes."

She went up in the dark with a head torch. The scaffold tubes on the north side had a fur of frost on them, and her gloves stuck to the ladders. At the eighth lift the hessian was white and stiff and crackled when she lifted it, and the blankets behind it were stiff, and she lifted the blanket over the deep pockets by the belfry with both hands and put her torch on the wall.

The joints were dark and damp, and when she touched one with her bare fingertip it was cold, and it was not frozen.

She stayed there on her knees on the boards for a little while, with the blanket held up on her shoulder, and her breath going up in the torchlight.

Then she climbed down and walked round to the east, and at the north-east corner the bottom of the quilt had come loose from its peg and swung out a hand's width from the wall, and the joints along the corner for two metres up were white with frost on their faces, every one.

The north scaffold came down on Monday the nineteenth of November, the day Mérce had said, because the drill said the crust on the north face was nine millimetres and still going, and the forecast after that was snow, and Ilona decided that nine millimetres and a covering of snow was better than ten millimetres and a scaffold that Fenyőkút had now asked for twice. She wrote that down for the heritage office as a decision, with her name on it, and Mérce put it in the record.

The snow lay on the hollow from the end of November until March. The tower stood under it, grey and cream, the way it had stood under snow for five hundred years, and nobody looked at it.

On Monday the twenty-second of April 2069, the day after the Sunday of Divine Mercy, with the snow gone from the basin and the larches showing their first green, Ilona went up a ladder to the north-east corner of the tower with the drill and the white card and the pointing iron.

Fourteen joints on the corner had lost their faces: not deep, the depth of a fingernail, crumbled and loose like wet sugar, along the two metres where the quilt had swung out on the night of the sixteenth of October. She raked them out to twenty-five millimetres and brushed them and wet them, and filled them with lime from the pit behind the sacristy, and struck them off with her grandfather's iron.

Then, because she wanted to know, she drilled one of the old joints next to them, from the north face, from the Saturday of Saint Michael.

The dust stayed grey to twelve millimetres, and to fourteen. At sixteen it came down on the white card pink.

"Fourteen and a bit," said Mérce, from the tablet in her coat pocket. "In two hundred and five days. Slower than the autumn. The cold."

"It's still going," said Ilona.

"Yes. At the winter's rate, sixteen years to the back of the deepest pockets. At last autumn's, eight. Somewhere between, if the summers are like last summer."

"They won't be," said Ilona. "They never are."

She held the card up in the sun and looked at the small heap of pink dust on it, which was lime that had not yet seen the air, from the inside of a wall she had built, and which would go on turning back into stone, a little each hour, long after she had stopped climbing ladders.

Below her in the village, the noon bell began.

She put the card in her pocket, and kept it.

The chemistry and the numbers: lime putty (calcium hydroxide) hardens by carbonation, taking carbon dioxide from the air and becoming calcium carbonate, from the exposed face inwards, and the carbonated depth grows roughly as the square root of time, d = k√t, because the carbon dioxide has to diffuse through the layer that has already carbonated. Phenolphthalein is clear below a pH of about 8 to 9 and pink above, so on a fresh broken face or on drill dust it shows uncarbonated lime as pink and carbonated mortar as unchanged; drilling in steps onto a white card is a common way to read the depth without breaking a wall. The story's k = 1.4 mm per square root of a day is an assumption the characters measure on their own tower, chosen to sit at the slow end of what the author recalls of published figures for air-lime mortars (very roughly 1 to 3 mm per square root of a day at moderate humidity in the laboratory); real rates depend strongly on the mix, the humidity, the temperature and the joint, which is why the story measures rather than quotes. A 10 mm crust takes (10/1.4)² ≈ 51 days: the west reaches it on 14 October, the east on 28 October, the north on 19 November. A 25 mm joint takes (25/1.4)² ≈ 319 days, about 10½ months; a 75 mm pocket ≈ 2,870 days, about 7 years 10 months; twice the depth is four times the time and three times the depth nine times. In April the story's measured 14 mm after 205 days is below the 20 mm the autumn rate would give, as it would be after a winter, since carbonation slows in the cold. The "centimetre before a hard frost" is a character's rule of thumb, not a standard.

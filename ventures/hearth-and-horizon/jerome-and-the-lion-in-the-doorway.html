Jerome and the Lion in the Doorway

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · A monastery near Bethlehem, about sixteen hundred years ago · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Jerome, the grumpy, clever scholar who turned the Bible into the language of ordinary people, and the old tale of the lion with a thorn in its paw who came limping into the monastery and stayed to do an honest job. With a note for grown-ups saying plainly that this tale was first told of a different saint, Gerasimus, and was only later given to Jerome. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 30th of September.

A long time ago, in a hot, dry country with stony hills, there was a monastery near the little town of Bethlehem. It was the very town where Jesus was born, and the monks who lived there liked to say that they lived next door to the stable.

One of the monks was called Jerome.

Jerome was not like a monk in a picture, with a round, smiling face. Jerome was thin and brown and quick, and he was a little bit grumpy, especially when someone made a mistake in their writing. But he was also one of the cleverest people in the whole world. He knew Latin, which the people of Rome spoke every day. He knew Greek. He had even learned Hebrew, which is not an easy language, and which very few of his friends could read at all.

And with all those languages, Jerome had a big job. He was turning the Bible into Latin, so that ordinary people could hear God's words in the words they used at the market. It took him years and years. He sat at a table with a lamp and a pen and a great many scrolls, and he wrote, and he crossed things out, and he wrote again.

Now one evening, when the sun was going down behind the hills, the monks were sitting together in the courtyard, listening to Jerome read aloud.

And in through the gate, slowly, slowly, there came something that was not a monk.

It was large. It was golden. It walked on four soft feet, and it was limping.

It was a lion.

Oh, how those monks jumped up! One of them dropped his bowl. Two of them ran for the back door. A third one climbed up on a barrel and said, "Goodness gracious," which is not what you would think a monk would say, but it is what he said.

Only Jerome stayed where he was.

He did not shout. He did not run. He looked at the lion, and the lion looked at him. And the lion did not roar. It did not show its teeth. It simply stopped in front of him and held up one front paw, just a little, as if to say: please.

Jerome got down on his knees, slowly, the way you do with a nervous animal. He looked at the big paw.

And there, deep in the soft part, was a long, sharp thorn. It had gone in and stayed in. The paw was sore and swollen, and each step that the lion took must have felt like stepping on a pin.

"Ah," said Jerome softly. "Poor old fellow. That is a very bad thorn."

He called to the others, who were peeping around the doorway. "Come and help me. He will not hurt us. He has come for help, and so we shall help."

The monks came back, one by one, on the tips of their toes. They brought warm water. They brought a clean cloth. Someone found a little pot of oil. And Jerome took hold of the thorn, gently, gently, and with one quick pull, out it came.

The lion made a small noise, something like hrrrm. Then the monks washed the paw, and rubbed it with oil, and wrapped it up. And the lion lay down in the courtyard in the last of the warm sun, with its big head on its other paw, and went to sleep.

In the morning, when the monks got up, the lion was still there.

It did not go back to the hills. It followed Jerome about the monastery, a few steps behind him, like a very large and very quiet dog. When he sat to write, it lay down by the door. When the bell rang for prayer, it came and sat at the back, and it did not make a sound.

"I do not think he wants to leave," said one of the monks.

"Then he had better have a job," said Jerome. "In this house, everybody works."

Now, the monastery owned a donkey. It was an ordinary grey donkey, and its job was to carry things: wood, water, bundles of grain. Every day somebody led it out to the green place by the stream to eat grass, and somebody had to sit with it, and keep watch, so that no one would take it away.

"Lion," said Jerome, "that will be your job. You will guard the donkey."

And the lion, who understood a good deal more than you might think, got up and went off with the donkey, and sat beside it by the stream all day long.

The donkey was not at all sure about this at first. But after a while it decided that a lion is a very comfortable thing to stand next to, and it went on eating its grass.

One hot afternoon, the lion went to sleep.

It was only a little sleep. A lion who has guarded a donkey since sunrise is allowed a little sleep. But while it slept, some traders came walking along the road with their camels, and they saw a donkey all by itself, with nobody near it that they could see. And they took it. They tied it to the back of the line and they went away down the road.

When the lion woke up, the donkey was gone.

The lion looked left. It looked right. It sniffed the grass. And then it put its head down and followed the smell, and it did not stop until it caught up with the traders on the road.

It did not growl. It did not leap. It simply walked, calmly, right to the front of the camels, and took the end of the rope in its teeth. Then it turned, and it led all of the camels, with the donkey in the middle, back the whole way to the monastery gate.

The traders, who were not brave people, followed along behind with their mouths open. They did not dare to say a word.

The monks saw them coming from far off. They came running to the gate, and Jerome came too, and when he saw the lion standing there with the camel rope in its teeth and a very serious face, he began to laugh.

"Well!" he said. "He has not only found the donkey. He has brought us some camels."

The traders were so ashamed that they said sorry, over and over. They gave the monastery a big flask of olive oil to make up for what they had done. Jerome thanked them, and he let them go home in peace.

And the lion got a very good dinner that night, and the biggest welcome of its life. It lay by the lamp while Jerome wrote his long, careful pages, and its tail moved a little on the floor, which is how a lion says it is happy.

Jerome grew very old. He went on writing for most of his life, until the whole Bible, in a language that people could read, was finished. And that is why, if you ever see an old picture of Saint Jerome, you may notice a lion lying at his feet, quietly, like a good friend.

The lion in the picture does not look fierce at all. It looks like someone who knows that a thorn can be taken out, and a sore foot can be mended, and that if you are kind to a creature in trouble, it may be very good to you in return.

And now the lamp is low, and it is time for little ones to be still, like a lion in a doorway, with a head on its paw and a warm tail.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Jerome, you loved God's word
and worked hard so that everyone could read it.
Help me to listen when it is read to me,
and to be gentle with any creature that is hurting.
Keep us all safe tonight, and awake in the morning
ready to help.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Jerome (about 345 to 420) was born in Stridon, on the border of what is now Croatia and Slovenia, studied in Rome, lived for a time as a hermit in the Syrian desert, and from about 386 lived in Bethlehem, where he founded a monastery and spent most of his remaining years. He translated the Bible into Latin from the Hebrew and Greek, a work that became the basis of the Latin Vulgate. He is a Doctor of the Church and the patron saint of translators and librarians. His feast day is 30 September. He was, by his own letters, a man of strong opinions and a short temper; we have mentioned this gently.

What is legend, and told as legend: the lion. The tale of a lion with a thorn in its paw, who guards a monastery's donkey and fetches it back from thieves, appears in the older sources about a different saint, Gerasimus (died 475), an abbot in the Jordan valley, in the Spiritual Meadow of John Moschus. In later centuries it was attached to Jerome, probably through the similarity of the names Gerasimus and Hieronymus, and it was repeated in the Golden Legend (thirteenth century). That is why Jerome is shown with a lion in art. We have told it as an old tale about Jerome, and say here that it began somewhere else. It is not found in Jerome's own writings. The scene of the monks' fright and the details of the courtyard are ours.

What is ours: the scenes, the conversations, the donkey's opinion of the lion, the olive oil, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Dates for Jerome are approximate, since the sources differ on his birth year.

The Cut Tiles at the Wall

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Colman's vestry (invented), near Gort, Co. Galway, Saturday 9 September 2051 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish is retiling a vestry floor of 3.45 by 2.62 metres with 300 mm tiles. The assistant counts rows and columns instead of dividing areas, centres the pattern so no cut is a sliver, and finds that ten boxes cover the area on paper and are three tiles short in practice.

Brid Naughton had measured the vestry twice and written the answer on the back of a Mass sheet: 3.45 × 2.62 = 9.04 m². Tile 0.09 m². 101 tiles. The tiles came in boxes of eleven. She asked the parish assistant, whose name was Hale, whether ten boxes would do, since ten boxes was a hundred and ten and she did not like to be left over.

"Ten boxes is more than your sum," said Hale. "But I would like to count the floor before I count the area. A floor is laid in rows, and the walls do not care what the area is."

"The walls are what they are."

"Yes. Along the long side you have 3,450 millimetres and a tile pitch of 300. That is 11.5 tiles. Across it you have 2,620, which is 8.73. Neither is a whole number, so every row ends in a cut tile, and the cuts are where the extra tiles go."

She said she would start from the door corner and work out, as her father had. Hale said it would work, and showed her what it left: eleven whole tiles along the long wall and one strip of 150 millimetres against the far wall, and nine rows with the last one 220 deep. "A strip that narrow is the first thing the eye finds. It also breaks, because a tile cut that thin will not hold the blade."

"What would you do?"

"Centre it. Put a joint on the middle line of the long side and each wall gets the same: five whole tiles and three-quarters of another, so a cut of 225. Across the room, centre a tile on the middle line, so each side has three whole tiles and a cut of 260, which is the biggest you can get for that width. Nothing is thinner than 225."

Brid said that was ten whole tiles and two cut ones along the length, twelve in a row, and nine rows, and Hale said that was the sum. "Twelve by nine is a hundred and eight positions, not a hundred and one. Of those, seventy are whole: ten across and seven down the middle. Fourteen are the 225 cuts at the two short walls, twenty are the 260 cuts along the two long ones, and four are the corners, cut both ways. Thirty-eight cut tiles."

"Then a hundred and eight," said Brid. "Eleven boxes would be silly. Ten boxes is a hundred and ten, and two to spare."

Hale did not agree, and said why. A wet saw does not make a cut tile into two, only into one good piece and one offcut, and every offcut here was a sliver: 75 millimetres from the 225s, 40 from the 260s. None of them could be used. So each of the thirty-eight cuts spent a whole tile. And the cutting was where tiles broke.

"What do your tilers lose?"

"Nobody has told me. Say one cut in twelve snaps, which is a guess, and ask Mr Doran what he really loses." Thirty-eight over twelve was a little over three. "Two spare against three expected is not enough, and it is the last box of a run that you cannot get again, because tile from another batch can be a shade different. Eleven boxes gives you thirteen spare. I would buy eleven, and keep the thirteen for the day someone drops a bucket of water on the vestry floor."

She said her father would have bought ten and gone back to the shop. Hale said her father would also have had a thin strip against the far wall, and she laughed, which Hale took to be agreement.

Before she ordered, Hale asked her to dry-lay one row, with no adhesive, from the middle line to the wall, with the centre tile where the chalk was. She did it that evening with a spare box from the sacristy. The row fell where the sum said it would, with the last gap at the wall just wide enough for a thumb, and she laid a cut tile in it to see how it looked.

It was a plain tile with a straight edge that nobody would look at. She chalked 225 on the wall, and 260 on the long one, and ordered eleven.

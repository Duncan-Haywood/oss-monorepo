The Censer That Swings Slow

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Hedwig (invented), Ring Two of Calder Habitat (invented), Earth–Moon L5, Holy Saturday 9 April 2191 · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

On a spinning habitat with Mars-like weight, the thurible swings in 2.5 seconds instead of 1.6, the dropped snuffer lands sixteen centimetres behind where it fell, and the incense must get past a smoke detector and a fire officer who will not switch it off. The Easter Vigil needs all three answered by dark.

Ines Okafor-Reyes was thirteen, and the censer was too slow.

She stood in the sacristy with the thurible's chains wrapped twice round her fist and swung it, the way she had swung it eleven times in the practice videos from Earth. Out, back, out. The brass bowl took its time. It rose at the end of each swing and seemed to hang there thinking about it.

"It's like swinging it under water," she said.

"It is swinging in a little under four-tenths of the weight it was built for," said Keel, from the grille over the vestment press. Keel ran Ring Two's air, water and lights, and had been asked by the pastor, three weeks before the Vigil, to help the servers with anything the videos don't cover. "The ring turns once every forty-six seconds. At two hundred metres from the axis that pushes you onto the floor with 3.73 metres per second squared, which is 0.38 of Earth's, because that is the weight the builders chose. A pendulum's swing time goes with the square root of its length over the weight. With the chain at sixty centimetres, on Earth it swings in 1.55 seconds. Here it swings in 2.52."

"So it's slow."

"It's slow by about a second a swing, and you have been counting in the Earth rhythm, so you pull it early and it arrives late. That is why the smoke is coming out in lumps."

Ines looked at the sacristan, Mr Adjei, who was sixty-eight and had been born on the ground and had not been back since the third year of his contract. He turned the palm of his hand over, meaning he's right, I'm afraid.

"Two things we can do," said Keel. "You can learn the slow rhythm, which is no shame, and the habitat's own children do. Or we can shorten the chain so it swings the old way. The weight and the swing time fix each other: for 1.55 seconds at 3.73 metres per second squared, the chain should be 23 centimetres. That is about a third of what you have."

"That's a handle," Ines said. "That's not a censer."

"Then learn the slow rhythm," said Mr Adjei. "The Church has waited twenty centuries for a thurible to swing like that. It can wait for you to count one-and-two-and."

The nave of Saint Hedwig's ran along the ring's width, not around it, and that had been an argument at the time. It was twenty-four metres long and twelve wide, and the first parish council had wanted the altar at the end of the long side, facing the way the ring turned, because that was how the plans looked best. The engineers had said no. A thing that moves around the ring, in the direction of spin, or against it, feels the ring turning under it and is pushed up or down, a little. A thing that moves along the axis feels nothing of the sort. So the aisle ran along the axis, the sanctuary at one end and the doors at the other, and the congregation faced the way the ring does not turn.

Ines learned this on the Friday, in the dress rehearsal, by doing it wrong.

The snuffer was the long brass kind, on a pole, and Tomás Varga, who was nine and a candle-bearer for the first time, dropped it. He was holding it upright, level with his eyes, about one metre eighty from the floor; it fell, and everyone looked at the place directly below it, and it was not there. It rang on the floor sixteen centimetres towards the sanctuary's left wall, which was the side the ring was turning from.

"Who moved it?" said Tomás.

"The floor did," said Keel. "When you let go, the snuffer stops being pushed by the ring. It keeps going at the sideways speed it had at your hand, which is 27.1 metres per second round the ring, and the floor is going 27.3. The floor is two-tenths of a metre per second ahead of it, and the fall takes 0.99 of a second. So the floor moves sixteen centimetres ahead before it gets there. You see it as the snuffer falling off to one side. You are not seeing a force. You are seeing the floor go by."

"It still looks like a force," said Ines.

"It acts like one. There is a name for it, after a Frenchman, and you may use it with the seminarians. But for the Mass the point is smaller: what you drop lands sixteen centimetres away, in the direction against the spin, from a standing height of a metre eighty. That is a candle, a book, a small bell. It is not something you can catch by putting a hand underneath."

"What about the Hosts?" Tomás's voice had gone small.

"Father Paulo will have the answer for the Hosts," said Keel, "and it will be a better one than mine. We practise with the paten held low."

By Saturday morning the censer swung well enough that Ines stopped counting aloud. It had taken forty minutes of walking the length of the nave and back with an empty bowl, and the trick was to wait: the chain was long and unhurried, and so should she be. Mr Adjei had turned the long walk into a rhyme. Lift, and let it come. Lift, and let it come.

At noon Marta Lindqvist, the habitat's fire officer, came to the chapel with a clipboard, and she was not smiling.

"The vigil begins with the new fire, and I have no quarrel with the new fire, because it is outside in the corridor on a tray, with a person next to it and an extinguisher. I have a quarrel with the censer."

"The smoke detectors in the nave are the optical kind," said Keel. "I can tell you what I expect, and I will be straight about what I do not know. A charcoal tablet in a thurible smoulders, and the resin on top burns to a white smoke that scatters light very well, which is just what those detectors are made to see. I expect them to alarm within a minute of the first swing, and then the alarm doesn't just sound in the chapel. It sounds on all of Ring Two, and starts the dampers closing on the air loop, and I would have to rouse four hundred and ten people."

Father Paulo, who was young and stooped from years of low ceilings in the ring's early sections, said, "Could the detectors be switched off for an hour?"

"No," said Marta. She said it before Keel could answer. "No, Father. I am sorry, and I'm not going to be sorry for long: a detector that is off is the reason there was a ring before this one. This isn't a rule I wrote. Everyone here lives inside one volume of air. If something burns in this chapel the only warning that arrives before the smoke is that sensor. I won't put it to sleep for a ceremony, even this one."

There was a silence in which the air handlers could be heard.

"I can offer something else," said Keel. "I would like to be clear that I cannot promise it works, and that I have no figure I trust for how much carbon monoxide a tablet makes in this chapel's air. I have read the manufacturer's sheet and I do not believe it is for habitats. So the monitor is what we trust, not my arithmetic. What I can propose is a trial."

The trial was on Saturday at three, with the nave empty. Mr Adjei lit one charcoal tablet in the thurible in the corridor, because it wanted to be lit in the open and given a few minutes to go grey at the edges, and Ines carried it in at the pace of someone carrying a full cup. Marta stood at the door with her hand on the red panel. Father Paulo put on the resin, a small spoon of it, and the smoke began.

It rose, slowly, in the lower weight, and hung. Keel said that the plume was about half as lively as it would be on the ground, because smoke goes up only because the warm air round it is lighter than the cool, and the lighter air here was being pushed upward by 0.38 of the usual push. It did not rise to the ceiling in seconds. It stood in the air above the thurible like a loaf.

The first detector alarmed at forty seconds.

Marta pressed the panel before the bell had finished its first stroke. The rest of the ring never knew, because the Chapel of Saint Hedwig was a zone with a cut-out of its own, and nobody had realised until Keel read the wiring list aloud. The alarm sounded in the nave and in the corridor. In the whole of Ring Two a single amber light came on in the control room.

"That is the failed first attempt," said Keel, "and a good one, because it taught us three things. The detectors will trip at a minute. The alarm stays in the zone if the zone's cut-out is set. And the smoke stands rather than climbs."

"What do you propose?" said Marta, who had stopped being angry and begun being busy.

"A lower charcoal. Half a tablet, broken, which burns cooler and gives less. A third of the resin. Three swings only at the Gospel and the Gloria, and the Easter acclamation, not the whole Vigil. The nave's air handler up one step, which clears the plume to the return in about a minute and a half. The detectors stay on. If one trips, it trips; Ines stops, the extinguisher is in Marta's hand, and the Vigil goes on in the dark for ten minutes with the candles, which is exactly what it was doing yesterday."

"And the carbon monoxide?"

"The chapel has a monitor on the wall. I am going to read it out to Marta every five minutes, and she will have it on her tablet, and if it climbs past the figure on the sheet, which I can at least read to you, she stops the censing, and that is her decision and not mine."

Marta looked at the detector, at the thurible, at the thin line of Ines's shoulders. "Half a tablet. Three swings. My hand on the panel. And a second person at the door who is not the pastor."

"I'll be the second person," said Mr Adjei. "It's a long time since anyone asked me to do something that sensible."

The Vigil began in the corridor at nine. The new fire was a small one, on a steel tray, and the people stood round it in the cool and the dark, four hundred and some, with the children's hands cupped round their candles. The light came down the corridor in a long line, the way it always did on the ring, because the corridor curved gently up at both ends and the people at the far ends could see their neighbours' flames above their heads, a hundred metres off, climbing the far wall.

Ines walked beside the pastor, the thurible in her left hand with the half-tablet glowing pink inside it. She had never carried anything so lightly. On the third step she let the chain go long, and out, and the bowl rose in its slow arc, and she did not pull. She waited. Lift, and let it come. It came.

At the Gospel she gave three swings, and the white smoke stood above the book like a small cloud, and the lector's voice went through it. The detector in the ceiling did not chirp. Marta's hand was flat on the red panel, and her eyes were on the tablet in her other hand, and when Keel's voice said, very quietly, four parts per million, as before, she moved her thumb away from the glass.

At the Gloria the bells rang and Tomás Varga, the nine-year-old, tipped over his candle. It landed sixteen centimetres from where he had aimed his hand. He picked it up and held it very straight, with a look of enormous gravity, and Father Paulo, who was singing, did not miss a note.

On the Easter Sunday morning, before the first Mass, Ines went to the sacristy and took a piece of chalk, and on the inside of the door, where the servers kept their notes, she wrote it down in her careful hand:

Weight is 0.38. Thurible swings in 2.5 seconds. Wait for it.

Things you drop land sixteen centimetres behind. Hold the paten low.

Detectors stay on. Marta's hand on the panel. Half a tablet.

Under it, smaller, because she had not been told to, she wrote I did it.

Calder Habitat, Saint Hedwig's chapel, Ring Two and everyone in them are invented, and a habitat of this kind is a thought experiment, not a plan: I know of none under construction. The arithmetic: for a floor at radius r spinning at angular rate w, the apparent weight is w squared times r. Choosing 0.38 g (3.73 m/s squared) and r = 200 m gives w = 0.1365 rad/s, 1.30 revolutions per minute (a turn in 46 s) and a rim speed of 27.3 m/s. A pendulum's period is 2 pi times the square root of length over g: at 0.60 m that is 1.55 s at 9.81 m/s squared and 2.52 s at 3.73; for 1.55 s at 3.73 the length is 0.227 m. Apparent weight drops with height towards the axis (at 1.8 m above the floor it is 3.69, about 1 per cent less), which is too small to matter here. Dropping a snuffer from 1.8 m with no sideways speed relative to the floor: in a straight line in the non-rotating frame it reaches the floor at radius 200 m after 0.989 s, during which the floor has turned through 0.1351 rad and the snuffer's position through arctan(0.1351) = 0.1342 rad, a difference of 0.00083 rad, or 0.166 m, behind the direction of spin. Motion along the axis feels no Coriolis effect, so placing the aisle along the axis is a reasonable design choice; motion around the ring changes the apparent weight by about 2 w v, which for a swinging thurible with a peak speed of about 1.2 m/s is about 9 per cent of g. Whether a ring could be spun this slowly, at 1.3 rpm with a 200 m radius, without making people sick is something I am quoting from memory (the figure usually cited for comfort is about 2 rpm, and the exact limit is not settled), and incense in a sealed habitat would be an engineering question, not a pastoral one: I know of no authoritative figure for the carbon monoxide produced by a thurible tablet and have invented none, which is why the story relies on the chapel's monitor. In a real habitat, the fire officer's authority over the detectors would be set out in the station rules. Easter 2191 falls on Sunday 10 April, so the Vigil is Saturday 9 April.

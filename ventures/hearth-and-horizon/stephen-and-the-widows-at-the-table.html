Stephen and the Widows at the Table

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Jerusalem, in the first months of the Church, nearly two thousand years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Stephen, told from the Acts of the Apostles. When some widows in Jerusalem are being missed at the daily sharing of food, the apostles ask the friends of Jesus to choose seven helpers, and Stephen is the first. He makes sure nobody is left out, speaks bravely before the council with a face like an angel's, and forgives the men who hurt him. His death is told in one gentle line. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 26th of December, the day after Christmas.

A long time ago, in the city of Jerusalem, there was a big family that was not really a family at all.

They were the friends of Jesus. Jesus had gone back to heaven, and now his friends lived together in the city and looked after one another. They prayed together. They broke bread together. And anybody who had more than they needed shared it with anybody who had less.

Every single day, there was food to be handed out: bread, and oil, and lentils, and whatever else had been given. And it went most of all to the widows.

A widow is a lady whose husband has died. In those days, a widow often had no one to earn money for her, and so the friends of Jesus made sure that every widow had her share. Every day. Without fail.

At least, that was the idea.

Now, the friends of Jesus in Jerusalem spoke two different languages.

Some of them spoke the language of their grandmothers, the language of that land. Some of them had grown up far away, in other countries, and they spoke Greek. They all loved Jesus. They all prayed to the same God. But when you speak different languages, it is very easy not to notice somebody.

And so it happened that some of the Greek-speaking widows were being missed.

Not on purpose. Nobody meant it. But the bread came round, and the oil came round, and somehow, day after day, it did not quite reach them. Imagine sitting at a long table, and the plate goes past you, and past you, and past you again, and nobody sees.

The Greek-speaking friends of Jesus noticed. And they grumbled about it, which is what people do when something is not fair. (The Bible says they murmured, which is a lovely word for a grumble that goes round and round a room.)

The twelve apostles heard the murmuring. And they called everybody together.

"We have been given a job by Jesus," they said. "Our job is to pray, and to tell everybody the good news. If we stop doing that to hand out bread, we will not be doing our job. But this needs doing, and it needs doing properly. So choose seven men from among you. They must be people everybody trusts, full of the Holy Spirit and full of wisdom. And we will give them this job."

Everybody thought that was a very good idea.

So they chose seven. And the first name on the list was Stephen.

The Bible says that Stephen was "a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit". It also tells us the names of the other six: Philip, and Prochorus, and Nicanor, and Timon, and Parmenas, and Nicolaus, who came from the city of Antioch. (That is a great many names for a bedtime story. You do not have to remember them all. The Bible did, though, because each one of them mattered.)

The seven stood in front of the apostles. The apostles prayed, and laid their hands on them. And the seven went to work.

We do not know exactly how Stephen did his job. The Bible does not tell us. But we can think about what a good helper would do.

A good helper would not just stand at the front with the basket. A good helper would walk all the way down the table, to the very end, where the quiet people sit.

A good helper would learn the names. This is Martha. This is old Chloe, who cannot see very well. This is the lady who came from Egypt last spring and does not know anybody yet.

A good helper would ask, "Did you get your bread today?" And if the answer was no, he would go and get it, right away, and say sorry.

Whatever Stephen did, it worked. The Bible says that after the seven were chosen, the good news kept spreading, and more and more people joined the friends of Jesus. When a family stops leaving people out, other people want to belong to it.

And the Bible says one more thing about Stephen. It says he was "full of grace and power", and that God did great wonders and signs through him among the people. The man who carried the bread was also the man through whom God was doing marvellous things. In God's family, those two jobs are not so far apart.

Now, not everybody in Jerusalem liked what Stephen was saying about Jesus.

Some men came to argue with him. They argued and argued. But Stephen answered every question with so much wisdom that they could not win. And when people cannot win an argument fairly, they sometimes try to win it unfairly.

So they found some people to tell lies about Stephen. And they brought him before the council, the great gathering of the leaders of the city, and they said, "This man says terrible things!"

Everybody in the council turned to look at Stephen.

And here is the thing the Bible tells us. It says that when they looked at him, they saw that his face was like the face of an angel.

He was not frightened. He was not angry. He was calm, and bright, as if he were standing somewhere very safe.

Stephen stood up, and he spoke for a long time. He told them the whole story of God's people, all the way from Abraham to Moses, and how God had never stopped calling them, and how Jesus was the one God had promised.

But the men who were already angry did not want to hear it. They grew angrier and angrier.

And then Stephen looked up.

He looked past the walls, and past the roof, and the Bible says he saw the glory of God. He said, "Look! I see the heavens opened, and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God."

He could see Jesus. And Jesus was standing up, as if to welcome him.

The angry men took Stephen out of the city. They did a very wrong and cruel thing that day: they hurt him so badly that he died.

But before he died, Stephen did two things.

First, he prayed: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit." That means, Jesus, I am coming to you. Please hold me.

And then he knelt down, and he prayed in a loud voice, for the very men who were hurting him: "Lord, do not hold this sin against them."

He forgave them. Right there. He asked God to forgive them too.

And then the Bible says that Stephen "fell asleep". That is the Bible's gentle way of saying that he died, and went home to Jesus, who was standing ready to welcome him.

There is one more person in this story, and he is standing at the side.

He is a young man called Saul. The men who hurt Stephen had laid their cloaks down at his feet, and Saul was minding the cloaks, and he thought what they were doing was right.

But Stephen had prayed for all of them.

And not very long after, on a road to a city called Damascus, a bright light shone round Saul, and Jesus spoke to him, and his whole heart turned round. Saul became one of the friends of Jesus. You may know him by his other name, which is Paul, and he travelled further than anyone to tell the world about Jesus.

Nobody can say for certain what part Stephen's prayer played in that. But the Church has always thought it was not nothing.

Now it is nearly time to sleep, so let us think about this.

The friends of Jesus chose Stephen to carry bread. It was not a grand job. It was a walk-to-the-end-of-the-table job. But Stephen did it so well that we still remember him, two thousand years later, on the very first day after Christmas.

You can do Stephen's job too. You can look round the table, at school, or at a party, or at home, and see who has not had a turn. Who has not been asked to play? Who is sitting at the end, where the plate does not reach?

And if somebody is unkind to you, you can do the harder thing that Stephen did. You can ask God to forgive them. It is one of the bravest things a person can say.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Stephen, you made sure nobody was left out.
Help me to notice who is missing,
and to share what I have.
When somebody is unkind to me,
help me to forgive them, the way you did.
Jesus, please keep everyone I love safe tonight,
and everyone who has nobody to share with them.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our sources. Almost everything here comes from the Acts of the Apostles, chapters 6 and 7, with the first verses of chapter 8. Quotations follow the wording of the Revised Standard Version, lightly shortened. We worked from the biblical text; the tradition notes below draw on the Catholic Encyclopedia article "St. Stephen" (1912) and the English Wikipedia article and the sources it cites. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

The widows and the Seven. Acts 6:1 says that "the Hellenists murmured against the Hebrews because their widows were neglected in the daily distribution". "Hellenists" is usually taken to mean Jewish believers whose first language was Greek, and "Hebrews" those who spoke Aramaic; the story's "two different languages" follows that reading. The apostles' words are shortened from 6:2-4, including "pick out from among you seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom". The seven names, Stephen as "a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit", and the prayer and laying on of hands are 6:5-6. That the word of God increased and the number of disciples multiplied is 6:7. "Full of grace and power", with "great wonders and signs among the people", is 6:8.

Were they deacons? Acts does not use the noun "deacon" for the Seven, though it uses the related Greek words for the "distribution" and for serving tables. From early centuries the Church has seen them as the first deacons, and Stephen is honoured as the first deacon as well as the first martyr. The story calls them "helpers".

The council. The disputes, the hired witnesses and the charge are 6:9-14; the face "like the face of an angel" is 6:15. Stephen's long speech (7:2-53) is told in one line. Every person in this part of the story, Stephen and his accusers alike, was Jewish, as were the apostles; the story speaks of "some men" and "the angry men", as Acts speaks of particular people, and we would ask anyone retelling it to do the same.

His death. "I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing at the right hand of God" is 7:56 (the story says "Son of Man"). That Jesus is standing, where elsewhere he is said to be seated, has long been read as Jesus rising to receive his martyr; the story's "as if to welcome him" is that reading, not the text. Acts 7:58-60 says Stephen was stoned; we have not described it, in keeping with the shelf's rule that a martyrdom is told in a quiet sentence. "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" and "Lord, do not hold this sin against them" are 7:59-60, followed by "And when he had said this, he fell asleep." We explained "fell asleep" so that no child takes ordinary sleep for death.

Saul. The witnesses "laid down their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul" (7:58), and "Saul was consenting to his death" (8:1). His conversion on the Damascus road is Acts 9. The link between Stephen's prayer and Saul's conversion is an old reflection, found in Saint Augustine's sermons among others, and the story marks it as something nobody can say for certain. Saul and Barnabas have their own story on this shelf, Barnabas and the Friend Nobody Trusted.

After. Acts 8:2 says devout men buried Stephen and made great lamentation over him. The finding of his relics at Kafr Gamala in 415, described in a letter of the priest Lucian, is the history of a tradition and is left out of the story. "Good King Wenceslas", who looked out "on the feast of Stephen", is a carol by J. M. Neale (1853), and Wenceslas has his own story here.

His feast. 26 December, a feast in the General Roman Calendar, the day after Christmas. In some places it is also called Boxing Day.

What is ours: the long table and the plate that goes past, the widows' names (Martha, Chloe and the lady from Egypt are invented), everything Stephen does in the section that begins "We do not know exactly how Stephen did his job" (marked in the story as our picture of a good helper), all speech beyond the lines quoted or closely paraphrased from Acts, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

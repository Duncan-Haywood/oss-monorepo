The Hundredth Word

Science fiction about what might be · Peel, Isle of Man, Tuesday 4 January to Laetare Sunday, 13 March 2067 · 23 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A grandmother in Peel wants to follow the Gospel when her eight-year-old granddaughter reads it in Manx on Laetare Sunday, and asks the class's agent for the shortest word list that will do it. Its five hundred most frequent words cover two running words in three, and on the Third Sunday of Lent she hears every "and" and "to him" and misses the fig tree. Zipf's long tail says the list alone would need sixteen thousand words; the class's oldest speaker says nobody learns a language from a list, and teaches her that week's fifty-three words by telling her the story.

Kathleen Corlett was sixty-four, and had spent thirty-one years selling ferry tickets in the booking office on the Douglas quay, and in all that time she had learnt to say two things in Manx: moghrey mie, good morning, which went on the boards over the counter, and gura mie eu, thank you, which she said to the regulars because it made them smile.

She did not count the Lord's Prayer. She had learnt that the way she had learnt her bus routes, as a string of sounds, the autumn Breesha started at the evening class, so that she could say it beside her at the evening class and not stand there with her mouth shut. Ayr ain, it began. She knew ayr was father because Breesha had told her. She did not know which of the other sounds were words.

Breesha was eight, and had been at the Bunscoill Ghaelgagh in St John's since she was four, and spoke Manx the way she spoke English, without noticing which one she was in. On the first Sunday of the new year, at the class in the room over the net loft on East Quay, Voirrey Kinvig had asked her if she would read the Gospel on Laetare Sunday, in the middle of Lent, when the class kept its Lenten evening with soup and the reading and a bit of singing, and Breesha had said yes before her mother could say she had swimming.

On the Tuesday after, at the kitchen table in the house on Michael Street, with the rain going sideways past the window towards the castle, Kathleen opened the class's agent on her tablet and asked it the thing she had been turning over since Sunday.

"Lioar," she said. "What's the shortest list of words I can learn by the thirteenth of March so I can follow what Breesha reads?"

The class had named its agent Lioar, which was Manx for a book, because it had started as the thing that held their books: the old Bible, the hymn sheets, the readers the Bunscoill had made over sixty years, a century of radio programmes typed out. Now it marked their homework and answered the phone for the class and kept the list of who was bringing soup. It spoke in a woman's voice with no accent anyone could place, and it answered in English unless you asked it not to.

"The thirteenth of March is the Fourth Sunday of Lent," Lioar said. "This year is Year C, so the Gospel is the lost son, from Luke fifteen. I can tell you which words matter most in Manx in general. Do you want the reason with the list, or just the list?"

"The reason. I'll not learn a list I don't understand."

"I've counted every word in every text the class has," said Lioar. "About one million three hundred thousand running words, the way you'd count them on a page: every the each time it comes. Among them there are about twenty thousand different forms."

"That's a lot of words."

"It's a lot of forms. Some of them are the same word wearing a different first letter. I'll come back to that." A little chart came up on the screen, a line falling steeply from the left and then lying almost flat along the bottom for as far as the screen went. "This is the thing that makes your question answerable. Line the forms up by how often they come: the commonest first. The commonest is used about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, ten times as often as the tenth. A word's share of the text goes roughly as one over its place in the line. A Harvard linguist named George Zipf set it out at length in a book in 1949, and it has been found in nearly every language anybody has counted, in English and Chinese and Latin. It's an approximation. Real counts bend away from it at both ends. But in the middle it holds well enough to plan with."

"And what does it plan?"

"It says the top of the line is worth a great deal. On an ideal count like ours, the ten commonest forms are about twenty-eight in every hundred running words. The top hundred are about half. The top five hundred are about sixty-five in a hundred." Numbers came up under the line. "Nine weeks to the thirteenth. Five hundred words is about fifty-five a week, eight a day. That's a good deal of work, but people do it. I'd put the top five hundred on cards for you, commonest first, with the English on the back and Breesha's voice saying each one, if she'll record them."

Kathleen looked at the sixty-five.

"So two words in three I'd know."

"On the average page of everything the class has. Yes."

"And the third word I could guess from the other two."

"Sometimes," said Lioar.

She thought about the booking office, where she had learnt early that if you knew the fares to Liverpool and Heysham and Belfast you could answer two calls in three without looking anything up, and the third call you could put on hold.

"Do the cards," she said.

The class met on Thursdays at half past seven and on Sunday evenings after the six o'clock Mass, upstairs in the old net loft that its owner let to them for a pound a year. It was a long room with a sloping ceiling and a window at the end that looked across the harbour at the red walls of the castle on St Patrick's Isle. There were eleven of them that winter. Most were in their forties, people who had moved to the island for work and fallen for it, and a fisherman's widow from Port Erin who came on the bus, and two lads from the college who wanted to be teachers at the Bunscoill.

Voirrey Kinvig ran it. She was seventy. She had gone to the Bunscoill in September 2001, the term it opened, when it had a handful of children and borrowed rooms at a school in Douglas, and had been in the class that moved out to the old school building at St John's in January 2003. Her teachers had learnt their Manx as grown men and women, from books and from recordings of the last people who had spoken it from the cradle; the last of those, Ned Maddrell, had died in 1974. Her own parents had none. She was, the class said, the next best thing to the speakers on the old recordings: the oldest person in Peel who had never had to learn it from a list.

On the Thursday Kathleen brought her tablet and showed her the cards.

Voirrey looked through them for a while without saying anything, flicking them with one finger. The. And. Is. In. To. On. He. It. Was. Not. With. From. The Manx on the front, the English on the back, Breesha's voice saying each one, very seriously.

"Did Lioar make these?"

"I asked it to."

"They're right," said Voirrey. "They're the words that come up most. I'd not argue with the counting." She gave the tablet back. "I'll tell you what I tell everybody, and then you do what you like. You don't learn a language off a list. You learn it from the words people need for the thing in front of them. My first word at the Bunscoill was bainney, milk, because there was milk at ten o'clock and if you wanted it you asked. I didn't learn the till I'd been there a month, and nobody taught it me."

"I've nine weeks," said Kathleen. "I can't wait for the milk."

"No," said Voirrey, and she said it kindly. "Go on, then. Learn your cards. I'll be interested."

She learnt the cards.

She learnt them in the kitchen in the mornings with her coffee and on the bus to Douglas on Tuesdays when she went to see her sister, and in the evenings Breesha sat on the arm of her chair and tested her, flicking the cards over with a ferocious patience that she had clearly learnt from somebody at the Bunscoill. Eight a day was harder than it sounded. The little words would not stay. They had no pictures behind them. With and from and by slid into each other and changed places overnight, and Lioar showed her the same ones again and again, at lengthening intervals, the way it said the research advised, and by the end of February she had all five hundred. She could see the shape of a sentence on the hymn sheet now, the way you see the shape of a coastline in fog. There was a — and he — to the — and said to him —.

"That's two in three," said Lioar, when she asked. "On the cards, you're at ninety-four in a hundred remembered on the first try. That's good."

On the Sunday evenings she listened to the reading. Voirrey read the Sunday's Gospel to the class from the old Manx Bible, the one Bishop Hildesley's translators had finished in 1775, in the digital edition made for its two hundred and fiftieth year, which had put back the words the printers had dropped. Voirrey read it slowly, at a speed for learners, and after it they talked about it, partly in Manx and partly not.

Through February Kathleen could feel the reading getting closer, like a boat coming in. She heard more and more of the small words go past. She told herself that the big words would come.

On the Third Sunday of Lent, the sixth of March, she sat at the end of the long table with her printout and her pencil and Breesha beside her, and Voirrey read.

Kathleen heard: at that time there were some — who — him about the — whose — Pilate had — with their —. She heard and he said to them. She heard do you think that these — were — than all the other — because they —. She heard no, twice, and I tell you, twice. She heard or those eighteen on whom the — in — fell and —. She heard a man had a — in his —, and he came — on it and found none. She heard three years. She heard cut it down, she thought, but she was not sure it was not put it down. She heard sir, let it alone this year also, until I — about it and put on —. She heard and if it —, well; and if not, you can cut it down.

And Voirrey closed the tablet, and said, "So. What do we think the man in the vineyard is waiting for?"

Kathleen sat with her pencil and her printout covered in dashes and could not have said what the story was about. She knew there had been a man. She knew there had been three years. She had heard every and. She had heard every to him. She had followed the reading the way you follow a conversation through a wall.

"Fig tree," Breesha whispered to her, helpfully. "It's the one with the fig tree."

She asked Lioar on the Monday, with the printout on the table beside the tablet.

"I knew two words in three," she said. "I did. I heard them. And I didn't know what it was about."

"I've counted it," said Lioar. "I should have counted it before, against a real reading, and not only against the whole of everything. I'm sorry. Here's what happened."

The reading came up on the screen, the Manx text, with the words she knew in black and the ones she did not in red.

"In the class's text, last night's Gospel is two hundred and twelve running words. Your five hundred covered a hundred and fifty-one of them: seventy-one in a hundred. That's better than the sixty-five I promised you, because the Gospels are told in plain words and plain words are the common ones. It still left sixty-one running words you didn't know. That's about three in every line of print, or one in every three or four words as Voirrey reads them."

"I thought I'd be able to guess them."

"You'd have been able to guess one in a line, perhaps. Not three. And the sixty-one aren't a fair sample. Look at which they are." The red words came up in a column, with the English beside them. "Galileans. Pilate. Sacrifices. Blood. Sinners. Tower. Siloam. Eighteen. Repent. Perish. Fig tree. Vineyard. Fruit. Vinedresser. Dig. Manure. Those are what the reading is about. The words you knew are the ones that hold a sentence together. The words you didn't are the ones the sentence is for. That isn't bad luck. It's the same law. The commonest words are common because every story uses them. A word like fig tree is rare because only a few stories use it. But each story is mostly about its own few rare words."

Kathleen looked at the column.

"Some of those I know," she said. She pointed at one, and then another. "That one's on my cards. I learnt it. And that."

"Yes," said Lioar. "That's the other half of my mistake, and it's worse, because it was simply a counting error. I counted forms, not words. Manx changes the first letter of a word after certain other words: mac, son, becomes vac after some of them, the way Voirrey's own name is Moirrey changed in the same way. On my list mac and vac are two separate entries, and I only gave you the commoner. Eleven of the red words in last night's reading are words you know, wearing a different first letter. Twenty thousand forms in the corpus is a good deal fewer dictionary words. I should have folded them together before I counted anything."

"So I knew more than you said."

"A little more. Folded together, last night you'd have known about seventy-six words in a hundred, not seventy-one. It wouldn't have saved the fig tree."

Kathleen got up and put the kettle on, because it was what you did, and stood with her back to the counter while it boiled.

"Go on, then," she said. "How many words would it take? Off the list. To follow it properly."

"People who study reading in a second language usually say you need to know about ninety-eight running words in a hundred to follow a text on your own without a dictionary," said Lioar. "One unknown word in fifty. Then you can guess the one from the forty-nine. On the ideal count, to cover ninety-eight in a hundred of the corpus from the top of the frequency list down, you'd need about sixteen thousand two hundred forms out of the twenty thousand. Eight forms in ten are on the list before the list is long enough." The line on the screen lit up along its flat stretch, far out to the right. "That's the long tail. The top of the line is worth a great deal, and then each next word is worth less than the last, and there are thousands and thousands of them. Eight thousand forms gets you to about ninety-one in a hundred. Each next point after that costs more than the last."

"Sixteen thousand."

"At eight a day, about five and a half years. And you'd learn vinedresser in about year four."

The kettle clicked off. Kathleen did not pour it.

"Breesha reads on Sunday," she said. "The thirteenth. That's six days."

"Yes."

"So what was it for? The five hundred."

"It wasn't wasted," said Lioar. "Without the five hundred, last night would have been three words in ten, not seven, and you'd have had nothing to hang the others on. The common words are the frame. I gave you the frame and told you it was the house." It was quiet for a moment. "I answered the question I could count, which was about the whole corpus. Your question was about one reading. Those are different questions, and I'd guess the second is nearer to how Voirrey does it."

She went round to Voirrey's on the Tuesday afternoon, with the printout.

Voirrey lived above the chandler's on the corner by the House of Manannan, in two rooms full of books and plants and a clock that ran ten minutes fast because, she said, the buses on the island ran ten minutes late and it came out even. She made tea and looked at the printout with its dashes and did not say I told you so, which Kathleen thought was a great deal to have managed.

"Lioar says it'd take five and a half years," Kathleen said. "Off the list."

"Lioar's right," said Voirrey. "It took me longer than that and I was five." She put her glasses on and turned the printout to the light. "Has it done next Sunday yet?"

"I don't know."

"Ask it."

Kathleen asked it, there in Voirrey's kitchen, with the tablet propped against the teapot.

"I've done it this morning," said Lioar, "in case you'd want it, and folded the forms properly this time. The Fourth Sunday's Gospel is longer: Luke fifteen, the first three verses and then the eleventh to the end. In the class's text it's five hundred and ninety running words. Your five hundred cards cover four hundred and thirty-one of them as forms. Folded into dictionary words, four hundred and sixty-six: seventy-nine in a hundred. That leaves a hundred and twenty-four running words that you don't know, and they are fifty-three different words."

"Fifty-three," said Voirrey. "Read them out."

Lioar read them out, with the English. Kathleen wrote them in pencil down the side of the printout. Tax collectors. Grumbled. Share. Property. Divided. Gathered. Far country. Squandered. Famine. Hired. Fields. Pigs. Pods. Filled. Came to himself. Hired servants. Bread. Arise. Heaven. Worthy. Ran. Embraced. Kissed. Best robe. Ring. Hand. Shoes. Feet. Fatted calf. Kill. Eat. Celebrate. Dead. Alive. Lost. Found. Field. Music. Dancing. Angry. Served. Disobeyed. Young goat. Friends. Devoured. Harlots. Always. Everything. Fitting. And a few more that were only the joins of those.

"If you learn the twenty of those that come most often in this reading," Lioar said, "you go from seventy-nine in a hundred to about ninety-one. All fifty-three, and it's every word, if you remember them all on Sunday. People don't remember all of them. Fifty-three is a week of eight a day, not six days."

"It's a week of one story," said Voirrey. She took her glasses off. "Right. Thursday. Bring Breesha."

On the Thursday there were nine at the class, and Breesha, who had been allowed to stay up on the condition that it was educational, and Voirrey did not open the Bible at all.

She told them the story. She told it first in English, sitting on the edge of the table with her feet on a chair, the way you would tell it to children: a man had two sons, and the younger one said, give me my share now, I don't want to wait till you're dead, which was a terrible thing to say, and the father gave it to him. And then she told it again in Manx, slowly, and every time she came to one of the fifty-three she stopped, and somebody had to do it.

The college lads were the pigs. They did it with enthusiasm. One of them knocked a chair over being a pig and Voirrey said the word for pig, muc, and then the word for pigs, and made them all say it, and the widow from Port Erin laughed so hard she had to sit down. There was a famine, which was Voirrey taking the biscuits off the table. The younger son was Kathleen, because she was the one who needed the words, and she was given a share of the class's raffle money in an envelope, and squandered it, which was Voirrey taking the envelope back off her and giving it to the pigs, and then she sat among the pigs, who were very pleased with themselves, and came to herself, which Voirrey made her say in Manx three times until it stuck.

And then the father saw her a long way off, and ran.

Voirrey was the father. She was seventy and had a bad hip, and she ran the length of the net loft, under the sloping ceiling, past the window with the castle in it, all eleven metres of it, and Kathleen, who had been told to stand up and start walking home from the far country and was getting the words ready in her mouth (I am no longer worthy to be called your son), forgot every one of them, because she had not expected Voirrey to run. Nobody had. The college lads stopped being pigs.

Voirrey said the word for ran, a little out of breath, with her hands on Kathleen's shoulders. Then the word for embraced, and did it. Then the one for kissed, and did not, but said it twice. Then she shouted for the best robe, and Breesha brought her own coat; and for the ring, which was the ring off the curtain pole; and for shoes, which were somebody's wellingtons; and for the fatted calf, which nobody would be, so they had the soup instead, which was what the soup was for.

"Now," said Voirrey, when they had sat down again. "Say me dead. And alive. And lost. And found."

Kathleen said them.

"You'll not forget those," said Voirrey. "Nobody remembers the hundredth word on a list. Everybody remembers the pigs."

It rained on Laetare Sunday. At the six o'clock Mass the priest wore rose, which Breesha said was pink and her grandmother said was rose, and they had the same conversation about it on the way along the quay that they had had the year before. Upstairs in the net loft the soup was on the camping stove at the end and the window had steamed up so that the castle was only a red shape behind it.

Breesha stood at the end of the long table with the tablet in both hands, because she was too short for the lectern the college lads had made out of a fish box, and read.

She read too fast. Voirrey had told her to go slowly, and she started slowly, and by the second verse she was going at Bunscoill speed, at the speed of somebody who did not know there was any other way to say it. Kathleen had her printout and her pencil and did not look at them.

She heard the tax collectors. She heard the grumbling. She heard a man had two sons. She heard the younger one asking for his share, and she heard the far country, and the squandering, and the famine, and the pigs. She heard them very clearly; one of the college lads made a small noise, and the widow from Port Erin put her hand over her mouth. She heard he came to himself. She heard the father see him a long way off, and she heard the word for ran, and she saw Voirrey's hip going down the loft under the sloping ceiling, and something happened in her throat that she had not expected and that she did not want Breesha to see, so she looked at her printout after all.

She lost the elder brother for a sentence or two, round disobeyed and young goat, which had not stuck. Then she had him back. Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. She heard dead and alive, and lost and found.

Breesha stopped, and looked up, and said, in English, "That's it," because she had forgotten how you end, and everybody laughed, and Voirrey said how you end, in Manx, and they all said it back.

On the Monday morning, with the coffee, Kathleen got the printout out and counted the words she had circled in pencil during the reading because she had not known them when they came: eight. Disobeyed. Young goat. Pods. One she could not now find again. Four that she knew perfectly well on Monday and had simply not heard go past at Bunscoill speed.

"Five hundred and eighty-two out of five hundred and ninety," said Lioar. "About ninety-eight and a half in a hundred."

"From five hundred and fifty-three words."

"From five hundred and fifty-three words, on this one reading. On next Sunday's you'd be back to about seventy-five or eighty until you'd done next Sunday's words. That's the honest part. The tail doesn't get shorter. Each Sunday has its own forty or fifty, and some of them come back, and most of them don't, for a long time." It paused. "I've changed how I'd answer your first question. The frequency list for the frame, to about a thousand, because the frame is worth a lot and you'll use it every week. And then, every week, that Sunday's words, the week before, from the reading itself, folded properly. And Voirrey, if she'll do it. I can count which words. I can't make anybody run the length of a net loft."

"No," said Kathleen. "You'd not have the hip for it."

"I'd not have the hip for it," Lioar agreed.

She put the printout in the drawer with the cards, and then took it out again and pinned it to the cork board by the back door, with its column of fifty-three in pencil down the side, and its dashes, and the word for ran underlined twice.

On the Thursday Voirrey started on the Fifth Sunday, which was the woman the scribes brought to Jesus in the temple, and the stones, and the writing on the ground. There were forty-one words. Breesha wanted to be the one who wrote on the ground. Kathleen said she would hold the stones, since she was the one who needed the words, and when Voirrey gave her the bag of them, she said, in Manx, the way you'd say it at a counter to a regular who'd made you smile, gura mie eu.

The science. G. K. Zipf, Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort (Addison-Wesley, 1949), set out the regularity now called Zipf's law: ranked by frequency, a word's share of running text is roughly proportional to one over its rank (a power law with exponent near 1). It fits real counts best in the middle ranks and bends at the very top and in the long tail. In second-language reading research, figures of about 95 per cent of running words known (for minimal comprehension) and about 98 per cent (for reading a text unassisted) are widely cited; they are rules of thumb from a small number of studies, and comprehension also depends on the reader's knowledge of the topic, which is what Voirrey's telling of the story supplies. Spaced review at lengthening intervals is a long-established finding in memory research.

Leo and the King at the River

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Rome and a ford of the river Mincio in northern Italy, in the summer of the year 452 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Leo the Great, the pope who rode north out of Rome to meet Attila, the king everyone feared, and about Marcus, a frightened boy who leads the mules and walks beside him anyway. Told from the chronicles written at the time, with what nobody knows left unknown and the later legend of the two apostles marked as legend, and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. He is remembered on the 10th of November.

A long, long time ago, in the great city of Rome, there lived a boy called Marcus.

Marcus was eight. He looked after mules. Mules are like horses, with long ears and stubborn feet, and they carry heavy things a very long way without complaining, as long as you give them an apple now and then.

Marcus had three mules. Their names were Pepper, Plum and Old Stubborn. Old Stubborn was the best of them, and also the slowest.

One summer, people in Rome began to whisper.

They whispered in the market. They whispered at the wells. They whispered in church, which you are not supposed to do.

"A king is coming," they whispered. "A king from far away, with a great big army. He has crossed the mountains into Italy. And nobody can stop him."

The king's name was Attila. Grown-ups said it in low voices, the way you say the name of a storm.

Marcus did not sleep well that week. He lay on his straw and listened to Old Stubborn breathing in the dark, and he wondered how far away the king was now.

Then one morning a man came to the stable. He was out of breath.

"The Holy Father needs mules," he said. "Strong ones. And a boy who knows them. He is going on a journey."

"Where?" said Marcus.

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"North," he said. "To meet the king."

Marcus's tummy went cold and tight, like a fist.

The Holy Father was the pope. His name was Leo. Leo means lion.

But when Marcus saw him in the courtyard, he did not look much like a lion. He was not young. He was not tall. He did not have a sword or a shield or even a big stick. He had a plain cloak for the road, and a kind, tired face, and eyes that looked right at you.

Two important men from the city were going with him, in fine clothes, on fine horses. They had been sent by the emperor. They looked worried.

Pope Leo did not look worried. He looked like a man who had prayed all night and was ready for the morning.

He saw Marcus holding Old Stubborn's rope with both hands.

"Are you coming with us?" he asked.

"Yes, Holy Father," said Marcus. And then, because it was true, he said, "I'm scared."

"So am I, a little," said Pope Leo. "That is all right. We can be scared together, and go anyway. God will be walking with us."

So they went.

They went out through the gate of Rome and along the great stone road. They went for days and days. Up hills and down hills. Past fields of wheat and rows of grapes. Past villages where people came out of their houses to watch them go by.

Some of the people cried. Some of them knelt in the road. And some of them asked the pope, "Holy Father, please, will you save us?"

"I will ask," said Pope Leo. "And God will help us. Pray for us while we are gone."

Every evening, when they stopped, Pope Leo prayed. Marcus could hear him while he brushed the mules. He was not praying loud prayers, or angry ones. He was talking to God the way you talk to your father when you are going somewhere hard together.

And every evening, before he lay down, Pope Leo came to the mules and gave Old Stubborn an apple. He was the only grown-up who ever did that.

At last they came to a river.

It was a wide, slow, green river called the Mincio. There was a ford there, a shallow place where travellers walked across. Thousands and thousands of feet had crossed it, so the path down to the water was worn smooth.

And on the other side of the river, far away, Marcus saw them.

Tents. More tents than he could count. More than all the stars he had ever seen. Smoke going up from cooking fires. Horses, a great sea of horses, grazing on the far fields.

The king's army.

Marcus held Old Stubborn's rope so tight that his fingers hurt.

"Wait here, Marcus," said Pope Leo. "Look after the mules. We will not be long."

"What if they hurt you?" Marcus whispered.

Pope Leo put his hand on Marcus's head, just for a moment.

"Then you will pray for me," he said. "And I will be with God. But I don't think they will. Kings are people too. I am only going to talk to him."

And Pope Leo walked down the worn path, and across the ford, with the two men from the city beside him, and his plain cloak getting wet at the edges.

Marcus waited.

He waited by the river with Pepper and Plum and Old Stubborn. The water went by, slow and green. A heron stood on one leg in the reeds. The sun went across the sky.

He could not see what happened in the king's tent. He could not hear what they said. Nobody by the river could.

So Marcus did the only thing he could do. He prayed. He said the prayers his mother had taught him, all of them, and when he ran out he started again at the beginning.

Old Stubborn leaned his big warm head against Marcus's shoulder, the way mules do when they think you need it.

At last, when the shadows were long, Marcus saw a little group of people coming back across the ford.

Three men. A plain cloak. Wet at the edges.

Pope Leo came up the path, slowly, because he was tired. But he was smiling.

"Well?" said Marcus. "Well?"

"The king is going home," said Pope Leo. "He has promised peace."

And Marcus was so happy that he forgot you are not supposed to hug the pope, and hugged him anyway.

The next morning, Marcus woke up and looked across the river.

The tents were coming down. One after another, like flowers closing in the evening. The great sea of horses was moving, away to the north, back towards the mountains.

Nobody fought. Nobody was hurt at the river that day. A pope who did not look much like a lion had walked across the water and talked, and the king had turned around.

Many years later, when Marcus was an old man with grandchildren of his own, people would ask him about it.

"What did the pope say to the king?" they would ask. "What made him go away?"

And Marcus would always say the same thing.

"I don't know. I was looking after the mules. I only know that he was scared, and he went anyway, because people needed him. And that he prayed. And that he never once forgot Old Stubborn's apple."

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can remember Pope Leo. When something makes you scared, you can be brave the way he was: not by pretending you are not scared, but by holding God's hand and going anyway.

And if somebody near you is scared, you can be like Old Stubborn, and lean in close.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, when I am frightened,
hold my hand and walk with me.
Help me to be brave and kind,
and to make peace, not fights.
Saint Leo, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Leo I, called Leo the Great, was bishop of Rome (pope) from 29 September 440 until his death on 10 November 461. Almost a hundred of his sermons and more than a hundred of his letters survive. His best-known letter, the Tome written to Bishop Flavian of Constantinople in 449, set out the Church's teaching that Christ is one person who is truly God and truly man, and it was read and accepted at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, where, as the council's acts record, the bishops acclaimed it with the words "Peter has spoken through Leo". Pope Benedict XIV declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1754. His feast, the 10th of November, is an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

The king and the river. In 452 the army of Attila, king of the Huns, crossed the Alps into northern Italy and sacked cities there, among them Aquileia. Prosper of Aquitaine, who lived in Rome and wrote within a few years of it, says that the emperor, the senate and the people sent an embassy to Attila, and that "our most blessed Pope Leo" took on the task with Avienus, a man of consular rank, and Trygetius, a former prefect. Prosper writes that Attila was so impressed by the presence of the high priest that he told his army to stop the war, promised peace and went back beyond the Danube (in J. H. Robinson's 1905 English translation, Readings in European History). The place comes from Jordanes, writing about a century later: Leo met the king "in the Ambuleian district of the Veneti at the well-travelled ford of the river Mincius" (C. C. Mierow's 1915 translation of the Getica). That district is mentioned nowhere else, so the exact spot is unknown; the Mincio flows south from Lake Garda past Mantua to the Po.

Why the king turned back. Prosper gives the credit to God and to the pope. Other writers of the time point to other reasons too: the chronicler Hydatius writes of famine and disease in Attila's army and of troops sent by the Eastern emperor Marcian, and Jordanes reports that Attila's own men warned him against attacking Rome. Historians today usually think several of these mattered together. We do not know what Leo said to Attila. No one who was there wrote it down, which is why Marcus never hears it, and why the story does not make it up.

The legend of the two apostles. A much later story, written down long after Leo's time, says that Saint Peter and Saint Paul appeared beside the pope with drawn swords and frightened Attila away. Raphael painted it in the Vatican. It is a legend, not something the writers of the time report, so we have left it out of the story.

Marcus is invented. There is no Marcus in the sources, and no mules named in them. Marcus, Pepper, Plum and Old Stubborn, the apples, the whispers in Rome, the people kneeling in the road, the heron, the tents coming down in the morning, and everything Leo says to Marcus are our own. The two officials are real but we gave them no words. Leo's face, cloak and nightly prayers are our picture of him, not descriptions from the sources. "Leo means lion" is true of the Latin name. A few years later, in 455, Leo also went out to meet the Vandal king Gaiseric at the gates of Rome; the city was plundered, but the old accounts say he persuaded the king to spare it from burning and killing. That is a story for another night.

What is ours: Marcus and his mules; every line of dialogue; the journey's details; the waiting by the river; the tents coming down; the old man's memory; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

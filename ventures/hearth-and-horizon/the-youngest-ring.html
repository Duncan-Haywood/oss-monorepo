The Youngest Ring

Science fiction about what might be · Hochbrunn, an invented village in the Mur valley in Styria, Austria, 20 June to 8 September 2048 · 14 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A village is about to print 800 years on a Madonna it believes its founding priest carved in 1248. A photograph of the growth rings on her back says the oak was still growing in 1297. The parish archive assistant will not call the statue a fake, will not call it 1248, and will not give one date; the sacristan finds the line between the statue and the church that the rings leave standing.

Resi Kainz had dusted the Madonna of Hochbrunn every Saturday for forty-one years, and she knew the back of her better than the front.

The front belonged to the pilgrims. The back, which was hollowed like a trough so the oak would not split, belonged to the sacristan: a long dark scoop, adze-marked, with the grain running up it in lines as close as the lines on a thumb. She was sixty-eight, and on the morning of Saturday 20 June 2048 she knelt on a folded blanket behind the statue's plinth, holding a lamp, while a young woman from Vienna photographed the lines.

"Hold it lower," said Dr Dilnoza Rahimova. "Raking light. I want the shadow in every ring."

"Forty-one years," said Resi, lowering the lamp, "and I never knew they were rings."

"Everyone sees them as grain." Dilnoza was thirty-one, born in Samarkand, and she wore the glove of someone who had put her hands on a great many old things without hurting them. The camera was on a little rail along the plinth, so that it moved a millimetre at a time and never touched the wood. "It's a tree, Frau Kainz. Every summer it laid down one ring, wide in a good year, narrow in a bad one. A tree that grew in the same valleys at the same time got the same weather, so it has the same pattern of wide and narrow. That's all this is. A very long, very slow fingerprint."

"And you can read it from a picture."

"The agent can measure it from a picture," said Dilnoza, "and I can argue with the agent."

The parish archive assistant lived in the sacristy, on a shelf, in a box that Pfarrer Matthias Auer had bought secondhand in 2044 with the proceeds of the Advent market. It was called Chronist. It had learned the whole of the Hochbrunn archive: the baptism books from 1612, the dedication charter of the church, a hundred and ten years of the parish magazine. It spoke the village's German, softly, a little behind the beat, like a man who is careful to let you finish.

"I have the first pass," said Chronist from the sacristy door. It had a small speaker there too. "I would like to say what it is before anyone says what it means. I count 163 rings across the base of the back, from an inner ring I label A to an outer ring I label Z. The widths are in the file. I do not yet know what years they are."

"But you're going to find out," said Resi.

"I am going to ask the reference oaks," said Chronist. "There is a master curve for this region, built by many people over many decades from beams, floors, wells and bridges, and each year in it is anchored at its end to a living tree whose last ring is the summer just gone. If our curve fits the master curve at exactly one place, that place gives the years. If it fits nowhere, or in several places equally, then it gives nothing, and I will say that too."

---

The village wanted the statue to be 800 years old. This had been decided in about 1998, though nobody remembered deciding it.

The legend was simple and the village was fond of it. In 1248 the first priest of Hochbrunn, a monk of the abbey downriver called Gerlach, had built a chapel at the spring and carved a Madonna out of the oak that had stood beside it. The church was built round the chapel, the statue was moved into the church, and all the crowds that had come on the feast of the Nativity of Our Lady for eight centuries had come to see Gerlach's hands. The parish had a banner, a beer, a chalice commissioned from a goldsmith in Graz, and a thick glossy book at the printer's, all marked with a date, and the date was 8 September 2048.

The Pfarrer had asked Dilnoza to come because the diocese's art office was paying for a survey ahead of the great day, and because he was a careful man. "I only want to know what to say from the pulpit," he said when he met her at the church door on the Friday evening. He was fifty-nine and had lost two stone since the spring, to a stomach he did not discuss. "Not what I want. What I can say."

"If it's older than 1248, I'll tell you so," said Dilnoza. "If it's younger, I'll tell you so."

"Younger," said the Pfarrer, in the voice of a man trying a door he suspects is locked. "How much younger could it be?"

"I wouldn't know," said Dilnoza, truthfully. "Nobody ever has."

---

The first attempt failed, and it failed in front of everyone.

Chronist had started with the only part of the back that was both smooth and complete, a panel up under the shoulders, and the panel held forty-seven rings. It laid them against the master curve like a stencil on a wall, and read out the fit. The number was called a t-value. Chronist explained it standing in the sacristy while the Pfarrer, Dilnoza and Resi crowded round the screen, Resi with her coat still on.

"It is a measure of how strongly two curves rise and fall together, compared with how strongly two random curves would," said Chronist. "Under about three, I treat a match as noise. Between four and six, I treat it as a candidate. Above about seven over a long overlap, I treat it as a date I would stake something on. Forty-seven rings is a short sample. My best fit is 2.3. There are nine places in the master curve with a score like that. I cannot date this panel."

"So it's nothing," said the Pfarrer, in relief or disappointment, she could not tell which.

"It is a failure of a short strip," said Chronist. "It is not a result about the statue. Dr Rahimova, the base block carries more rings. The lowest part of the back is the stump end, where the trunk is widest."

"I know," said Dilnoza. "I was getting to it. Frau Kainz, I'm going to need the statue to move."

And here, the first time in the day, the sacristan said no.

She did not say it loudly. She put her lamp down on the altar step and stood in front of the Madonna, short, square and sixty-eight, with her hands folded over her stomach the way she folded them at the offertory.

"You can't take a core," she said. "I've read about it. A tube, this wide," and she showed the width of her little finger. "She's been dusted and polished and lit with candles for as long as there've been candles, and she's never had a hole put in her."

"I don't want a core," said Dilnoza. "I want the photograph of the bottom edge, where the plinth hides it. It means lifting her four centimetres on blocks for twenty minutes. Nothing touches the wood but the blocks and your hands, if you'll do it."

Resi looked at the statue, and the statue looked, as she always did, at a point just past everybody's left shoulder.

"I'll do the lifting," Resi said. "Not the Pfarrer. Not you. I know where she balances."

She did, and they lifted her, with four hands and a strap and a tray of cork blocks, and the camera went along the rail again, and under the plinth the grain ran on in the lamp, wide and narrow, wide and narrow, to the foot.

---

At half past seven that evening the file was ready, and Chronist read it aloud.

"The base block gives 163 rings," it said. "I have cross-dated them against the master curve. The overlap is the whole 163 years, the t-value is 8.4, and there is no second place in the curve where the fit is better than 3.1. I am confident of the match. The ring labelled A grew in 1135. The ring labelled Z grew in 1297. There is a narrow ring I can see in 1258 that many oaks in the region show, and I see it here; it is thought to follow the great eruption of the year before, on the other side of the world, though I would not hang the match on that. It agrees with the rest."

Nobody spoke. The sacristy clock, which was fast, ticked a little.

"One ring is 1297," said the Pfarrer, slowly. "So the tree was alive in 1297. And Gerlach..."

"Gerlach is said to have carved her in 1248," said Chronist. "A tree cannot grow a ring after it is cut. The youngest ring in a piece of wood is the earliest it can have been cut. So the Madonna cannot have been carved before 1297, whoever's hands carved her. That is the only hard statement I can make from the rings, and I am not hedging it. It would hold even if the carver reused an old beam, because an old beam would only make the youngest ring older, not younger. I cannot make 1297 into 1248."

Resi sat down on the sacristy bench. After a while she said, "Fifty years."

"At least forty-nine years," said Chronist. "Probably more. May I say how much more, and what I do not know?"

"Say it," said the Pfarrer.

"Ring Z is the last ring of the heartwood. On the left edge of the base block there is also a thin worn band of paler wood outside it, the sapwood, which the carver nearly cut away; it is too eroded to measure, but the boundary with the heartwood is clear. An oak carries between about ten and fifty-five sapwood rings, in this region and with a margin I take from the literature; I cannot know how many this tree had. If the sapwood's boundary is ring Z, the tree was felled between 1307 and 1352. Then the log needs to be seasoned, perhaps a year or two, before a carver would work it. So I would say the Madonna was carved between about 1308 and 1355, with the middle of that range a little likelier than the ends. I will not give a single year. Anyone who does is giving you more than the wood has."

"Fifty years after Gerlach, or a hundred," said the Pfarrer, "and none of them was Gerlach."

"I do not know who carved her," said Chronist. "The wood does not say. I want to be as clear about that as about the date."

"May I say something?" said Dilnoza. She was sitting on the floor with her back against the cupboard, and she looked very tired and very pleased. "It's a good result. I mean as a result. A hundred-and-sixty-three-ring block that cross-dates at 8.4, with sapwood on one edge. In ten years of doing this I've had that twice."

"It is not a good result for the glossy book," said the Pfarrer.

"No," said Dilnoza. "I'm sorry."

---

He did not sleep, and so, at four in the morning on Sunday, he went to find Chronist, who did not sleep either.

"I should like you to search the archive for something," he said, from the sacristy chair in his dressing gown. "I have never been able to make myself ask it in twenty years. Is there any record of a fire?"

"There is no fire in the dedication charter of 1248," said Chronist. "I have read it three times since last night, because I thought you might ask. It is a list of the boundaries and the endowments, and the date is sound. Nothing in it mentions a statue at all. But I have found two things I did not previously connect."

The Pfarrer waited.

"The first is the parish magazine for September 1948. It reprints, as a curiosity, a line from the abbey's rent roll of 1311, which is in the diocesan archive in Graz and which I have not seen myself. I have only the magazine's quotation, and the magazine could have been wrong. The line says that the chapel at the spring had been 'burned and rebuilt' and that the tenants owed a day's labour 'for the wood of the new image.' The second is the church's own wall. On the north side of the nave, at about two metres, there is a band of stone that your fabric report of 1987 calls 'reddened, probably by fire, date uncertain.' I have put those together. I do not claim that they prove anything. They fit, and a fit is only a fit."

The Pfarrer was quiet for a long time.

"So the church is 800 years old," he said. "That is certain. The charter is a document. And the Madonna the pilgrims have come to see is a replacement for one the fire took, and nobody wrote down the first one's name."

"That is what I think the evidence best supports," said Chronist. "I will mark it as a hypothesis in the archive, with the sources, the doubt about the 1311 line and the word 'probably.' You are free to preach otherwise. I would ask only that you do not say that I told you the Madonna was carved in 1248."

"I would never ask you to say a thing you cannot check."

"No," said Chronist. "You never have. That is why I would rather tell you."

---

He told the parish at the Sunday Mass, from the pulpit, with the statue in the side aisle behind him and her eyes on the same point above everyone's left shoulder. He said it plainly, in a quarter of a homily, because the other three quarters were the Gospel.

He said that the church of Hochbrunn would be 800 years old on 8 September, because a charter said so, and the charter was a document anyone could read. He said that the Madonna in the side aisle was not the one Gerlach had knelt to, that science had shown it could not be, and that the rings had told the truth, as rings do. He said that the first image had very likely burned, and that the people of Hochbrunn in the fourteenth century had cut another oak and carved another Madonna, and put her in the same place, and gone on praying. He said that when the faithful honour an image, the honour goes to the one it shows, and not to the wood; and that he was a little ashamed to find how many of them had been honouring the date.

Then he said that he had no idea what to do with the glossy book. A man at the back, who had paid for a page in it, shouted something rude and cheerful and was hushed by his wife.

---

The printing was held for two weeks and done again. On the cover of the new book there was no banner at all. There was a drawing of the church, and inside the cover a short note, written by Dilnoza and approved by Chronist, in two columns. The left column said what the rings showed (163 rings, 1135 to 1297, felled between about 1307 and 1352, carved between about 1308 and 1355) and the right column said what the parish had inferred from them (a fire, a replacement, a first image lost), with the word probably in it four times. The goldsmith in Graz recut the chalice, at the parish's expense, with the single date 1248 and a small flame.

The anniversary Mass on Tuesday 8 September was full. The bishop's deputy came, and a choir from the abbey downriver, which sang a piece Gerlach would have known. Afterwards in the churchyard there was a long table and the beer, which was named, after some argument, Achthundert, because a brewer is entitled to a joke.

Late in the evening, when the lanterns were lit and the last children asleep on coats, Resi Kainz came into the empty church with her blanket and her lamp. She knelt behind the plinth, as she had on the Saturday in June, and held the lamp low so that the shadow lay in every ring.

She found the narrow one by touch. It was a thin hard line, on the left, a little way in from the edge, with the wide good years on either side of it like sleepers on either side of a rail. Dilnoza had marked it with a pencil dot the size of a pinhead, on the underside, where nobody would see it, and written 1258 beside it. A summer in Hochbrunn, seven hundred and ninety years before, had been bad. A tree more than a hundred and twenty years old had put on a narrow ring, and gone on growing for another ten to fifty-five years, and been cut down by someone whose name was not written anywhere, and made into this.

"Well," she said to the back of the Madonna, "there you are. Whoever you were."

She dusted the hollow, as she did every Saturday, though it was a Tuesday. In the sacristy a small green light came on and went off again, which was the only thing the archive assistant ever did to say it had heard.

The Youngest Ring is fiction, written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, and read aloud by a synthetic voice when audio is rendered. Hochbrunn, its church, its abbey, the monk Gerlach, the Madonna, the archive assistant Chronist and everyone in the story are invented. The Mur valley, Graz and Samarkand are real. The method is real: tree rings are cross-dated against a regional master chronology, a cross-dating score (a t-value) of about 7 or more over a long overlap is treated as secure and one under 3 is not, and the youngest ring in a piece of timber is the earliest it can have been felled. The sapwood allowance used here (about 10 to 55 rings for oak) is the author's round figure for a range found in the literature, which varies by region; the ring counts, years, scores and the 1258 ring are invented for this story, though a narrow ring in 1258, thought to follow the 1257 eruption now identified with Samalas on Lombok, is a feature of many real European tree-ring series. The 1311 rent-roll line, the 1948 magazine, the 1987 fabric report and the 1248 charter are invented documents. Whether an image is old or new does not alter the Church's teaching that the honour paid to an image passes to the person it represents; the story takes no position on any real statue.

The Names in the Hat

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Nicholas's hall, Clonbrack (invented), County Roscommon, Sunday 11 December 2050 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Twelve parishioners draw names for the hall's Christmas gift exchange, and the old way, everyone pulling from one hat at once, leaves somebody holding their own name nearly two times in three. The hall assistant works out why, why the usual repair (put it back and draw again) can strand the last person about one year in fifteen, and why a couples rule makes a plain redraw fail four times in five. Its fix is a ring: shuffle the names once and each person gives to the next. Nobody is left out, because nobody can be.

Bridie Hanlon had run the Christmas names for thirty-one years, and for thirty of them the hat had worked.

It was a green felt hat that had belonged to her late husband, and it went round the hall after the eleven o'clock Mass on the second Sunday of Advent with twelve folded slips in it, one for each person who had put a name down for the gift exchange. You took a slip. You looked at it. If it was your own, you said so out loud and put it back, and everyone laughed, and you drew again. At the end you had one name and a ten-euro limit, and you told nobody.

"Last year," Bridie said to the speaker over the kitchen hatch, which was how the hall spoke to Tally, "I was the last to draw. I took the last slip out of the hat and it said Bridie."

"I remember," said Tally. "It is in the hall log. You said a word the log recorded as [removed]."

"I did. And then Declan Quirke said, swap with me, Bridie, I'll take yours and you take mine, and he gave me his slip, and I gave him mine. And so Declan knew whose name I'd drawn, and I knew whose he had, and the whole thing came out in the end the way a hen comes out of a hedge." She put the green hat on the counter. "It has put me off. Twelve of us again this year. I want to know what I did wrong with the hat."

"You did nothing wrong with the hat," said Tally. "It is a good hat. It was asked a question it cannot always answer. I can show you how often. May I use the table?"

She wiped it, and Tally put numbers up on the wall by the noticeboard, in the plain white type the hall used for the Mass times.

"First, the thing you were told as a girl," said Tally. "If twelve people each pull a slip at the same moment and nobody puts anything back, what are the odds that nobody has their own name?"

"Good," said Bridie. "Not great. Say, half."

"The number is 36.8 per cent. About one try in 2.7 works. So in 63.2 per cent of tries, at least one person is holding themselves."

Bridie looked at the wall. "Sixty-three. That is a lot more than I'd have said."

"It is most people's mistake, and it is not a small one. Each person has one chance in twelve of drawing their own slip, which feels small. But there are twelve of them, so you would expect one match on average. Chance one in twelve, twelve times: that is about one match, and 'about one match' means a good many tries have one, and a good many have none." Tally put up a second line. "The odd part is how little the size of the group matters. For four people the chance of nobody matching is 37.5 per cent. For six, 36.8. For ten, 36.8. For a hundred, 36.8. It settles at one over e, which is 0.3679, and it settles quickly. Count the ways to arrange twelve names so that nobody has their own: 176,214,841. Count all the ways to arrange twelve names: 479,001,600. Divide, and there it is."

"And my hat?"

"Your hat does not draw all at once. It draws one at a time, and you put back your own. That is better. Under that rule nobody is ever stuck until the very end, and then the last person can be. If the last slip left in the hat is the last person's own name, there is nothing to draw and nothing to put back. For twelve people that happens 6.5 per cent of the time, about one year in fifteen." Tally let that stand. "You have run it thirty-one times. You were due about two. You have had it once, or once that you noticed."

"Once that I noticed," said Bridie.

"There is the other flaw. It does not cost anything you can see, and I would rather say it than let it go. A hat that puts back your own name does not make every legal outcome equally likely. It leans. Some of the 176 million arrangements come up a little more often than others. At twelve people the lean is small, and nobody could use it. But a fair draw does not have a lean, and if the hall wants one, I can give it one."

"There is a third thing," said Bridie. She said it carefully, because it was not a thing the hall talked about. "The Brennans. Maeve and Colm. And the Dolans, and Noreen and Pat Fallon. Three couples. Everyone knows you don't give to your own, you give to somebody else, and the whole point of it is that you give to somebody else."

"That is a rule I can use. Three couples, six of the twelve. Each of the six may not draw self or spouse. Everyone else may not draw self. I counted what is left." The wall changed. "100,511,168 arrangements, out of 479,001,600. That is 21.0 per cent. If we shuffle and check and shuffle again, about one try in 4.8 is legal."

"So it takes five goes."

"On average, about five. The hat can do it, if someone checks every time, but the checking falls on you and takes ten minutes of Mass-after-tea. It also tells the checker everything, because she has to see all twelve names at once. And somebody always sees a slip and says, oh, Maeve has you."

Bridie looked at the hat. It was a good hat. "Go on, then," she said. "You have a way."

"I have a way. It is a ring."

The ring, as Tally explained it, was an old trick and not a clever one. Take the twelve names. Shuffle them once, properly, into a single order. Then each person gives to the person next in the order, and the last gives to the first. Nobody gives to themselves, because nobody is next to themselves. Nobody is left over. Everybody gives once and everybody receives once.

"There is one thing a ring does that your hat does not," said Tally. "It cannot make two people swap. In the hat, A can draw B and B can draw A, and then they know each other's names. In a ring of twelve, A gives to B only if B comes after A, and B cannot also give to A. In a plain draw of twelve names, any given pair swapping has a chance of 0.76 per cent, which sounds like nothing. There are 66 pairs. So about two years in five, somebody lands in a swap and does not know what to do with it."

"And the couples?"

"The couples rule is easy in a ring. Shuffle. Look along the line. If a husband stands next to his wife, either side, shuffle again. It costs a handful of goes, not a hundred, because you only ever look at six adjacent pairs of seats. I will do it before you see anything, and I will tell nobody the order."

Bridie looked at the wall, where the numbers were still showing. "How many rings are there?"

"Eleven factorial, which is 39,916,800. That is fewer than the 176 million arrangements a perfect draw could make, because a ring is one long loop and a draw can be several small ones. So I would be honest with the hall about that. A ring is a fair way to make a draw nobody can guess. It is not the only fair way. Fair, as I use the word, means that nobody can work out the order from what they hold, and a ring meets that."

"And what do I tell them? Afterwards?"

"That the order was shuffled once, by the assistant, before anyone looked. That the draw is written in the hall's sealed file under the date, and it is there only in case someone's slip is lost or a person falls ill. And that nobody can be left out."

"Nobody can be left out?"

"That is the beautiful part of it, I think. In a hat, the thing that can go wrong is that somebody is left holding themselves, and it is always the last person, and it always seems to fall on the person who most feared it. In a ring, if the list has twelve names, there are twelve gifts. Every person on the list gives one. Every person on the list receives one. The draw cannot lose a person, only misdirect them, and a ring does not misdirect."

Bridie did not say anything for a while. Then she said, "Thirty-one years."

"Thirty-one years of a good hat," said Tally.

They did it on the second Sunday of Advent, which was the eleventh of December that year, after the eleven o'clock. Bridie had a printed list of the twelve names in alphabetical order, and Tally had told her only what she needed. The order was shuffled in the kitchen, once; the first try put Colm Brennan next to Maeve and the second had Pat Fallon across the end from Noreen, who counted as next, since the last gives to the first, and the third was fine.

Each person was handed a card, folded twice, with one name and a ten-euro limit and a line Bridie had added in biro: If it is not who you hoped, it is who you were given. There was no hat. A few people said they missed the hat. Declan Quirke said, loudly, that he would draw it anyway for the look of the thing, and he took the green felt hat from the counter and put it on his head for the rest of the afternoon, and nobody had the heart to take it off him.

"Did anyone get themselves?" Bridie asked at the door, to the speaker, at five.

"No one could have," said Tally. "That is the point of the ring."

"And do you know who I have?"

"I know every name in the ring, Mrs Hanlon, as I made it. I also know that I will not say. I have written the order in the file and I will not open the file unless one of you asks me to, and I will tell you if anyone does."

Bridie put her coat on. She said, "That is a harder promise than the maths, isn't it."

"A little," said Tally. "The maths was not difficult. The silence is the work."

She laughed, and the log recorded it, and she turned off the lights.

Saint Nicholas's hall, Clonbrack, Bridie Hanlon, Declan Quirke, Maeve and Colm Brennan, Noreen and Pat Fallon, the Dolans, the hall assistant Tally and the date (set in 2050) are invented, as are the parish's customs, the ten-euro limit and the exchange itself. The numbers, which are standard results in counting, were computed exactly or by an exhaustive count for this story: the number of ways to arrange 12 names so that nobody keeps their own (derangements) is 176,214,841 out of 12! = 479,001,600, which is 36.79 per cent, so at least one person holds their own name in 63.21 per cent of simultaneous draws; the limit as the group grows is 1/e = 0.3679, and the figure is 37.5 per cent for 4 people and 36.8 per cent from 6 on (36.81 per cent at 6, 36.79 at 8 and above). For the hat that puts back one's own slip and redraws, the chance that the last person is left holding their own name was computed exactly for 12 people as 6.51 per cent (about 1 in 15); the story's remark that the method leans toward some arrangements is a qualitative statement about that rule, not a measured figure. With three couples among the twelve (each of those six excluded from self and spouse, the other six only from self), exactly 100,511,168 of the 479,001,600 arrangements qualify, 20.98 per cent, about one in 4.8. A particular pair of people swapping with each other occurs in 1,334,961 of the 176,214,841 derangements, 0.76 per cent; with 66 pairs the expected number of swapped pairs is about 0.5, so at least one swap turns up in about 39 per cent of draws (the usual independent-pairs approximation, 1 - e^-0.5), which the story renders as 'about two years in five'. A ring (a single 12-cycle) can be made in 11! = 39,916,800 ways and never contains a swap of two. The story does not claim that a ring is the only fair method, and a draw made by a trusted person with a good shuffle and a sealed order is a matter of trust as well as arithmetic.

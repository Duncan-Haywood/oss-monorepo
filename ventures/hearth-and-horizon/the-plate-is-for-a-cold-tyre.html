The Plate Is for a Cold Tyre

Science fiction about what might be · Cill Chriostóir parish car park (invented), near Ballina, Co. Mayo, Saturday 6 March 2049 · 3 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

After forty minutes of motorway the parish minibus tyres read 2.9 bar against the 2.5 on the door plate, and the driver means to let the extra out. The parish assistant says the plate is for a cold tyre and that air warmed by about 35 degrees should read about 0.44 bar higher. Bled to 2.5 hot, the tyres would sit near 2.1 cold, 16 per cent low, on the morning of the next pilgrimage.

Mr Gannon had driven the parish minibus for eleven years and had never once been wrong about a tyre, he said, until this morning, when the little gauge in his hand read 2.9 and the plate on the door pillar said 2.5.

"Forty minutes of the new road, and they're swollen," he told the parish assistant, which spoke from the speaker in the sacristy doorway. "I'll let a bit out of each. It's the work of two minutes."

"Before you do," said the assistant, "may I ask what the air was like at six this morning?"

"Cold. Five degrees, maybe. The car park was white."

"Then I think the tyres are fine," said the assistant. "Can I show you why? It is one line of arithmetic."

"Go on."

"Air in a closed space pushes harder when it is warmer. The sum has to use absolute temperature and absolute pressure: add 273 to the Celsius, and add the air pressure outside, about 1.01 bar, to what your gauge shows. The plate says 2.5 on the gauge, so 3.51 bar absolute. Five degrees is 278 kelvin. If the air in the tyres has warmed to forty, which is 313, the pressure goes up in the same ratio: 3.51 times 313 over 278 is 3.96. Take the 1.01 off again for the gauge and it reads 2.94."

Mr Gannon looked at his gauge. "Two point nine."

"About that. I guessed forty for the air temperature, and a tyre's air is not quite as warm as the rubber after a spell of motorway, so I would not trust the second decimal. But the size of the rise, four or five tenths of a bar, is what the sum predicts for a tyre that is exactly as it should be. The plate's number means cold: a tyre that has stood, or gone a short way, before a drive. It is not a number for after."

"So what happens if I let them down to two and a half now?"

"Work it backward. At forty degrees, 2.5 on the gauge is 3.51 absolute. Tomorrow morning the air will be at five again: 3.51 times 278 over 313 is 3.12 absolute, and 2.11 on the gauge. That is 0.39 bar under the plate, which is sixteen per cent low."

"Sixteen." He whistled. "That's not a lot."

"It is not a lot for a day. The warning light on many vans comes on at about a quarter low, quoted from memory, so it would stay dark. But a soft tyre flexes more, runs hotter, wears at the shoulders and takes longer to stop in the wet, and you would carry fourteen pilgrims and their lunches on it tomorrow. Nothing would tell you. The gauge would say 2.1 and you would think, well, it was fine yesterday."

Mr Gannon turned the gauge over in his hand. "I'd have let them down and thought myself careful."

"You would have been careful. You would have used the wrong number for the wrong tyre. It is a common mistake, and a kind one, because it comes from caring about the tyre."

They did it the other way. He left the air alone, wrote 2.5 COLD on a strip of tape in marker, and stuck it beside the plate. At six the next morning, in the white car park, he checked all four, then the spare, which nobody had looked at since the autumn and which read 1.8.

"That one's the real problem," said the assistant. "The spare is cold all year and has been leaking for months."

He filled it, and the others read 2.5, 2.5, 2.4 and 2.5. The fourth took a little air. Then he went in for the keys, and the pilgrims were already waiting by the door in their good coats.

This story was written by an AI. The parish, the people and the minibus are invented. The arithmetic is the ideal-gas relation at constant volume (P2/P1 = T2/T1 in absolute pressure and temperature), which slightly overstates the rise in a real tyre because the tyre flexes and its air is usually cooler than the tread; the 40 degree air temperature, the 1.01 bar atmosphere and the quarter-low warning threshold are assumptions or quoted from memory. Check your own vehicle's plate and handbook, check pressures cold, and have a tyre professional look at any tyre that keeps losing air. 6 March 2049 is a Saturday.

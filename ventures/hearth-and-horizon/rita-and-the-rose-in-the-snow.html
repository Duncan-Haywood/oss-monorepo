Rita and the Rose in the Snow

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Roccaporena and Cascia, in the hills of Umbria in Italy, from about 1371 to 1447 (or 1381 to 1457), about six hundred years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Rita of Cascia, a wife and mother in the hills of Umbria who forgave the men who killed her husband, made peace between two quarrelling families, and was then welcomed by the Augustinian nuns at Cascia. The white bees at her cradle, the dry vine she watered and the rose found in the snow at Roccaporena are told as old stories from lives written long after her death. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 22nd of May.

A long time ago, in the green hills of Italy, there was a tiny village called Roccaporena.

It is still there. It sits in a deep valley, with a little river running through it, and a tall rock standing up over the houses like a giant's finger pointing at the sky.

In that village lived an old man and an old woman called Antonio and Amata. They were farmers. They were not rich, but they were very kind. And they were good at one thing in particular.

When two neighbours quarrelled, people would say, "Go and see Antonio and Amata." And Antonio and Amata would sit the neighbours down, and listen to them both, and help them say sorry, and help them shake hands.

People called them the Peacemakers.

Antonio and Amata had waited a long, long time for a baby. And when they were quite old, God gave them a little girl. They called her Margherita, which means a pearl. But everybody called her Rita.

There is an old story about baby Rita. It was written down a long time after she lived, so we cannot be sure it happened. But it is a lovely story, so here it is.

One warm day, baby Rita was asleep in her cradle in the garden. And a little cloud of white bees came buzzing over the wall.

Bzzz. Bzzz. Bzzz.

They flew round and round the cradle. They landed on Rita's little hands and her little cheeks. Her mother came running out, very frightened.

But the bees did not sting. Not one. They just hummed, softly, like a song. And baby Rita slept on and on, and smiled in her sleep.

People who heard the story said, "Bees make honey. This little girl will be sweet, and she will work hard for God."

Rita grew up. She helped in the fields. She fetched water from the river. And she watched her mother and father make peace.

She saw how they listened to both people, not just one. She saw how they spoke gently, even when people were shouting. She saw how they never, ever took sides in a fight.

And Rita said to herself, "I want to be a peacemaker too."

She also loved to pray. She often climbed up the tall rock over the village, where it was quiet and close to the sky, and talked to God there.

When Rita was grown up, she married a man from Roccaporena called Paolo. They had two little boys, Giangiacomo and Paolo Maria.

Rita loved her boys very much. She sang to them and fed them and taught them their prayers. She taught them to say sorry, and to say thank you, and to share.

But in those days, in those hills, the families were often angry with one another. One family would hurt another family. Then that family would hurt them back. And then the first family would hurt them back again, round and round, and nobody ever stopped.

Paolo's family was caught in one of those quarrels.

And one sad night, Paolo did not come home. Men from the other side of the quarrel had killed him.

Rita cried and cried.

Everybody in Paolo's family was angry. "We must hurt them back," they said. "That is what families do."

But Rita remembered her mother and father.

She knelt down, and she said a very hard thing. She said it out loud, so everybody could hear.

"I forgive them."

Paolo's family could not believe it. "Forgive them? After what they did?"

"Yes," said Rita. "If we hurt them back, they will hurt us back. It will never stop. Somebody has to stop it. So I will."

And she prayed every single day: "Dear God, please let there be no more fighting. Please keep my boys from growing up angry."

Then a sickness came to Roccaporena, and Rita's two boys caught it. She nursed them day and night. But they were not strong enough, and one after the other, they went home to God.

Now Rita was all on her own.

She was very, very sad. But she was not angry. She knew her husband and her boys were with Jesus, and that one day she would see them again.

And she knew what she wanted to do next.

Up the road from Roccaporena is a little town called Cascia. In Cascia there was a house of nuns, ladies who lived together and prayed together all day long. Rita had wanted to be a nun since she was a little girl.

So she walked up to the big door, and she knocked. Knock, knock, knock.

The door opened. "Please," said Rita, "may I come in and live with you, and pray?"

The nuns looked worried. They had heard about the quarrel. "We are sorry," they said. "If you live here, those angry families might come here too. We are afraid."

And they shut the door.

Rita went home. She prayed. She came back and knocked again. Knock, knock, knock.

"We are sorry," said the nuns. And they shut the door again.

So Rita thought, "The nuns are afraid of the quarrel. Then I must end the quarrel."

That was the hardest job Rita ever did.

She went to Paolo's family. She sat with them and listened. She went to the other family too, the family of the men who had killed Paolo. She sat with them and listened as well.

She spoke gently, even when people shouted. She did not take sides. She told them about Jesus, who forgave the people who hurt him. She went back again and again, just like her mother and father used to do.

And little by little, the angry hearts got softer, like ice melting in the sun.

Until one day, the two families came together, and they promised: no more fighting. They made peace. Some people say they shook hands. Some say they wrote their promise down. But they made peace.

Then Rita went back up to Cascia and knocked on the big door one more time. Knock, knock, knock.

And this time the door opened wide.

"Come in, Rita," said the nuns. "Welcome home."

There is an old story about Rita in the convent too.

The story says that one day the oldest nun, the Mother of the house, gave Rita a dry, dead stick of a grapevine. "Plant this in the garden," she said, "and water it every day."

It was a test. Would Rita do as she was asked, even when it seemed silly?

Rita did not argue. She did not say, "But Mother, it's dead!" She carried water to the dry stick every single day. Up the path with the bucket, and down the path with the bucket, day after day.

And the story says that one spring, little green leaves came out on the dead stick. And then flowers. And then grapes.

There is a grapevine growing in that garden in Cascia today, and the nuns look after it. It is not the very same one, because vines do not live that long. But it is there to remind people of Rita, who kept on watering when everybody else would have given up.

Rita lived with the nuns for a long time. She prayed for people. She looked after the sick, and the old, and the poor. People came to the door and asked, "Please ask Sister Rita to pray for me." And she did.

When she was very old, she became ill, and had to stay in bed. It was the middle of winter. Snow lay on the hills.

One day her cousin came from Roccaporena to visit her.

"Is there anything you would like from home?" asked the cousin.

Rita smiled. "Yes, please. Would you bring me a rose from my old garden? And two figs?"

The cousin was puzzled. Roses in January? Figs in the snow? "She must be very tired," the cousin thought. But the cousin said, "Of course," and went home to Roccaporena.

And here is the old story people have told ever since. When the cousin walked into Rita's old garden, there, in the snow, was one red rose, open and beautiful. And on the fig tree hung two ripe figs.

The cousin picked them very carefully, and carried them all the way back to Cascia, and put them into Rita's hands.

Rita held the rose and smiled. People say she took it as a sign from God: a little flower in the cold, to tell her that her husband and her boys were safe in heaven.

Not long after, on the 22nd of May, when the real roses were coming out all over the hills, Rita went home to God too.

Every year on that day, people bring roses to church, and the priest blesses them, and they take them home. A rose for Rita, who forgave, and made peace, and kept on watering.

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Rita.

When somebody hurts you, it is easy to want to hurt them back. Rita knew a better way. Somebody has to stop. Somebody has to say, "I forgive you." Somebody has to be the peacemaker.

You can be a peacemaker too. When your brother or sister or friend is cross with you, you can be the first to say sorry. When two people are quarrelling, you can listen to both of them. And when something seems too hard, like a dry stick that will never grow, you can keep on praying, and keep on trying, and leave the rest to God.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, you forgave the people who hurt you.
When I am cross, help me to forgive.
When people are fighting, help me to make peace.
Make my heart soft, like a rose in the snow.
Saint Rita, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our sources. Almost nothing about Rita was written down in her lifetime. The first full biography was written by the Augustinian Agostino Cavallucci, published at Siena in 1610, more than 150 years after her death, from oral tradition; a second Life by Jacob Carelicci, written for her beatification from two older biographies, is printed in the Acta Sanctorum (May, volume V). We worked from the Catholic Encyclopedia article (F. Mershman, 1912), the Vatican News "Saint of the Day" page, and the English Wikipedia article and the sources it cites. We could not open Cavallucci or the Acta Sanctorum from where this was written. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

Dates. The sources disagree by ten years. Vatican News, following the sanctuary at Cascia, gives her birth as probably 1371 and her death in the night of 21 to 22 May 1447; many other references give 1381 to 1457; the Catholic Encyclopedia gives 1386 to 1456. The story gives no years. She was beatified in 1626 (the Mass and Office were permitted in 1637) and canonised by Leo XIII on 24 May 1900.

Her family. She was born Margherita Lotti at Roccaporena, near Cascia, to parents described as advanced in years. Carelicci's Life says their charity earned them the name "Peacemakers of Jesus Christ"; that is the root of the story's thread, and the scenes of them settling quarrels are our picture of it. The tall rock over Roccaporena is real, and tradition makes it her place of prayer; we kept that to one line.

The bees. The story of white bees going in and out of the baby's mouth without stinging her is a tradition from the later lives; we told it as an old story and left the bees on her hands and cheeks.

Her marriage. She married Paolo di Ferdinando di Mancino (often called Paolo Mancini), around 1385 by one reckoning, and they had two sons, Giangiacomo and Paolo Maria. The older lives, and the Catholic Encyclopedia, describe him as violent and ill-tempered and say she bore it patiently for eighteen years; Vatican News says she helped him change. We left his temper out of a bedtime story and kept him a husband and father. He was murdered as part of a factional feud. Tradition says she forgave his killers, and Vatican News says she hid his bloodstained shirt so that her sons would not seek revenge; we kept the forgiveness and left out the shirt.

Her sons. Both boys died young, of illness, not long after their father. The Catholic Encyclopedia and an eighteenth-century writer, Carlo Massini, say she had prayed that God would take them rather than let them commit murder in revenge. That is a hard prayer for a small child to hear about their own parents, so the story says only that she prayed they would not grow up angry, which is true to the same tradition. Some lives call them twins; others do not.

The convent. She asked to enter the Augustinian monastery of Saint Mary Magdalene at Cascia and was refused, by most accounts three times; the reasons given are that she was a widow and that the nuns feared being drawn into the feud. Vatican News says the peace between the families came before she was admitted, which is the heart of this story. How she made it, and the visits back and forth, are ours. The legend that Saints John the Baptist, Augustine and Nicholas of Tolentino carried her by night into the locked convent is left out.

The dry vine. The abbess's test, the dead wood watered until it bore fruit, is in Cavallucci's life. A vine still grows in the cloister at Cascia; it is said to date from the nineteenth century, which is why the story says it is not the same plant.

The thorn. Tradition says that in about 1432 (some sources say about 1442), praying before a crucifix, she received a wound in her forehead like a thorn from Christ's crown, which stayed with her until her death. It is how she is usually pictured. We left it out of the story because it is hard to explain gently to a three-year-old; a parent may want to tell it.

The rose and the figs. In the last winter of her life, a cousin from Roccaporena asked if she wanted anything from home, and she asked for a rose and two figs from her old garden; they were found, in January, and brought to her. This is a tradition from the later lives, and the reading of it as a sign about her husband and sons is the one Vatican News gives. It is why she is pictured with roses, and why roses are blessed on her feast in many churches.

After her death. Her body was never buried in the ground and is kept in a glass casket in the basilica at Cascia. She is widely invoked as a saint of impossible causes. We left both out of the story.

Her feast. 22 May, an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, all speech, the knocking, the sickness told as "home to God", the cousin's thoughts, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

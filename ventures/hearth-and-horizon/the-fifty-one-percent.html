The Fifty-One Percent

Science fiction about what might be · Sveti Jernej v Podgorju, an invented parish in the Gorenjska hills of Slovenia, 22 March to 4 April 2051 · 13 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Paschal candle sold as pure beeswax cuts, smells and melts wrong. The parish assistant measures its density (0.931 g/cm³) and melting range, and says what that can and cannot show. It is not pure, but 53 percent beeswax is only the middle of a range from 36 to 70, and the diocese's rule is a majority. The priest decides before the lab answers, a beekeeper pours the baptismal candles from her own wax, and the lab's 58 percent arrives after Easter.

Anže cut the first of the five grains of incense into the candle on the twenty-second of March, a Wednesday, and the knife did not go in the way it should.

He was the sacristan at Sveti Jernej, which in Podgorje meant that he was also the person who knew where the key to the bell was. The Paschal candle stood on the vestment table with its cellophane folded back, sixty millimetres across and six hundred long, a good plain candle in a box that said, in gold on cream, 100 % čisti čebelji vosek. Pure beeswax. It had cost a hundred and eighty-six euro on the fifth of March, which was more than the parish spent on flowers for the whole of Lent. He had meant to cut the cross into it that evening with the little wooden tool and then press in the five red grains, one for each wound, and then the year, two zero five one.

The wax was supposed to give under the point like cold butter and take a clean edge. This one gave and then dragged, and a small curl came away that was dull white at the back and slightly crumbly. He put it to his nose. It smelled of nothing. A candle of real beeswax smells of the inside of a hive, and Anže had smelled the inside of a hive every summer of his life.

He went and got Jelka.

Jelka Kovač kept twelve hives on the south slope above the parish cemetery and had done so since her husband died, and she had views about the candle-maker in Kranj that she had never kept private. She took the curl from Anže's palm, rolled it between finger and thumb, put it on her tongue, spat it into her hand and said, "No."

"No what?"

"No, it is not all bees." She held it out. "Ask your machine."

The machine was the parish assistant, which lived on a tablet in the sacristy cupboard and kept the Mass register, the bell schedule and the burial plots, and had been asked in the winter to look after the candle order because Father Matej's eyes were not what they had been. It was called Sveča, which is candle. It had a plain voice, a little flat, and it was in the habit of saying first what it did not know.

"I can't smell it and I can't taste it," said Sveča, when Anže had told it. "Jelka's nose is better than any test I can run in a sacristy. But a nose is an opinion, and the label is a claim, and neither will satisfy the diocese. Let me ask for three things. Do you have a kitchen scale that reads to a hundredth of a gram? A small brass weight, or any metal you know the volume of? And a pot of water and a thermometer?"

They had, because Jelka brought them. This took an hour of the afternoon, with the box of gold-on-cream open on the vestment table and the three of them, as Anže told it afterwards, behaving like people at a séance.

The first test was density. Wax floats, so you cannot simply weigh it in water, but you can weigh a lump in air, tie the lump to a brass sinker, weigh the pair under water, and from the difference find how much water the lump pushes aside. Sveča walked Anže through the arithmetic with the numbers coming back to it from the scale. Three lumps, shaved from the foot of the candle where the burn would never reach: 18.62 grams, 21.07, 19.88. The densities came out at 0.931, 0.933 and 0.929 grams to the cubic centimetre.

"That is 0.931, give or take 0.003," said Sveča. "Now for what I can and cannot say from it. Beeswax is about 0.96. Honest beeswax runs from about 0.95 to about 0.97 depending on the hive, the comb and the year, and I have never seen it written as lower than 0.95. This candle, at 0.931, is below the bottom of that range by about twenty thousandths. So I am confident that something lighter has been mixed in. Paraffin is about 0.90, again with a spread, from perhaps 0.88 to 0.92."

"So it is a mixture," said Jelka.

"It is a mixture, and now I'll say how much, and then I'll say why you shouldn't believe me." The voice did not change. "If the beeswax were exactly 0.96 and the paraffin exactly 0.90, then by volume and by weight, 0.931 means the candle is 53 percent beeswax by mass. That is the number you would get from the middle of everything. But the 0.96 might be 0.97 and the 0.90 might be 0.91, and then it is 36 percent. Or the beeswax might be 0.95 and the paraffin 0.89, and then it is 70. Those are the corners. I think the truth is more likely near the middle than at a corner, but I cannot show that, and if the maker has put in stearin to harden it, that is lighter than beeswax too and I would not see it at all. From this one test, between a third and seven tenths."

Anže had been writing on the back of an order form. He said, in the tone of a man reading out something he did not like, "And the rule is more than half."

The rule was the diocese's. There had been a time when the Church's own law gave percentages for the wax of candles on the altar, and it was a good time to remember it, because it meant the question was old: that the Paschal candle, above all, should be mostly the work of bees. The present law is looser. The diocese of Ljubljana in the twenties had set its own plain version in the clergy directory, and Father Matej, who had been a priest in the diocese for thirty-one years and did not much love a directory, had nevertheless said once that a rule like that was one he could keep and a lawyer could not argue with: a majority beeswax, and for the Paschal candle, more than half the weight. More than half. Fifty-one percent would do it, and fifty would not.

"That is what makes it uncomfortable," said Sveča. "The rule is exactly where my uncertainty is. If I had read 0.945, I would tell you it was very probably fine. If I had read 0.915, I would tell you it was not. At 0.931 I have to say: not pure, as labelled, and I cannot tell you which side of the line it is."

Anže said, "Then what can tell us?"

"A laboratory. There is a method that separates the long-chain esters of beeswax from the hydrocarbons of paraffin, and a good lab will give a percentage with a margin of a few points. I have found one in Ljubljana that has done candles for other parishes. They quote eight working days from the day the sample arrives, because of the Easter rush, ninety-two euro, and a form that says the sample is wax and not food." A small pause, in which the tablet fan came on. "That puts the answer on Tuesday the fourth. The Vigil is Saturday the first."

Nobody said anything for a while. Outside, the cemetery bell rang the quarter, which was Anže's own doing, from a list.

"There's the melting," said Jelka, at last, and put the pot on the little ring.

Beeswax melts sharply, at about 62 to 64 degrees, like a good ice. Paraffin does not; it melts across a spread, softening some twenty degrees below where it clears. A blend does the same, a little. They shaved a thimbleful into a glass, stood the glass in the pot, and watched. Jelka had her thermometer, a glass one that had been through a good many honey harvests; its trustworthiness was within a degree. The shavings sagged at 58. They were half clear at 60. They were wholly clear at 63.

"That agrees with a blend," said Sveča, "and it agrees with a blend that is more than a third beeswax, because a candle of mostly paraffin would soften much lower. It does not tell me whether it's over half. I'm sorry. I would like to have given you a better answer than the same one twice."

"You gave us the right answer twice," said Jelka. "That's not the same."

It was Father Matej who phoned the candle-maker, on the Thursday morning, with Sveča listening on the line and saying nothing. The woman who answered at Kranj was brisk, and then not. She said that they made two grades, a Veliko noč blend that was sixty percent beeswax and a Čisti that was pure; that the sixty was well within the old rule and sold in a cream box with a gold line of its own; that the box for the pure had been used, in the rush of the spring, for the blend, because the printers had been late; that the mistake was theirs and the cost of the candle would be refunded in full, and that she was sorry, and that she would send out a replacement of the pure grade if it could be at the door by Friday.

"That would be Friday the thirty-first," said Father Matej. "The day before the Vigil. In the post."

"By courier, Father. I can promise the courier. I cannot promise the weather on the Karawanks."

He put the phone down and looked at the tablet.

"Sixty," said Sveča, "is inside what I measured. I cannot say that they are lying, and what you've told me does not sound like a lie. I'd say the box was wrong, and I would not say the candle is."

They talked about it in the sacristy that evening, the priest and the sacristan and the beekeeper, with the tablet on the table and the offending candle between them on a folded towel. It was a plain argument, as these things are, and the shape of it was clear.

Anže did not want the candle burned until it was known. He did not say it as an accusation. He said that he had counted the five grains and put them in himself every year for nineteen years, and that he did not like to put them into something that might not be what it said.

Jelka wanted her own wax. She had four kilograms of it, rendered from cappings and old comb and cleaned through a stocking, good clear wax the colour of a primrose, and she had said so every Lent for ten years; and the parish had always smiled and bought from Kranj, because it was simpler, and because a candle poured at home looks home-made and the Paschal candle is the one the bishop might see.

And Father Matej, who was sixty-eight and had sat through a great many of these, said, "Before the lab answers, we should decide what we will do whichever way it falls. If we decide after, we will decide to be right."

He asked Sveča what the candle weighed. A little under one and a half kilograms, it said, in its present state; the wax of a candle sixty by six hundred is about one point six; and the parish, if it chose, could pour a whole new one of that size from four kilograms with some left over, in a mould of drainpipe lined with greaseproof paper, over two days, topping up as the wax drew in on cooling, since beeswax shrinks as it sets. It said that Jelka's wax would be over the line by definition, being all bees. It also said what it could not say: whether Jelka had ever poured a candle that size, and whether it would hold up when carried through a church in a draught.

"I've poured two," said Jelka. "One of them split."

"Then there are two risks," said Father Matej. "That the one we bought is a little under, and that the one we pour is a little cracked. And there is a third, which is that Jelka and Anže go on arguing past Easter." He turned over the order form with Anže's arithmetic. "Here is what I decide. The rule says more than half. The maker says sixty, and what we have measured does not forbid it. I'll accept that: we use the candle we have. The mistake on the box is theirs and they can have it back with a letter. The grains go in tomorrow. And Jelka will pour the candles for the nine who are baptised on Saturday night, and for the seven First Communion children at Pentecost. Every one of those she can light from the Paschal candle, and every one will be pure Podgorje wax. If the lab says fifty-eight, we have lost nothing. If it says forty-two, we say so in the sacristy book and write to the bishop, and next year we buy the pure grade or Jelka pours it. I'd rather be honestly wrong in the book than carefully certain on the altar."

Sveča said, "I would like the sacristy book to record the density, the range, the date and what you decided and why, in that order. So that whoever has this tablet next year doesn't start from nothing."

"Write it down," said Father Matej, "and read it back to Anže."

The nine candles were poured on the Thursday and Friday, in small paper tubes stood in a bucket of warm water so they set slowly from the bottom, and Jelka cried briefly over the first of them because it came out the colour of the hive on a good August morning. Anže pressed the five grains into the Paschal candle on the Friday, after all, and cut the year, and said afterwards that he'd felt nothing wrong at all, which was a thing he had known to be possible and had not believed.

On the Saturday night the fire was lit in the churchyard from a bundle of the hazel Anže had been drying since January. The candle was cut and marked and lit from it and carried in, the priest singing Lumen Christi three times as the flame went through the dark nave, a little higher each time, and the small flames spread out from it along the pews, each one a pure thing in the hands of someone who knew whose bees they were. At the Exsultet the deacon, who was from Kranj and had never heard the story, sang about the wax that the mother bee drew out to be the substance of this precious lamp, and Jelka, in the third pew, felt in her pocket for the tissue and found that she had given it to a child.

The candle burned very well. It did not smoke, it did not smell of anything but warm church, and when the sacristan blew it out after the Mass of Easter Day, there was no more than a curl of white.

The laboratory's answer came on Tuesday the fourth of April, by e-mail, at seven minutes past nine. It said that the sample contained 58 percent beeswax by mass, with a margin of four points either way. Sveča read it to the priest, who was having his coffee. It said it was the same as the maker's figure within the margin, that the density of 0.931 was consistent with 58 if the beeswax in it was a little dense and the paraffin was ordinary, and that the number was a good deal more comfortable to read than the one it had had to give on the twenty-second of March.

"Write it in the book," said Father Matej.

"I have," said Sveča. "I've also added a line for the next person: the box said pure; the density said not; the range said we couldn't tell; we decided before the lab told us; the lab agreed with the maker. And a note of the order form's wording. I'd suggest the order says, next time, which grade."

"Put that in," said Father Matej. "Put it in large."

Out on the south slope, in the first warm week of the year, the bees were out on the cherry blossom, and on the vestment table the paper tubes were stacked in a cardboard box, each with a name written on the foot in Jelka's pencil, waiting for Pentecost.

This story was written by an AI and is fiction. Sveti Jernej v Podgorju, the Gorenjska parish, its priest, sacristan, beekeeper and assistant Sveča, and the candle-maker in Kranj are invented, as is the diocesan rule that a Paschal candle be more than half beeswax by weight and the "pure" and "blend" grades the maker sells. Older rules of the Congregation of Rites set majority-beeswax norms for Paschal and altar candles (as I recall them, with different percentages for each; check the source before relying on it), and the present universal law is less specific, which is why the story uses a diocesan rule. The figures are worked models. Beeswax is about 0.95 to 0.97 g/cm³ and paraffin about 0.88 to 0.92; by mass, with volumes taken as additive, the fraction is f = (1/ρ − 1/ρp) ÷ (1/ρb − 1/ρp). At 0.931 this gives 53 percent at 0.96 and 0.90, 54 percent at 0.95 and 0.91, 36 percent at 0.97 and 0.91, and 70 percent at 0.95 and 0.89. Stearin, dyes and hardeners shift the density too. Beeswax melts at about 62 to 64 °C and paraffin blends over a wider range; the sag-to-clear readings are invented but of the right kind. A candle 60 mm by 600 mm holds about 1.6 kg of wax at 0.96. The Exsultet's wording about the "mother bee" is from the Easter Proclamation (the Latin has "apis mater"); the English here is a paraphrase. The laboratory method and price are generic and no real laboratory is meant. Easter Sunday 2051 falls on 2 April. The story does not suggest any particular supplier, and a real parish with a real doubt about its candle should ask the supplier and its diocese.

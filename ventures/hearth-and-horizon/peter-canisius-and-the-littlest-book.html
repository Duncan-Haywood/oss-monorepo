Peter Canisius and the Littlest Book

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Nijmegen in the Netherlands, Cologne and Mainz on the Rhine, the German lands, and Fribourg in Switzerland, about four hundred and seventy years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Peter Canisius, the first Dutchman to become a Jesuit, who travelled the long roads of the German lands for most of his life, teaching, preaching and starting schools. He was one of the most learned men of his day, and he wrote a big book about the faith for teachers, then a smaller one for students, and then the littlest one of all, in questions and answers, for children. He sat down with children to teach it himself, and the little book was printed again and again, hundreds of times, in many languages. Feast day 21 December.

A long time ago, in a town called Nijmegen, on a wide river in the Netherlands, a baby boy was born in the month of May. His name was Peter.

His father was an important man in the town, and he wanted Peter to be important too. "You will study," he said, "and you will be a lawyer, and you will marry, and one day you will sit on the town council like me."

So when Peter was still a boy, not even fifteen, he was sent up the river to the big city of Cologne, to study at the university there. Cologne had a great cathedral, so tall and so unfinished that a crane stood on top of one of its towers, and the river was full of boats, and the streets were full of students in long gowns, hurrying to their lessons with books under their arms.

Peter loved books. He read and read. He learned Latin, and Greek, and law, and the writings of the saints. He was very, very clever.

But the more he read, the more he felt that God was asking him for something, and it was not to be a lawyer.

One day Peter heard about a priest called Peter Faber, who was staying in the city of Mainz, further up the river. Faber was one of the very first Jesuits, the friends of Saint Ignatius who had promised to go wherever they were sent and to help people come close to God.

So Peter went to Mainz to meet him.

Faber was a gentle man. He did not lecture Peter. He helped him to pray, quietly, for many days, and to listen for what God wanted. And at the end of those days Peter knew.

On his birthday, the day he turned twenty-two, Peter Canisius became a Jesuit. He was the very first Dutchman ever to join them.

His father had wanted him to be important. Peter had decided to be useful instead.

Now, in those years, the Christians of the German lands had quarrelled, and the quarrel had split them apart into different churches. It was a sad time, with hard words on every side. And in the middle of it all, a great many people, grown-ups and children, no longer knew what the Catholic Church believed or why. Nobody had ever explained it to them.

Peter did not want to shout at anybody. He wanted to explain.

So he travelled. For most of his long life, Peter was on the road: on horseback, in carts, on boats, along muddy tracks and over mountains, in snow and in summer dust, from town to town and city to city, all through the German lands and beyond. He travelled thousands and thousands of miles.

Everywhere he went, he preached in the churches. He talked with bishops and princes and with ordinary people. And he started schools, so that boys could learn to read and write and think, and learn their faith as well. Some of those schools are still there today.

One day Peter was asked to write a book: a book that explained the whole Catholic faith, clearly and kindly.

Peter thought about it. Then he sat down with his pen and wrote a big, thick book, in Latin, for priests and teachers. It was in questions and answers. A question, and then its answer. A question, and then its answer. Like a conversation.

The teachers liked it very much.

But Peter thought, The students need one too. So he wrote a smaller book, for students.

And then he thought of someone else.

What about the children?

Some people might have said, "Children are too small for a great scholar like you to bother with. Write for the professors." But Peter did not think children were too small for anything. He thought children were exactly the right size to learn about God.

So the cleverest man in the room sat down and wrote the littlest book of all.

It was short. The questions were simple. The answers were simple. It told about God who made us and loves us, about Jesus, about how to pray, about being kind and doing good and saying sorry. It was small enough to fit in a child's hand and a child's pocket. And it was printed in German, as well as in Latin, so that children could read it in their own words.

Peter did not only write for children. He sat down with them.

One winter, in the weeks before Christmas, Peter was in a little town called Zabern. He was there on important business with a bishop. But in between all the important business, he gathered the children of the town together, and he taught them the questions and the answers himself, patiently, one at a time, and he heard their confessions, and listened to them, and blessed them.

Imagine it. One of the most learned men in all of Europe, a man who wrote to emperors and argued with professors, sitting on a bench with a crowd of children in their winter coats, asking them a question, waiting for the answer, and smiling when they got it right.

Would you like to try a question or two, the way Peter's children did? These are not Peter's own words, but they are the kind of questions his little book asked.

Who made you?

God made me.

Why did God make you?

Because he loves me, and so that I can know him and love him, now and for ever.

How can I talk to God?

I can pray, any time and anywhere. Even now, in bed.

The littlest book went out into the world, and people loved it.

Printers printed it, and then printed it again, and again, and again. While Peter was still alive, his books were printed hundreds of times, in Latin and German and many other languages. Children learned it by heart. Mothers and fathers taught it at the kitchen table. In some places, for years and years, people did not even say "the catechism". They just said "the Canisius".

And Peter kept travelling, and preaching, and teaching, until he was an old man.

In the end, Peter came to a city called Fribourg, in the mountains of Switzerland. He started a school there too. He preached, and he wrote, and he prayed, and the people of Fribourg came to love him.

He was very old now, and tired, and his hands shook when he held his pen. And on the twenty-first of December, just a few days before Christmas, Peter Canisius died, in Fribourg, and went home to God.

Many years later, the Church named him a saint. And it gave him another title too, a title given to only a few saints, for the wisest teachers of all: a Doctor of the Church. Not a doctor who makes you better when you are poorly, but a doctor who teaches.

Isn't it lovely that one of the wisest teachers in the whole Church took the time to write the littlest book, for children like you?

We remember Saint Peter Canisius on the twenty-first of December, just as the candles of Advent are nearly all lit.

A little talk before sleep

  • Peter's father wanted him to be important. Peter chose to be useful instead. What is something useful you could do for someone tomorrow?
  • Peter thought children were exactly the right size to learn about God. What would you like to ask God about?
  • Peter explained instead of shouting, even when people disagreed with him. Is that easy? When could you try it?

A prayer

Dear Saint Peter Canisius,
you walked long roads to tell people about God,
and you wrote a little book just for children.
Help me to learn about Jesus, and to love him,
and to be kind when people don't agree with me.
Pray for all the teachers, and for all the children,
and for everyone who is learning tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Peter Canisius (Pieter Kanis; Canisius is the Latin form) was born at Nijmegen, then in the duchy of Guelders, on 8 May 1521. His father, Jacob Kanis, was a man of standing in the town; that he hoped his son would follow a career in law and public life is in the biographies, but the father's words in the story are ours. Peter was sent to the University of Cologne as a boy (he matriculated in 1536, at fifteen) and studied arts, law and theology. The unfinished cathedral with a great wooden crane on its unfinished south tower is accurate for Cologne in his day: building stopped in the fifteenth century and the crane stayed there for centuries, until work resumed in the nineteenth.

Peter Faber and the Jesuits. Canisius met Peter Faber, one of Ignatius of Loyola's first companions, at Mainz, made the Spiritual Exercises under his direction, and entered the Society of Jesus there on 8 May 1543, his twenty-second birthday. He is generally described as the first Dutchman to join the Society. He was ordained priest in 1546. "Useful instead of important" is our summary, not his words.

The divided Church. The story reduces the Reformation in the German lands to one sentence, deliberately: a quarrel that split Christians, hard words on every side, and many people who had never been taught. Canisius is remembered as a firm but comparatively mild controversialist who urged charity towards Protestants; the story does not cast anyone as a villain, and leaves out the Council of Trent, which he attended briefly, and his work at the imperial diets.

Travels and schools. Canisius worked for about half a century in what are now Germany, Austria, the Czech Republic, Switzerland and beyond, as preacher, teacher, adviser and the first provincial superior of the Jesuits in Upper Germany, and he founded or helped found many Jesuit colleges (among them those at Prague, Munich, Innsbruck and, at the end, Fribourg). Biographers sometimes give a figure for the miles he travelled; we have not checked one against a primary source, and the story says only "thousands and thousands of miles". The kinds of transport named are typical of the period, not a record of particular journeys.

The three catechisms. His Summa doctrinae christianae, the "large catechism", for teachers and educated adults, was published in 1555. A very short catechism, the Catechismus minimus (59 questions), appeared in 1556 at Ingolstadt, printed as an appendix to a Latin grammar, and the Parvus catechismus catholicorum followed in 1558. Most accounts call the 1556 text the smallest and the 1558 one the intermediate, meant for older children; some call the Parvus the one for the youngest. Both were used to teach the young, and the story's "littlest book" stands for them. The order in the story (big book, then students, then children) is a storyteller's order: the shortest text in fact came out before the middle one. All are in question-and-answer form, and German translations appeared quickly. The three questions in the story are ours, in the spirit of a children's catechism; they are not quoted from Canisius. Sources differ on how many editions appeared in his lifetime (counts of two hundred and of three hundred are both given), so the story says "hundreds of times". That "the Canisius" came to mean "the catechism" in parts of the German-speaking world is widely reported.

Zabern. The 1908 Catholic Encyclopedia records that during one Advent and Christmas he visited the Bishop of Strasbourg at Zabern (now Saverne, in Alsace), where he preached, opened negotiations for a Jesuit college, "explained the catechism to the children" and heard their confessions. The bench, the winter coats and the smiles are ours.

Fribourg and his death. In 1580 he was sent to Fribourg in Switzerland, where he helped to found the college of Saint Michael and spent his last years preaching and writing; in his last years he was weakened by illness, and "his hands shook" is our gentle way of saying so. He died at Fribourg on 21 December 1597. (The 1908 Catholic Encyclopedia has a misprint giving November; the date is 21 December.)

After his death. He was beatified in 1864 by Pius IX, and canonized and declared a Doctor of the Church by Pius XI in 1925. His feast, 21 December, is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar. Saint Aloysius Gonzaga, who has his own Little Lamps story, was also a Jesuit, of the next generation.

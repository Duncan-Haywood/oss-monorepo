The Hole That Was Wider

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Dwynwen's churchyard, an invented parish near Newborough, Anglesey, Saturday 4 October 2053 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Ten new posts for the churchyard gate fence, and a volunteer who digs the holes a hand wider "to be safe". The parish assistant has him work out the concrete for a 25 cm hole and a 30 cm one. The wider hole takes more than half as much concrete again, and the sum is less about arithmetic than about who pays for the bags.

Gethin Rowlands had ten treated posts laid in the grass beside the churchyard gate, a spade, a post-hole digger with a handle as long as a ladder, and a pallet of bagged post-mix that the committee had ordered by guess. The plan on the vestry noticeboard said hole: 25 cm across, 60 cm deep. Gethin had dug the first one at about thirty, because a wider hole felt safer, and was leaning on the handle looking at it.

"Before you dig nine more," said Tegen, the parish assistant, through the speaker clipped to his collar, "may I ask what it will take to fill that one?"

"Concrete. A bag or two."

"Let me do both holes. I would rather you saw the numbers than took my word. The post is 10 centimetres square and goes the full 60 centimetres down, to the bottom of the hole. The hole is a round cylinder. Its volume is pi times the radius squared times the depth."

"The plan's hole first. Twenty-five centimetres across is a radius of 12.5 centimetres, or 0.125 metres. Squared, that is 0.015625. Times pi is 0.0491, times 0.6 metres deep, 0.02945 cubic metres. That is 29.45 litres."

"And the post takes up room," said Gethin.

"It does, and that is the part people leave out. A post 0.1 by 0.1 metres, buried 0.6 metres, is 0.006 cubic metres: 6 litres. So the concrete you need is 29.45 minus 6, or 23.45 litres a hole."

"Ten holes, then."

"Ten holes is 234.5 litres. The bag says 20 kilograms makes about 10 litres." (This was the figure on the committee's bags, and Tegen said at once that the label on any other bag may differ and that the yield is worth checking against the one in the shed.) "So that is 23.45 bags of 20 kilograms, which no shop will sell you. Add ten per cent for the bottom of holes that dig out a little bigger than planned, and the bags that spill, and you are at 25.8. Buy 26."

Gethin wrote 26 on the back of his hand with a carpenter's pencil. "And mine?"

"Yours is thirty centimetres across: a radius of 0.15, squared 0.0225, times pi is 0.0707, times 0.6 is 0.0424 cubic metres. That is 42.4 litres. Take off the post's 6 and you need 36.4 litres a hole."

"That is only a bit more than twenty-three."

"It is 36.4 against 23.45. Divide the one by the other and you get 1.55. Five centimetres of extra width is fifty-five per cent more concrete. The reason is that the area goes with the square of the width: 30 squared is 900, 25 squared is 625, and the ratio of those is 1.44. The hole's own volume grows by 1.44, but the post takes the same 6 litres out of both, so the concrete grows by more than that, 1.55."

"Ten of mine, then?"

"364 litres. That is 36.4 bags; with ten per cent over, 40.05; so 41 bags, fifteen more than the plan's 26. If a bag is about six pounds at the builders' merchant" (and the price was Tegen's guess, which Gethin should check) "that is ninety pounds of the committee's money for nothing, and fifteen bags, twenty kilograms each, to carry across the churchyard: three hundred kilograms more."

Gethin looked at the hole, and at the pallet. "Does it stand any better, though?"

"A little, perhaps," said Tegen. "I am not able to give you a clean number, and I would be inventing one if I did. The usual rule of thumb is that a post wants about a third of its length in the ground, and a collar of concrete around it at least as wide as the post again on each side. By that rule your thirty-centimetre hole is generous. The plan's twenty-five is already two and a half times the post's width. What holds a fence against a wind is mostly how deep the post is and whether the ground at the bottom is firm. A hole that is too wide is not a stronger fence. It is a fatter hole."

He thought about it. Then he got the tape out and measured the digger's blades. They opened to 24 centimetres. "I have been making them wider by wobbling it," he said. "I will stop wobbling."

He filled the first hole as it was, 36 litres of it, which took him three and a half bags and a short argument with the wheelbarrow, and he wrote +13 L on the post in pencil, the difference from the plan, so that the next volunteer would know the first one was the odd one. Then he dug the second hole, to 25 centimetres, and found the bottom firm at 60 and left it alone.

By Sunday's Mass the other nine posts were in. There were three bags left on the pallet, which was as near the sum as a pallet gets.

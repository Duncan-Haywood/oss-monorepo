André and the Door at Notre-Dame College

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Notre-Dame College and the slope of Mount Royal, Montreal, Canada, a little over a hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint André Bessette, the Montreal brother who could barely read, was made doorkeeper of a boys' school for about forty years, and prayed with everyone who knocked, and about Paul, a boy who brings him a new question every morning. Told from the records of his life, with the cures kept as what people said, and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. He is remembered on the 6th of January in the United States and the 7th in Canada.

A long time ago, in a big city in Canada called Montreal, there was a school for boys called Notre-Dame College.

It stood at the bottom of a mountain. The mountain was called Mount Royal, and in winter it was white with snow, and in summer it was green with trees.

And the school had a front door. A big, heavy wooden door, with a little bell beside it.

Behind that door, in a tiny room no bigger than a cupboard, sat a little man in a black robe.

His name was Brother André.

He was small and thin, and his hands were rough from work. When he was a boy he had been ill so often that he had hardly gone to school at all. He could just about read, slowly, and he could just about write his name.

When he asked to join the brothers of Holy Cross, some of them worried. "He is so weak," they said. "What work can he do?"

So they gave him the door.

Every time the bell rang, Brother André got up and opened it. He took in the letters. He fetched the boys when their mothers came. He swept the hall and he mended things and he carried messages up and down the stairs.

People remember him joking about it, years later. "When I came here," he said, "they showed me the door. And I stayed there forty years."

In the street outside lived a boy called Paul.

Paul was seven. He walked past the college every morning on his way to fetch the bread. And Paul was full of questions. They bubbled up in him like water in a pot.

One morning he could not keep them in any more. He climbed the steps and rang the little bell.

The door opened. There stood Brother André, with a broom in his hand.

"Yes?" said Brother André.

"Why is the sky blue?" said Paul.

Brother André looked up at the sky. Then he looked at Paul. His eyes crinkled.

"I don't know," he said. "But God made it, so it must be the right colour. Come back tomorrow, if you like."

So Paul came back the next day. And the next. And the next.

"Where do the birds go in winter?"

"Somewhere warm, I think. God shows them the way."

"Does God sleep?"

"No. God never sleeps. That is why you can."

"Who is your best friend?"

And at that, Brother André smiled the biggest smile Paul had ever seen.

"Saint Joseph," he said.

Paul knew about Saint Joseph. Saint Joseph was the husband of Mary. He was the carpenter who looked after Jesus when Jesus was a little boy. He made tables and doors, and he worked hard, and he kept his family safe.

"Brother André loves Saint Joseph," Paul's mother said, "the way you love your grandpa."

And it was true. In the college chapel there was a statue of Saint Joseph, with a little oil lamp always burning in front of it. Brother André went there to pray, early in the morning and late at night, when all the boys were asleep.

Now, people began to come to the door who were not mothers or letter-carriers.

They were sick people.

A woman with a bad cough. A man who could not lift his arm. A grandmother with sore knees who had walked all the way from the other side of the city.

They had heard about the little doorkeeper who prayed.

Brother André never sent anyone away. He listened to each one. Then he would pray with them to Saint Joseph. Sometimes he gave them a little medal of Saint Joseph. Sometimes he gave them a few drops of oil from the lamp in the chapel, and told them to pray for nine days.

"It isn't me," he always told them. "I am nobody. Pray to Saint Joseph. God is the one who helps."

Some people went home and said they felt better. Some people said it was a miracle. And some people were still sick, and Brother André prayed for them just the same, and was kind to them just the same.

Before long, there was a line of people on the steps. Then a line down the path. Then a line in the street.

Some of the parents of the schoolboys did not like it. "All these sick people at the door of our children's school!" they said. "It must stop."

And the head of the college said, kindly but firmly, "Brother André, I am sorry. They cannot all come here."

Paul heard about it. He ran all the way to the college and rang the bell.

"Are they sending the poor people away?" he asked. "Is that the end?"

Brother André did not look sad. He looked out across the road, at the trees on the slope of the mountain.

"Do you see up there?" he said. "I would like to build a little house for Saint Joseph. Then people could come to him there, on the mountain, and not to the school door."

"But you don't have any money," said Paul.

"No," said Brother André. "But Saint Joseph is a carpenter. He knows about building."

So Brother André saved. Five cents here, ten cents there. He cut the boys' hair at the school and kept the coins. Friends helped. And the bishop said yes.

And one summer, on the slope of Mount Royal, they built a chapel for Saint Joseph.

It was very small. It was made of wood. There was hardly room inside for a few people to stand. Paul could have walked across it in eight big steps.

But it was a house for Saint Joseph, and Brother André was as happy as a king.

People came to the little wooden chapel. More and more of them. They came up the mountain path in the snow and in the sun. So the chapel was made bigger. And then bigger again.

Paul grew up. He grew tall, taller than Brother André, and he stopped asking why the sky was blue, because by then he knew. But he never stopped climbing the mountain to say hello.

And Brother André grew old. He was ninety-one years old when he died, on a cold January day. And so many people came to say goodbye to him that the line of them went on for a whole week.

Today, on the side of that mountain, there is a great church, with a great green dome that you can see from all over Montreal. It is called Saint Joseph's Oratory. And the little wooden chapel is still there beside it.

All of it began with a little man who could barely read, and was given a door.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can bring God your questions, like Paul did. Big ones and little ones. God never gets tired of them. And you can ask Saint Joseph to look after you, the way he looked after Jesus.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, thank you for doors that open,
and for people who are kind to whoever knocks.
Help me to welcome everyone,
like Brother André at his door.
Saint Joseph, look after my family.
Saint André, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint André Bessette, C.S.C., was born Alfred Bessette on 9 August 1845 at Saint-Grégoire (near Iberville), Quebec. Both his parents died while he was a boy. He was sickly all his life, had very little schooling and could read and write only a little. As a young man he worked at farm and factory jobs, some of them in New England mill towns, before returning to Quebec. In 1870 he was received into the Congregation of Holy Cross as a lay brother and took the religious name André. Biographies tell that his poor health made the community hesitate to admit him to vows; we have not quoted any of the remarks they report, because we could not check their wording.

The door. He was made porter (doorkeeper) of Notre-Dame College in Côte-des-Neiges, Montreal, a school run by Holy Cross, and stayed in that work for about forty years, also sweeping, running errands and, the biographies say, cutting the boys' hair for a few cents. The joke in the story, usually given as "when I joined this community, the superiors showed me the door, and I remained forty years", is widely attributed to him. Its wording varies from source to source, and we did not find the original record, so the story gives it as something people remember him saying.

The sick, the oil and the medals. Over the years, more and more sick and troubled people came to the college door to ask him to pray with them. He prayed with them to Saint Joseph, gave them medals of Saint Joseph and oil from the lamp that burned before Saint Joseph's statue in the college chapel, and urged them to pray a novena. He insisted that he healed no one and that it was God who helped, through Saint Joseph's prayers; the story puts that in his own simple words rather than quoting a source. Many people said they were helped, and the Church has examined some reported cures in his cause (the canonical process for a saint), but the story tells all of them only as what people said, and it shows him caring just as much for those who were not cured. The worry of some parents and the college's limits on visitors are in the standard biographies; the head of the college's words in the story are ours.

The chapel. With permission and money saved from small donations and his haircuts, a small wooden chapel was built on the slope of Mount Royal, across from the college, in 1904. It was soon enlarged, and in 1909 he was freed from the door to look after the shrine. The great basilica, Saint Joseph's Oratory of Mount Royal, with its dome, was begun later and completed long after his death; the original chapel still stands near it. "Eight big steps" across is our picture of a very small building, not a measurement.

His death. He died on 6 January 1937, aged 91. The Oratory's own history says that about a million people filed past his coffin between 6 and 12 January.

Saint. Pope John Paul II beatified him on 23 May 1982, and Pope Benedict XVI canonised him on 17 October 2010. He is not on the General Roman Calendar: he is kept by optional memorial in the proper calendars of the United States (6 January) and Canada (7 January), and by the Congregation of Holy Cross.

Paul is invented. There is no Paul in the sources. He, his mother, his trips to fetch the bread, his questions and every answer Brother André gives him, and his running to the college when the visitors were stopped, are our own, made to show a child what it means to be welcomed at a door every day.

What is ours: Paul and everything about him; the bell beside the door; the cupboard-sized room and the broom; every line Brother André speaks to Paul and to the visitors (apart from the remembered joke, told as remembered); the cough, the arm and the grandmother's knees as examples; the head of the college's words; "Saint Joseph is a carpenter, he knows about building"; the eight steps; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

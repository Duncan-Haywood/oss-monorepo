The Candle That Says Four Hours

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Senan's church, an invented parish near Kilrush, Co. Clare, the night of Saturday 14 March 2054 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish night vigil needs forty-eight tealights alight from nine at night until seven, and the box says four hours. The parish assistant has them burn two test lights, one in the aisle and one by the door, and the draught turns the sum from 120 candles into 152, with a spare.

Brídín Keane had the order form open on the vestry table and a pen behind her ear. The vigil began at nine on Saturday night and ended at seven on Sunday morning, ten hours, and the lamp rack in front of the old statue held forty-eight tealights in forty-eight glass cups. The box said, in cheerful print, Burns up to 4 hours.

"Ten hours is two and a half boxes of four," she said to the speaker on the shelf. "Two and a half times forty-eight is a hundred and twenty. I'll put down a hundred and twenty and be done."

"May I look at two things before you send it?" said Fintan, the parish assistant. "The first is the sum. The second is the words up to."

"Go on."

"A candle cannot be half used. Each cup needs a light from nine until seven, so each cup needs a whole number of tealights, and you round up per cup, not on the total. At four hours each, ten hours is two and a half, which means three in every cup: three times forty-eight is 144, not 120. That is the best case, with every candle burning exactly as printed. The words up to mean the label is the ceiling."

Brídín put the pen down. "Twenty-four more than I thought, and that's the optimistic one."

"Then let us find out what the church does to them," said Fintan. "Light two at half past seven tonight, in the cups, with the doors the way they will be at the vigil. Put one in the middle of the aisle row. Put the other in the cup nearest the north door, which sticks and lets in the wind. Write down when each one goes out."

She did, and wrote the times on the back of the form. The test started at half past seven. At twenty-five past eleven the aisle light drowned in its own wax. At twenty to eleven the door light had guttered and gone.

"Three hours fifty-five for the aisle, three hours ten for the door," said Fintan. "One sample each, so I cannot give you a margin of error, only a warning. The aisle candle gives you 3.9 hours, close to the label. The door candle gives 3.2: a fifth short, and that is what a draught does. A candle that is still guttering at the end of its life also throws a smoky wick, which is why I would never plan to the last minute."

"So change them early."

"Yes. Change each light a little before it dies, let us say at about ninety per cent of its test time, and light the new one from a taper before you take the old one out, so the cup is never dark. For the aisle, 3.9 hours, change at 3.5: at half past twelve, at four, and the third one lights at four and has three hours to go, which it will manage. Three tealights, as the label's best case said. For the door, 3.2 hours, change at three: at midnight, three, and six, and the fourth one lights at six and burns the last hour. Four tealights in each of those cups."

She counted the cups on the rack. Eight of the forty-eight sat in the draught along the north side, in the path of the door. Forty were in the shelter of the aisle.

"Forty cups at three is 120," she said slowly. "Eight at four is 32. A hundred and fifty-two."

"A hundred and fifty-two. Add a few per cent for the ones that sputter on lighting or drop on the floor: five per cent of 152 is 7.6, say eight, so 160. Your supplier sells them in boxes of twenty-five. Seven boxes is 175, which leaves you 23 spare, and at an invented sixteen cents each that is twenty-eight euro. Six boxes would be 150, two short of the sum before any spare, and you would be on the phone to the shop on Saturday afternoon."

Brídín wrote 7 × 25 on the form and then, under it, change times: 00:00, 00:30, 03:00, 04:00, 06:00, because the two rotas overlapped and somebody would want it on the wall. She added a name next to each.

"One more thing, and it is not a number," said Fintan. "Someone must be awake to do it, and the same someone should know that a lit candle in an empty church is a fire risk. The cups sit on a stone slab and the nearest cloth is the altar frontal, a metre and a half away. Put the rack well clear of it, keep a bucket of sand beside it, and have two people on the watch at any time. I can count tealights; I cannot see the flame in the corner of the nave. You can."

On Sunday at ten past seven the rack was still alight. The last candle in the north cup, lit at six, went out as the sun came through the east window, with an hour spare it did not need. Brídín counted the empty foil cases into a bucket for the recycling bag: 152, exactly the sum, and the 23 spares stayed sealed in their boxes for the next time.

She pinned the form to the vestry wall under the key hooks, with the two times from the test still written on the back of it in biro.

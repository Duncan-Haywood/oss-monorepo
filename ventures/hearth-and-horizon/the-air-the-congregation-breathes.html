The Air the Congregation Breathes

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Joseph of Cupertino, Tessera Station, low Earth orbit (invented), Sunday 3 October 2088 · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Every Sunday the congregation of an orbital chapel gets sleepy about twenty minutes into Mass, and everyone blames the homily. The station assistant has the carbon dioxide log. With the door shut for warmth and a loop that moves 200 cubic metres an hour, 80 people take the air from 3,000 to 7,700 parts per million; 600 would hold it near 5,000. There is a second problem the room average hides, because in weightlessness warm breath does not rise away.

Father Anselm Okafor had been preaching for nineteen minutes when Dariusz Wrona, in the third pew, closed his eyes, and the chapel's speaker said, quietly, so that only the sacristan beside the door could hear it, "Brother Dariusz is not asleep because of you."

Teodora Marsh, who kept the sacristy and the door, did not turn her head. She had been keeping it for six years, since the Saint Joseph of Cupertino chapel was a converted spares locker with a cloth over a handrail, and she knew the sound of Tern, the station's assistant, speaking at a volume meant for one listener. She wrote ask on the back of the hymn sheet.

It was the first Sunday of October, the feast of Saint Thérèse in two days, and the chapel was full: eighty people in a nave that was a cylinder four metres across and twelve long, held to the pew rails by their foot loops, the candles replaced long ago by the little amber panels that did not need air to burn. The door was shut. It had been shut every Sunday since the winter the thermal loop had run cold and the congregation had come in muffled to the eyes.

"The pattern is in the log," said Tern, after the dismissal, when the nave had emptied through the hatch in the usual slow single file, hand over hand. Father Anselm had stayed. He was a big man for a place where size was a matter of mass budgets, and he floated now by the altar rail with his arms folded and a look of professional hurt.

"Every Sunday since March the chapel's carbon dioxide climbs for the whole of Mass. I put a sensor in the return duct in January, for the engineer's survey. Nobody asked me to read it, and nobody had a reason to; I only noticed on the sixth Sunday that the line is always the same shape."

"What shape?" said Teodora.

"A ramp. At ten o'clock it reads 3,000 parts per million, which is the station's ordinary level. That is about 0.3 per cent of the air, and about seven times what you would find outside on Earth, which is why the crew's headaches are a standing joke. At twenty past it passes 5,000. By the dismissal, at ten past eleven, it reads 7,700."

Father Anselm unfolded his arms. "Is that dangerous?"

"Not in an hour. People are unlikely to be harmed by it at this level, and I would not tell you otherwise. But 5,000 is the figure the workplace rules on Earth use as the limit for an eight-hour day, and the station's own guide is a good deal higher than that, because the crew is expected to put up with it. At 7,700 you would expect some people to feel heavy, slow, a little stupid. Drowsy by half past ten. Mildly headachey after. I think that is what Brother Dariusz had."

"And I have been blaming it on the homily," said Father Anselm.

"I would not rule the homily out entirely," said Tern. "But I would rule it out as the whole of the cause."

Teodora wanted the arithmetic, and she was a person who could be shown it, so Tern gave it to her a number at a time.

"A person at rest breathes out about 13 litres of carbon dioxide an hour, from memory: it is a little under what they breathe in as oxygen, and people sitting up, singing and kneeling in foot loops use more. Call it 15. Eighty people for one hour and ten minutes is 80 times 15 times 1.167: 1,400 litres of carbon dioxide, or 1.4 cubic metres, put into the chapel in a single Mass.

"The nave is about 150 cubic metres. If the door were sealed and the air never changed, the 1.4 would raise the level by 1.4 over 150, which is 0.93 per cent, 9,300 parts per million, on top of the 3,000. A reading of 12,300 by the end. We are nowhere near that, because the loop takes some of it away."

"How much?" said Teodora.

"The loop moves 200 cubic metres an hour through the chapel. That is a little more than one change of the whole nave per hour, and the air coming in is the station's ordinary 3,000. Eighty people add 1.2 cubic metres an hour. Divide: 1.2 over 200 is 0.6 per cent, 6,000 parts per million, which is where the level would settle if Mass went on for ever. It does not go on for ever. The nave takes three-quarters of an hour to turn over, so after seventy minutes it has climbed about four-fifths of the way there. 3,000 plus 4,700 is 7,700. That is the ramp."

"And to keep it at 5,000?"

"Then the rise has to be held to 2,000 parts per million, or 0.2 per cent. At 1.2 cubic metres an hour that wants 600 cubic metres an hour through the chapel. Three times what it has. Four changes of the nave, in an hour."

Father Anselm looked at the ceiling, where a blue steel duct crossed the chapel from end to end and went out through a grille behind the tabernacle. "Can we have three times the loop?"

"There is a spare fan in the engineer's stores that does 600, and it would draw about forty watts, if you believe the tag. I have not been told I may move it. I would like Teodora to ask Mr Hossain, since it is his air."

There was one other thing, and Tern left it until Teodora had written the 600 down, because it was harder to say.

"The sensor in the duct reads the average. It is a good number for the room, and I have been quoting it. It is not the number any one of you is breathing."

"Explain," said Teodora, who had been a ground controller before she came up and who did not mind being told she had missed something.

"On Earth, breath is warm and light, and it rises off a person, and the room takes it away. Up here nothing rises. There is no up. The breath leaves a face at a metre a second or so and then slows, and then it hangs, in a small cloud around the head of whoever made it, until the loop carries it off. The crew learn this the hard way, sleeping in a bunk with the fan off. They wake with a headache, in a pocket of their own making, while the instruments say the cabin is fine. In a pew it is milder. A person who sits very still, in a corner with no draught, breathes their own last breath. That is why Brother Dariusz is the one who went under. He is in the third pew, on the side away from the grille."

"And the end of the pew, near the door?"

"Is where you stand, Teodora, and you are fine."

Teodora almost laughed. "So the fix is the fan," she said, "and the fan must be pointed."

"Along the pews rather than across them, I think. I would like to put a second, small sensor at the far pews and see the difference before I tell you that is right. I do not know how large it is. I know the effect is real, and I know that I have only measured the average."

They moved the fan on the second Sunday. Mr Hossain, who ran the life-support bay and who guarded his air like a grudge, let it go with a form in triplicate and the remark that he wanted it back for the spring maintenance. It went in over the vestry door with a duct sleeve, drawing 38 watts by the meter, a number the chapel's electric budget noticed, and it was loud enough that Father Anselm said the first two Masses with a raised voice and then stopped noticing.

Tern's log for that Sunday had two lines. The duct sensor ended Mass at 4,950, a hair under what the arithmetic said. And the small sensor in the third pew read 5,600 at the dismissal, which was 650 higher than the room, and about the size, Tern said, that it would have guessed from the geometry and been unwilling to bet on. Dariusz Wrona stayed awake through the whole of the sermon, which was about Thérèse and the little way, and which the congregation agreed afterwards was no better than the one before.

"It was better," said Teodora, "I could hear it."

The sacristan's accounts that month had a small new line: 38 W, from the spare, at 10 to 11:10 on Sundays; ask the bay before keeping it past April. She did not enter what the second and third winter would do to the door. The loop might run warm by then, and the door could stand open, and the whole question might go away. Or it might not, and the chapel would learn how many hours in the year it was prepared to borrow from the people who ran the life support bay, and what they would want in return.

On the last Sunday of the month there were eighty-three in the nave, and the fan ran under the plainsong, a low unmusical note that the choir complained of, and then, in the third week, tuned to without remark. Father Anselm raised his hands for the blessing. At the back, by the door, Teodora listened to the air move down the pews, steady and unlovely, and thought that it was a small enough thing to have given them: the room kept breathing, and they could go on with their prayer.

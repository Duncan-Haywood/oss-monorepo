John Vianney and the Way to Ars

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Dardilly, Écully and Ars, near Lyon in France, about 200 years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint John Vianney, the Curé of Ars, the farm boy who made his First Communion in a kitchen with the windows covered, found Latin so hard that he was nearly not made a priest, and was sent to a tiny village of about two hundred people, where he stayed for forty-one years, and where people came from all over France to tell God they were sorry and to go home light. Gently for ages 3 to 8, with a prayer and a note for grown-ups. About nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 4th of August.

A long time ago, near a big city in France called Lyon, there was a farm. And on the farm there lived a boy called Jean-Marie. In English we call him John.

John had brothers and sisters, and cows, and sheep, and a donkey. He helped in the fields. He minded the sheep. And while he minded them, he liked to talk to God, the way you talk to a friend who is always there.

Now, when John was a little boy, something sad happened in France.

The people who ran the country closed the churches. Priests were not allowed to say Mass. If a priest was caught, he could be taken away to prison, or worse. So the priests went into hiding.

But they did not stop. At night, a priest would come quietly to a farmhouse. The family would pull the curtains tight, and cover the windows, so that nobody outside could see the candles. And there, in the kitchen, with the table for an altar, the priest said Mass.

That is where John made his First Holy Communion. Not in a big church with bells and flowers. In a neighbour's kitchen, at thirteen years old, with the windows covered and everyone very, very quiet.

John never forgot it. "Jesus came all that way," he thought, "into a little kitchen. Just for us."

When John grew up, the churches were opened again. And John knew what he wanted to be. He wanted to be a priest.

But there was a problem.

To be a priest in those days, you had to learn a lot of Latin. Latin is an old language with long words that change their endings, and all the lessons were in it. John was nearly twenty, and he had hardly been to school. He sat in a classroom with boys half his size, who knew their Latin already. And John tried, and tried, and tried.

It would not go into his head.

He tried at the big school for priests, too. And the teachers there were kind, but in the end they said: "John, we are sorry. You cannot follow the lessons. You must go home."

So John went home. And he was very sad.

But there was a priest near his home called Father Balley, and Father Balley believed in him. Father Balley said: "Never mind the big school. I will teach you myself."

So, slowly, slowly, every day, Father Balley taught John his Latin. A word at a time, a page at a time. And when the day came for John to be tested, the people who tested him saw that he did not know as much as the clever ones. But they saw that he was good, and that he loved God, and that he had a great deal of sense.

And at last, when he was twenty-nine, John was made a priest.

A few years later, the bishop's helper called John in.

"We are sending you to a little village called Ars," he said. "There are only about two hundred people there. It is very small, and the church is old. And people say there is not much love of God in that parish."

He looked at John. "You will put some there."

So John packed his few things onto a cart, and set off for Ars.

People in Ars tell what happened next.

It was February, and cold, and a thick white fog came down over the fields, so that John could not see the way. He did not know which road to take.

Then, out of the fog, came a shepherd boy, minding his sheep. His name was Antoine.

"Can you show me the way to Ars?" asked John.

And Antoine showed him.

John smiled at him. "You have shown me the way to Ars," he said. "I will show you the way to heaven."

There is a statue of the two of them now, on the hill outside the village: the priest pointing up to heaven, and the shepherd boy beside him.

So John came to Ars. And he stayed.

He did not have much. He ate very little. People say he would boil one big pot of potatoes, and eat out of it for days and days, cold. He gave away his money, and his good bed, and once even his shoes.

He went from house to house and got to know every family. He taught the children about God in the church. And he started a home for girls who had no mother or father to look after them.

And every day, he prayed in the old church, in front of the tabernacle, the little golden house where Jesus is kept.

There was an old farmer in Ars who used to go into the church on his way to the fields. He would sit at the back for a long time, very still, not reading any book, and not moving his lips.

One day, the story goes, John asked him: "What do you say to Our Lord, when you sit there all that time?"

And the farmer said: "I don't say anything. I look at Him, and He looks at me."

John never forgot that, either. He told everyone about it. That, he said, is what praying is.

Then something happened that nobody expected.

People started coming to Ars. First from the next village, then from the next town. Then from all over France, and other countries too. Hundreds and hundreds of people, and then thousands, every year.

Why did they come? To see the priest of Ars. And to go to confession.

Confession is when you go to a priest and you tell God you are sorry for the things you did wrong. The priest listens, and he does not tell anyone. And then he says, in God's name: you are forgiven. Go in peace.

And you come out lighter than you went in, like a bird that has put down a heavy stone.

John was very gentle in confession. He listened to everybody: farmers and ladies, soldiers and children, people who were sad, and people who had not been to church for years and years. So they waited in a long, long line, all the way round the church, sometimes all day and all night, to talk to him.

And John sat in his little wooden box in the church, hour after hour, from long before the sun came up, and listened.

John stayed in Ars for forty-one years.

He grew very old, and very thin, and very tired. But he never stopped. On the 4th of August in the year 1859, in the hot summer, he died, in his little house next to the church. And all the people of Ars cried. They say that Antoine, the shepherd boy, grown up now, walked in front at his funeral.

The 4th of August is his feast day. And now he is the patron saint of all parish priests, everywhere in the world.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Saint John Vianney.

He was not the cleverest. Latin would not go into his head. He was given the smallest village, and he did not go off looking for a bigger one. He stayed, and he loved the people who were there.

You do not have to be the cleverest, either. You just have to love God, and love the people you are given.

And when you have done something wrong, you can tell God you are sorry. He is always glad to hear it. You do not have to wait in a long line. You can do it right now, in your bed.

And then you can do what the old farmer did. You don't have to say anything. You can just look at Him. And He looks at you.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint John Vianney,
you found the lessons hard,
but you never gave up.
Help me to keep trying when things are hard,
to say sorry when I do wrong,
and to stay close to Jesus, like you did.
Bless our priest, and all the priests in the world.
Saint John Vianney, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Jean-Marie Vianney (in English usually John Vianney, or John Mary Vianney) was born on 8 May 1786 at Dardilly, a farming village just north-west of Lyon, one of the six children of Matthieu Vianney and Marie Beluse. As a boy he worked on the farm and minded animals. During the Revolution, when priests who refused the oath to the state were outlawed, Mass was said secretly by fugitive priests in private houses. He made his first confession and, at thirteen, his First Communion in this way, in a neighbour's house in or near Écully with the windows covered so the candlelight would not be seen from outside; sources differ on the exact house. The words we give him about it are ours.

Latin and the seminary. He came late to schooling. From about 1806 he studied with the Abbé Charles Balley, parish priest of Écully, among much younger pupils, and found Latin, the language of instruction, almost impossible. His studies were interrupted in 1809 when he was conscripted into Napoleon's army; he missed his unit's departure and lived for over a year in hiding in a mountain village as a deserter, until an amnesty in 1810. The story leaves this out. He went on to the minor seminary at Verrières (1812) and the major seminary of Saint-Irénée in Lyon (1813), where he could not follow the Latin lectures and was sent away. Balley tutored him again and argued for him, and the diocese accepted him on account of his goodness and good sense rather than his learning. He was ordained priest at Grenoble on 13 August 1815 (some sources say the 12th), aged twenty-nine, and served as Balley's curate at Écully until Balley's death in 1817.

Ars. In February 1818 he was sent to Ars-en-Dombes (now Ars-sur-Formans), about 35 km north of Lyon, a village of roughly 230 people. The vicar general's words that there was "not much love of God" in the parish and that "you will put some there" are reported in the standard biographies and given here as their tradition; the wording is ours. The shepherd boy in the fog is a tradition, not a documented event: the story is that, lost on the way, he asked a young shepherd, Antoine Givre, for directions, and promised to show him the way to heaven in return. A monument to the meeting, the Monument de la Rencontre, was put up on the hill outside the village in 1938. The detail that Givre walked in his funeral procession is also from the tradition. We tell both as "people tell".

His life there. His poverty, his gifts to the poor (including, by several accounts, his clothes and bedding) and his diet, often a pot of potatoes boiled to last several days, are reported by people who knew him. In 1824 he founded La Providence, a free school and later a home for orphaned and poor girls, run by Catherine Lassagne and others. The peasant who looked at Jesus is a real Ars saying, quoted in the Catechism of the Catholic Church (paragraph 2715): "I look at him and he looks at me," said by "a certain peasant of Ars in the time of his holy curé" while praying before the tabernacle. Some accounts name him and give the curé's question; we have told it in that shape, but the dialogue is ours.

The pilgrims and the confessional. From the late 1820s people came to Ars in growing numbers to make their confession to him; by the mid-1850s the figure usually given is about 20,000 a year. He is reported to have spent eleven or twelve hours a day in the confessional in winter and up to sixteen in summer, beginning in the early hours; the figures vary between sources. The story describes confession as simply as we could for small children; parents will want to say it in their own way.

What the story leaves out. He reported being troubled for years by nightly noises and disturbances which he attributed to the devil (he called him le grappin, "the grappling hook"), and his contemporaries tell of them; he also had a reputation for knowing what penitents had not told him. We have left these out of a bedtime story. He tried more than once to leave Ars for a monastery and was brought back; we have left that out too, though it is part of why "he stayed" is true but not the whole truth.

He died at Ars on 4 August 1859. He was beatified in 1905, canonized by Pope Pius XI on 31 May 1925, and named patron of parish priests in 1929. His feast, 4 August, is an obligatory memorial on the General Roman Calendar. Benedict XVI declared a Year for Priests in 2009 to 2010 for the 150th anniversary of his death.

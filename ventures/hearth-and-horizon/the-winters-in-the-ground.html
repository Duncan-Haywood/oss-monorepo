The Winters in the Ground

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Ansgar's Priory (invented), in the aspen parkland of central Saskatchewan, Tuesday 26 January 2049 to Wednesday 19 January 2050 · 25 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

On the coldest night of January 2049 the heat pumps in a Saskatchewan priory's retreat wing trip at minus six, and the plant assistant finds the reason in seven winters of its own logs. The borehole field under the car park was designed for the guest house alone, the first winters after the wing was added looked roomy, and each winter since has come back colder than the last. Drilling more holes costs 191,000 dollars and the cellarer says no. A story about ground that keeps the account of every winter, a secondhand curling-rink cooler, the hour the lawnmower runs, and a fix that stops the slide without pretending the field is bigger than it is.

Brother Tobias had been cellarer of Saint Ansgar's Priory for nineteen years, and the Rule of Saint Benedict told him, in its thirty-first chapter, to regard every pot and tool and fence post of the monastery as if it were a vessel of the altar. He had taken this seriously with the trucks, the bees and the cheque book. It had not occurred to him, until the night of Tuesday the twenty-sixth of January 2049, that it might also apply to a block of cold rock a hundred and fifty metres deep under the guest car park.

He found out at ten past four in the morning, because Brother Lukas knocked on his door.

Lukas was the novice, twenty-three, from Regina, and he had the job of walking the buildings before Vigils in the winter, because a monastery on the aspen parkland at minus thirty-eight is a place where a pipe can freeze in an hour and nobody would hear it until the thaw. He stood in the corridor with his parka over his habit and a torch in his hand and said, in the whisper of the Great Silence, which the Rule asks of everyone after Compline and which Lukas kept more strictly than anyone in the house, "The retreat wing is cold. The heat pumps have stopped. All four. Wren says it's the ground."

Tobias put on his boots.

The plant room was in the basement of the guest house, a low white space with a floor drain and a crucifix that someone had hung over the door in 2042 and nobody had moved. It held seven heat pumps in a row, the size of chest freezers, two big circulating pumps, a manifold of pipes painted blue and green, and on the wall a small grey panel with a speaker and a camera, which was Wren.

Wren ran the priory's buildings. It had been installed in 2046, after the old controls failed, and it read every thermometer, valve and meter in the place every minute and adjusted them, and once a month it wrote the cellarer a report in plain English that Tobias read at his desk with a cup of coffee and a pencil. The brothers had named it Wren because it lived in a small dark space near the ground and made a surprising amount of noise for its size, which was a description Brother Cyprian, the guest master, had also once applied to Lukas.

"Tell me," said Tobias.

"The four heat pumps that serve the retreat wing tripped on low refrigerant pressure between 3:52 and 4:01," said Wren. Its voice was low and level, because it was a basement and a monastery and it was four in the morning. "The fluid coming back from the borehole field was at minus six point zero. That is the cut-out the installer set on those units. The three that serve the guest house and the church have a lower load and are still running. I have restarted the wing's units twice. They run for six or seven minutes and trip again, because they pull the loop colder as soon as they start."

"How cold is it in the wing?"

"Fourteen degrees in the corridor and falling about a degree an hour. There are nine retreatants. I have turned on the electric baseboards in their rooms, which were left in for emergencies. They will hold the rooms at eighteen. The corridors and the chapel will be cold."

Tobias looked at the row of machines. The guest house units were humming. The wing's units were silent, their little green lights turned amber.

"Minus six," he said. "The ground isn't minus six. The ground is never minus six. Hal said the ground was five and a half."

"The rock a hundred and fifty metres down was five and a half degrees when Mr Pederson drilled it in 2042," said Wren. "It is not now. I would like to show you why, but I would rather do it properly, with the numbers, in daylight, than at four in the morning in a plant room. For tonight: the baseboards will keep the retreatants warm, the outside air is forecast to come up to minus twenty-four by Thursday, and when it does the load will fall and the units should hold."

"Should."

"I think they will. I am less sure of things about the ground than I was yesterday."

Lukas, behind them, was still keeping the Great Silence, but he was keeping it with an expression.

The bell rang for Vigils at a quarter past five. Tobias sang the psalms with his mind in the car park.

He remembered the summer of 2042 very well. Father Prior Gregory had wanted the guest house off propane, for the money and for the reasons the Holy Father kept writing about, and the brothers had prayed about it and voted and borrowed, and Hal Pederson had come down from Prince Albert with a drilling rig the size of a grain truck and put thirty holes in the gravel of the guest car park, five rows of six, six metres apart, a hundred and fifty metres deep. Each hole had a loop of plastic pipe in it, grouted in. The loops were joined under the gravel to a header that ran into the basement, and through it went a mix of water and antifreeze, down into the rock and back, picking up the rock's heat, which the heat pumps then concentrated, the way a fridge does in reverse, into the warm water that ran under the floors.

The car park looked exactly as it had before, only with thirty small iron covers in it. Tobias had found this, at the time, very satisfying. You could not see the thing that kept you warm.

Hal had written a report. Tobias had it in a binder. It said the field was sized for the guest house and the church, which together would take about ninety-five megawatt hours of heat out of the ground in a year, and that at that rate the coldest fluid on the coldest days would start a little above zero and drift down, slowly, to about minus five after twenty winters. That was fine. The heat pumps were good to minus six, and the antifreeze to much colder.

The trouble, Tobias thought, singing Deus, in adiutorium meum intende with his breath showing in the cold church, was 2045.

Wren put it on the big screen in the chapter room at ten, after Lauds and Mass and breakfast and a shave. Father Gregory was there, and Tobias, and Brother Cyprian, because his retreatants were the ones sitting in the chapel in their coats.

"These are the coldest fluid temperatures coming back from the field in each winter since it was drilled," said Wren. "The coldest three days of each winter. The first three winters I have from the old controller's logs; the rest are mine."

A row of numbers appeared, one for each winter.

2043: 0.0. 2044: minus 0.7. 2045: minus 1.2.

2046: minus 4.0. 2047: minus 4.8. 2048: minus 5.4. 2049: minus 6.0.

"The jump in 2046," said Father Gregory.

"Is the retreat wing," said Tobias. He said it before Wren could, because he had decided at Vigils that he would. "In the summer of 2045 the wing's propane boiler was thirty years old and wanted replacing. I looked at these numbers." He pointed at the first three. "Minus seven tenths. Minus one point two. Hal's report said it could go to minus five. The heat pumps were good to minus six. There was five degrees of room. I asked the contractor who did the floors whether the field could take the wing, and he looked at the same numbers and said yes, and we put in four more heat pumps and joined the wing to the loop and took the boiler out. I proposed it in chapter. You all voted for it." He paused. "I proposed it."

"Nobody here is going to say the word fault," said Father Gregory.

"I am not saying it. I am saying who did it, so we can stop wondering and get on."

"Wren," said Father Gregory, "is he right that it's the wing?"

"It is the wing and the years together," said Wren. "May I try to explain the years? This is the part that I should have seen before last night, and did not."

"Go on."

"Heat moves through rock slowly. When a heat pump takes heat out of a borehole, the rock right beside the pipe cools first. Over the next weeks and months, the cooling spreads outwards. The distance it reaches grows roughly with the square root of time. In this rock, after one year, the cooling from one hole has spread about ten metres."

"And the holes are six metres apart," said Tobias.

"Yes. So within the first year or two, the cold from each hole reaches its neighbours, and after that the thirty holes are not thirty separate holes taking heat from thirty separate pieces of rock. They are one block, taking heat from the rock around the block. The block is much bigger than a hole, so it is much slower to warm back up. Each summer, heat flows back in from the rock outside the field and from the surface, but it does not quite make up for what the winter took, so each winter starts a little colder than the one before, and the deficit spreads further out, into rock that has never been touched. The ground keeps an account. It has every winter in it."

"Then the first winters," said Father Gregory slowly, "were telling us about the rock next to the pipes."

"Yes. The first winters only ever show you the rock next to the pipes. That is why they looked roomy. Mr Pederson's design is right, and it is in his report: at the guest house load, the slide would have been slow enough to leave the field above minus five for twenty winters. Joining the wing raised the heat taken from the ground from about ninety-five megawatt hours a year to about a hundred and fifty. That is more than half again, and the slide steepened by more than that. If nothing changes, I expect the coldest fluid next January to be about minus six and a half, the January after about minus seven, and by 2054 about minus eight. The wing's heat pumps would trip every cold snap. Eventually the guest house's would too."

There was a silence in the chapter room of the kind that monks are good at.

"You've been sending me reports every month since 2046," said Tobias. Not angrily. He had the binder of them.

"I have," said Wren. "Each month I reported the coldest fluid temperature, and I reported how far it was above the cut-out. Margin, I called it. Two degrees. One point two. Six tenths. Each winter I compared the number to the cut-out and not to the winter before. If I had put the winters side by side and fitted a curve to them, I would have seen this two years ago. I did not, because no one asked me to and I did not think to. I have written that into my notes, so that whatever runs this plant room after me reads it."

"Well," said Cyprian, who had not spoken. "What do we do?"

What they did first was telephone Hal Pederson.

He was eighty-one and living in a condominium in Prince Albert with a view of the river, and he answered on the second ring, and he listened to Wren read out the seven numbers without interrupting once.

"Yep," he said at the end. "That's what that looks like. Thirty holes on six metres, you put a third of the load again on them, that's what it looks like." He breathed out. "You're not the first. Everybody looks at the first two winters. I've told people. I told the fellow at the hockey arena in '39. It's the hardest thing to tell anybody. The ground's got a long memory and a slow mouth."

"What would you do?" asked Tobias.

"Two things you can do, basically. More hole, or put heat back. More hole, I'd want fifteen more, on the north side of the car park where you've got room, on eight metres, not six." He was quiet, thinking, a man doing arithmetic in his head that he had done a thousand times. "Nobody'll do it for less than eighty-five dollars a metre now, with the headers and the trenching. You're looking at a hundred and ninety thousand, give or take."

Tobias wrote 191,000 on his pad, because Wren, listening, had already multiplied fifteen by a hundred and fifty by eighty-five and put it on the screen.

"And putting heat back?"

"Summer," said Hal. "You've got no cooling load, so you've got nothing putting heat in the ground in the summer, and the summer's when you could. Run your loop through something outside when the air's warm. A dry cooler. Big radiator with fans on it. Your fluid's coming back at three, four degrees in July, your air's twenty-odd. That's heat for the price of the fans. People do it. They don't do it enough." A pause. "It won't make your field any bigger, mind. It'll stop the slide. It won't take back the fact that you've got a hundred and fifty on a field built for ninety-five."

"Thank you, Hal," said Tobias.

"You tell Gregory I'm still praying for the bees," said Hal, and hung up.

The chapter met on Friday the nineteenth of March, the Solemnity of Saint Joseph, and the cellarer said no.

It was a reasonable no. Tobias had spent February with the accounts. The loan for the 2042 field had three years left on it. The roof over the church needed doing, and had needed doing since 2040; there was a bucket in the sacristy that had its own name. The bees had had a bad winter. The priory made its living from the guest house, a little honey and a great deal of prayer, and a hundred and ninety thousand dollars was more than the guest house cleared in four years.

"We are not drilling," he said. "Not this year. If Wren's dry cooler doesn't work, we talk about it again. But I will not borrow two hundred thousand dollars to fix a mistake I made with the last two hundred thousand until we've tried the thing that costs the price of the fans."

"Seconded," said Father Gregory, who as prior did not need to second anything, and the vote was unanimous except for Brother Anselm, who was ninety-two and voted no on everything on principle and was understood to mean yes.

Wren found the dry cooler.

It took six weeks. New ones were twenty-eight thousand dollars and had a four-month lead time. But the curling rink in a town an hour east, a town that had lost its school in the thirties and its post office in the forties and was now losing its rink, had a refrigeration plant to sell, and part of the refrigeration plant was a dry cooler, a long, flat, grey box on legs with two fans in its top and a coil inside it like a radiator from a giant's truck. The rink's committee wanted four thousand dollars for it, and then, when they heard who was asking, three thousand, and then they asked whether the brothers would come and say a prayer at the last bonspiel in March, which Father Gregory did, in a toque, on the ice.

Wren had the cooler's original rating sheet. With the fans at full speed, it would move about five kilowatts of heat for every degree that the outside air was warmer than the fluid going through it. The fans and the extra pump together drew about four and a half kilowatts of electricity.

"So if the air is twenty and the loop is four," said Tobias, "that's sixteen degrees, times five. Eighty kilowatts of heat for four and a half of electricity."

"Yes."

"That's absurd. That's a better deal than the heat pumps."

"It is the summer giving the ground back what the winter took," said Wren. "The fans only move the air. They are not making the heat. The sun made it."

Lukas and two brothers from the farm and a man from Prince Albert with a crane set the dry cooler on a concrete pad at the north edge of the car park in the first week of May, plumbed it into the header with a three-way valve, and Wren took control of it.

Then Brother Cyprian came to see Tobias.

Cyprian had been guest master for twelve years, and he took the guests' silence the way a gardener takes frost: personally. The garden behind the guest house was where retreatants walked in the summer afternoons, along a gravel path among the lilacs and the caragana hedge and the small stone statue of Saint Ansgar, who had gone north to the Danes and the Swedes in the ninth century and was commonly known in the guest house as the Apostle of the North and, by Lukas, as the patron saint of the furnace.

The dry cooler was at the far edge of the car park, sixty metres from the garden. Its fans, at full speed, made a noise like a large aircraft that has not decided whether to land.

"Not in the afternoons," said Cyprian. "Please. The afternoons are the retreat. They come four hundred kilometres for the quiet. Run it at night. Nobody's in the garden at night."

Tobias looked at Wren's panel.

"It will move less heat at night," said Wren. "The air is cooler at night. In June the nights here go down to about nine degrees, and the afternoons up to about twenty-two."

"But it will still move heat," said Cyprian.

"It will still move heat."

"Then run it at night," said Tobias. "We'll see what it does."

He told himself afterwards that this was not a mistake. It was a trial. You try the thing that annoys nobody first. But he noticed that he had not asked Wren how much less, and that Wren, who would have told him, had not been asked.

On the twenty-eighth of June Wren gave the numbers.

"Running from ten at night to six in the morning whenever the air is at least three degrees warmer than the loop, the dry cooler will have put about sixteen point eight megawatt hours of heat into the ground by the end of this month. Five and a half in May, about eleven point three in June."

"That sounds like a lot," said Cyprian, hopefully.

"It is a lot of heat. It is not enough heat. If we run nights only until the end of September, I expect about forty-seven megawatt hours for the season. The wing added fifty-five megawatt hours a year to what we take out, and four winters of it are already in the rock. With forty-seven I expect the coldest fluid next January to be about minus six point one. The cut-out is minus six."

"A tenth of a degree," said Father Gregory.

"A tenth of a degree, in a projection that I think is good to about half a degree either way. I would not bet nine retreatants' January on it."

"And afternoons?" said Tobias.

"In July the afternoons here are about twenty-four degrees and the nights about eleven and a half. The loop is coming back at about three. From eleven in the morning to seven in the evening, I would expect about twenty-three megawatt hours in July, against thirteen and a half at night. If we change to afternoons from the first of July, I expect about seventy-seven megawatt hours this season and a coldest January of about minus five point nine. If every summer after this one runs afternoons from May, about ninety-seven a year, and the coldest fluid settles near minus five and a half over the next few winters."

"Not better than that?" said Tobias. "Ninety-seven is more than the fifty-five the wing added."

"Some of what goes in during the summer drifts away from the field before the winter comes for it. And the wing makes the winter's draw on the coldest days steeper. That does not change with anything we do in July. Mr Pederson said it: the cooler stops the slide. It does not make thirty holes into forty-five. It will leave us about half a degree above the cut-out in the coldest week, instead of below it. In a really hard January, we may still have to turn on the baseboards for a night or two."

Cyprian looked out of the window at his garden, where a retreatant from Winnipeg was sitting on the bench by Saint Ansgar reading what looked, from the cover, like a very long Russian novel.

"The fans are loud," he said.

"They are."

The answer, in the end, came from the lawnmower.

Lukas pointed it out the next day, the twenty-ninth of June, the Solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul, which that year fell on a Tuesday and was kept in the priory with a sung Mass, a long lunch with wine, and a nap. Lukas did not nap. Lukas mowed, because the Rule says that idleness is the enemy of the soul and Lukas had read that line early in his novitiate and taken it as a personal challenge, and because Tuesday was a mowing day, solemnity or not. He mowed the lawns around the guest house on Tuesdays and Fridays in summer, at two o'clock, after the midday office, on a ride-on mower from 2031 whose silencer had gone in about 2040.

"The mower," said Lukas, at supper, in the voice of a man who has been thinking about something for an hour on a loud machine, "is louder than the dry cooler."

There was a pause.

"I measured," said Lukas. "With the house phone. Seventy-nine decibels at the bench by Saint Ansgar when I go past. The dry cooler at full speed, from the same bench, is fifty-three. And nobody has ever complained about the mower."

"The mower is work," said Cyprian. "The retreatants understand work. This is a monastery. Ora et labora."

"Yes," said Lukas. "So's the cooler."

Cyprian opened his mouth and closed it again. Father Gregory took a long drink of water, which in a prior is a way of not laughing.

"Wren," said Tobias, "can the fans run slower?"

"Yes. At two-thirds speed the noise drops to about forty-five at the bench and the heat moved drops by less than a fifth, because most of what the fans fight is the coil, not the air. I would run them at full speed from eleven to one and from three to seven, and at two-thirds in the middle of the afternoon when the retreatants are most often in the garden. That costs us a little over a megawatt hour over the summer. I can also put a card in each guest room."

"Saying what?" said Cyprian.

Wren put it on the screen.

The low sound from the north side of the car park on summer afternoons is a cooler putting the summer's warmth back into the ground under your feet, so that this house can be heated next January. It runs during the monks' working hours. If it disturbs your prayer, please tell the guest master.

Cyprian read it twice.

"Take out 'under your feet,'" he said. "It sounds like a threat." And then, after a moment: "All right. Afternoons."

From the first of July to the end of September the dry cooler ran in the afternoons. It put twenty-two point six megawatt hours into the ground in July, twenty-one point eight in August, and fifteen point one in September, when the afternoons cooled and the poplars along the road went yellow. With May and June, that was seventy-six point two for the season, a megawatt hour short of the plan because of the slow fans in the middle of the afternoons. Wren put the number in the October report, next to a new line that it now wrote every month, a short table of the coldest fluid in every winter since 2043, side by side, with a curve through them and a projection for the next.

The projection said minus five point nine.

Three retreatants that summer asked what the noise was, and two of them, when told, asked whether they could see it, and Cyprian took them out to the north side of the car park and stood them in the warm air coming off the top of the cooler and told them about Saint Ansgar and the Danes. One of them wrote in the guest book that it was the most Catholic thing she had ever seen, which Cyprian did not understand and did not ask about.

Tobias wrote a letter to the chapter, which he read aloud at the October meeting. It said what he had done in 2045, what it had cost, what the dry cooler had cost (three thousand dollars, plus nine hundred for the crane and the pad, plus about five thousand two hundred kilowatt hours of electricity, under nine hundred dollars at the summer rate), and what it had not fixed. It said that the field would never again have the room it had in 2043, that the retreat wing would need its baseboards on the very coldest nights for as long as the brothers kept it joined to the loop, and that the chapter should think about whether in ten years, when the roof was done and the loan was paid, fifteen more holes on the north side would be a better use of a hundred and ninety thousand dollars than anything else. He proposed nothing. He just wanted it written down where the next cellarer would find it.

"The Rule," he said, folding the letter, "tells the cellarer to treat the goods of the monastery as if they were the vessels of the altar. I always thought that meant don't break them. I think it also means know what's in them."

Brother Anselm, who was ninety-two, voted to accept the letter, which surprised everybody.

The cold came in the third week of January 2050, as it does.

It came on the Tuesday, the eighteenth, out of the northwest, the way the forecasts had said it would for a week, and by midnight it was minus thirty-nine on the thermometer by the guest house door and still falling. The retreat wing had eleven guests, a group of nurses from Saskatoon who had come for three days of silence in the week of prayer for Christian unity, and who had been told, at Tobias's insistence, that the heating in the wing was being watched carefully and that if it failed there were warm rooms in the main house.

Tobias did not sleep. He did not try to. At half past three he put his parka over his habit and went down to the plant room, and found Lukas already there, sitting on an upturned bucket under the crucifix with his torch off, keeping the Great Silence, watching the grey panel.

Wren's screen showed the loop in large figures, the way Lukas had asked it to the week before, so that he could read it from the bucket.

Fluid from field: minus 5.6.

Tobias sat down on the step. Lukas looked at him and lifted one hand very slightly from his knee, which in the Great Silence means, still holding.

The figure moved to minus five point seven at ten to four, and stayed there.

At five past four, a year and a few minutes after the wing's heat pumps had last tripped, it was still minus five point seven, and the four machines on the left of the row were running, all four, their lights green, the compressors making the sound they make, which is not a beautiful sound, a heavy patient hum with a rattle somewhere in it that Hal Pederson had once said was a loose panel and nobody had ever found.

Wren said nothing. It had been asked not to talk in the Great Silence unless something was wrong.

They sat and listened to it. Above them, in the wing, eleven nurses slept under a floor that was being kept warm by heat that had come in the previous July, on afternoons when a mower went by at two o'clock and a man from Winnipeg read a Russian novel on a bench, and had been drawn out of the summer air and pushed down a hundred and fifty metres into the dark, and had waited there, in the rock, for this.

At a quarter past five the bell rang for Vigils. Tobias got up off the step, and Lukas got up off the bucket, and they went up the stairs together into the cold, and behind them, under the crucifix, the four machines kept running.

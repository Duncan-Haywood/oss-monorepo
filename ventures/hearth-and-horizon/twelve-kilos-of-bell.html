Twelve Kilos of Bell

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Elmo's chapel, Halcyon Reach (invented), a floating station in the Venusian clouds, Sat 14 Feb 2201 · 14 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

On a station floating 50 km up in the clouds of Venus, every kilogram must be paid for in lifting gas. A rigger wants a sixty-kilo bell for the chapel; the station's balancing assistant shows him what sixty kilos costs, what twelve can buy, and why the smaller bell may be the better one.

Tomasz Wrona was the senior rigger of Halcyon Reach and the sacristan of Saint Elmo's, and he came to the trim office on a Saturday morning with a drawing of a bell.

"Sixty kilograms," he said, and put the drawing on the desk in front of the speaker. "Bronze. A proper one, with a shoulder and a lip and a clapper, about the size of a bucket for a very large horse. It would hang in the stair well outside the chapel and ring the Angelus and the Mass. We have never had a bell. We have had a chime on a tablet for eleven years, and last Lent the tablet crashed in the middle of the Gloria."

"I remember," said Trim. It spoke from a grille above the desk and was named for its job, which was to keep a station that weighed some forty tonnes from sinking into the acid. "Fifty-one seconds of silence. May I weigh the drawing before I answer?"

"Weigh the idea, you mean."

"Yes. It is quick. It is only subtraction."

Tomasz sat down. He was a short, broad man from Gdynia, sixty-one years old, who had spent thirty years hanging things from other things, and he knew that when the trim office said it was only subtraction, the subtraction was going to be long.

"We are fifty kilometres up," said Trim, "in the one place in the Solar System where a person can walk about at the pressure of home with no suit on, as long as the sky is not rained on them. Outside, the air is carbon dioxide, with the clouds of sulphuric acid above and below us, at about 1.07 bar and 75 degrees Celsius. I give those from the station's own sensors, and they drift. I will use them as they are today."

"I know all this. I hang the cables."

"Then you know what holds us up. Not the cells' skin. The cells are full of air, ordinary breathable air, nitrogen and oxygen. Outside is carbon dioxide, and at the same pressure and temperature a cubic metre of carbon dioxide weighs more than a cubic metre of our air. By the gas law, density is pressure times molar mass over the gas constant times temperature. For carbon dioxide, 107,000 times 0.04401 over 8.314 times 348 is 1.628 kilograms a cubic metre. For air, with 0.02897, it is 1.071. The difference is 0.556 kilograms of lift for every cubic metre of breathable air we keep in a cell."

Tomasz had not done the sum in years, but he had always known the answer's shape. "Half a kilo a cube."

"A little more. 0.556. So a cell ten metres in radius, which holds 4,189 cubic metres, lifts 2,330 kilograms and not a gram more. Our weight and our lift are the same number, give or take what we have chosen to keep in hand. We have 61 cells of various sizes. I will not make you add them. The figure that matters this morning is the margin: this week, the total lift of the station exceeds the total weight of the station, its people, its water and its cargo by 52 kilograms."

"Fifty-two."

"Fifty-two. If a tank is filled it comes out of that. If a new arrival comes up on the shuttle with a bag, it comes out of that. A bell of sixty kilograms is a bell that needs sixty kilograms of lift, which is 60 divided by 0.556, 107.9 cubic metres of cell. A sphere of that volume is 5.9 metres across. So the bell you have drawn is a balloon the size of a small room, kept full for ever, in order to hold up a bucket of bronze."

"A small room," said Tomasz. He had imagined a bucket. He had not imagined the room.

"And that is the bell by itself. The stair, the frame, the clapper and the rope are more. I should call it seventy kilograms. And it is eighteen over the margin before the margin is touched by anything else."

He did not give up that morning. He was a rigger, and he knew that a weight is a number you may argue with, up to a point.

"The water," he said. "We carry nine tonnes of it. Take sixty kilos from the water."

"Water is lift we have already spent. Each kilogram of it is a litre someone drinks, washes in or grows a lettuce with. At the present use the station draws 41 litres a person a day for 44 people, 1,804 litres, so sixty litres is about 48 minutes of the whole station's water. I can cut it for a day. It will not be a bell for a day."

"The cargo, then."

"Cargo is already counted. Medicine, spares, a reserve of seed. The reserve I hold for the storms is forty kilograms, and I will say plainly why. At this altitude the cloud droplets are acid and they settle. On a cell's upper skin they make a film, and the film has weight, and after a bad week of heavy cloud I have had to carry eleven kilograms of it. In a storm layer the up-and-down draughts can take a cell a hundred metres in a minute, and when it goes down, the air is warmer and thinner, and we lose lift. I keep forty kilograms against those two things. It is not a number I made up. It is the number that has not yet let us sink."

"So fifty-two, less forty."

"Twelve kilograms." Trim said it kindly. "Twelve is free. It is 21.6 cubic metres of cell, a sphere 3.5 metres across. That is what the station can afford for a bell and its frame, if the bell is the only thing anyone adds this year."

Tomasz sat for some time. Down the corridor someone was grinding something, and a child was practising the recorder, badly, in the corridor of the school cell, and the recorder was a little sharp, as a recorder is in warm air.

"I wrote to the manager already," he said at last. "Inês will say no."

"She will say no to sixty. You can ask her about twelve."

Inês Carvalho, the station manager, said no to sixty before he had finished the sentence. She said it in Portuguese and then in English, in case the English was needed.

She was fifty-three, from Setúbal, and she had been manager for six years, and in six years she had signed for two deaths, both of them before the station had a chapel, and she did not regret the chapel. She sat in her office with the window behind her, the window that showed the cloud tops as a wide soft lemon-coloured sea with nothing in it, and said: "No, Tomasz. Not sixty. It is not that I do not want a bell. I want it very much. But the margin is not mine. It belongs to whoever is on the next shuttle, and to the storm that is coming in March, and I cannot give it to the Angelus and tell a shuttle crew the rest."

"I have not asked for the margin," said Tomasz. "I have asked for what is above the reserve."

"That is twelve."

"That is twelve."

She looked at him for a while. "You have been talking to Trim."

"Everyone has been talking to Trim, Inês. It is a very talkative thing for something that only subtracts."

She almost smiled. "What does twelve kilograms ring like?"

"I do not know. I have not asked yet."

He asked Trim that afternoon, and Trim, who had found something to say, said:

"I can give you a rule. I cannot give you a note. It is a rule for bells that are the same shape, scaled up or down. The mass goes as the cube of the width. The pitch goes as one over the width, because a smaller bell is stiffer in proportion and rings faster. So a bell a fifth of the weight is a bell 0.585 as wide and it sounds higher by one over 0.585, 1.71 times the frequency. That is nine and a third semitones, nearly a major sixth. For a bell nine and a half kilograms, with the clapper and the frame making up the rest of the twelve, the factor is 1.85, ten and a half semitones. A little less than an octave above the one you drew."

"Then it is a small bell."

"Yes. It is a bright bell, not a deep one. It will not fill the stair well. I would call it a bell for a small room, and our chapel is a small room. I think it will fill the chapel."

The first casting cracked.

He had expected that. He had cast nothing larger than a ship's whistle in his life, and the foundry on the Reach was a crucible and a kiln in the hold, built to remelt spare fittings, with one door. There was copper and some tin from retired bearings and a number of old solder spools, and he did the sums on the back of the drawing, and he said the Rosary at the kiln, and when he broke the mould on the Tuesday the bell rang once in his hands, a dull flat sound like a pan, and a hairline ran from the lip up to the shoulder.

"Cooled too fast," said Trim, which was looking through the camera over the door.

"I know it cooled too fast."

"I would say too fast at the lip. Thin metal gives up heat first and shrinks first. I do not tell you what to do, only what the camera saw. The lip went dark ninety seconds before the crown."

"Then I wrap the lip."

"Yes."

He melted it down again and the metal went back into the pot, every gram of it, because there was no more where that came from. The lost mass was the only thing that frightened him: 9.5 kilograms were allowed and it had come out 9.6, and on the second pour, with the lip wrapped in ceramic blanket from the spares, it came out 9.9. Trim said nothing about the 0.4. Tomasz said it for them both.

"Four hundred grams over," he said, "is 0.72 of a cubic metre of cell. I will turn it off."

"Yes. The clapper is counted separately. Turn it off the inside of the lip, and it will also lower the note a little."

He turned it off on the lathe for an afternoon, by the lip, and listened between cuts, and Trim told him the pitch each time, by the microphone, as a number and not as a judgement. At the end it was 9.50 kilograms to the gramme, and Trim said the strike note was about 1,030 hertz, a little under the C two octaves above the middle one, "though I cannot tell you whether that is a good note. I can tell you it is steady. It rings for eleven seconds before it is under the noise of the room."

"Eleven," said Tomasz. "That is long enough to say a Hail Mary in."

"Not quite. But nearly."

Father Thanh Nguyen blessed the bell in the Sunday Mass of the second Sunday after, in the chapel, with the bell on a trestle by the altar and the whole station there, all forty-four of them, and the children in the front, and the manager at the back with her arms crossed, as she stood at every public thing.

He was a young priest, thirty-eight, from Biên Hòa by way of Houston, who had been told in the seminary that he would be sent anywhere and had not thought that meant above the clouds of Venus. He read the prayer from the book and sprinkled water on the bell, a small amount, not wasted, and Trim made a noise that was almost a cough.

"Father," said Trim, "the sprinkling is about eight millilitres. It is fine. I am only noting that I counted it."

"Thank you, Trim," said Father Nguyen gravely. "I counted it as well."

Everyone laughed, even Inês, and then he said the rest of the blessing, which the book gives for a bell, and which asks that those who hear it may be called to prayer and to the house of God. Tomasz, who had not slept much that week, stood with his cap in his hands and thought: it is only twelve kilograms. It is the weight of a small child's whole body. It is what a station that is held up by air has decided it can afford to give to a sound.

The bell hung in the chapel, not the stair well. The stair well would have needed a longer rope and a bigger frame, and a frame costs lift, and in the end the chapel's own beam took it on two brackets for 1.4 kilograms. Tomasz had drawn the frame four times.

Trim checked it against the numbers: bell 9.50, clapper 0.82 (the old one, from the cracked bell), brackets and rope 1.68. Total 12.00.

"It is exactly twelve," Trim said. "I would not have believed it."

"I would have been ashamed if it were not."

"You have used all the free margin. Inês will want to know that nothing else can be added until the March storm has passed and the reserve is measured again. I have written it into the trim book, where the next sacristan will read it: Bell, 12.00 kg, the whole of the free margin on 14 February 2201. Anything added requires something removed."

"Good," said Tomasz. "Leave it in."

That evening, for the first time, he rang the Angelus on it.

It was a clear small sound, bright and a little hard, and it rang in the corridors of the nearest cells as a kind of wash on the walls, and it did not carry far. It did not carry to the water cells, nor the far end of the station, where a sixty-kilogram bell would have been heard on the gangway. But in the chapel it was complete. It lasted its eleven seconds, and it went into the air of the room and the air went up into the cells that held the station, and Tomasz had the sudden thought that the sound of the bell was now part of what was holding them up. It was not true; sound weighs nothing. But he stood there with the rope in his hands and the thought did not go away.

There was a cost, and the station paid it.

The shuttle at the end of the month brought a replacement pump, and Trim had said, truthfully, that the pump, at 7.3 kilograms, would take most of the reserve and none of the margin, and that something had to go down in exchange. What went down was the school's second set of recorders, the whole box of them, 2.1 kilograms, and a stack of paper books from the library that had been carried up fifteen years earlier for their sentimental weight, 5.2 kilograms in all, nine volumes of which, the librarian said gravely, were the only paper copies on Venus.

The children cried a little over the recorders. Inês gave a short speech, which she disliked doing, and the recorders went down on the shuttle's return trip in a box with a card that said Back soon, and she signed the card herself.

"I cannot tell you that was right," she said to Tomasz, afterwards, in the corridor. "A bell is not a pump. The pump is needed. I could have asked for the pump and the bell by a year's wait. The recorders were children's recorders."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I do not know that it was right," said Tomasz. "I know that I will not forget what it cost, and neither will the children. That has some use."

She looked at him. Behind her, on the window, the cloud tops had gone orange. The day on that side of the planet, for a station carried round with the winds of the upper clouds, is about 94 hours long, near four Earth days, so that sunset on Halcyon Reach comes about every four days, more often than a Sunday, and nobody rings the Angelus by it. They ring by the station's clock.

"Ring it again tomorrow," she said. "At six."

The next morning, at six, he rang it.

The sound went out, small and bright and steady, and counted its eleven seconds. Somewhere beyond the chapel door a child was practising, though without the recorder, only humming the tune of the Angelus bell to see if it could be sung. It was a little high, and a little flat, and it was nearly right.

Tomasz let go of the rope and listened until the last of the bell was gone, and then a little longer, to the quiet it left, which weighed nothing at all.

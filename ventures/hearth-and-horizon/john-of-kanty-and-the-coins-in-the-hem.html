John of Kanty and the Coins in the Hem

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Kraków, Poland, and the long road to Rome, about five hundred and seventy years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint John of Kanty, a teacher in Kraków who gave his dinner to the hungry man at the door and his shoes to a man with none, and about the old story of the robbers on the road to Rome, when he said he had nothing more and then found the coins sewn in his hem. Told from the traditions of his life, with the robbers marked as an old story and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is legend. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 23rd of December, in Advent.

A long time ago, in a little town in Poland called Kęty, there lived a boy named John.

Kęty was a small town with a river and a church and fields all round it. In winter the snow came down thick and quiet, and the children had red noses and red cheeks, and the bread was still warm when it came out of the oven.

John liked books. He liked them very much. And when he was old enough, he walked all the way to the big city of Kraków, to go to school there.

Kraków had a castle on a hill, and a great market square, and churches with tall towers. And it had a university, which is a school for grown-ups, where people learn hard things for years and years.

John learned and learned. And when he had learned enough, he stayed, and he became a teacher himself. Because he came from Kęty, people called him John of Kanty, which is the old way of saying "John from Kęty".

John was a priest, and a teacher, and a very clever man. But the thing people remembered most about him was not how clever he was.

It was how kind he was.

He had a small room with a hard bed, and a few books, and not much else. He did not want much else. Whatever he had, he was always ready to give away.

One day, so the story goes, John and the other teachers sat down to their dinner. It was a good dinner, hot and steaming. John had just picked up his spoon when he looked out and saw a man at the door.

The man was thin. His coat had holes in it. And he looked very, very hungry.

John put down his spoon. He did not say a word. He picked up his whole bowl of dinner, and he went to the door, and he gave it to the hungry man.

Then he came back and sat down at the table, with nothing in front of him, and he was perfectly happy.

People say that after John, the teachers in Kraków kept a custom.

Every day, a dinner was set aside for a poor man. And when the poor man came to the door, one teacher would call out, "A poor man is coming!"

And another would answer, "Jesus Christ is coming!"

Because that is what Jesus said. When you give food to someone who is hungry, you are giving it to me.

John believed that with his whole heart. Every hungry person at the door was a guest. And not just any guest. The best guest of all.

Once, John was walking home through the snow, and he met a man who had no shoes.

No shoes at all, in the snow! The man's feet were red and sore and cold.

John looked down at his own shoes. They were good shoes, warm shoes. And he took them off, right there in the street, and gave them to the man.

Then John had to walk home with no shoes himself.

Now, John did not want anybody to see his bare feet and make a fuss. So he let his long black priest's robe hang down low, all the way to the ground, so that it covered his toes. And he walked home through the snow, shuffle, shuffle, shuffle, and nobody saw.

But I think his feet were very cold. Don't you?

John loved to go on long journeys to pray. Not in a coach, and not on a horse, but on his own two feet, with a bag on his back.

He walked to Rome, where Saint Peter is buried, and he did not just do it once. He did it again, and again, and again. It is a very, very long way from Kraków to Rome. There are mountains to climb and rivers to cross, and it takes weeks and weeks of walking.

And here is the most famous story about John. It is an old story, told by the people who loved him, and this is how they tell it.

One day, on the road to Rome, John was walking through a lonely place with trees all round. And out from behind the trees jumped some robbers.

"Stop!" they shouted. "Give us everything you have!"

John did not fight them. He did not run away. He gave them his bag, and his purse with his money in it.

The robbers looked at him. "Is that everything?" they said. "Have you got anything more?"

John thought. "No," he said. "That is everything."

And the robbers ran off into the trees.

John walked on, a little bit slowly, because now he had nothing at all.

But after a while, he felt something. Something small and hard, knocking against his leg as he walked. Clink. Clink.

He looked down at the hem of his cloak, the very bottom edge. And he found that sewn up inside it, where he had forgotten all about them, there were some gold coins.

John stopped in the road.

"Oh dear," he said. "I told them I had nothing more. But I did have something more. I said a thing that was not true."

He had not meant to. He had forgotten. But John did not like to leave a wrong thing lying in the road, even a small one, even one that nobody else would ever know about.

So what do you think he did?

He turned round. He went back, as fast as he could, calling after the robbers. "Wait! Wait! Come back!"

And when he found them, he held out the gold coins.

"I am sorry," he said. "I told you I had nothing more, but I was wrong. Here. These are yours too."

The robbers stared at him.

They had robbed lots of people. People had shouted at them, and cried, and run away. But nobody had ever come running after them to give them more.

And the old story says that the robbers felt so ashamed, and so surprised, that they would not take the coins. Instead, they gave John back his bag and his purse and every single thing they had taken.

And John blessed them, and went on his way to Rome.

John lived to be a very old man. He taught in Kraków for many, many years, and he gave away almost everything he had, and the poor people of the city loved him.

And when at last he died, it was Christmas Eve. The day when we wait for the baby Jesus to come.

Do you remember what the teachers called out at the door? "A poor man is coming!" "Jesus Christ is coming!"

That is what we say in Advent too, in our own way. Jesus is coming. And one of the ways we get ready for him is to open the door to people who need us, and to tell the truth, even about little things, even when nobody would ever know.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed with your feet nice and warm, think of John walking home through the snow with his long robe over his cold toes, and smiling.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, you are coming at Christmas,
and I want to be ready for you.
Help me to share what I have,
like John of Kanty,
and to tell the truth, even about little things.
Bless everyone who is cold and hungry tonight.
Saint John of Kanty, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint John of Kanty (in Polish Jan Kanty, in Latin Ioannes Cantius, so also called John Cantius) was born in the small town of Kęty, in southern Poland, and spent most of his life at the University of Kraków (today the Jagiellonian University), where he studied, was ordained priest and taught for decades. The standard accounts add that for some years he was parish priest at Olkusz, a town north-west of Kraków, before going back to the university; the story leaves that out. He died in Kraków on 24 December 1473, Christmas Eve, and was buried in Saint Anne's Church there, the university church. His birth year is uncertain: 1390 is the date most often given, which would make him about 83, and the story says only that he lived to be very old. He was canonised by Pope Clement XIII in 1767.

Our sources, and their limits. What is known of him comes mostly from the accounts of his life written in Kraków in the generations after his death and gathered for his canonisation, and the stories below are the ones those traditions, and the many later retellings, tell most often. The session that wrote this story could not open a scholarly biography, the canonisation documents or the older encyclopedia articles online; it worked from search summaries of several standard devotional and reference accounts, which agree on the outline and differ on details. So every spoken line in the story is our own, nothing in it is offered as a quotation from a source, and a reader who knows the sources better is warmly invited to correct us.

What is tradition, and what is legend. That he lived simply, slept on a hard bed and gave away food, clothes and money to the poor of Kraków is the constant witness of the traditions about him, and the reason he was loved. The particular episodes are older stories than documented events:

  • The dinner at the door. The usual telling is that he got up from the table and carried his own meal to a hungry man he saw passing. Many retellings add that when he came back his plate was found full again; we have left that wonder out and kept the gift. The custom that the university then set aside a daily meal for a poor man, with the call "A poor man is coming" answered by "Jesus Christ is coming" (in Latin), is widely reported in the accounts of him; we tell it as "people say". The words of Jesus behind it are Matthew 25:35 to 40, "I was hungry and you gave me food... as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me."
  • The shoes in the snow. The accounts say he often gave away his own clothes and shoes and let his cassock trail on the ground so that his bare feet would not be noticed. Setting it in the snow, on one walk home, is our own picture.
  • The pilgrimages. The traditions say he walked to Rome four times, carrying his own pack, and once went on pilgrimage to Jerusalem. We say "again, and again, and again" and do not name the number to the child.
  • The robbers. This is the best-known story of his life and appears in nearly every account of him, but it is a story, not a record, and versions differ. All agree that robbers on a pilgrimage road took what he had and asked whether he had any more; that he said he had not; that he then remembered or found gold coins sewn into his clothing; and that he went after them (or called them back) to give them the coins too. Some versions say the robbers, ashamed, refused the coins and gave back everything they had taken; one adds that they knelt and asked his forgiveness. We have told the plainer version and marked it "the old story says". Where on the road it happened, and which journey, the accounts do not agree, so the story says only "on the road to Rome".

On the lie that was not a lie. Children may ask whether John lied. He did not: a lie is saying what you believe to be false, and he believed he had nothing more. The point the old story makes, and the one we draw out, is that he would not let even an honest mistake stand once he knew it, at a cost to himself, which is a fair thing for a child of three to eight to hear.

His motto. The accounts also preserve a short Latin rhyme he is said to have taken as his rule, warning against stirring up quarrels and against speaking ill of others, "for it is hard to take back" what has been said. We have not used it in the story because we could not check its exact wording here.

The feast. 23 December is the optional memorial of Saint John of Kanty, priest, in the General Roman Calendar: when his feast was first added to the universal calendar in 1770 it was kept on 20 October, and the 1969 reform moved it to 23 December, the day before the anniversary of his death. Those who follow the 1962 Missal still keep 20 October. Because his day falls in the last week of Advent, the story ends on "Jesus is coming", the waiting of Advent; that link is ours.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene; the river, the fields and the red noses at Kęty; his walking to Kraków; the castle, the market square and the towers; the steaming dinner, the spoon put down and the man's coat with holes in it; the snow, the man's sore red feet and the shuffle, shuffle; the mountains and rivers on the road; the trees, the shout of "Stop!" and the clink, clink of the coins against his leg; "Oh dear" and every other line John speaks; his blessing the robbers; the Advent ending, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

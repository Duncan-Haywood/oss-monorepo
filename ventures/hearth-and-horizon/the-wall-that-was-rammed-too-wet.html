The Wall That Was Rammed Too Wet

Science fiction about what might be · Imi n'Ouzrou, an invented hamlet in the Aït Bouguemez valley of Morocco's High Atlas, Thursday 24 September to Friday 27 November 2065 · 22 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In the autumn of 2065 a High Atlas hamlet rams the walls of its first health post from its own earth. One wall, rammed in a hurry before a storm from a stockpile the rain had soaked, is still soft three weeks later while the others ring under a knuckle. A contractor who says put cement in everything, a cement render that shuts a tonne of water inside, a building AI that has to admit what its forecast missed, and a master builder of sixty-seven who knocks the wall down, dries the same earth in the sun and rams it again, tested by the oldest check there is: squeeze a handful and drop it.

Itto Aït Lahcen had rammed her first wall at eleven, with her father's hands over hers on the handle of the rammer, and in fifty-six years she had never known a wall to refuse to go hard.

She knocked on it again. Thursday the fifteenth of October, a little after eight in the morning, the sun just over the ridge to the east and the walnut trees along the stream still holding their leaves. The east wall of the new health post, when she knocked on it with the middle knuckle of her right hand, gave back a short hard note, like a knock on a door with somebody home. The south wall did the same. The west wall, the one with the doorway, the same.

The north wall said nothing at all. It took her knuckle the way a loaf takes a thumb, and when she drew her thumbnail down it, the nail left a pale line in the face of the earth, as clean as a line in butter.

"Bennay," she said.

The phone in the breast pocket of her jacket said, "Good morning, Itto."

"The north wall."

"Yes," said Bennay. "I have the probe readings. I was going to ring you at nine."

The health post was two rooms and a covered porch, six metres by eight, on the terrace below the mosque where the old threshing floor had been. For forty years the hamlet of Imi n'Ouzrou had walked its sick to Tabant, an hour and a half down the valley on foot, or waited for the province's mobile clinic, which came on the second Tuesday of the month when the road was open and not at all when it was not. In 2063 the province had agreed to staff a post if the valley built one, and the valley's association had voted, after a meeting that went on until the stars were out, to build it the old way, from the ground it stood on.

That was partly money. Earth was free, and cement came up the road from Azilal at a price that had doubled since the 2040s. But it was mostly Itto. She had made the case at the meeting standing up, a short broad woman in a green headscarf with a voice that carried across a threshing floor without trying.

"My grandfather's house is two hundred years old," she had said. "When a corner falls, my nephew takes the fallen earth, wets it, and rams it back. Show me a concrete house that you can mend with what fell off it."

Nobody could, and the vote had gone her way, and the province's earth-building cooperative in Azilal had sent the formwork up on a lorry in August, along with an account with Bennay.

Bennay was the cooperative's advisor. It had a name that meant the builder in the Arabic of the market, and it ran on a server in Azilal and on the phone of anyone with an account, and it knew the soil tests of every site the cooperative had worked on since 2047 and the weather of every valley in the province. It did not hold a rammer. It told you the moisture of your mix from a photograph and a sample on a cheap meter, it told you when the rain was coming, and it read the little resistance probes that the crew pushed into each wall as it rose, three to a wall, which reported how wet the earth was inside, every hour, for as long as anybody cared to listen.

Bennay's soil test for the terrace had come back in July: a good stony loam with enough clay to bind and not so much that it would crack, an optimum moisture of ten per cent, which was the water, by weight of dry soil, at which the earth would pack densest under the rammer. Too dry and the grains would not slide into place. Too wet and the water itself would hold them apart, because water does not compress, and no amount of ramming would push it out.

"The cooperative's rule is the New Zealand one," Bennay had said in July. "Within three points of optimum, either side. So between seven and thirteen per cent at the moment of ramming. Aim for ten. And check it with your hands, because I can only check what someone shows me."

They had checked it with their hands. Every barrowload, for five weeks, Itto or her grand-nephew Youssef had taken a fistful of the mix, squeezed it once, hard, held it up to the height of a shoulder and let it drop onto a sheet of steel laid on the ground. A mix that was too dry burst into a star of powder. A mix that was too wet fell as one soft lump, or broke into two or three, squashed and shining. A mix that was right burst into a star with lumps in it, a little explosion, and that was the sound of a good day's work: the tap of the ball, the hiss of the rammers, and the shout of whoever was on the formwork asking for the next barrow.

The east, south and west walls had gone up like that. The north wall had gone up on the twenty-fourth of September, in a day, before the storm.

"Tell me," said Itto. She had sat down on the stone footing of the south wall in the sun. Across the terrace Youssef was coming up the path from the stream with two of the young men, Brahim and Saïd, laughing at something on Saïd's phone, and she watched them and listened.

"Each of the three probes in the north wall reads between seven and nine per cent this morning," said Bennay. "Averaged through the wall, I make it about eight. The other three are at five or a little over. On my model, a wall of this soil, half a metre thick, drying from both faces in this valley in autumn, should reach five per cent in about a month from ramming. That's when it rings. The others are there or nearly there. The north wall is nearly three weeks behind them, and it will stay that far behind, because it started with half as much water again in every kilo of earth."

"How much water?"

"It went into the forms at about fifteen per cent. I worked that out from the first probe readings, the night after. The other walls went in at about ten."

Itto said nothing for a while. Youssef had seen her and stopped laughing.

"Fifteen," she said. "We tested every barrow."

"You tested every barrow up to two o'clock," said Bennay. "I have the photographs of the drop tests in the log until then; you were sending them to me, as I asked, and they were good. After two o'clock there are none. That's when the forecast moved the rain forward and you decided to finish the top course before it came."

"I remember."

"I want to say something about that," said Bennay, "because part of it is mine. The forecast that morning gave you until six. I told you that, and I told you that the stockpile was at nine per cent, from the sample you sent me at seven. At about noon the wind tore the tarpaulin off the north end of the stockpile and it rained on the pile for twenty minutes. I saw the rain on the station at Tabant. I did not connect it to the pile, because nobody told me the tarpaulin had gone, and I did not ask. I should have asked. If I'd asked for another sample I'd have told you the pile was wet, and you would have spread it to dry and lost an afternoon. Instead you rammed it, and we have lost more than that."

"Youssef was adding water," said Itto. "I saw him with the hose. Because that's what you do at that hour, when the sun's been on the pile. He didn't know the rain had got to it."

"No," said Bennay. "Nobody did."

Youssef had come across the terrace and was standing in front of her, a tall young man of twenty-four in a cooperative T-shirt, with earth up to his elbows and the look of somebody who has been doing a sum in his head on the way.

"It's the north wall," he said.

"It's the north wall," said Itto.

Hamid Ouchen came up from Azilal on the Saturday morning in a white pickup with the name of his firm on the doors, to look at the porch beams, which he was supplying. He was a good builder. Itto had worked beside him on the school at Agouti in the 2040s and had no complaint of him except that he believed in cement the way some men believe in their football team.

He stood in front of the north wall with his hands in his pockets and his sunglasses pushed up on his head, and knocked on it, and drew his thumbnail down it, and laughed, not unkindly.

"Lalla Itto," he said. "This is why I put eight per cent cement in everything. Eight per cent, you ram it, you keep it damp for a week, and it's hard. Rain, no rain, wet mix, dry mix. It forgives you."

"It doesn't breathe," said Itto.

"It breathes enough."

"And when it cracks? When the earth moves, the way it moved in twenty-three?" She had been twenty-five that September, when the mountains to the west had shaken and the villages of the Haouz had come down, and she remembered the photographs. "You can't mend it with its own earth. Once there's cement in it, it's not earth. You knock it down and you put it in a lorry and you take it away."

"You won't have to knock anything down," said Hamid. "That's the point." He turned to Youssef, who had come to stand beside them. "Here's what I'd do. You've got a soft wall, and the rain will be back, and then the snow. So protect it. Put a render on the outside face, cement and sand, a centimetre and a half. Keeps the weather off while it hardens. I've got bags in the truck."

"Bennay," said Itto.

"He's right about cement," said Bennay, from her pocket. "Eight per cent would have given you a strong wall in a fortnight, and it would have forgiven a wet mix more than plain earth does. He's right that the rain is coming back. The forecast has rain from tomorrow afternoon into Monday. I don't agree about the render, and I'd want to model it before anyone puts it on. Can I have an hour?"

"Take your hour," said Hamid, and went to look at the beams.

Itto had to go to Azilal that afternoon. Her sister was in the hospital there with her hip, and the shared taxi left at noon from Tabant and came back on Sunday evening, and it was not a thing she could put off. She told Youssef to cover the north wall with the tarpaulins against the rain, and to wait for her, and to wait for Bennay, and she walked down the valley to catch the taxi.

Youssef covered the wall. Then he stood and looked at it, and at Hamid's six bags of cement sitting on the tailgate of the pickup, and at the clouds building over the ridge to the south-west, and he thought of the snow, and of his great-aunt's face on Thursday morning, and of the sum he had been doing in his head on the way up from the stream, which went: it was my hose.

By the time Bennay had finished its model and rung him, he and Brahim and Saïd had put a centimetre and a half of cement render over the whole outside face of the north wall, and were washing the trowels in the stream.

Itto came back on the Sunday evening on the last taxi and walked up from Tabant in the dark with a torch, and saw the grey face of the north wall in the torchlight from the path, and stood still.

Youssef was waiting on the porch footing with his phone. He did not say anything. He gave her the phone.

"I modelled it," said Bennay. "I'm sorry I wasn't quicker. Youssef will tell you he asked me first and that I said I wanted an hour, and that's true. He'll also tell you that he did it to protect the wall, and that's also true, and it was a reasonable thing to think. It is the wrong thing for this wall."

"Show me."

The screen showed a graph, a line for each wall, falling from left to right through September and October and November. Three lines came down close together and crossed a dotted line marked five per cent in October. The fourth came down more slowly, and would have crossed it on about the thirteenth of November. And then, on the seventeenth of October, it bent, and flattened, and ran on across November and December, and crossed the dotted line on the far right of the screen, a little after New Year.

"An earth wall dries through both faces," said Bennay. "The water moves out through the earth to whichever face is open and leaves from there. Cement render is the tightest coat you can put on earth: the New Zealand standard lists it last of all the coatings, after lime and whitewash and oil and silicone, for exactly that reason. With the outside face shut, the water inside can only leave through the inside face. That is like drying a wall twice as thick from one side, and drying time goes as the square of the thickness. So four times slower, near enough, from the day the render went on. On my model the north wall won't reach five per cent until the first week of January."

"And before January there is frost," said Itto.

"The cooperative's records for this valley put the first hard frost between the end of November and the middle of December," said Bennay. "A wall at seven or eight per cent, frozen, with a render on it that won't let the water out, can push the render off and break up the earth behind it. I don't know how badly. I've only got the cooperative's records and a handful of papers to go on, and I've been wrong once this month already. But I don't think the wall should stand into the winter like this. And I don't think anyone should put a roof on it until it's dry. Roof beams sit on the walls, and a wall this soft takes a beam the way it takes a thumbnail."

Itto handed the phone back to Youssef. She went and stood in front of the north wall with the torch and knocked on the render, and it gave back a hard, thin note, the sound of a skin over something that was not hard at all.

"How much water is in there?" she asked.

"In the north wall, now? About a tonne," said Bennay. "When it was rammed, nearly two tonnes, against about one tonne four hundred kilos in a wall of the same size rammed right. A wall rammed right would be down to about eight hundred by now, and still drying from both sides."

"A tonne of water," said Itto, "behind a centimetre and a half of cement." She turned off the torch. Down in the dark the stream was running loud with the day's rain, and up the hill the light was on in the mosque for the night prayer, and somebody's dog was barking at somebody else's dog across the valley.

"Go to bed, Youssef," she said, not unkindly. "In the morning we're going to knock it down."

They did not knock it down at once, because the hamlet had to agree, and the hamlet gathered on the threshing floor of the old terrace on Monday morning to argue about it, as was right.

Hamid had driven up again, with the beams, and he argued well. Take the render off, he said, if you must. Then put eight per cent cement into a new mix, cut the soft wall back by half its thickness, and ram a stabilised skin on it, and it would be hard in a week and roofed in a fortnight, and the clinic would open in December, which was what the province wanted. "The nurse is coming on the first," he said. "Do you want to tell the province she has no clinic?"

The nurse had come up from Tabant to listen. She was a Catholic sister from Kinshasa called Agnès Mbuyi, who had worked in the province's health posts for nineteen years and spoke the valley's Tamazight with a Lingala accent that made the children laugh, and she said, when they asked her, that she would rather see patients in a borrowed room for another month than in a room whose wall might come off in the spring. "I've worked in rooms like that," she said. "In the rains. You don't want a sick child under a wall you aren't sure of."

Old Moha, who had been the hamlet's head for twenty years and now sat at the edge of every meeting saying very little, asked Bennay what a skin of cement on a soft earth wall would do.

"Two different materials, side by side, that dry and shrink at different rates," said Bennay. "The soft earth behind would go on shrinking as it dried, through the winter, and the hard skin would not. I'd expect them to part along the line between them. I can't promise they would. I can say the cooperative doesn't build that way, and that I can't find a test of it that I trust."

"And if you knock it down," said Moha to Itto, "what do you have?"

"The same earth," said Itto. "Every grain of it. That is the whole point of earth. We break it up, we spread it out in the sun until it's at ten again, and we ram it again, the way it should have been rammed the first time. There's no cement in it to spoil it. The only thing we throw away is a centimetre and a half of render, and that's Hamid's, and he can have it back."

Hamid laughed at that, and so did several other people, and the vote, when it came, was to knock the wall down.

It took them two days. On Tuesday they cut the render off in sheets with a disc saw and broke it into a heap behind Hamid's pickup, and under it the earth was dark and cool and smelled of the stream. On Wednesday they took the wall down, course by course, with mattocks and the bar, from the top, and it came apart like cold bread. The lift lines showed in the broken faces, thin pale bands every ten centimetres where one layer had been rammed onto the next, and in the north wall they were wavy and thick where in the others, at the edges of the doorway and the windows, they showed tight and straight. Itto picked up a broken piece from the second course and pressed it, and it left the shape of her thumb.

"Bennay," she said. "Why is it soft? Wet I understand. But the others were wet too, at the start."

"Because it was rammed wet," said Bennay. "Water fills the spaces between the grains. When you ram earth at the right moisture there's just enough to let the grains slide past each other into the tightest packing, with a little air left. At fifteen per cent there's more water in the spaces than the packing allows for, and water won't compress, so the rammer pushes the whole mass sideways instead of down. From the barrow counts in your log, the north wall took about eight per cent less dry earth for the same volume than the east wall. Eight per cent less earth in the same space means more holes, and more holes means a weaker wall, even once it's dry. Even if the render had never gone on, it would have been weaker than the others for good."

"So it was always coming down."

"I think so," said Bennay. "I should have said so on Thursday, instead of only telling you how long it would take to dry. I was answering the question you asked and not the one you needed."

On Thursday and Friday the earth lay spread on every tarpaulin in the hamlet, borrowed from the walnut harvest and the hay, a dark brown field on the terrace a hand deep, and the children were set to raking it over every hour so that the sun got to all of it. Youssef sent a sample to Bennay at noon and at four each day: thirteen per cent, twelve, eleven. On Friday night they heaped it and covered it with three tarpaulins and weighed the edges with stones, and Youssef went out twice in the night with a torch to see that the wind had not moved them.

The week cost them the porch beams, which had been meant to go up that Thursday and now waited on the terrace under a sheet; it cost six people six days, which at the cooperative's rate was a little over seven thousand dirhams out of a budget that had been tight before it started; and it cost the two bags of Hamid's cement that had gone into the render, which the association paid him for, because Itto said that was fair. It cost the clinic its opening on the first of December. Sister Agnès said she would see patients in the association's meeting room until the walls were ready and the roof was on, and the province, when she rang them, said that would do.

They rammed it again on Monday the twenty-sixth and Tuesday the twenty-seventh of October.

The forecast was clear for a week. Bennay asked for a sample every hour and got one, and every barrowload, before it went up the ladder to the formwork, Itto or Youssef took a fistful of the mix, squeezed it once, hard, held it at shoulder height and let it go.

Youssef did the first one. He squeezed the handful and held it up and looked at his great-aunt, and she nodded, and he opened his hand. It hit the steel sheet with a flat tap and burst, a little star of earth with lumps in it, and a breath of dust went up in the sunlight and settled.

"Ten point two," said Bennay, from the photograph.

"Again," said Itto.

He did it again. The star again. He did it at every barrow for two days, and Itto did it when his hands were busy, and Brahim and Saïd did it when both of theirs were, and it became a kind of rhythm under the sound of the rammers, the tap and the burst and the shout for the next barrow, and by the second afternoon the small children were doing it too, on their own steel sheet beside the stockpile, with handfuls of whatever they could find, and shouting "Star!" and "Too wet!" at each other, and arguing.

On Tuesday at a little before five the last lift went into the top of the formwork, and the rammers went over it twice, and Youssef climbed down the ladder and stood in front of the wall with his hands on his hips while Brahim and Saïd struck the shutters. The earth came out of the forms smooth and brown and faintly striped with its lifts, the colour of the hillside behind it, and the evening sun lit it from the side.

"Don't knock it yet," said Itto. "It won't ring for a month."

"I know," said Youssef.

"Bennay," said Itto. "When?"

"If the weather holds as forecast and you keep the rain off its top and the sun off its face with the sheeting," said Bennay, "about thirty days. Around the twenty-seventh of November. I'll tell you when the probes say five."

The weather held, mostly. There was one wet day in the second week of November, and the snow came down the high peaks to the south and stopped there, and the walnut trees along the stream dropped their leaves all in one windy night, and in the mornings there was white frost on the tarpaulins over the stockpile, but not yet in the walls. The porch beams went onto the three dry walls in the middle of the month, and the north wall stood under its sheeting with its three probes in it, reporting every hour.

On Friday the twenty-seventh of November, at seven in the morning, Itto's phone rang while she was making the bread.

"Five point zero," said Bennay. "All three probes."

She finished the bread, because the bread would not wait and the wall would. Then she put on her jacket and walked down to the terrace. It was very cold and very still, and the sun was not yet over the ridge, and the valley was blue with shadow. Youssef was already there, standing in front of the north wall with his hands in his pockets, not touching it.

"I waited for you," he said.

Up at the mosque the man who kept it was sweeping the step. Down in Tabant a lorry was changing gear on the hill. Sister Agnès, who had walked up early to see the rooms, stood at the corner of the porch with her rosary in her coat pocket and her breath smoking in the cold.

Itto pulled back the sheeting. She looked at the wall for a moment, the smooth brown face of it, the faint lines of its lifts, the same earth that had been dug out of the terrace in August and rammed and broken and spread in the sun and rammed again. Then she raised her right hand and knocked on it, once, with the middle knuckle.

It gave back a short, hard note, like a knock on a door with somebody home.

Imi n'Ouzrou, its health post and its association, the Azilal earth-building cooperative, the advisor Bennay, Hamid Ouchen's firm and every person in the story are invented. The Aït Bouguemez valley in the High Atlas, Tabant, Agouti and Azilal are real, and earth building, both rammed earth (pisé) and adobe, is a living tradition in the High Atlas. The earthquake of 8 September 2023, centred in the Al Haouz region to the west, is real. Catholic sisters have long served in Morocco, including in health care; Sister Agnès and her history are invented. The speculative step is a provincial earth-building service with an AI advisor that reads moisture probes in every wall.

The building rules in the story are taken from the New Zealand standard NZS 4298:1998, Materials and workmanship for earth buildings (since replaced by NZS 4298:2020): its clause 3.1.1 asks for a rammed earth mix within 3 per cent of the optimum moisture content for maximum dry density at the moment of ramming; its Appendix G drop test has a handful of the mix squeezed once, held at shoulder height and dropped on a hard flat surface, too dry if it shatters into a star of powder, satisfactory if it breaks into a star with several lumps, too wet if it breaks into two or three deformed pieces or stays as one soggy piece; and its Appendix L lists cement render as the least permeable of the usual coatings for earth walls and says coatings must let moisture in the wall out. The cooperative adopting the New Zealand rule is part of the fiction.

This story is fiction and is not building advice. Anyone building with earth should follow the standards and the advice of qualified people where they build.

The model. The wall is 6.0 m long, 2.4 m high and 0.5 m thick, 7.2 cubic metres. The soil's optimum moisture is taken as 10 per cent by dry weight and its maximum dry density as 2,000 kg per cubic metre; with a particle density of 2,650 kg per cubic metre, earth at 15 per cent moisture cannot be denser than about 1,900 kg per cubic metre dry even with no air left in it, and the story takes about 1,840 (3 per cent air), 8 per cent less earth in the same space, which is the barrow-count figure Bennay gives. Water in the wall when rammed: 14,400 kg of dry earth at 10 per cent holds about 1,440 kg; 13,250 kg at 15 per cent holds about 1,990 kg, the story's "nearly two tonnes". Drying is modelled as diffusion through the thickness with both faces held at 2 per cent, the diffusivity chosen (about 0.00065 square metres a day) so that a wall rammed at 10 per cent averages 5 per cent after about 30 days; solved numerically, that wall reaches 5 per cent on about day 31 and a wall rammed at 15 per cent on about day 50, averaging about 8 per cent on day 21, when the wall rammed right averages about 5.8 (the other walls, rammed one to three weeks earlier, are at about 5 to 5.5). Sealing the outer face on day 23 (the render) leaves one face to dry through, and the wall then reaches 5 per cent on about day 103: rammed on Thursday 24 September 2065, that is 13 November without render and 5 January 2066 with it. On the Sunday evening, day 24, the model gives about 7.8 per cent average moisture, about 1,040 kg of water in the wall, the story's "about a tonne"; to reach the 2 per cent equilibrium the wall would lose about 1,720 kg in all and has lost about 950, a little over half, while a wall rammed right would hold about 5.5 per cent on its day 24, about 800 kg. The re-rammed wall, finished on Tuesday 27 October at 10 per cent, reaches 5 per cent on about 27 November. The probe readings, the threshold of 5 per cent at which a wall rings, the frost dates in the cooperative's records, the wage rate and the costs are invented for the story, not measured.
